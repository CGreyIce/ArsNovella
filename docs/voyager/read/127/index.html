Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 4: The Second Row

Chapter 127

Their Names

7,298 words · 32 min read

They buried him at the evening turn.

Wendy had never seen a burial at sea. She’d read about them, in books, and she’d thought of them as something grand and terrible, with a flag and a drum and somebody playing a long sad note on a pipe. It wasn’t like that. It was quiet, and quick, and practical, the way everything the tide-folk did was practical. It was like watching people put away a boat for the winter.

Pell sewed him in.

He did it on the main deck, in the afternoon, kneeling, with a curved sail-needle as long as his hand and a ball of tarred twine. The canvas was the Patience’s spare foresail, new, never set, still creased from where it had been folded in the sail locker. Marrit had gone down and got it herself. “He made her,” she’d said. “He can have her best.” Nobody had argued.

Pell worked from the feet up. Neat close stitches, the needle going in and out with a small hard sound, tuk, tuk, and his great hands very gentle. At the feet, inside the canvas, a stone. A smooth round grey one from the ballast in the bottom of the hold, which had come from Hythe harbour and had been in the Patience since before she swam. Kip had chosen it. He’d gone down into the hold and come back up with it in both arms, staggering, and given it to Pell without a word.

Before Pell got to the chest, Marrit stopped him.

She knelt on the other side and took her grandfather’s left arm in her hands, out of the canvas, and began to unwind the cords.

All eleven. The brown ones at the wrist first, then up, and up, the black one last, from just under the elbow. She did it slowly. They’d been on him so long that they’d left marks, pale grooves in the brown old skin, a ladder of them up his arm. She held the cords in her lap when she’d done. A tangle of tarred string, eleven lengths, knotted all along.

Jago, standing at the wheel, made a sound.

“They go with him,” he said. Very low. “Marrit. The cord goes with them. Always.”

“Not these,” said Marrit.

“Marrit—“

“They’ve been in the water long enough.” She didn’t look up. “Every one of them. Three hundred years of them. They’ve been in the water long enough, Jago.” Her hands closed on the cords. “These stay up here. With us.”

Jago didn’t say anything else.

Pell sewed. Up over the chest. The shoulders. When he got to the face, he stopped, and looked at Marrit, and she nodded, and he put the last stitch through. Wendy didn’t watch that bit. She’d read about that too, in the books, and she found she didn’t want to see it. She looked at Yan instead.

Yan was standing at the mainmast. Where he’d sat all night. He was looking at the canvas. At the place in the canvas where the old man’s chest was.

Wendy knew what he was looking at. He’d told her on floor 6, on the lip of the pit, vaguely, the way you’d tell somebody about a thing you weren’t sure was real. Everything’s got a light. And she’d said she’d take the shots he couldn’t. She’d meant it. She’d meant it every day since. It was the simplest promise she’d ever made and she’d never once wanted to take it back.

He’d told her the rest on floor 8. River-folk. The light that stayed in them like a boat with no current. The causeway, and the Edge.

And he’d told her this morning, at dawn, in about six words, with his hands shaking. It didn’t rise. It’s still in him.

So she knew what he was looking at. She just couldn’t see it.

She stood next to him and didn’t say anything. She was good at that.

At the turn, Kip rang the bell.

The Patience had a small brass one, hung in a frame by the wheel. Marrit had the tide in her head, the way all of them did; she didn’t need to look at anything. She’d said now, quietly, and Kip had pulled the rope once, and the bell had rung once, a thin clear note out over the deep, and then stopped.

That was all. In Hythe, the tide-bell rang once at every turn. Out here, there was nobody to hear it but them.

Pell and Jago and the Lusk brothers carried him to the rail.

They laid him on a plank there, a long scrubbed board, with one end on the rail and one end on a cask, and his feet toward the sea. The tide-folk stood round it. All seven of the crew, and Marrit. And behind them, a little way back, not too close, the thirty-six. Everybody. Nobody had told them to come. They’d just come.

The sun was going down behind the ship. Behind the singer, somewhere out ahead under the water. The light came along the deck long and gold, the way it had come at dawn, and lay on the canvas, and on Marrit’s hair.

And nobody said anything.

Wendy understood that was how it was done. She could see it in their faces, the tide-folk, even Marrit’s. You stood. You didn’t speak. You put your hand on the canvas, each of you, once. She watched them do it. Pell’s huge hand. Kip’s small one. Ottery, who had come out of the galley and stood at the very end, put hers flat on the canvas over the old man’s feet and left it there a long time, and Wendy remembered Cursiffe telling her, this morning, about a crime against the sea, and Ottery standing over him, and him laughing till he coughed.

Then the plank tipped, and he went, and you didn’t say his name. You went to your cord, afterwards, on your own, and tied a knot in it, and you never said his name again. Not over the water. Because the water hears.

That was how it was done. Three hundred years.

Pell and Jago bent to the end of the plank.

“Corran,” said Marrit.

They stopped.

Marrit was standing at the head of the plank with her grandfather’s eleven cords wound round both her hands. She had her face up. Not looking at the canvas. Looking out, over the rail, at the deep. At the sea going gold and grey and endless to the edge of the sky.

“Corran Patience,” she said. Clearly. Loud. Out over the water. “Shipwright. My grandad. He built this ship in eleven years with his own hands and everybody said he’d never finish her and he finished her and she swam.” Her voice went, and she kept going through it. “He said all of them. Every one. Last night. He wasn’t frightened. He said you’re not to be frightened either. Any of you.” She drew a breath that shook all the way in. “Corran. That’s his name. You can hear it if you like. I don’t care. It’s his.”

Silence.

The ship lifted on the swell, and fell.

And then Pell said, in his rope-voice, very low, “Corran.”

Then Jago. “Corran.” Then Kip, high and cracked, too loud, the way a boy’s voice goes. Then the Lusk brothers, both at once, and they looked at each other. Then Morva, at the end, who never said anything. And Ottery, with her hand still on the canvas over his feet, whispering. “Corran.”

Wendy said it. She hadn’t known she was going to. She heard her own voice do it, and then Salty’s beside her, and Tiger’s, and Cursiffe’s, slow and exact, the way he said everything, and then all of them. Thirty-six voices. Not together. Just everybody, saying it, a word each, the way you’d each put a hand on the canvas. Corran. Corran. Gabe. Rachael, very quietly, with her arms folded. Ice. Wendy heard Ice say it. Hana, crying. Maigo, last, so quiet it was hardly anything at all.

“Corran,” said Yan.

And Pell and Jago lifted the end of the plank, and the old man went over the side, feet first, into the deep, with hardly a splash at all.

Wendy watched the place where he’d gone in.

Nothing. Rings on the water. The gold light on the rings. The ship going on.

Then Yan’s hand closed on her sleeve.

Hard. So hard she felt his fingers through the cloth, cold, the way they went after Shelter, though he hadn’t used Shelter. He was staring over the rail. Down. Into the water, where the canvas had gone. His face had gone from white to something else, something with light in it, and his mouth was open.

“It stopped,” said Yan.

Wendy didn’t move.

“It went down with him,” said Yan. Fast. Barely loud enough for her. “I saw it. Going down through the water like a lamp down a well. Down and down. And it was going to go, Wendy, it was starting to go sideways, I could see it, it was going—“ His hand pointed. North-west. Behind them. He didn’t seem to know he’d done it. “And then she said his name. And everybody said it. And it stopped.”

“Where is it?”

“There.” He was looking down, over the rail, into the water by the hull. “It’s just there. Under the water. Hanging there. Like a lamp somebody’s put down.” He swallowed. “It’s not going anywhere. It doesn’t know where to go. But it’s not going there.”

The ship went on. West, into the last of the sun.

Yan leaned out over the rail, and watched, and didn’t let go of her sleeve.

“It’s coming,” he said, after a moment. Very softly. As if he couldn’t believe it. “It’s following us. It’s coming with the ship.”

Behind them, at the rail, Marrit stood with the eleven cords round her hands, and the tide-folk stood round her, and nobody went to their cord. Wendy watched them not do it. Kip’s fingers went to his wrist, out of habit, and found his two knots, and then he took them away again, and looked at his empty hand, and looked at Marrit.

“No knot?” said Kip. Very small.

Marrit looked down at him.

“You don’t need a knot,” she said, “for somebody whose name you can say.”

“So where would it have gone?” said Wendy.

It was late. The foredeck, in the dark, right up in the bow where the bowsprit went out over the water and the only sound was the sea against the stem. They’d come up here because it was the only place on the ship that was more or less theirs. Yan’s party had been sleeping in the bow since Hythe, five bedrolls in the angle of the bulwarks, and nobody bothered them there.

Salty was asleep. Or doing a very good impression of it, on his back with his arm over his face, which meant he’d be gone again in an hour and back before dawn smelling of the hold. Cursiffe was asleep, properly, sitting up, with the new book on his chest. Tiger was out on the end of the bowsprit, lying along it like a cat on a branch, with his tail hanging down, looking at the singer’s pale shape in the water ahead.

Yan was sitting against the bulwark with his knees up and Suzu round his neck.

He didn’t answer for a long time.

“Where the others went,” he said eventually.

Wendy waited.

“Every one of them,” said Yan. “Three hundred and ten. More. Everybody who ever died on this sea and went in without anybody saying who they were. They burn like river-folk. Low and long. There’s nothing left over when they go. Nothing to carry it up. So it goes down. With them.” He looked out to the north-west, into the dark, the way he’d looked over the rail. “And there’s something down there that wants it.”

Hyvred on the crosstrees, three days ago. There’s something in it. It’s full.

Aleaka, white, with her hand on the rail. It just says more.

“Oh,” said Wendy.

“Yeah.”

She sat with it. She was good at sitting with things. She’d sat with a great many bad things in her life and she had found that they mostly didn’t get better if you jumped up and down about them. They just got louder.

“So the thing that eats ships,” she said, slowly, “has been eating their dead for three hundred years. And they’ve been feeding it. By being frightened. By not saying the names, so the water can’t hear. And it’s because nobody says the names that it can have them.”

“Yes.”

“Like the wax.”

“Yes.”

“That’s horrible.”

“Yes,” said Yan.

“Right,” said Wendy.

She looked at the dark for a while.

“What’s your window say?” she said. “The Edge one. Still?”

“Learn their names.”

“Well,” said Wendy. “You’ve got them. Cursiffe’s got them. Three hundred and eleven. He wrote all night.”

“That’s not learning them,” said Yan. “That’s having them.” He held up his hands in the dark and looked at them. “On floor 8 I walked Neth. I knew her. I’d sat with her. I’d watched Tiger fail her knot thirty-one times. When she went, her light leaned toward me, a bit, before it leaned toward the Edge. Because it knew me. You can’t walk somebody who doesn’t know you’re there.” He put his hands down. “They don’t know me. Any of them. I don’t know them. I know there’s three hundred and eleven of them in a book.”

Wendy thought about it.

“Then learn them,” she said.

He looked at her.

“Properly,” said Wendy. “By heart. All of them. And who they were. Everything Corran said.” She shrugged. “It’s a list. You’re good at lists. You learned every single person’s name in Hearthstead in two days, the first week, and the names of all their dogs, and Tiger still can’t remember the woman at the Copper Spoon.”

“Mother Crane.”

“See.”

Yan didn’t say anything. But something in his face had changed.

“There’s three hundred and eleven,” he said.

“Then I’ll test you,” said Wendy.

Out on the bowsprit, without turning round, Tiger said, “I’ll test you as well. I’ll shout numbers at you. When you’re not expecting it.”

“Please don’t.”

“Seventy-four,” said Tiger.

“I haven’t started.”

“That’s a no, then,” said Tiger, and laughed, and went back to looking at the singer.

Suzu put her small white head on Yan’s collarbone. Wendy couldn’t hear what she said. Nobody could, except Yan. But she saw Yan’s face, and she thought it was probably yes.

Marrit gave him the cords the next morning.

Wendy didn’t hear him ask. She only saw the end of it, from the bow, Marrit and Yan at the wheel at dawn with the tangle of tarred string between them, Marrit looking at him for a long time with her sharp, practical, red-eyed face. She saw Marrit say something short. She saw Yan nod. Then Marrit put the eleven cords into his hands, and closed his hands round them with hers, and let go, and went back to the wheel.

“What did she say?” said Wendy, when he came back.

Yan looked down at the cords.

“Learn them,” he said. “Then give them back.”

He wound them round his own left arm. The way Corran had. He was slower at it, and he got the order wrong the first time and had to start again, the black one at the top, just under the elbow, and then down, newer and browner, to the wrist. They didn’t fit him the way they’d fitted Corran. His arm was a different shape. They sat loose. He didn’t seem to mind.

And then he sat down on the foredeck with his back to the bulwark, in the morning sun, with Cursiffe’s book open on the deck beside him and his right hand on the top of the black cord, and found the first knot with his thumb.

“Treeve Fulmar,” he said. “Gran’s uncle. Crab. Fog. Before she was born.”

Wendy picked up the book.

He was slow at first.

That surprised her. She’d thought, because it was Yan, it would be easy. Yan was good with names. Yan was good with people; it was the whole of him, really, under the jokes, under the talking to the snake. But these weren’t names, to start with. They were just words. Morwen Gull, Bryok Gull, Hedra. They didn’t have faces. They didn’t have voices. They were sounds, written in Cursiffe’s small square hand in a book, with a line or two beside each of them in smaller letters still, and they slid off him like water off a sail.

He got to nine on the first morning and then went back to the start and couldn’t remember four.

“Old Hedra,” said Wendy.

“Old Hedra.” He shut his eyes. “No boat. Walked into the sea at the bottom of the tide and kept walking.” He stopped. “Gran said she was tired.”

“Yes.”

“I don’t want to forget her because I’m bored, Wendy.”

“You’re not bored,” said Wendy. “You’re tired. You sat up all night with a dead man. Go to sleep.”

He didn’t go to sleep. He went back to the start.

It got better.

It got better because of the tide-folk, mostly. Wendy hadn’t expected that either. She’d thought they’d stay away from it. It was their dead, after all, and it was their oldest, deepest rule, and here was a stranger off the stair sitting in the bow of their ship in the sun saying the names out loud, one after another, all day, over the water.

They didn’t stay away.

Kip came first. Of course Kip. He came down the foremast on the first afternoon and sat on the deck at the edge of the bow, the way Cursiffe said he’d sat in the bow on the first day, like a bird that might go at any moment. He listened. He didn’t say anything for an hour. And then Yan got to a name about two-thirds of the way along the fourth cord, Dannet, no boat, sculled off the harbour wall, taught the children, and hesitated over the next bit, and Kip said:

“He had a red hat. He wore it in summer. Even when it was hot. He said the sun couldn’t find him in it.” His thin brown face was very still. “And he couldn’t swim. He taught everybody to scull and he couldn’t swim. He said it made him careful.”

Yan looked at him.

“Thank you,” said Yan.

“A red hat,” said Kip. “Put that in.”

Cursiffe, who’d been asleep against the bulwark with his mouth open, woke up and wrote it in.

After that, they came. Not all at once. One at a time, between jobs, the way people come to a fire. Pell, in the afternoon watch, sitting down on the deck with a sigh like a ship settling at a mooring, telling Yan about Seal Holm. All nineteen. Slowly, in his rope-voice, who each of them had been, which boat, which storm, which one sang, which one cheated at knucklebones, which one had a laugh you could hear across the harbour. Jago, at night, from the wheel, calling a name forward along the deck when Yan got near it, and a line about them, as if he’d been waiting. The Lusk brothers, who between them remembered their grandfather Hob as two completely different men, and argued about it, out loud, in front of everybody, which made Marrit laugh so hard she had to hold onto the wheel.

And Ottery fed him.

That was how Ottery did it. She didn’t come to the bow. She sent things. A bowl of something hot, at noon, on the second day, carried forward by Kip, set down on the deck beside Yan without a word. And on the third day, when Yan went aft to give the bowl back himself, Wendy saw Ottery take it from him at the galley hatch and hold it, and look at him, and say something. One word. Wendy didn’t hear it. She didn’t need to. She’d been there when Corran said it.

The ship changed.

Wendy couldn’t have said when. It wasn’t one thing. It was Kip in the rigging, on the fourth morning, singing. A real song, a tide-folk one, about a boat called the Gannet and a girl who swam like a seal, and Kip putting a name in the middle of it, Tamsin, loud, out over the water, and stopping, and looking down at the deck, terrified, and Ottery coming out of the galley and standing there with her arms folded and looking up at him, and nodding. Once. And Kip going on.

It was Jago at the wheel, saying Bastian would’ve had this sail reefed by now, about nobody, to nobody, just a thing you’d say about somebody you missed.

It was Marrit at dusk on the fourth day, standing at the stern rail looking at the sea behind them, where Yan said Corran was, under the water, following.

“Thirty years,” she said, to Wendy, who was there because she’d come aft to fill a water skin and hadn’t left. “Thirty years I’ve been frightened of a word.” She shook her head slowly. “Jory.” She said it like trying a door that had been locked for a long time and finding it opened. “Jory. Jory. My uncle Jory. He had a coat that smelled of tar.”

“You said,” said Wendy.

“I did, didn’t I,” said Marrit. “I’m going to keep saying it. I’ve got thirty years of it to say.” She looked at the sea. “Is he really there? Grandad. Your lad says he is.”

Wendy thought about how to answer that.

“I can’t see it,” she said. “Nobody can, except Yan. But I’ve known him for a long time. He doesn’t lie to people about this. Not ever. Not even to make them feel better.” She looked at the water. “If he says your grandad’s there, he’s there.”

Marrit was quiet.

“He always said he’d take her west himself,” she said. “The Patience. He always said.” Her mouth did something. “Stubborn old goat.”

Tiger shouted numbers.

He’d said he would, and he did. He did it the way he did everything, which was fast and without warning and from somewhere he shouldn’t have been. From the top of the mainmast. From under the bowsprit, hanging by his knees. From behind a water cask, so that Yan, walking aft with a bowl, jumped a foot in the air.

“A hundred and twelve!”

“Ysella. Corran’s wife’s sister. Net-mender. Went out in a squall to bring in somebody else’s nets.” Yan put his hand on his heart. “Seven ticks. Tiger.”

“Two hundred and four!”

“Elowen Petrel. Eleven. A dare, and a seal, and a rope that wasn’t tied.” He didn’t even stop walking. “Gran never got over that one.”

“Ninety!”

“Tiger. I’m eating.”

“That’s a no, then.”

“Bastian Fulmar,” said Yan, through soup. “Fisherman. Pell had him too. He’d have had the sail reefed.”

Tiger fell off the cask laughing.

Wendy watched them, and thought about Tiger on floor 8, running into a net thirty-one times, and Neth’s hand on his head. You are not late. He’d stopped flickering after that, Yan said. Tiger’s light. It had stopped vibrating, for the first time since the white room, as if it had learned to sit. She looked at Tiger now, on his back on the deck by the cask with his tail going and his face split open, and she thought she could almost see it herself.

Salty didn’t test him.

Salty was hardly there. Salty slept on his back in the bow with his arm over his face and was gone every night by the middle watch, and came back before dawn smelling of the hold, of tar and fish and, very faintly, of hot metal, and lay down again, and was asleep by the time anybody looked. He thought nobody noticed. Wendy noticed. Wendy had noticed on floor 8, at Ressa’s forge, a man on a stool with a small old hammer in his lap that he never used, and she’d elbowed him in the ribs on a jetty in front of a hundred river-folk and he’d said ow.

“Where’s Salty?” said Yan, on the third night, looking at the empty bedroll.

“Hold,” said Wendy.

“Doing what?”

“Being Salty.”

Yan looked at her for a long time.

Then he looked away, and didn’t ask. The way Gabe had taught him. Some things you wait for people to give you in their own time.

She learned them too.

She didn’t mean to. She’d been holding the book for five days, reading him the start of each line when he got stuck, saying yes and no and that’s Pell’s, not Corran’s, and somewhere in the middle of the fourth day she’d realised she didn’t need to look any more. She knew what came next. She’d hear him start a name and the rest of it would already be in her mouth.

At night she lay in her bedroll in the bow with the silent headphones on, the way she’d worn them every night since the first, not for sound, there was no sound in them, just for the weight of them on her head, the way they’d felt every night of her life on Earth. And she’d lie there with the stars going over and the singer’s hum coming up through the planks, and the names would go round. Treeve Fulmar. Morwen Gull. Bryok Gull. Hedra. Not like a list. More like a crowd. Like walking through a market where you knew everybody’s face.

She wasn’t the only one awake.

Ice was, most nights. She’d see him at the stern, by the rail, a dark shape against the stars with another one beside it. Thea. They’d sit there for an hour, close, not talking much. And sometimes, once a night, maybe, he’d say something to her. One word. Wendy was too far away to hear what. She could only see his face turn, and his mouth move, once. And Thea, beside him, would say it back. Just as quietly. The same shape. Like putting something away somewhere safe.

And then they’d sit there.

Wendy watched them, the first night, and then she made herself stop watching, because it was theirs. But she lay there with the headphones on, and the names going round, and she thought: everybody on this ship is learning somebody’s name.

She didn’t know why she thought it. It was just a thing you’d think.

She’d known Ice mostly through Yan. Back home. A voice on late calls. Somebody who’d say something idiotic and then something kind, very fast, before you’d finished being annoyed about the first thing. I think he’s an idiot, she’d told Yan once, and he’s fine. She’d meant it. She still did. But she lay in the bow of the Patience and watched him at the stern with Thea, saying one word a night, and she thought he looked like somebody carrying a very long list in his head, and putting it down, a name at a time, because it was too heavy to carry all at once.

She didn’t write that down. She didn’t keep a book. She left that to Cursiffe.

He couldn’t learn Annis.

Wendy noticed on the fourth day. He’d been going so well by then. He could do the black cord without stopping. He could do the first six. He’d stopped needing her for anything but the very end, the Seal Holm ones that Pell had told him, and even those were coming. And every time, on the eighth cord, about two-thirds of the way along, his thumb would stop on a knot, and he’d frown, and say, “Wait,” and nothing would come.

“Annis,” said Wendy, the first time.

“Annis. Yes.” He shut his eyes. “Sorry.”

The second time, the same. The third time, on the fifth morning, he got there, and stopped, and sat with his thumb on the knot, and didn’t say wait. He just sat.

“Annis,” said Wendy.

“I know,” said Yan. “I know it’s Annis. I keep losing her.”

He looked down at the book in her lap. At the line. Wendy looked at it too. It was the shortest line on the page. Annis. No boat. Mended sails on the harbour wall. Went down the steps at night after a dropped needle-case. Nobody saw.

And beside it, no ticks at all.

Only one person had ever had her on their cord. Corran.

“Everybody else,” said Yan, slowly, “when I say them, somebody on the deck goes still. Or says something. A red hat. Or Ottery’s face goes. Somebody knew them.” He touched the knot. “Nobody knew her. Only him. And he only had one line.” He looked out at the water. “When I say her, nobody does anything. It’s just a word. It just goes.”

“So she’s the one you have to learn hardest,” said Wendy.

He looked at her.

“The others,” said Wendy, “everybody’s helping you. Kip and Pell and Ottery. They’re carrying half of them for you. You just have to hold the other end.” She shrugged. “She hasn’t got anybody to carry the other end. So you’ll have to carry all of her.”

Yan was quiet for a long time.

Then he got up, with the cords on his arm, and went aft.

Wendy watched him go to Marrit, at the wheel, and ask. She saw Marrit shake her head. I was small. I don’t know. He went to Pell. Pell shook his head. Jago. The Lusk brothers, who for once agreed on something, which was that they’d never heard of her. Morva, who thought for a long time, and then said something, and Yan came back with his face lit up.

“Morva says there was a woman on the wall who mended sails,” he said, sitting down. “When Morva was a girl. Very old. Very small. She had a needle-case made of a seal’s tooth, and she never talked, and she hummed. Morva doesn’t know her name. She just remembers the humming.” He looked at the knot. “She didn’t know she’d died. She thought she’d just stopped coming.”

Wendy didn’t say anything.

“She went down the steps at night,” said Yan, “for a needle-case. Her needle-case. The seal’s tooth one. And she fell, or she slipped, and the tide was in, and nobody saw.” He put his thumb on the knot. “And Corran tied a knot for her. Because nobody else was going to.”

“Annis,” said Wendy.

“Annis,” said Yan. “She hummed.”

He didn’t lose her again.

Every morning after that, before he started, before Treeve Fulmar, before anything, he’d go to the bow and lean on the rail and say one name out over the water on its own. Not loud. Just out. Wendy heard it, every morning. Annis. Like saying good morning to somebody on a wall.

And every night, he looked over the stern.

He’d done it since the burial. Every night, last thing, before he lay down, he went aft and leaned on the rail by the wheel and looked down into the black water behind the ship. A long time. Wendy went with him, mostly. She couldn’t see anything. Black water. The wake, white, going away. And sometimes, a long way off, far down, a paleness that was the singer, which had started, since the burial, to drop back every night and swim under their stern for an hour before it went on ahead again.

“Still there?” she’d say.

“Still there,” Yan would say. “Just under. Low and long. Like a lamp being towed.” And then, once: “The singer’s with him. It’s keeping near. I don’t know if it knows what it is. I think it knows it’s something.”

“What happens to him?” said Wendy. “In the end. If he’s just following.”

Yan didn’t answer for a long time.

“I don’t know,” he said. “On floor 8 there was an Edge. You walked them there, and they went over, and they went somewhere. Suzu says somewhere. Not nowhere.” He leaned on the rail. “There isn’t an Edge on the sea. There’s just more sea.”

Ice said there was, Wendy thought. On the ledge. On the first day. She remembered it, because she’d been sitting next to Tiger and Tiger had gone ooh. The Last Light. A white tower on the last rock, where the sea goes over the edge into white.

She didn’t say it. She didn’t know if it was true. Higu had a question mark on the rock about it.

But she thought it.

On the night of the three hundred and fourteenth day, he did all of them.

He didn’t say he was going to. He sat down in the bow after supper, the way he had every night, with his back to the bulwark and the cords on his arm, and Wendy sat down with the book, and he said, “Don’t open it.”

She didn’t open it.

“Annis,” said Yan, to the sea, first. Then he put his thumb on the top knot of the black cord, under his elbow.

“Treeve Fulmar,” he said. “Gran’s uncle. Crab. Fog. Before she was born.”

And went on.

It took a long time. It was dark before he’d finished the black cord. The ship was quiet. People had been going about their business, the way people do on a ship at night, the watch changing, somebody laughing aft, Hyvred and Calvin arguing about something in the waist. And then one by one they weren’t. Wendy didn’t see it happen. She just looked up, somewhere in the third cord, and saw the bow was full.

Not crowded. Just full. People sitting on the deck in the dark, along the bulwarks, on the hatch, the way they’d sat round the mainmast on the night Corran said them. Kip, right at the front, cross-legged, with his lips moving, a word behind every name. Pell. Ottery, at the back, standing, with her arms folded. Salty, who’d come back early from the hold and was sitting very still against the foremast with something wrapped in oilcloth across his knees that he didn’t seem to know he was holding. Cursiffe, with his eyes shut, listening the way he listened to everything, as if he’d be asked about it later. Tiger on the bowsprit, not shouting numbers.

And the rest. Hana and Milli. Rachael, standing, arms folded. Gabe. Maigo. Aleaka, with her head tipped, the way she tipped it when she was listening to the singer. Ice and Thea, at the very back, by the foremast. Ice with his eyes on the deck.

Yan didn’t look up. He didn’t need to. He had his thumb on the cords and his eyes half shut and he went along them knot by knot, the way the old man had, slowly, not stopping.

“Kitto Sanderling,” said Yan. “Corran’s brother. Fourteen. Old Shears. He let go of his hand.” He held the knot. “Seventy years.”

Somebody at the back said, “Kitto.” Wendy thought it was Marrit.

Yan went on.

“Dannet. No boat. Sculled off the harbour wall. Taught the children. He had a red hat. He couldn’t swim. It made him careful.”

“Red hat,” said Kip, under his breath.

On. Cord after cord. The fifth. The sixth. Tamsin Gannet. Ottery’s girl. Eight. Swam like a seal. Better than her mother. Ottery’s face, at the back, in the dark, didn’t move, and her lips did. The seventh. The eighth.

“Annis,” said Yan. “No boat. Mended sails on the harbour wall. She had a needle-case made of a seal’s tooth. She hummed.” He held the knot a long time. “She went down the steps at night to get it back, and nobody saw. Corran tied her. Because nobody else was going to.”

Nobody on the deck said anything.

And then Morva, at the side, by the rail, very quietly, hummed. Three notes. A little tune. Wendy didn’t know it. Nobody did, except Morva. And then stopped, embarrassed, and looked at her hands.

Yan’s head came up at the hum, quick, the way it did when a thing fitted. Then he went on.

“Jory Kittiwake,” he said, near the end of the tenth. “Corran’s son. Marrit’s uncle. Twenty-six. The long calm. He folded his shirt on the rail first.” He swallowed. “He always asked. He always wanted to know things.”

“Jory,” said Marrit, out loud, from the back, and her voice didn’t break this time.

The eleventh cord. The last ones, the new ones, the people everybody on the ship had known. Then Pell’s nineteen, from Seal Holm, which Yan said slowly and carefully in Pell’s own order, the way Pell had told them, and Pell sat with his great hand over his face and his shoulders going.

Then there was one left.

Yan didn’t have a knot for it. It wasn’t on any cord. He took his hand off the cords, and put both hands in his lap, and looked up. For the first time. Out over the bow. At the sea.

“Corran Patience,” he said. “Shipwright. Marrit’s grandad. He built her in eleven years and saw her swim.” His voice was very steady. “He said them all. He wasn’t frightened of the water. Eighty-nine. Two Rs.”

Somebody laughed. Wetly. It was Cursiffe.

“Three hundred and eleven,” said Yan.

And the window came up in front of him.

Wendy saw it happen. She saw his face change, the way faces changed when a window opened, the eyes going to a place in the air just in front of them. She couldn’t see it. Nobody could see anybody else’s. But he read it out, in the flat voice, so they all could, the way they always did now.

The deck was very quiet.

“It will turn,” said Cursiffe, softly, with his eyes shut, the way you’d say a line back to make sure you had it right.

And then the third. Yan read it slower. As if he didn’t understand it yet.

Keep an Edge. ]

Nobody said anything for a long time.

“Keep an Edge,” said Wendy eventually. “What does that mean?”

“I don’t know,” said Yan. He was looking at the air in front of him where the window had been. “The first one said walk one to the Edge. Do not keep them.” He swallowed. “This one says keep an Edge.”

“Not them,” said Cursiffe. “It.”

“Yeah.”

Suzu put her head on his collarbone and said something nobody else could hear, and he put his hand up, and touched her, and didn’t say what it was.

Most of them went to bed after that. Slowly. The way people leave a fire. Kip last, who’d fallen asleep against Pell and had to be carried aft, like a sack, with his mouth open.

Wendy didn’t go. She sat in the bow with the closed book in her lap and watched Yan.

He’d got up. He was standing at the rail, right up in the bow, by the root of the bowsprit, looking out. Not west. Not ahead, where the singer was. North-west. Off to the right, into the dark, where there was nothing at all to see. The way he’d looked over the rail when Corran’s light had started to go sideways.

“Yan,” said Wendy.

“Wait,” said Yan. Very quietly.

She waited.

He stood with his hands on the rail and Corran’s cords on his arm, and looked out into the dark, and then he said a name. Not loud. Out. The way he said Annis in the morning.

“Kitto Sanderling.”

Nothing. The sea. The stem going through it. The hum.

Wendy watched his face.

And then it changed. It went still. Absolutely still. The way Cursiffe’s candle was, he’d told her once. Like light through a bottle of ink, not moving at all. He stood there with his hands on the rail, staring north-west into nothing, and his lips came apart.

“Something turned,” said Yan.

Wendy stood up.

“Out there.” He didn’t take his eyes off the dark. “A long way. Under the sea. Further than I can see. I didn’t see it, Wendy, I felt it, like—“ He stopped. “Like when you say somebody’s name across a room. And they look up.”

“Kitto,” said Wendy.

“He’s down there.” Yan’s hands had gone white on the rail. “In it. In the hole. With all of them. I said his name and he heard me. He turned round.” His voice didn’t crack. Wendy noticed that. It went the other way: quick, and low, and lit, the way it went over a knot he’d nearly got undone. “Seventy years in the dark, Wen, and I said his name and he turned round to see who it was. They hear it. They come when they’re called.”

Wendy put her hand on his arm. On the cords. She didn’t say anything. She was good at that.

She thought about Hana, nine days ago, on a coil of rope with a bloody nose, looking north-west. The way Yan was looking now. Everybody on the deck had seen her do it. Nobody had needed to ask what she was thinking.

“Then we go back,” said Wendy.

Yan turned his head and looked at her.

“Not tonight,” said Wendy. “Not on our own. Not without telling Higu, and the rock, and all of them, and doing it the way that brings everybody back.” She took a breath. “But we go back. You’ve got three hundred and ten people down there, and one of them just heard you say his name. That’s not a thing you can un-say.”

Yan looked at her for a long time.

“You’re very bad at being a pessimist,” he said.

“I’m a brilliant pessimist,” said Wendy. “I think it’s going to be horrible. I think it’s going to be the worst thing we’ve ever done. I think we’re all going to be soaked.” She looked out at the dark. “I’m just not going to let you do it on your own.”

Suzu, round his neck, lifted her small white head and looked at Wendy with her small dark eyes. And then reached out, very slowly, and touched Wendy’s hand on the cords with her nose.

Behind them, a long way down in the black water astern of the ship, Corran followed.
