Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 3: Party of One

Chapter 90

The Edge

5,134 words · 22 min read

The river-folk burned low.

Yan had noticed it on the stair, the first evening, when Oarin came up out of the spray to meet them. And he’d kept noticing it every day since, until it was the first thing he saw in anyone in Wendmere, before their faces. Their candles were deep. Deeper than anybody’s he’d ever seen. Set far down in them, under the breastbone, and turned low. Not small. Low. Like a lamp somebody has turned down for the night, so it will last until morning. A long, slow, steady flame, burning down so gradually you could have watched it for a year and not seen it move.

That was what four hundred years looked like, he supposed. You didn’t burn bright. You burned long.

He’d stopped being able to look at them for very long. It was too much like looking at the sea.

Three more boats went over in the ten days after the first one.

It was autumn. The river-folk said the old went before the ice, the way leaves did. They said it simply, the way they said everything, and went on with their slow lives. And three times more, at dusk, the low humming started all over the city, and the lamps went out from the top of the falls down, and a reed boat with one small light at its bow went out across the lake on the end of Aud’s line.

He watched every one. He couldn’t not.

Every time, it was the same. The candle didn’t rise. On every floor below this one, when something died, a thin thread went up off it like smoke off a snuffed wick, and went somewhere. Here, it didn’t. It stayed. Sitting in the boat, under the green net, with the body. Small, and uncertain, and turned very low, the way a lamp sits in a window after everybody’s gone to bed, not knowing whether anybody is coming back to put it out.

And Aud walked it to the Edge. And waited there, with the water running over her feet. And cut the line. And it went.

Every time, it went. Over, into the white, and gone, somewhere. It knew where to go then. As if it had only needed somebody to walk it to the place where the knowing started.

And every time, Aud’s arms shook a little more on the pole.

He watched her come back along the causeway in the dark, after the third. Slowly. Leaning on the long pole with its curved blade like an old woman on a stick. Twice she stopped, and stood in the running water with her head down, and didn’t move for a long time. And when she got to the jetty, two river-folk had to help her up the last step.

He’d looked at her candle as she went past him in the dark.

It was very low. Lower than any of the others. Turned down so far it was almost out.

Yun, said Suzu, round his neck. Softly.

“I know,” said Yan.

Aud came to the house for lights on the morning of the hundred and seventieth day.

He was sitting at the long table with his bowl of Ice’s porridge, which was very good, when the door opened and she was standing in it. Tall, even bent. Very thin. Her hair so white it looked like the spray off the falls, and her skin gone the grey of a winter lake. She had the long pole in her hand, with its curved blade at the top, wrapped in a strip of green cloth. She’d never come to the house before.

The room went quiet. Thirty-five people with spoons.

Aud looked round at all of them. Slowly. One after another, the way the river-folk looked at everything, with all the time in the world.

Her eyes stopped on him.

“The one who watches the boats,” said Aud.

Her voice was very deep and very faint, like water under ice. He put down his spoon.

“Yes,” said Yan.

They sat on the end of the last jetty in the thin cold sun. Just the two of them, and Suzu. Aud lowered herself onto the planks a joint at a time, and laid the long pole across her knees, and for a long time didn’t say anything at all.

He waited. He was good at waiting. Wendy said it was the snake in him.

“I feel them,” said Aud at last.

He looked at her.

“In the boat,” said Aud. “When we have laid them in. When the net is tied. I put my hand on the line, and I can feel them. Through it. Like warmth.” She laid her old grey hand flat on the pole across her knees. “A little warmth, in the boat. Not knowing. Waiting. And I walk them out, and I stand at the Edge, and I wait. Until the warmth goes still. Until it is lying down where it is going. Then I cut.”

He didn’t say anything.

“I have done it a hundred and ninety-one years,” said Aud. “I was apprenticed to Hessel, who did it before me, and he was apprenticed to Coll. Back and back. There has always been an edge-keeper.” She looked out at the Edge. At the long straight lip at the end of the lake, the water going over it in a smooth green curve, into the white. “There has been no apprentice for a hundred years. The young are afraid of it. Of the causeway. Of the Edge.” She was quiet. “Of the warmth. They do not like to feel it.”

“I’m not surprised,” said Yan.

“No.” She turned her large dark eyes on him. “You see them.”

It wasn’t a question. He didn’t pretend it was.

“Yes,” he said.

“All of them? Every one?”

“Everything that’s alive,” said Yan. “I don’t know why. I could see them from the first morning. Little lights. Like candles. Everyone’s got one.”

Aud looked at him a long time.

“What does mine look like?” she said.

He hadn’t been asked that since Salty, on this same jetty. Does mine look all right? He looked at the middle of her chest, under the grey robe. He’d looked at it every evening for ten days and not let himself think about it.

“Very low,” he said. Gently. He’d always believed that you didn’t lie to people about their own light. “Turned down. Like a lamp at night. But steady. It’s very steady.”

Aud nodded slowly, as if he’d told her the weather.

“My arms are done,” she said. “One more. Perhaps two. Then the ice. And in the spring, there is nobody.” She lifted the long pole off her knees, slowly, in both hands, the green cloth round its blade. “And next spring you will be gone up. All of you. With your lights.” She held it there, between them, over the planks. “So I do not ask you to stay. I ask you for one. Walk one with me. Tonight.”

He didn’t take it.

“Who?” he said. Though he knew.

Aud looked down the lake. Toward the low jetty, and the row of black posts, and the net strung between them under the water.

“Neth,” she said.

Tiger was sitting with her when he got there.

He’d been sitting with her every morning for two weeks. Yan knew that. Everybody knew that. He’d stopped running the low jetty and started walking it, and then he’d started sitting on it, every dawn, with a corner of net in his lap. And then on the eighth night he’d gone into the black water after her, Tiger, who hated water, and come out with a round ring of tooth marks in his shoulder that Kei said would scar. And then they’d mended the hole together. Six days. And yesterday Tiger had tied the last knot.

Neth hadn’t picked up another cord since.

She was lying on the end of the low jetty, wrapped in the blanket Wendy had brought her. With her head on a folded coat, Tiger’s teal one, and her long white hair spread out over the planks and down into the water. Her eyes were open. She was looking at the net. At the place in the middle, under the surface, where the hole had been.

Tiger looked up when Yan sat down. He didn’t say anything. His face was very calm, for Tiger. Calm in a way that wasn’t like him at all, that made Yan’s chest hurt to look at.

Yan looked at Neth’s light.

It was so low. Lower than Aud’s. Turned down until there was almost nothing left of it but a small blue bead, sitting deep in her, very still. It wasn’t guttering. He’d expected it to be guttering, the way a candle gutters at the end, flaring and sinking and flaring. It wasn’t. It was perfectly still. Just very, very small.

“You,” said Neth. Without looking away from the net.

“Me,” said Yan.

“The one who looks at us like that.” Her voice was barely there. A thread. “Aud said.”

“Yes.”

“Will you walk me?” said Neth. “Aud’s arms are tired.” She was quiet for a long time. Breathing slowly, through the gills at her neck. “The quick one can’t. He’d run.”

Tiger made a sound that wasn’t a laugh.

“I’d walk,” said Tiger. “I’ve learned to walk.”

“Hm,” said Neth. And very slowly, her old webbed hand came out of the blanket, and found the top of his head, between his ears. And stayed there.

Yan didn’t mean to do it.

He’d thought about it, walking down from the last jetty. He’d thought about it all the way, and decided he wouldn’t, and then he sat down on the planks beside her and looked at that small blue bead of light, so small, and his hands came up on their own.

Cupped. Round it. The way you cup a candle in a draught.

Shelter. He felt it go out of him. The cold, starting in his fingers, the way it always did. And the light under his hands steadied. Held.

You can’t make the light bigger, he’d said to himself, on the first floor, the first time. You can keep the wind off it.

He could keep the wind off it. He could keep it for an hour. Two. Longer, if he didn’t mind his hands going white to the elbow. He could sit here on the jetty and hold it, and she’d stay. She’d stay, and Tiger would stay, with her hand on his head—

The light leaned away from his hands.

He saw it. He’d never seen a light do that. They leaned toward things. Toward people. Thea’s leaned toward the east, on six, toward the stair, like a plant on a windowsill. Gabe’s leaned up the stairs. Lights leaned toward what they loved.

Neth’s leaned away from his hands. Very slightly. Very gently. Out, over the water. Toward the Edge.

Yun, said Suzu.

She’d come up off his neck. Her pale head, by his ear. Her voice was very soft.

She doesn’t want the wind kept off, said Suzu. There isn’t any wind. She wants to go.

He sat there with his hands cupped.

You told Salty, said Suzu. On the jetty, the first night. That isn’t what it needs.

“I know,” said Yan. His voice came out very low.

Then put your hands down.

He put his hands down.

It was the hardest thing he’d done on any floor. Harder than the Prism. Harder than lowering his staff in the glass while Wendy drew. He laid his hands on his knees. They were white to the wrist. He let them lie there, and let her light be.

It settled. Leaning out over the water. Small and blue and very still.

And in the corner of his eye, very quietly, a window came up. One he’d never seen.

He read it. He didn’t touch it. He didn’t need to.

She went in the afternoon.

There wasn’t anything to it. That was what he would remember afterwards. He’d thought, the way you think about things you’ve never seen, that it would be a moment. A last breath. A word. It wasn’t. The sun moved along the lake. The mist burned off. Tiger sat with her hand on his head and didn’t move, and Yan sat beside them with his white hands on his knees, and the net lay under the water from post to post with all its knots in it.

And at some point Neth said, very faintly, “Quick one. Sit.”

And Tiger said, “I’m sitting.”

And she said, “Hm.”

And that was all.

Yan watched the light. He watched it the whole time. He’d told Wendy on six that somebody should. He watched the small blue bead go stiller, and stiller, until it was so still it wasn’t burning at all. Just there. Sitting in her. Not going out. Not going up. Not rising off her in a thin thread the way every light on every floor below had risen, every single time. Just sitting in her, very small, as if it had been told to wait somewhere and had sat down to do it.

Her hand slid off Tiger’s head onto the planks.

Tiger looked down at it for a long time.

Then he picked it up, very carefully, in both of his, and held it, and didn’t say anything at all. He didn’t cry. Yan had thought he would. He didn’t. He just sat there on the jetty holding her old webbed hand, the way she’d held the cords. Over. Wait.

The light sat in her and waited.

Yan looked at it, and understood, finally, what he’d been seeing for ten days.

It didn’t know where to go. The river-folk lived so long, and burned so low, and so slowly, that when the burning stopped, there wasn’t anything left to carry it. No flare. No thread of smoke. It just stopped where it was. Like a boat with no current. Like somebody at the bottom of a very long stair who has forgotten which way is up.

Somebody had to walk it.

They made the boat that afternoon. The river-folk came down to the low jetty, slowly, a few at a time, with bundles of pale gold reed, and sat on the planks, and wove. He’d never seen them work so fast. Which was to say, it took four hours. Oarin came. Ressa came up from under the second arch with her burned hands and wove with the others and didn’t say anything. Mott the eel-man. People he didn’t know. Old ones, mostly. Neth had mended their net for two hundred and sixty autumns, and every one of them had a knot in it, and had come.

Tiger wove too. He didn’t know how. Nobody told him to stop.

When it was done, they laid her in it. And covered her in a net. Not a new one. A length of the old lake net, cut from the shore end, where it was oldest. Grey and soft with three hundred years, every knot in it tied by somebody. They wrapped her in it from her feet to her head, and drew the edges together over her chest.

And then Oarin held out the last two cords to Tiger.

Tiger looked at them. And at Oarin. And took them.

He tied the slow knot. Over. Wait. Under. Wait. Through. A finger’s width at a time. The whole jetty watched him do it. The rest of the thirty-six on the shore, and twenty river-folk on the planks, and nobody said a word. He drew it down snug over her chest. And let it go.

Oarin reached out one long finger, slowly, and touched it.

It held.

Aud brought him the pole at dusk.

She came down the low jetty in the last of the light, very slowly, leaning on it. All across the city the humming had started. Long, low notes, from every balcony and window, Wendmere starting to sing. And the lamps going out, from the top of the falls down, in that slow dark wave.

She held it out to him. Across the boat. With both hands.

He took it.

It was long. Longer than he was tall, by a head. Pale wood, worn dark and smooth where hands had held it for a hundred and ninety-one years. And at the top, set across the end of it, the blade. Ressa’s blade. Long and thin and curved like a new moon. Grey steel, folded two thousand times, with a faint ripple in it like water when the last of the light caught it. Aud unwrapped the green cloth from it and let it fall.

He held it upright beside him.

It felt—he didn’t know what it felt like. Like the right weight. Like a word he’d known once and forgotten, coming back into his mouth. He’d carried a plain staff since floor 1. It had always been fine. He’d never once thought about what it might be like to carry something else.

Oh, said Suzu. Very quietly, by his ear. Oh, Yun.

“Hm,” said Yan. He didn’t trust himself with anything longer.

Behind him on the jetty, he heard Salty’s breath go out. Very slowly.

“The line,” said Aud. She put the end of it in his other hand. A thin cord, tied to the boat’s bow. “Keep it short. The current is stronger than it looks. Walk slowly. Do not look at the Edge until you are there.” She looked at him with her large dark eyes, in the dusk, with her low, low light. “When you are there, wait. Until it lies down. You will know. You will see it.” She was quiet. “Then cut.”

The causeway started at the end of the last jetty.

He hadn’t stood on it before. He’d only watched. A line of flat stones, set in the lake bed, just under the surface, running out across the flat green water toward the far end of the lake. And the water went over it. Ankle-deep, and fast, all in one direction. Toward the Edge.

He stepped down onto the first stone.

The cold went through his boots at once. And the pull. He hadn’t understood the pull, watching. The water didn’t just run past your feet. It leaned on them. All the time. Steady and strong and patient, like a hand in the small of your back, pushing you gently, gently, toward the end of everything.

He drew the boat down off the jetty on its line. It came. Light. Pale gold, with Neth in it under the grey net, and one small lamp at the bow that Ressa had lit. And in it, with her, the small blue light. Waiting.

He started to walk.

The humming went on all over the city behind him. The lamps went out. One after another, in the windows behind the water, until the only lights left in the whole of Wendmere were the one in the boat, and the stars coming out, and, behind him on the jetty, thirty-four small balls of white light at thirty-four shoulders, and Ice beside them with nothing at his that anybody could see. He didn’t look back at them. He could feel them, though. The way you feel people standing behind you in a dark room.

He walked. The stones were slick. The water pulled. The pole in his hand, with the curved blade at the top, swayed a little with every step, and he found that if he set its butt down on the stone ahead of him, it steadied him. So he did. Pole, step. Pole, step. Like an old man with a stick. Like Aud.

Halfway, the boat jerked.

A gust, or a current, or something under the water. It swung out hard on its line, sideways, and the line went tight in his hand and pulled, and his left foot went off the stone.

He went down on one knee in the running water. The cold hit him to the waist. The line was pulling. The boat was swinging out toward the deeper water, toward the Edge, too fast, and he could feel the small blue light in it flicker. The first time it had moved since the jetty. Frightened. Flaring and sinking, the way they all did when they were frightened, the way the Prism’s had flared when Wendy’s arrow went in.

He jammed the pole down.

The curved blade went into a crack between two causeway stones, and caught. And held. Like an anchor. He hung off it, one-handed, on his knee in the water, with the line wrapped round his other fist and the boat straining on the end of it.

Oh, he thought. It’s not only for cutting.

He got his foot back on the stone. Got up. Slowly. Drew the boat back in, hand over hand, until it was close beside him again on a short line. The light in it was still flickering. Small and blue and afraid.

He didn’t know what to say to it. He’d never spoken to a light. He’d never thought they could hear.

So he did what the city was doing. He hummed.

He didn’t know the note. He just found it. Low, the way his voice was low. Under the roar of the Edge, under the hiss of the water round his knees. The same long note that was coming from every dark window in Wendmere behind him. He hummed it, and walked, pole and step, pole and step, with the boat beside him on its short line.

The light stopped flickering.

At the end of the causeway, there was the Edge.

He didn’t look at it until he was there. Aud had said not to. He looked at his feet, and the stones, and the boat, and the small blue light. And then there were no more stones, and he stopped, and looked up.

The lake ended.

Right there, in front of his feet. A long straight lip of dark stone, smooth as a table edge, running away from him on both sides into the dusk. And the water went over it. All of it, the whole of the lake, in one wide smooth curve, green and glassy and silent at the top, and then white. And then mist. And under the mist, nothing. He couldn’t see the bottom. Nobody could. The roar came up out of the white from somewhere so far below that it wasn’t even loud. Just big. Like the sound the sea makes in a shell.

He let the line out.

The boat drifted forward, past him, to the lip. And over it. Not falling. Just hanging there, over the curve of the water, at the end of its line, held. The little lamp at its bow, out over nothing. And in it, under the grey net, Neth. And her light.

He waited.

Until it lies down, Aud had said. You will see it.

He didn’t know what that meant. He stood at the Edge with the water pulling at his knees, and the line in his fist, and the long pole upright in his other hand, and he watched. The way he’d watched the Prism. The way he’d watched every light he’d ever watched go out. Because somebody should.

The light sat in the boat. Small. Still. Not afraid any more, he thought. But not going, either. Just sitting there at the very end of everything, the way it had sat on the jetty. Waiting to be told.

He thought about Tiger. Over. Wait. Under. Wait. Let the cord sit. Each time. Let it lie down where it is going before you move it again. If you move it before it has lain down, it has not got there. It is only passing through.

He waited.

And after a long time, the light leaned.

Not away from anything. Toward. Out, over the bow of the boat. Toward the white. Toward wherever it was that the water went, under the mist, where nobody had ever seen. The way Thea’s leaned toward the stair. The way lights leaned toward the things they loved. Slowly, very gently, it leaned out over the Edge, and settled there, and was still.

Lying down where it was going.

He lifted the pole, and turned it, and laid the inside of the curved blade against the line, and cut.

The boat tipped over the Edge.

It went like a leaf. The lamp went with it. A little gold spark, falling, into the white. And the light went too. Not up. Not as a thread. It went with her. Over, and down, and on, into the mist, small and blue and steady, and he watched it until he couldn’t see it any more. It didn’t go out. He was sure of that. It went somewhere. It knew where.

The humming stopped.

All across Wendmere, all at once, in the dark behind him, the lamps came back on.

He read them standing at the end of the causeway in the dark, with the water pulling at his knees. He read them twice.

Learn their names.

Yun, said Suzu, by his ear. She sounded very far away and very close at once. Come back now. You’re cold.

“Yes,” said Yan.

He walked back. It was easier, coming back. The water pushed against him instead of with him, and that was harder for his legs, but easier for the rest of him. Pole, step. Pole, step. Toward the jetty, and the lights on it. All the lamps in Wendmere, gold behind the water. And thirty-four small white ones, at thirty-four shoulders, and Ice, waiting at the end of the causeway.

Aud was sitting on the last step. Two river-folk beside her. She watched him come all the way in.

He held the pole out to her.

She didn’t take it.

“Keep it,” said Aud. “Until somebody makes you your own.”

Behind her, at the back of the people on the jetty, very low, Salty said, “Working on it,” under his breath.

He heard a soft thump, which was Wendy’s elbow. And Salty saying “ow.”

Yan looked at the pole in his hands, and didn’t look at Salty at all. He’d learned that from Gabe. Some things you wait for people to give you in their own time.

“Thank you,” he said to Aud.

Aud looked at him for a long moment. Her low, low light, very steady, in the dark.

“You waited,” she said. “At the Edge. You did not cut too soon.” She was quiet. “The young always cut too soon.”

“Somebody taught me,” said Yan. “A knot.”

Aud looked past him at Tiger.

Tiger was standing on the end of the low jetty. On his own. Looking out at the Edge. His coat was still folded on the planks where Neth’s head had been. He wasn’t moving. He didn’t look like he was going to move for a long time.

“Hm,” said Aud.

He sat up late that night, on the bench outside the house for lights, with the pole across his knees and Suzu round his neck and Kei’s warm drink going cold in his white hands.

He looked at the lights.

He did it on bad nights. It helped. He couldn’t have said why. Thirty-five candles, through the windows of the house, burning in the dark. Salty’s, roaring softly like a banked forge, out at the second arch where he’d gone again, thinking nobody knew. Wendy’s, small and steady, right by the door where she’d put her bedroll. Tiger’s, still on the jetty. Not flickering. For the first time since the white room, Tiger’s light wasn’t flickering at all. It was just burning. Very steady. As if it had learned to sit.

And Ice’s.

He was in the kitchen. Yan could see his light through the wall. He’d been looking at it every night since the trial, eleven days ago. He’d been looking because of what he’d seen in the first one, on the first morning, for one second before Suzu covered his eyes. A candle burned almost all the way down. Wax run down all its sides. Nearly spent, and the brightest light in the room.

It was still burned down. It was still the brightest.

But eleven days ago, in the trial, it had stopped running.

The wax. It had been running down the sides of that candle since the white room. Every time he looked. Slowly. A little more every week. Ice spending it, all of it, on them. And in the white room, when all thirty-five of them had stood round him in the light and the light had gone out of him to all of them and come back, he’d looked, and the wax had stopped.

Not longer. It wasn’t getting longer. Yan didn’t think candles could.

Just not shorter. For the first time in a hundred and seventy days, not any shorter.

They’ve started keeping the wind off him, said Suzu, by his ear.

“Yes,” said Yan.

He sat with the edge-keeper’s pole across his knees and watched it, through the wall, until the kitchen light went out.
