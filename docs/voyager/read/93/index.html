Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 3: Party of One

Chapter 93

Roses Have Thorns

5,293 words · 23 min read

Jared made the thorn one-handed, which Ressa said was stupid, and then held the tongs for him anyway.

It was the third evening. He’d come down the stair inside the second arch every night since the pool, with his left arm strapped across his chest and Kei’s terrible poultice on it, and sat on a stool against the wet wall, and watched. The first night he hadn’t even asked. He’d just watched Ressa fold her iron, a finger’s width at a time under the great slow hammer, the way Salty had told him she did. Salty was there too, every night, on his own stool, with an old small hammer in his lap that he never used. Just sat there, listening. Jared didn’t ask him about that either.

On the second night he’d asked Ressa for a bit of iron.

On the third night he’d started.

It was small. That was the point. Not much bigger than his thumb. A short, thick, curved spike, wide at the base and wicked at the tip, bent back on itself a little, like a hook. He’d drawn it in the dust on the forge floor with his finger before he started, and Ressa had looked at the drawing for a very long time, and said, “That is a thorn.”

“Roses have them,” said Jared.

She hadn’t said anything to that. She’d just picked up the tongs.

So she held the iron, with her burned lace hands, steady as a vice. And he hit it, one-handed, with a hammer that was too light for the job, because his right hand was all he had. Tink. Tink. Off the beat of the big hammer. Between its blows. He’d learned to hear the gaps. Four seconds, and in the four seconds, tink tink tink, and then the great hammer came down beside them, THUD, and he waited, and went again.

“Why a thorn?” said Gabe.

Gabe had come down on the second night too. Of course he had. The nail guild. The three of them on stools against the wall of a forge under a waterfall, like the first week on floor 1, a hundred roofing nails each and Ulla Dray grunting.

“It hit me twice,” said Jared. Tink. “In the same place.” Tink. “Right on the boss. It came down and it pressed. Didn’t bite. Just leaned on it, with all its weight. With those teeth going on the rim.” THUD. He waited. “And the second time it came down harder. Same place. Right on the middle.”

“It’ll do it again,” said Salty, from his stool. Not a question.

“It’ll do it again,” said Jared. “I’m a Guardian, man. That’s the whole job. I stand where it hits.” Tink. “So I’m gonna stand there. And this time, when it leans on my shield with all its weight, it’s gonna lean on this.”

Gabe was quiet for a long time.

“Roof of its mouth,” said Gabe.

“Roof of its mouth.”

Gabe looked at the little curved spike in Ressa’s tongs, going from orange to dull red as it cooled.

“Hana’s gonna kill you,” said Gabe.

“Alice,” said Jared, “is gonna be on the shelf below me, putting a whole waterfall in its head.” He hit the thorn one more time. Tink. “She’s got her own stuff going on.”

He lifted the shield on the morning of the hundred and eighty-first day. Three days, like he’d said.

Rachael didn’t like it. Rachael said so, at length, standing in front of him in the house for lights with her arms folded. It’s not fine, Jared. Healing doesn’t take it back, it just closes it. The bone’s still learning it’s a bone. He said he knew. He said he’d felt every minute of it closing. He said he was going to lift the shield anyway.

“Why,” said Rachael. “Azac can take your shelf. Chris can. Gabe—“

“Because it knows my shield,” said Jared. “It hit it twice. It knows where it is. When it comes back up for Newish, it’s gonna come straight down on the thing it already knows is there.” He looked at her. “I want it to.”

Rachael looked at him for a long time.

Then she unfolded her arms, and put her cold blue light on his arm one more time, very hard, without asking. And went away without saying anything else, which Jared took as a yes.

He strapped the shield on. Round, heavy, plain grey iron, with the little iron rose worked into the inside of the rim where his hand went, so he’d know which bit was his. And in the middle of the boss, now, bolted through from the inside where Ressa had drilled it, the thorn. Short and black and curved and mean. He’d filed the tip until it would have gone through a boot.

It hurt to lift. His whole forearm sang with it. He held it up anyway, and stood there in the house for lights with it raised, and breathed.

“Jared,” said Newish.

He felt it land.

He didn’t know how else to say it. It landed on him. Right on him, out of nowhere, like somebody had thrown a ball at his chest and he’d caught it without thinking. And with it, under it, her healing. Not ragged, the way it had gone in on the ledge. Clean. All of it, all at once, on one, right into his arm. And the arm stopped singing.

He looked at her. She was sitting on the end of the long table, swinging her legs, trying very hard to look like she hadn’t done anything.

“You can heal again,” said Jared.

“I never stopped,” said Newish. “I just had to find where the one goes.” She shrugged, elaborately. “It goes where I say. Turns out.”

“Turns out,” said Jared.

She grinned at him. The first real one since the pool.

They went up on the hundred and eighty-second morning, all thirty-six, in the grey before dawn, and nobody talked on the stair.

It wasn’t that kind of quiet. It wasn’t frightened. Jared had been in frightened quiet. On floor 4, in the red dusk, with the dead stacked against the doors. This wasn’t that. This was the quiet of thirty-six people all thinking about the same thing very hard, each in their own way. Like an exam.

Drew in front. Higu beside him with her map. Then the parties. Then, in the middle, between Gabe and Thea, Ice. Where they’d put him on the first day. He walked with his head down and his hands in his pockets and didn’t look at the falls going past.

Jared fell back to walk with him for a bit.

“You good?” said Jared.

Ice looked up. “Yeah.”

“You were not good. On the shelf. When Drew put his hand up.” Jared hitched his shield up on his arm. “I saw your face, man. From under the thing. You looked like you were gonna die.”

Ice was quiet for a while.

“I nearly went,” he said. “I was going. Off the shelf. Into the water. I had my sword out.” He looked at his own hands. “And Drew put his hand up, and I stopped. And I stood there and watched it come down on you. Twice.” He stopped. “I’ve never done that. Not once. Not in—“ He stopped again. “Not ever.”

“You did good,” said Jared.

“I did nothing.”

“Yeah,” said Jared. “That’s what I’m saying. You did nothing. On purpose. While it was hitting me.” He looked at Ice sideways. “That’s hard, man. Way harder than going. I’m a Guardian. I know.”

Ice looked at him for a long moment.

“Today I’m kneeling,” he said.

“I know. Higu said.”

“Right behind you. On your shelf.”

“I know.” Jared grinned. “Good. You can watch me do the job properly this time.”

The wrens were waiting.

He’d been told about it. Aleaka had told all of them, at the long table, in her slow careful stumbling way, and Higu had drawn it on the map, and Drew had put it on the rock. It was different seeing it.

The big pool lay in its black bowl of rock under the Great Fall, turning slowly. The white bloom heaving under the fall. The shelves going down to the water in steps. Same as before.

Except the cliffs were full of birds. And as the thirty-six came down onto the shelves and spread out round the pool in the places Drew had chalked, every falls-wren on those cliffs came down off the ledges.

Not all at once. Not like the scream, the last time, when they’d all gone up together. They came down in ones and twos and handfuls. Small dark round bodies with white bibs, flying low and fast and straight across the black water, the way they flew. And landed on it.

On the water. In the middle of the white, where the fall hit. Where the foam was heaving and boiling. They landed on it like it was solid, and sat there, bobbing on the churning white, more and more of them, until there was a ring of them. A loose, shifting ring of small dark birds riding the foam, about twenty metres across.

And the ring was moving.

Slowly. Under the white. Round and round under the fall. The birds rode it, bobbing, lifting off when the foam got too wild and landing again a little further on, so that the ring of them drifted across the white water like a slow cloud.

It was over the Cataract. Wherever it went, down in the white where nobody could see. The birds walked under the water, Aleaka had said. They could see it. They’d always been able to see it. So they sat on the water right over it, and followed it, and showed everybody else.

Aleaka stood on the middle shelf, above Jared, with her bow in her hands and Tuppence on her shoulder and her eyes full of tears. Sleet sat against her legs and didn’t growl at anything at all.

“There,” said Aleaka, very softly. “There it is.”

Thirty-six people looked at a ring of birds riding the white water, and knew exactly where it was.

Newish sat on the middle shelf, cross-legged, behind Jared, with her hands in her lap. Calm. She’d stopped swinging her legs. She wasn’t saying anything.

And behind her, on the back of the shelf, against the rock, Ice knelt down.

Jared felt it.

He hadn’t expected to. Nobody had told him what it felt like from the front. They’d all been in the white room, all thirty-five of them, but that had been different. That had been the light going out and coming back. This was quieter. Ice knelt on the wet rock behind them with his sword laid down flat in front of him and the lamp shield on top of it, and put his hands on his knees.

And something settled on Jared’s back.

Between his shoulder blades. Warm. Like a hand, laid flat there. Not pushing. Just there. The way somebody puts their hand on your back when you’re standing at the edge of something, so you know they’re behind you.

His arm stopped hurting. Not healed. It just stopped, the way pain stops when you stop being afraid of it.

Where you stand, they are safe.

He looked round. Down the shelves. And saw it on all of them. Not a light. Nothing you could see. Just the way they were standing. Gabe, on the low shelf to the left, had stopped shifting his feet. Salty’s shoulders had come down. Calvin, up on the cliff path, had gone very still. Thirty-five people standing round a black pool with something warm laid flat between their shoulder blades.

Drew lifted his hand on the top shelf. Flat. Then he pointed it at the ring of birds.

And Newish said, quite quietly, in no time at all, “Milli.”

Milli went down the shelf below like a dropped knife.

He’d been waiting on the low shelf on the left, with Hana, crouched, with his tail very still and his two daggers out. Jared saw the one land on him. Saw him go, the way Calvin had jumped on the jetty. Down the last step and off the edge of the shelf and onto the water. Onto the ring of birds. They lifted round him in a little dark cloud, and he came down where they’d been, on the white, onto something under the white that was solid, and drove both daggers in.

The water under him went red. Pale red, thin, like a watercolour. It was the first time any of them had seen it bleed.

The ring of birds jerked sideways. The Cataract, under the foam, flinched.

And Milli was already gone. Back up the shelf, wet to the waist, grinning his fanged grin. Out of time. Out of nowhere. Gone before it could find him.

The ring of wrens drifted. Slowed. Sat on the water, bobbing. Under them, something was turning, slowly. Searching.

“Hyvred,” said Newish.

An arrow from the cliff, straight down into the middle of the ring. The wrens scattered and resettled.

Nothing. A long nothing. Nobody moved. Jared stood with his shield up and his heart going like a hammer and made himself not count anything.

“Winter.” Two seconds later. “Ina.” Then nothing at all for half a minute. Then: “Rui Xiang.”

Frost. Fire. A knife. Each one landing on its own, in the ring, wherever the birds said, and each one gone again before the next. No pattern. No beat. Newish sat behind him with her hands in her lap and called names into the roar of the fall like somebody dropping stones into a well, whenever she felt like it, and the Cataract turned and turned under the white and couldn’t find a single one of them.

It couldn’t find the time. There wasn’t any.

The ring of birds began to move faster.

It came up on the ninth name.

“Salty,” Newish said, and Salty went down the shelf on the right with his chipped greatsword, and the ring of birds burst upward in front of him all at once, screaming. And the water stood up.

It came out of the white the way it had the first time. A wall of foam, rising, alive. The pale see-through neck, thick as a tree, with the white water moving inside it. The mane of long fins down its back, streaming spray. The flat pike’s head, with the two blind frosted patches where its eyes should have been. And blood in it now. Thin red threads in the clear grey, from Milli’s daggers, from Rui Xiang’s knife, from a dozen hits that had landed on their own, out of time.

It rose up over the pool, and swayed.

Not toward Salty. Not toward anybody. It swung its blind head round the shelves, slowly, side to side. Thirty-six people, frozen. Nobody breathing. Nobody moving. Nobody in time with anybody.

It was hunting. And there was nothing to hunt.

And on the shelf below Jared, Hana stood up.

She’d taken her crown off. She was holding it in one hand, by her side, with the iron rose in the front of it. Her tail was very still. She was looking up at the thing over the pool with her one visible violet eye, the fringe blown back off the other one by the spray, so that for once you could see both. Calm and smooth and thinking.

It’s blind, she’d said. It doesn’t see. So I can’t put anything in its eye.

But it loves something.

Jared knew what she was going to do. She’d told them at the table. He still didn’t believe it until he saw it.

Hana lifted her free hand toward the Cataract, palm out, the way she’d lifted it on floor 2 at a Rimehound. Beguile. Make them love the wrong thing. Just for a moment.

And then she turned her hand. Slowly. Away from the Cataract. Away from the shelves and the people on them.

Toward the Great Fall.

The Cataract stopped swaying.

Jared saw it happen. Its blind head, which had been swinging round the shelves, hunting, went still. Then it turned. Slowly. Like something hearing music from the next room. Away from all of them. Toward the fall.

The Great Fall. Coming down out of the cloud in a single white column, a hundred and fifty metres of water hitting the pool with a roar so big it was a weight on your chest. It had been falling into that pool for longer than anybody could remember. Pounding. Pounding.

It had a beat. Jared had never heard it. Nobody had. It was too big to hear. But the Cataract heard it. A slow, enormous, endless pulse, under the roar, as the column of water surged and eased and surged again, the way big falls do. The biggest rhythm on the whole floor. And the Cataract had been lying in it, under it, its whole life, and had never once noticed it was there.

Hana made it notice.

It loved it.

It went for the fall. All of it, the whole impossible length of it, out of the water and up and across the pool in one long pale curve, and into the white column of the Great Fall, head first, as if it were going home.

A hundred and fifty metres of falling water came down on it.

Jared heard it hit. Over the roar, over everything, a sound like a building coming down. The Cataract went under. Under the fall, into the base of it, where the water came down hardest, onto the rocks. And the fall drove it down, and down, and pounded it, and pounded it, and the white bloom under the fall went red, and the long pale fins of its mane came up out of the foam torn to rags and floating.

Hana lowered her hand. She was shaking. Milli caught her before she sat down.

“It wanted to,” said Hana. Very quietly. “It really, really wanted to.”

It came out of the fall slow.

It came out of the white the way a drowned thing comes up. Rolling. Its fins in tatters. One of the blind frosted patches on its head was cracked right across. Blood coming off it in the water in long pale clouds. It surfaced in the middle of the pool and lay there for a moment, half under, moving its head very slowly from side to side.

Then it lifted it.

It was hurt. It was angry. And it was hungry. Jared could see it. It swung its blind face round the shelves, slowly, the way it had before, and you could see what it wanted. It wanted a beat. Any beat. Something to come to. It had been fed the biggest beat on the floor and nearly drowned in it, and now it wanted something smaller. Something it could have.

Newish stood up behind him.

He felt her do it. Felt her stand, on the shelf at his back, with Ice kneeling behind her against the rock. He didn’t look round. He set his feet. He brought his shield up. Round, and heavy, and grey, with the rose on the inside where his hand went.

And the thorn on the front.

“Okay,” said Newish. Very quietly. Just to him. “Okay, Jared. Here it comes.”

And then she lifted her voice, so it went out over the whole pool, clear and loud and steady.

And counted.

“One.”

The Cataract’s head snapped round.

“Two.”

It came. Across the pool, through the water, toward the middle shelf, toward her voice, fast. Faster than before. All of it, everything it had left. The water piled up in front of it in a white wave.

“Three.”

It came up out of the water at the foot of the shelf and rose, over him, over her. The comb teeth. The white throat. The head, coming down, the way it had come down twice before, onto the same shield, in the same place, right on the middle. On the thing it knew was there.

Jared held.

He held the way a Guardian holds. Feet set on the wet rock. Shoulder in. Knees bent. His broken arm locked behind the shield and the warm hand of Ice’s Sanctuary laid flat between his shoulder blades. And the whole weight of the Cataract came down on him, pressing, the way it had pressed before, with everything it had.

And pressed itself onto the thorn.

He felt it go in. Through the shield, up his arm. The little curved spike, through the white roof of its mouth, all the way to the base. And the Cataract stopped. Its whole length went rigid, from its head on his shield to the tip of its torn tail in the pool. It tried to lift. It couldn’t. The thorn was curved. It had gone in like a hook.

It was pinned.

It was pinned on Jared’s shield, on the middle shelf, with its blind head over him and its throat over Newish, and it couldn’t go up and it couldn’t go back, and it couldn’t do anything at all but hang there.

Jared held it. His arm was screaming. His boots were sliding on the rock. He held it.

“Alice,” he said, through his teeth. Not loud. Just because he wanted to say it.

And Newish said the one.

Not a name. Not this time. The one. Thrown, not at one person, but at all of them. All thirty-six. The way she’d thrown it on seven, at thirty-six ropes, in a tower that sang.

“ONE.”

Everybody went.

All at once. Every single one. Exactly on it. Jared felt it go out past him from Newish, the one, landing on thirty-five people round a black pool in the same instant, the way it had landed on him in the house for lights. And they went, all of them, at once, like the bells.

He couldn’t see most of it. He was under the shield. He heard it. He heard Gabe’s shield-edge and Salty’s chipped greatsword and Azac’s blade all land on the neck below him in the same moment, one sound, like a door. He heard Hyvred’s arrow and Winter’s and Aleaka’s and Wendy’s, all at once, thock. He heard Rui Xiang’s knife and Milli’s daggers and Tiger’s blades and Calvin’s, both of them, both hands. He saw a white flash that was Ina’s fire and a dark streak that was Cursiffe’s ink, writing something on its side, and he heard Yan, on the shelf above him, with the long curved edge-blade that the old edge-keeper had given him, bring it round in one smooth arc. And he felt, under all of it, Rachael’s cold blue and Xola’s water and Malcolm’s blessing and Deston’s gold, all coming in at once, landing on the one, through Ice’s warm hand flat on his back.

One hit. Thirty-six people. Everybody.

The Cataract went still.

It hung on his shield for a long moment. Then the weight changed. It didn’t press any more. It just lay. Heavy, on the thorn. And he felt it go out of it. The way you feel somebody fall asleep against you.

He let it down.

It slid off the thorn, slowly, and down off the shelf, and lay half in the water and half on the black rock at the foot of the shelves. Long and pale and still. Its torn fins floating out round it on the water. And as Jared watched, the see-through grey of it began to go cloudy. White. Like ice going white in the sun. Until it lay there at the bottom of the shelves like a long pale stone.

It didn’t go to light. Nothing did, any more. Not since four. It stayed where it fell.

And then the fall stopped.

Jared would remember it for the rest of his life. He’d tell it to people. He’d tell it badly, because there wasn’t a good way to tell it.

The Great Fall stopped.

Not stopped falling. Stopped. The whole white column of it, a hundred and fifty metres of water coming down out of the cloud, just hung there. Every drop. Every curl of spray. Every thread of foam. Hanging in the air, perfectly still, like a picture of a waterfall. And the roar stopped with it. All of it. The silence was so sudden and so total that it hurt his ears.

Every wren on the pool, and on the cliffs, and on Aleaka’s shoulder, went absolutely still.

It lasted about one heartbeat.

Then it fell again. All at once. As if nothing had happened. The roar came back so loud half of them staggered.

Jared stood on the shelf with his shield still up, with the thorn on it red to the base, and looked at the fall. Falling. The way it always had.

Behind him, he heard Ice get up.

He turned round. Ice was on his feet on the back of the shelf. He’d gone white. The whitest Jared had ever seen anybody go. He was looking up at the Great Fall, and his hands, at his sides, were shaking.

Somebody else was on the shelf too. Thea had come along it from the left. She was standing beside Ice, with the lantern in her hand, looking up at the fall the same way. Very calm. Very pale.

“It looked,” said Thea. Quietly.

Ice didn’t answer.

“Didn’t it,” said Thea. “It stopped. And it looked at us.”

“Yeah,” said Ice. His voice was barely there. “It looked.”

Further down the shelves, Chris had his notebook out and was writing. Not fast. Very slowly, and carefully, the way you write something you think you’ll need to read again.

They sat on the shelves for a long time afterwards, round the pool, round the long pale shape at the bottom. Nobody wanted to go anywhere.

Rachael came and took Jared’s arm out of the shield straps without asking, and said things to him about it that he deserved, and put her cold blue light on it. Newish sat down next to him, very suddenly, as if her legs had stopped. Aleaka was crying into Sleet’s ruff, and Sleet was letting her, and Tuppence was sitting on Sleet’s head, and Sleet was letting that too.

“Every time,” said Newish, eventually. Hoarsely. “Every single time, I’m gonna have to remember not to count.”

“You counted at the end,” said Jared.

“I counted at the end on purpose.” She leaned her head back against the rock. “That’s different. That’s me deciding. That’s—“ She stopped. “That’s me giving it the beat. Not it taking it.”

Jared looked at the Great Fall. Falling.

“The tower took your count,” he said. “And you took it back.”

Newish didn’t say anything for a long time.

“Yeah,” she said at last. Quietly. “Yeah. I did.”

Hana came up the shelf and sat down on his other side, with her crown back on, crooked, the iron rose in front. She was still a bit grey. Milli was right behind her, and stayed right behind her, with his tail round her wrist.

“Jared,” said Hana. “That thorn.”

“Roses have them, Alice.”

“You put a spike on your shield. With a broken arm. And let it land on you. On purpose.” She looked at him. “Gabe said you’d say that.”

“Gabe knows me.”

Hana looked at him for a long moment. Then she put her head on his shoulder. The good one. Just for a second.

“Thank you,” said Hana.

“It’s the job,” said Jared. And then, because she was still there: “You made it fall in love with a waterfall, Alice. Don’t talk to me about spikes.”

Ice came and found him at dusk. On the shelf, with his shield across his knees, with the thorn still on it. Cleaned now. He’d decided to keep it on.

Ice sat down next to him. He had colour back in his face. Some. He had something in his hand. Flat, and pale, and rectangular, about the size of a playing card.

He held it out.

Jared looked at it.

It was a piece of river-folk paper. The stiff pale kind they made out of pressed reed. And on one side, in ink, very carefully, there was a drawing. A jetty. A lake. The falls behind. And sitting on the end of the jetty, with his legs over the water, a man with short swept hair and a strand over one eye, eating fried fish out of a paper cone with enormous concentration. There were little lines coming off the fish to show it was hot.

Across the top, in Ice’s handwriting, in careful capitals, it said:

GREETINGS FROM WENDMERE.

Jared stared at it.

“Gabe told me,” said Ice, “that on floor 5, at the Linkworks, you said I owed you a postcard.”

Jared turned it over.

On the back, in the same handwriting, smaller:

Alice — look who I found. Still eating. — I.

Jared laughed.

It came out of him so suddenly and so hard that it hurt his arm and he didn’t care. He laughed until he couldn’t breathe, on the wet black shelf at the edge of the big pool, with a dead serpent at the bottom of it and the Great Fall roaring and a postcard in his hand. It was the stupidest thing. It was the oldest bit they had. A photo of him eating, on Earth, in some parking lot, that Ice had turned into a postcard and sent to Hana, years ago, to tease her. And then they’d kept doing it. All of them. For years. And then Ice had died. And then a white room. And then nothing.

And now here it was. Drawn in ink on a piece of reed. On floor 8.

He was crying. He was still laughing. He didn’t know which.

“ALICE,” he shouted down the shelf. “ALICE. LOOK.”

Hana looked. She came up the shelf, and took it out of his hand, and looked at the front, and turned it over, and read the back.

And then she put her hand over her mouth and laughed exactly the way he had.

Ice sat on the shelf next to them and watched them both, and didn’t say anything at all. But Jared saw his face. And whatever had been on it on the far shelf four days ago, when Drew put his hand up and he stood there and watched, it was gone.
