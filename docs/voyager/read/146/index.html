Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 4: The Second Row

Chapter 146

Weather

5,065 words · 22 min read

Rui Xiang had read a lot of weather in his life, and Tell had more of it than anywhere.

That was the first thing he’d noticed, coming up the well. Before the market went quiet. Before the clerk. Before the cord. He’d come out of the well-house behind Xav, into the light, into a square full of a few hundred people who’d stopped breathing, and his eyes had done what his eyes always did. They’d gone looking for the weather.

Who leaned toward who. Who stood with their back to a wall. Whose hands went still. Whose weight went onto their heels, ready to go, and whose went onto their toes.

On every floor below this one, the weather had been simple. On floor 1 there hadn’t been any; the people had stood where they stood like painted skies on a ceiling. Further up it had got more real, floor by floor, until on the tenth there’d been a whole city of hold-folk who leaned and flinched and stood their ground like anybody. But even there, it had been one kind of weather. Everybody in the Bastion had been facing the same way. Up the pass. At the cold.

Tell wasn’t facing anywhere. Tell was every weather at once. Every face in that market had been doing its own thing, for its own reasons, and changing its mind while he watched.

He’d been reading it for twenty-two days now. He still wasn’t tired of it.

Two days after the host’s hand, the weather in the Low Town was warm.

You could feel it walking down the Wellgate. People said morning to them. A knife-grinder in the Shambles who’d written Rui Xiang at the Tellhouse door, in a fierce small hand, the quiet one who watches my wheel, waved his hat at him every time he went past. The old woman with the half-door, Nell, who’d written Gabe, the big one who catches things, kept a bun back for him in a cloth every morning and pretended she hadn’t. A lamplighter who’d written John stood at the foot of the copy-room steps at dusk on the second night and lit the lamp over the stable door, which hadn’t been lit in thirty years, and went away without saying anything.

Up the hill, the weather was cold.

The third ring and the fourth hadn’t come to the host’s hand. Their shutters had stayed shut. When Rui Xiang went up the Wellgate past the second wall, the faces up there didn’t look at the grey thread on his wrist and then away, the way they had before. They looked at the slip of cream paper in his pocket, the corner of it, which he’d started carrying because Calvin had, and their weather went hard and flat and fast, like a pond icing over.

On the third night somebody threw a stone through the copy-room’s end window. Nobody was hurt. It came wrapped in a bit of paper with one word on it, in a careful upper-ring hand. CLIMBERS. Just that. As if the word was enough.

Hyvred pinned it to the wall over her blanket.

“It’s spelled right,” she said. “So that’s something.”

He went to Cooper’s Lane on the seventh day after Calvin’s first time, because Calvin asked him to.

“Bring Rui Xiang,” Calvin had said. To Xav. As if Rui Xiang weren’t sitting right there. “He reads faces. I want somebody to read it. I can’t see her face from behind and Hawise can’t look.”

So he went.

It was noon. The lane was hot and smelled of new barrels. Hawise was on her step with her hands in her lap, the way Calvin had described, and when she saw Rui Xiang come round the corner with Calvin, her weather went very complicated: embarrassed, and angry with Calvin for bringing somebody, and under all of it, so far down she probably didn’t know it was there, something that leaned forward. Toward the end of the lane. Toward the corner.

Hope, he thought. That’s what that is. Hope, when you’ve told yourself you haven’t got any.

He stood in the doorway of a cooper’s yard across the lane, where he could see. Calvin stood in the middle of the road with his fists at his sides.

At noon, a figure in long plain grey came round the corner at the end of the lane.

Rui Xiang had looked at a lot of clerks in twenty-two days. He’d tried to read them. He couldn’t help it. And every time, there’d been nothing. Not a blank face, exactly. People had blank faces all the time. A blank face was weather too, a kind of held breath. This was different. This was a face with no weather in it at all. Like a sky painted on a ceiling. You could look at it as long as you liked, and nothing would ever move across it.

She came up the lane. Tall. Brown hair pulled back. Light step.

She came level with the cooper’s yard. With Hawise’s step.

“ALYS!” said Calvin.

He didn’t shout it the way he had the first time, Rui Xiang thought. He’d thought about it. You could see he’d thought about it all week. He said it loud, but not like a dropped pan. Like somebody calling across a field to somebody they knew was there.

The clerk stopped.

Rui Xiang watched her. All of her. That was the trick: not the face. The face last. First the feet, which stopped mid-stride, the way Calvin had said. Then the hands. The right one, with the grey pen, came up off the book a finger’s width and shook.

And then the head.

It turned. Not far. Not round. A little way. To the left. Toward Hawise’s step. The way you’d turn your head on a train, half asleep, when somebody across the carriage says something that sounds, for a second, like your name.

And Rui Xiang saw her face.

For about the time it takes to breathe in, there was weather on it.

Not much. Not a storm. The very beginning of something. The way the sky goes at the far edge of a flat country, an hour before rain, when the light changes and you can’t say how. A darkening. A long way off. Something moving, very slowly, across a sky that had been painted on a ceiling for twelve years.

Then it was gone. The head turned back. The pen went down. She walked on up the lane, not hurrying, and round the corner, and was gone.

Nobody in the lane moved for a long time.

“Well?” said Calvin. Hoarse. “Rui Xiang. Well?”

Rui Xiang looked at Hawise. She was sitting on her step with both hands flat over her mouth and her eyes squeezed shut.

“She looked,” said Hawise, behind her hands. “She looked.”

“Something moved,” said Rui Xiang.

They both turned to him.

“In her face. For a second.” He didn’t know how to say it better. He’d never been good at saying it. Only at seeing it. “It was a long way down. But it moved.”

Hawise took her hands away from her face. She looked at him for a long time, with her eyes wet and very sharp.

“Next week,” she said. To Calvin.

“Next week,” said Calvin.

The clerks were everywhere now.

Not more of them, he thought. The same number. But they’d moved. Since the Bench, since the host’s hand, they’d stopped standing on the corners of the Low Town and started standing on the corners of the Wellgate itself, all the way up, one at every turn of the road, like milestones. You couldn’t go anywhere in Tell without walking past four of them.

And they’d started watching. That was the other thing. Before, they’d stood with their books and looked at nothing. Now they looked at something. Rui Xiang had checked. He’d walked up the Wellgate three times in a day, at different hours, by himself and with others, and watched where each clerk’s face was turned as he went past.

At him, when he was alone. At the group, when he was with the group.

At Thea, when Thea was there.

Every time. Every clerk on every corner. Whatever else was in the street, whoever else, when Thea came up the Wellgate with her lantern, every painted face on every corner was turned toward her, the whole way, like flowers following the sun.

He told Xav. Xav already knew. Xav had seen it on the second day.

“Does Thea know?” said Rui Xiang.

Xav looked at him for a while.

“Thea knows everything,” said Xav. “That’s sort of the problem.”

He’d been reading Thea for a long time. Since floor 1, if he was honest. She was one of the ones who was interesting to read, because she was always reading something herself, and her weather was always somewhere else. Under the thing she was looking at. Under the window.

But since the top of floor 10, it had changed.

He’d noticed it on the morning after the aurora. They’d come back round the tarn, Declan and Thea, in the grey before dawn, to the fire Gabe had built at the water’s edge, and Rui Xiang had been awake, because he always was, and he’d watched them come. And Thea’s weather had been something he’d never seen on anybody.

It was the weather of somebody carrying a full cup up a stair.

Not a heavy thing. A full thing. Something that wasn’t hard to carry, exactly, but that you couldn’t spill. Not a drop. So every step was careful, and every step you were thinking about it, and you didn’t look up from it. Not even when somebody called your name.

She’d been carrying it ever since. A hundred days. Up four floors. And she still hadn’t spilled any.

Gabe’s weather was different. Gabe had been carrying something since floor 10 too, since the Breath, since the morning on the barracks step with Hearthwall. But Gabe’s was the weather of somebody standing on a wall. Feet set. Shield up. Facing out. Not carrying anything in his hands at all. Just standing in front of it, whatever it was, so nobody could get at it.

And the thing Rui Xiang had noticed, on this floor, on Tell’s hot loud stony streets, was that every time Thea was in the room, Gabe watched her.

Not the way you watch somebody you like. The way you watch somebody carrying a full cup past you on a stair. Ready to put a hand out. Not putting it out.

And Thea never once looked back at him.

Two people holding the same thing, Rui Xiang thought. From different ends. And neither of them able to let go of their end long enough to pick up the other one properly.

He didn’t know what the thing was. He’d decided, on floor 10, on the barracks roof at dawn, that he didn’t need to. You’re holding something, he’d said to Xav. Put it down. In the place. And Xav had. With Gabe. And Rui Xiang hadn’t asked what.

He still didn’t. He just watched the weather.

On the fifth night, it was Xav’s party’s watch.

They’d taken to keeping one, since the stone through the window. Two at a time, two hours each, on the lead roof over the copy-room, where you could see down the Wellgate one way to the Lantern Market and up it the other way to the second wall. And straight across the road, past the Tellhouse’s wide front steps, to the Countinghouse.

Rui Xiang had the middle watch. Midnight to two. Malcolm had the first half with him and fell asleep sitting up against the chimney at about half past twelve, with his healer’s hands loose in his lap, the way he always did, and Rui Xiang let him. Malcolm worked at the pump all day with Rachael. Malcolm needed sleep more than the roof needed two people on it.

The Wellgate was empty. Tell went to bed early. A few lamps in the upper rings. A dog, a long way off. The clerk on the corner below the Tellhouse, standing in the dark under the dead lamp with its book, not moving.

And the Countinghouse.

He’d been looking at it for twenty-two days. Tall and narrow and grey. A plain door at the top of four plain steps. No windows on the street. No windows on the sides, either; he’d walked round it. Nobody ever went in it but clerks. And clerks, in his experience, went in at dusk, one at a time, and came out at dawn, one at a time, and never once at any other hour.

At about a quarter past one, a man came up the Wellgate.

Rui Xiang saw him from a long way off. Lamps or not, he’d have known him by the walk. A big soft man, heavy on his heels, in a long dark cloak with the hood up, coming up the hill from the second wall on his own at a time when nobody in Tell was on the Wellgate on their own. Not hurrying. Not dawdling either. Walking the way people walk when they’ve decided to go somewhere they’d rather not, and want to get there before they change their mind.

The man stopped at the foot of the Countinghouse steps.

He looked up and down the road. Rui Xiang watched him do it. Up, to the second wall. Down, to the Lantern Market. Across, at the Tellhouse. Never up, at the roofs. People never looked at the roofs.

He pushed his hood back a little to look, and the lamp over the copy-room stable door, the one the lamplighter had lit, caught his face, and his hand on the hood.

Rings. On every finger.

Hamo Tull went up the four steps of the Countinghouse. He didn’t knock.

The door opened anyway.

Rui Xiang didn’t wake Malcolm.

He thought about it. He’d tell Xav that, after, and Xav would look at him the way Xav looked at people, and he’d say it again: I thought about it. He looked at Malcolm asleep against the chimney with his mouth slightly open, and he thought about waking him, and Xav, and Gabe, and the rule that was on the rock and had been since floor 5. Nobody goes alone.

And then he thought: the door’s open now. It won’t be open when I’ve woken three people and explained.

He was already on the ladder.

He’d always been first. Declan had said it, at seventeen, laughing, in a room Rui Xiang couldn’t picture any more, about something that didn’t matter. You always get there first, RX. You just don’t know what to do when you’re there. He’d been first to twenty-five on floor 4. First to forty-five on floor 10. First under a wing and into a heart, more than once.

He crossed the Wellgate in the dark in about four seconds.

The clerk under the dead lamp on the corner turned its face toward him as he went past. He didn’t look at it.

The Countinghouse door had closed behind Tull. But not all the way. A finger’s width of grey light came out of the gap at the edge, onto the top step. As if somebody had shut it and not quite pushed.

As if it had been left.

He stood on the top step with his back flat against the wall beside the door, and listened, and heard nothing.

Then he went in.

It was bigger inside than out.

That was the first thing. He knew how big the building was. He’d paced round it. Thirty paces long, ten wide. And the door opened onto a narrow stone passage, ten paces, plain, grey, with no lamp but a grey light coming from the far end of it. And the far end opened onto a hall that was longer than the building.

Much longer. It went back, and back, under a high grey ceiling held up by grey pillars in rows, further than the Countinghouse could possibly go, further than the Tellhouse across the road, further, it seemed to him, than the hill. The light didn’t come from anywhere. There were no lamps, no windows, no candles. The air itself was grey, and lit, the way the sky is lit on a day with no sun in it.

And it was full of clerks.

Rows of them. Hundreds. At long grey desks, one behind another, all facing the same way, toward the far end of the hall, each with a grey book open in front of them and a grey pen in their hand. Writing. All of them. All at once. The sound of it filled the hall. Not loud. A dry, steady, whispering scratch, like rain on a roof a long way off, or like the ticking of the Down on floor 9. Hundreds of pens. Never stopping.

He’d never seen so many faces with no weather in them. It was like looking at a field of painted skies.

He went sideways, behind the first row of pillars, into the grey shadow there, and stopped.

At the far end of the hall, on a raised step, there was a lectern. Tall and grey. And on it, open, there was a book. A great one. Bigger than any book he’d ever seen. Bigger than a door. Its pages were grey and its writing was grey and it lay open on the lectern like something sleeping.

A clerk stood at it. Just one. Taller than the others. Not writing. Turning a page, slowly, as he watched. And then another.

And in front of the step, at the bottom of it, with his hood down and his rings on his folded hands, stood Hamo Tull.

He was talking. Rui Xiang couldn’t hear it at first, over the pens. He went along behind the pillars, one at a time, slowly, the way he’d gone along the roofs on floor 4, until he was close enough. Four pillars back. Three.

“—at the Reckoning,” Tull was saying. Low. Not the warm voice from the Bench. Something smaller, and faster, and much less sure of itself. “From the steps. As you used to. I’ve asked it of the Bench and the Bench can’t refuse the Count, it never has, not in three hundred—“ He stopped. Breathed. “And the corded man’s two hands. In full. Before the Magistrate, and before the whole of Tell. Not just told and not told. Every line. Read out.”

The tall clerk at the lectern turned another page.

“Told,” it said.

The voice was flat and ordinary, like the clerk at the well. But it filled the hall. Every one of the hundreds of pens, for the space of that one word, went still. And then went on.

Tull swallowed. Rui Xiang saw it. He saw all of Tull’s weather from three pillars back, and it was terror. Plain, cold terror, the kind that’s been there so long it’s got used to itself. Not of the climbers. Not of the Magistrate.

Of this. Of the thing he was standing in front of. He’d come here, at a quarter past one in the morning, on his own, to ask it for something. And he was more frightened of it than of anything in the world.

“And the woman,” said Tull.

The page didn’t turn.

“The one you look at,” said Tull. “The one with the lantern. They all look at her. Every one of you on every corner. I’ve seen it. What do you want with—“

The tall clerk’s hand stopped on the page.

It didn’t say anything. It didn’t look up. It just stopped, with its grey hand flat on the great grey book, and stood there. And after a long moment, Tull’s weather went somewhere Rui Xiang had never seen on anybody: the weather of a man who puts his hand flat on a door in the dark, and feels something on the other side put its hand back.

“Never mind,” said Hamo Tull. Very quickly. “Never mind. It’s not my business. I’m sorry. I don’t need to know.”

He turned round and walked back down the hall between the desks, fast, almost running, with his cloak flapping and his rings clicking, past the pillars, past Rui Xiang without seeing him, into the passage, and out. The door didn’t close behind him.

Rui Xiang stood behind the pillar and didn’t breathe.

And every pen in the hall stopped.

All of them. At once. Hundreds. The sound of rain on a roof, gone, like a tap turned off. In the silence he could hear his own heart.

And every clerk at every desk lifted its head.

And turned it.

Toward the pillar.

He didn’t wait to see their faces. He knew what was on them. Nothing. Hundreds of painted skies, all turned toward him, all at once, with nothing moving across any of them at all.

He went. Back along the pillars. Not running; you didn’t run. Into the passage. Ten paces of grey stone. And out, past the door that stood open a finger’s width, onto the top step, into the warm dark of the Wellgate, under the real sky.

Nothing followed him.

Behind him, very quietly, the Countinghouse door closed. All the way. On its own.

And through it, after a moment, very faint, he heard the pens begin again.

Xav was on the roof when he got back.

Sitting on the parapet, in his shirt, with his arms folded. Malcolm was awake beside the chimney, white-faced, half up. Rui Xiang came up the ladder and stopped at the top of it and looked at them both, and Xav looked back at him, and the weather on Xav’s face was the weather Rui Xiang had seen on it exactly twice in nine years.

“You went in,” said Xav. “Alone.”

“I got there first.”

“You always get there first.” Xav’s voice was very quiet. “Dec said that. When you were seventeen.”

“I know what Dec said.”

“Nobody goes alone, Rui. It’s on the rock. It’s been on the rock since floor 5. You voted—“

“I know.” Rui Xiang came the rest of the way up and sat down on the lead, with his back against the parapet, because his legs had decided to. “I know. I’m sorry.” He looked at his hands. They were shaking. They never shook. “Xav.”

“What.”

“I didn’t know what to do. When I got there.” He was quiet. “So I came back.”

Xav didn’t say anything.

“That’s what he said I never did,” said Rui Xiang. “Dec. You don’t know what to do when you’re there. And I didn’t. I stood behind a pillar and I didn’t know. And every one of them turned round and looked at me.” He looked up at Xav. “And I came back. I didn’t do anything. I just came back and told you.”

Xav looked at him for a long time.

Then his weather changed. Rui Xiang watched it. The anger went out of it, slowly, the way heat goes out of a stone. And something else came in. Something Rui Xiang had seen on Xav’s face on the barracks roof on floor 10, when Rui Xiang had told him to put the thing down.

“That’s new,” said Xav.

“Yeah.”

“Okay.” Xav unfolded his arms. “Tell me.”

He told them. All of it. The hall that was longer than the building. The pens. The great grey book on the lectern, and the tall clerk turning its pages. Tull, and his terror. At the Reckoning. From the steps. The corded man’s two hands. In full. Before the whole of Tell.

And the woman. The one you look at.

The hand stopping on the page.

When he’d finished, nobody said anything for a while. Malcolm had his arms round his knees. Down the Wellgate, the clerk on the corner under the dead lamp stood with its book, facing the copy-room. Facing them.

“In full,” said Xav, at last. Very quietly. Not to anybody.

Rui Xiang read him.

He knew Xav’s weather better than anybody’s. Nine years. And there was something in it, under the quiet, that he’d felt there since floor 10 and never looked at directly. Because he’d decided not to. Because it wasn’t his.

“You know what it says,” said Rui Xiang.

Xav didn’t answer straight away.

“I know what we think,” said Xav.

“We.” Rui Xiang nodded. “Your club.” He didn’t say it unkindly. He’d known about the club since floor 6. Everybody had, more or less. You couldn’t have thirteen people go quiet every time you came into the hay hall without knowing there was a club. “Then you’d better tell whoever needs telling. Before the Reckoning. Before they read it out.” He looked at his hands, which had nearly stopped shaking. “Not me. I don’t need it.”

“Who, then?” said Xav.

Rui Xiang looked at him.

“Thea already knows,” he said.

Xav went very still.

“Not what you think,” said Rui Xiang. “What is.” He didn’t know how he knew the difference. He just did. It was in her weather. A full cup, up a stair, for a hundred days, and never once spilled. “Look at her. You don’t carry a guess like that.” He leaned his head back against the parapet. “She’s not guessing. She knows. Somebody told her.”

Xav didn’t say anything at all.

Beside the chimney, Malcolm made a very small sound.

The crier came up the Wellgate at noon on the three hundred and eighty-third day, in his blue coat, with his bell, and stopped on the Tellhouse steps, where the whole Wellgate could hear him. Two paces behind him, as always, stood a clerk.

Rui Xiang was in the copy-room yard with the others. All thirty-six of them. They’d heard the bell coming up the hill and come out to the gate to see.

“By the Count’s asking,” cried the crier, “and the Warden’s petition, granted by the Bench: at the Reckoning, for the first time in three hundred years, the Count’s clerk shall cry the climbers from the Assize steps. And the disputed man’s two hands shall be read in full. Every line. Before the Magistrate, and before Tell.”

He rang his bell. And went on up the hill.

Rui Xiang didn’t watch him go. He was reading the yard.

He’d been reading them for three hundred and eighty-three days. He knew all of their weather. He watched it now, all at once, move across the yard like the shadow of a cloud across a field.

Most of them were just puzzled. In full. Every line. What did that mean? What lines? Calvin was frowning. Tiger’s tail had gone round his ankle. Dennis had his merchant’s face on, doing sums.

Some of them weren’t puzzled. The thirteen. The club. Their weather had all gone the same way at once, and it was the weather of people who’d been afraid of something for a long time and had just heard its footstep on the stair. Hyvred. Chris. Winter, with her jaw set. Xav, beside him. Very still.

Rachael wasn’t in the club. Her weather went the same way as theirs anyway.

He looked at Declan.

Declan was by the stable door, with the cord on his wrist, with Gabe on his right and Thea on his left. He’d heard it. He was standing exactly as he’d been standing before he heard it.

And for the first time since a kitchen on floor 7, Rui Xiang read him.

He’d never been able to before. Not really. Declan’s weather had always been somewhere a long way under, like the river-folk’s lights that Yan talked about, turned down low and long for a night that never seemed to end. You could watch him all day and only see the surface. Which was calm. Always calm.

It wasn’t calm now.

Rui Xiang saw it come up. Up from wherever it lived. Through all the long low years of it, to the surface, and stay there, plain on his face, for anybody to see who knew how to look.

Fear.

Not for himself. Rui Xiang could tell that much. You could always tell, with fear, who it was for; it leaned. And Declan’s leaned left. Toward the person on his left, with the lantern.

Thea’s weather didn’t change at all.

That was the thing that frightened Rui Xiang most, afterwards. Every other person in that yard moved. Thea didn’t. She stood beside Declan with the lantern in her hand and the full cup she’d been carrying for a hundred days, and when the crier said every line, she didn’t spill a drop. She just looked up the Wellgate. At the Countinghouse. At its plain grey door.

The way you’d look at a door you’d decided to go through.

And Gabe, on Declan’s right, didn’t look at the Countinghouse. He didn’t look at the crier, or at Declan.

He looked at Thea.

Two people, Rui Xiang thought. Holding the same thing. From different ends.

He looked at Xav.

Xav was already looking at him.
