He couldn’t stop shaking.

It wasn’t the cold. Or it was, but not the cold of the night, which was the first night on fifteen that hadn’t been cold at all, a soft wet spring night at the foot of the Narrows with the river going past fast and brown and loud and full of nothing worse than branches. It was the other cold. The one he’d taken. It had gone out of him, most of it, thirty-five ways. But some had stayed. In his chest, in the bones of his hands, the way the smell of smoke stays in a coat. And his body didn’t know what to do with it except shake.

They’d made a fire on the rock at the foot of the cliff, under the ledge. A big one. Every bit of driftwood the flood had left. Thirty-six people round it, and Sabe and Tuli, and nobody talking much.

Everybody was shaking a bit.

That was the thing he kept seeing. He’d thought it would be him. He’d thought he’d sit by the fire shaking and they’d sit round him not shaking, and look at him, and he’d say I’m fine, and they’d say you’re not, the way it went. It wasn’t that. Calvin was shaking. Dennis had a blanket round him and his teeth were going. Hako was sitting very still the way Hako sat when he’d taken a hit he hadn’t let anybody see, with the dog’s head on his knee. Bee was wrapped round her own knees. Chi was holding Waddles against her chest inside her coat and Waddles was the only one who seemed warm.

He’d asked. And they’d taken it. Not thin. Not lightly. At the end they’d taken a whole river’s winter between them because he’d asked them to, all at once, and gone down on one knee on the rock with it, and got up again.

And now they were all sitting round a fire on the first warm night of the year, shaking.

“Stop it,” said Xola.

She was kneeling in front of him with both hands on his chest. Her hands were warm, the way healers’ hands were warm, and her face was the face she’d had every day on fourteen in the healing: furious, and very careful, and not looking at his face.

“I’m not doing anything.”

“You’re looking round at everybody like it’s your fault they’re cold.”

“It is my fault they’re cold.”

“It’s their fault they’re cold, Icey. They picked it up.” She pushed a bit more warm into him, and it went in and met the cold in his chest and didn’t do much. “You asked. They said yes. That’s what yes means. You don’t get to feel bad about people saying yes.” She sat back on her heels. “God. You’re like a fridge.”

“Sorry.”

“Don’t say sorry. Say a number.”

“Sixty-eight.”

“That’s better than it was. It’s not good.” She put her hands back on. “And I’m going to sit here till it is. And you’re going to let me. And you’re not going to say it’s fine, see to the others, because Kei and Maigo and Newish and Malcolm are seeing to the others and I’m seeing to you, because Thea asked me to, because she’s—“

She stopped.

Thea was beside him. On his right. She’d been there since the pan touched the rock. She had her hand in his and her head on his shoulder and her eyes shut, and she wasn’t asleep. She hadn’t said anything since I care that it was. She’d just stayed. Leaning. The way the lantern leaned.

“Because she’s busy,” said Xola. More quietly. She looked at Thea for a moment. Then back at his chest. “Seventy?”

“Seventy-one.”

“Good,” said Xola. “Ice coob.”

He nearly laughed. It hurt.

Gabe sat down on his other side after a while.

He didn’t say anything for a long time. He’d taken Hearthwall off his back and propped it against the rock behind them, and the shield was warm, and the warm came off it into Ice’s back. Gabe sat with his elbows on his knees and looked at the fire.

“There was a bit,” said Gabe eventually, “where there weren’t any numbers.”

“I know.”

“Rachael was shouting. You’d gone white. Whiter. Your eyes were open.” Gabe looked at the fire. “I had my hands in your collar and I was saying Dec. I don’t know how many times.”

“I heard some.”

“About four hundred, Rachael says.” Gabe was quiet. “I kept thinking: I’m right here. I’m right here, I’m next to you, I’m not on a different floor, I’m not eighty-six days off in a furnace hall standing in the shape you left. I’m right next to you with my hands on you. And you’re going anyway.”

Ice didn’t say anything.

“That’s worse,” said Gabe. “I didn’t know. I always thought it’d be better. Being there.” He rubbed his face. “It’s worse, Dec.”

“I know,” said Ice. “I saw it. From—“ He stopped. He didn’t know how to say it. “In the dark. I saw what it looks like. Somebody holding everything on their own. From the outside. I’d never seen it.” He looked at his hands. They were still shaking. “It looks like somebody going.”

Gabe didn’t say anything.

“So I asked,” said Ice.

“Late.”

“Late. Everybody’s going to say late.”

“Everybody’s going to say late for about a year,” said Gabe. “You’re going to have to live with that.” Then he leaned over, slowly, and put his shoulder against Ice’s, hard, the way he had when they were thirteen and fourteen and sitting on a wall somewhere after something had gone wrong. And left it there.

“Next time,” said Gabe, “ask earlier.”

“I’ll try.”

“Ask for a sandwich first. Warm up.”

He did laugh then. It hurt all the way down. He laughed anyway, with Thea’s head on one shoulder and Gabe’s against the other and Xola’s hands on his chest, and across the fire Rachael, who’d been asleep sitting up against the cliff with her white arms folded, opened one eye and looked at him laughing and shut it again.

He slept for fourteen hours.

He didn’t mean to. He’d meant to sit up. He always sat up, the first night after something; he always took the watch, because he knew what came on the first night after, on every floor, in the first life. Except he didn’t. Not on this floor. He didn’t know anything about the first night after the Breaker. Nobody did.

He woke up because the sun was on his face.

It came down the gap between the cliffs, late in the morning, warm, and lay across the rock and the ashes of the fire and the people asleep round it in heaps. He lay there for a while and looked up at it. Blue, in the gap. A thin blue strip of sky, the first one he’d seen on fifteen that wasn’t grey.

Good morning, said Alea. On his shoulder. She’d been there all night. She was always there all night. You slept. You actually slept. I’ve got it written down. A pause. Ninety-one. The rest’s coming back slowly. It’s not hurt, Ice. It’s just tired. Like the rest of them.

He sat up.

The river was a river.

He’d never seen it like that. He’d come onto fifteen in the last of the ice, and watched it break, and walked beside it for two weeks while it ground and heaved and roared, and he’d never once seen what it was under all that. It was brown. Wide, and fast, and brown with the melt, going through the Narrows in a long smooth pour like beer out of a jug, with a few small white floes riding on it and a lot of branches. That was all. A big river in spring. On the black cliffs on either side, high above it, a dark line where the lake had stood, the height of three houses. Below the line the rock was wet. Above it, dry.

Nothing was frightened.

He didn’t know how he knew that. He just looked at the river and knew it. It was loud and fast and could drown you in a minute and wasn’t frightened of anything.

Sabe was standing at the water’s edge with her pole.

She’d been there a while, he thought. She had that look, the way old people stand at a place they’ve known all their lives that’s changed in the night. She wasn’t listening. Her pole was on her shoulder, not on the ground. She was just looking. Down the Narrows, through the door, at the brown water going through.

He got up. It took a while. He went and stood beside her.

“Never seen it,” said Sabe. Without looking round. “Not in spring. Not like this. In summer, yes, when she’s down. Never in spring. Never the door open with the ice gone through slow.” She looked at the water. “My gran said she saw it once. When she was a girl. Nobody believed her about that either.”

“Did she say what happened?”

“Said somebody sat with it.” Sabe’s mouth did something. “Said it was a woman from up the river who’d lost her husband at the Narrows the spring before, and she went down onto the jam with her pole and sat. Just sat. Talked to it. Nobody knew what she said. And it let go slow, that year.” She was quiet. “I thought it was a story. A gran’s story. To make you less frightened of the ice.”

“Maybe it was.”

“Maybe.” Sabe looked at him with her milky eyes. “Maybe a story’s a thing somebody did once that nobody wrote down.”

He didn’t say anything.

“Your one with the notebook,” said Sabe. “The one with the arm. He’s writing it down?”

“Every word.”

“Good.” She put her pole down. Tock. She didn’t listen. She just put it there, the way you’d put your hand on a dog. “Somebody should.”

Yan came down off the ledge at noon.

He’d stayed up there all night. Wendy had stayed with him. They came down the cliff path together, slowly, Yan with the scythe over his shoulder and Suzu round his neck and Wendy a step behind with her hand on the back of his coat, and when they got to the bottom Yan didn’t go to the fire. He went to the water’s edge, to where the pan had been, where the river was running smooth and brown over the place the heart had gone down. He stood there looking at the water a long time.

Ice went and stood beside him.

“It’s down there,” said Yan. Without looking round. “Right at the bottom. A long way. I can see it if I look.” He was quiet. “Most lights I see are small. Old things, especially. They get small. They pull in. Hers did. On floor 1. Pulled right in, at the bottom, tight, like a fist.” He looked at the water. “That one’s not small any more.”

“How big?”

“Middle.” Yan tipped his head. “It’s sitting in the middle of itself. Not at the bottom. Not going up. Just in the middle.” He was quiet for a while. “I’ve never seen one do that. Go up, and come back, and sit in the middle.” He turned his head and looked at Ice. “Have you?”

“No,” said Ice.

Yan looked at him for a moment longer. His face did nothing. It never did. But Ice had the feeling, the way he sometimes had with Yan, that Yan was looking at something other than his face. Further in.

“Hm,” said Yan.

And looked back at the river.

“I’d like to see it,” said Yan. “Next spring. When it wakes up. To see what it does with its light when it wakes up and finds somebody there.” He was quiet. “I won’t, though. We’ll be up.”

“Somebody’ll be there.”

“Somebody,” said Yan. Not quite a question.

Behind them, Wendy said, to nobody in particular, very cheerfully, “He shouted.”

“It was going up,” said Yan. Without turning round.

“You shouted, though. I’ve known you forever. You’ve never once—“

“It was going up, Wendy.”

“I know. I’m just saying. Shouted.” Wendy came and stood on Yan’s other side and put her arm through his. “It was really loud. Everybody heard. ICE. IT’S GOING UP.” She did the voice. It wasn’t very good. “I’m going to remember that forever.”

Yan didn’t say anything. He didn’t take his arm away either.

The Float came through the door that afternoon.

They watched it from the rock. All of them. It came down out of the reed-bay half a mile above, where it had settled on the reeds when the lake ran out and then lifted again on the new river in the night: three hundred people on a town of logs and rope and houses, coming down the brown water toward the Narrows. Slowly. Steering. With poles out all along both sides, and the long steering oars at the back, and on the front, at the very front, Haddo, standing with one hand up.

The door was open. The jam was gone. There was nothing in the Narrows now but river. But Ice watched three hundred people on the Float look at it as they came, and saw their faces, and knew that none of them had ever in their lives gone through the Narrows in spring. They’d waited for summer. Every year. Moored above, in the bays, and waited for the river to go down and the door to be safe, because in spring the door was where the ice went through all at once and took whatever was in front of it.

They came through it slowly. The black cliffs going up on either side, higher than anything, the Float small between them. Poles out. Nobody touching the rock.

Haddo looked up as they passed the rock at the foot of the left cliff. He saw them. Thirty-six people and an old woman and a girl, standing on the rock with the dark line of the lake high on the cliff above them.

He lifted his hand off the steering signal and put it flat on his chest. And held it there, the whole time they went past. Didn’t say anything. Then the Float was past, and through, and out the other side of the Narrows into the wide water below, where the river spread out between low green banks and reed-beds, and turned toward the summer moorings.

Something came off the back of the Float as it went.

Ice saw it go. A small shape. Swimming. It came across the brown water toward the rock, steady, ears flat, and climbed out, and shook itself all over Hako.

The dog.

Hako looked at it. Then at the Float, going away downriver, with a small boy at the back rail waving both arms. Then at the dog.

“Classic,” said Hako.

The boy at the rail was still waving. Hako lifted one hand. Then, after a moment, picked the dog up, which was enormous and wet and not the kind of dog you picked up, and held it up so the boy could see it, the way you’d hold up a fish you’d caught. The boy on the Float jumped up and down and waved harder.

“He’s giving it to you,” said Bee.

“I can see that.”

“Because you went back in for it.”

“I know, Bee.” Hako put the dog down. It sat on his foot. “I don’t know what I’m going to do with a dog. On sixteen. In the dark. I don’t know what sixteen is.”

“It’s always fine,” said Bee.

Hako looked at her for a long time.

“This way,” said Hako finally. To the dog.

The dog wagged.

They went back up the river to Keddle the next day.

All of them, with Sabe and Tuli. It was a day’s walk along the left bank, the way they’d come, except it wasn’t the way they’d come at all. The river had gone down. The ice was gone off the banks. Where there’d been a grinding heaving slope of broken floes there was mud now, and gravel, and drowned reeds lying flat, and here and there a slab of ice the size of a cart stranded high on the bank and melting in the sun, dripping. Everything smelled of mud and water and green. Things were coming up already. Small things. Through the mud.

Keddle’s people met them on the path.

They’d come down off the cliff by the stair door the morning after the lake went out. Eighty-seven of them, with their poles, standing in the mud where their village had been, looking at it.

Six houses on their stilts. Standing. With a great tangle of dark thorny branches wound round their legs, holding them together, still, a week after. Gigi’s bramble. Tight round the legs like a fist.

And fourteen gaps.

Just legs, at some of them. Stumps. The houses gone off the top. At others, nothing at all. Holes in the mud where the legs had been. And Sabe’s.

Sabe walked to it. They let her go first. Nobody went with her. Even Tuli stayed back, on the path, with her mother’s pole.

There were four legs left of Sabe’s house. Out of twelve. Leaning. Old black wood, thick as a man, with the marks on them where the water had been, year after year, a ladder of dark lines going up. The house was gone off the top of them. The ladder was gone. There was a pot in the mud below. An iron pot, upside down, with a dent in it.

Sabe stood under the four legs for a long time. She put her hand on one of them.

Then she bent down, slowly, an old woman of fifty-eight thaws bending in the mud, and turned the pot over, and looked in it, and tipped the water out.

“Well,” said Sabe. “That’s the pot.”

“Every summer we put it back,” said the old man.

The one who’d said it on the cliff by the stair door, the day Hako held the ladder, with the river going over the porches below. His name was Hobb. He was very old, older than Sabe, with a back like a bent pole and hands like roots. He stood in the mud beside Hako, looking up at the four leaning legs of Sabe’s house, and said it again, as if it were the answer to something.

“How?” said Hako.

Hobb looked at him.

“You said,” said Hako. “On the cliff. Every summer we put it back. Your big one’ll know, he held the ladder.” He looked at the legs. “I held the ladder. I don’t know. I said I’d find out.” He was quiet. “So how?”

Hobb looked at Hako for a long moment. Then something happened in his face, a long way down under all the lines, like Sabe’s had on the ledge.

“Legs first,” said Hobb.

It took nine days.

Legs first. New ones. Out of the forest on the hill above Keddle, where Keddle had always cut its legs, the tall straight black trees that grew in the wet ground by the river and didn’t rot. Hako and Drew and Tiger and Salty cut them. Hobb showed them which. Not that one. That one’s too young. That one. Feel it. Feel how heavy it is. That’s the one. They dragged them down the hill on Burr’s rope, two at a time, four people to a leg.

Then you had to put them in.

That was the part nobody had ever told Ice about stilt houses. You didn’t put the legs in the mud. Mud was nothing. Mud went with the first spring. You put them in the riverbed. In the gravel under the mud, under the water, down where the river had laid it down a thousand years ago. You had to find where the gravel was hard and the water ran the right way round the leg and the ice, next spring, would come at it edge-first and not flat.

Maigo found it.

He stood barefoot in the shallow brown water at the river’s edge, up to his shins, with his eyes shut, for most of a morning. Then he walked out into the river, slowly, to his knees, to his waist, and stopped, and said here. And Hobb, on the bank, looked at where he was standing for a long time and said my grandfather’s leg was there. And they drove the first leg in where Maigo was standing.

Twelve legs. Maigo found every one.

Salty did the cross-beams. Salty had always been the one with the nails. He’d driven his hundredth nail into the rings Hana and Milli wore; he stood on a ladder against the new legs with a mouthful of iron and a hammer and did the beams in one long day, and when Hobb came along the bank in the evening and looked up at them he didn’t say anything for a long time. Then he said that’s how my uncle did them. Salty went very red and said it’s just how they go. Hobb said I know. That’s what I said.

Jared made the straps. Iron, out of the old pot, and two others nobody wanted any more, on a forge he built out of river stones on the bank with Mairead’s tongs that she’d left him when the Float went. Iron straps round the tops of the legs, so the beams sat true.

Gigi asked the bramble to let go of the other houses.

He watched her do it. On the third morning. She stood under the first of the six bound houses with her hand on the dark thorny tangle round its legs, and her eyes shut, and her lips moving. It took a long time. It doesn’t let go, the skill said. He’d heard her say that more than once, laughing, a little proud. Grow it where you need it. It doesn’t let go. Now she stood there with her hand on it and talked to it, quietly, for most of an hour.

And it let go.

Slowly. A bit at a time. The thorns uncurling from round the legs like fingers, one, then another, then the whole tangle loosening and slackening and slipping down the black wood into the water, and floating away down the brown river in long dark loops.

Gigi opened her eyes. She looked at the house standing on its own legs again, and at her hand, and at the bramble going away down the river.

“Huh,” said Gigi.

“It let go,” said Ice.

“It let go.” She looked at her hand. “It’s never done that. Not once. Not when I asked. It doesn’t.” She was quiet. “I told it the house was fine now. That somebody had it. That it could let go a bit.” She looked at him, sideways. “I got the idea from somewhere.”

Ina’s hands came back on the fourth day.

They’d been grey. Then they went grey with pink at the fingertips. Then, on the fourth morning, Ice came down to the river and found Ina sitting on a stone at the water’s edge with his hands in his lap, palms up, looking at them. Pink. All the way. A bit swollen. Raw-looking, like hands that have been in snow too long and are coming back.

Winter was sitting beside him. She was fletching arrows. Mairead had left her a bundle of arrowheads, Float iron, forty of them, wrapped in an oiled rag, the morning the Float went through, without a word, and Winter had looked at them for a long time and said thanks to the back of Mairead’s head. She’d been fletching them for three days with goose feathers from the Keddle birds. She had eleven done.

“Look,” said Ina. To Ice. Holding up his hands.

“They’re back.”

“They’re back.” Ina turned them over. “Kei says another week. Before I should put fire in them.” He looked at his palms. “I’ve been putting a little in. Just a little. In the mornings. When he’s not looking.”

“Ina.”

“A little.” Ina looked at him with his calm face. “You took a bit of mine. On the ledge. I’ve been thinking about it.” He was quiet. “I’d never had anybody take a bit of mine before. I’d always been the one who did the taking. The fire, for everybody. The step.” He looked at his hands. “It was strange. Having somebody take a bit. It was lighter.”

“That’s the idea.”

“I know. I’d just never felt it from that side.” Ina looked at the river. “Like you. On the jam. Seeing it from the outside.”

Ice looked at him.

“I didn’t tell anybody that,” said Ice. “About seeing it from outside.”

“You said it in your sleep,” said Ina. “The first night. By the fire. That’s what it looks like. Over and over.” He tilted his head, a fraction. “Thea heard. And me. And Winter.”

Winter didn’t look up from her fletching.

“We’re keeping it,” said Winter.

Tuli made him a pole on the sixth day.

She came and found him on the bank in the evening, where he was sitting with his feet in the water watching Maigo find the tenth leg, and put it down on the mud beside him without a word. A pole. Black wood, from the offcut end of one of the new legs. Taller than him. Straight. With the bark taken off and the end shaped, the way the Keddle poles were, to a blunt round point, and a dark band burned round it a hand from the top.

He looked at it. Then at her.

“Everybody on the river has one,” said Tuli. She sat down on the mud beside him, not close. She had her mother’s pole across her knees. “Gran said you should. You listened. On the first day. When I showed you. You stood still.” She looked at the water. “Most people from off the river can’t stand still. They talk. Or they move. You stood still and you listened and you heard the ice going tock under you before I did.” She was quiet. “And then on the jam. You sat with it.” She looked at her mother’s pole. “So you should have one.”

He picked it up.

It was heavier than it looked. The legs were. Feel how heavy it is. That’s the one. He put the end down on the mud. Tock. It didn’t sound like anything. Mud.

“Not there,” said Tuli. “In the water. Where it’s gravel.”

He stood up. He stepped into the river, to his shins, the way Maigo did. Cold, but not that cold. Not the cold. Just a river. He put the end of the pole down through the water onto the gravel bed.

Tock.

“Now listen,” said Tuli.

He listened.

The river. Going past his legs, fast and brown. The gravel under it, shifting a little, small stones rolling. A long way off, a bird. A hammer, Salty’s, on the new beams. Under all of it, nothing. No groan. No crack. No ice anywhere, talking. No once.

Just the river.

“What do you hear?” said Tuli.

“Nothing,” said Ice. “It’s just a river.”

“Yes,” said Tuli. She was quiet for a long time. “That’s what it sounds like. In summer. When nothing’s frightened.”

He stood in the water with the pole.

“Next spring,” he said. He didn’t look at her. “Somebody should go down to the Narrows. When it wakes. Before the first word. And sit with it.”

“I know.”

“It’ll know how now. A bit at a time. It saw. But it’ll wake up in the dark with everything on top of it again, and it won’t remember, maybe, not at first. And somebody should be there.” He looked down at the pole in the water. “To say hello.”

“I know,” said Tuli. “Me.”

He looked at her.

She was sitting on the mud with her mother’s pole across her knees, fourteen, very small, very straight, looking at the river where it went away downstream toward the Narrows a day off.

“Gran’s too old for the jam,” said Tuli. “And she’ll say it’s a story. Next spring she’ll say it was a story, the man from off the river who sat with it. Because that’s what she does. That’s what Gran’s gran did.” She looked at the water. “But I’ll go. Before the first word. With my pole. I’ll go down onto the ice over it and sit, and I’ll say hello.” She was quiet. “And I’ll tell it there’s somebody holding some. Me. And Gran. And Keddle. And the Float. And next spring after that, somebody else. And a bit at a time.”

He didn’t say anything for a long time.

“That’s where my mum went through,” said Tuli. “The Narrows. In the spring it let go all at once.” She didn’t cry. She’d done that on the pan. “I’m going to go and sit there. Every spring. And say hello to it.”

He stood in the river with the pole.

“Thank you,” he said.

“For what?”

He didn’t know how to say it. For being the one who’s here next spring. When I’m not. When none of us are. For taking a bit.

“For the pole,” said Ice.

Tuli looked at him with her flat face for a long moment. Then, for the first time since he’d met her on the stilts of Keddle in the grey of the first morning, she smiled.

“It’s a pole,” said Tuli.

Hako’s spear turned up on the seventh day.

Bee found it. She’d gone downriver along the bank in the morning with her charcoal and her birch-bark, to draw Keddle from below, from where the river bent. And on a gravel bar at the bend, half a mile down, where the water had piled stones and branches and mud when the lake went through, there was a spear. Standing up. Point down in the gravel, shaft up, at a lean, with a wreath of drowned reeds hung on it like a flag.

She ran all the way back. She was out of breath when she got to the bank. She grabbed Hako’s sleeve and pulled him, without saying anything, the whole half mile, with the dog running alongside barking. Ice went too. Most of them did. It was that kind of morning.

Hako stood on the gravel bar and looked at his spear.

It was his. You could see it was. The shaft had his marks on it, from four floors of his hands. It had gone into the river at Keddle the day he held the ladder, and gone under, and the whole river had gone over it for a week. And the lake had gone out through the Narrows, half a mile of water, and it had carried his spear down to the bend and put it in the gravel. Standing up.

“It’s always fine,” said Bee. Very seriously.

Hako looked at her.

Then he pulled the spear out of the gravel, and took the reeds off it, and looked along the shaft, the way you’d look at a friend who’d been away.

“This way,” said Hako.

And Bee, standing on the gravel bar in the sun, with the river going past, burst into tears, and Hako put the spear over his shoulder and his other arm round her and walked her back up the bank to Keddle without saying anything at all, with the dog going ahead.

Ice watched them go.

He’d never in his life seen anything come back. In two lives. The things that went into the river didn’t come back. The people who went in front of him on the pass didn’t. That was what he’d always known. You held everything, because what you let go of was gone.

He stood on the gravel bar for a while after they’d gone.

Then he put his new pole down in the water. Tock.

Just a river.

Sabe’s house was finished on the ninth day.

Twelve new legs, driven into the gravel where Maigo said, where Sabe’s gran’s had been. The four old ones, left standing beside them, leaning, with their ladder of dark water-lines, because Sabe said leave them. They were my mother’s. Salty’s beams. Jared’s straps. A floor of planks off the hill. Walls. A roof of reeds that the Keddle children had cut and tied and that Hana had helped them tie, in bundles, laughing, with her sleeves rolled up and reed-dust in her hair. A ladder. A porch.

The iron pot, dented, on a hook by the door.

Sabe went up the ladder first. Slowly. She stood on the porch and put her hand flat on the door, the way Hyvred put her hand on the worn places by doors. Then she went in.

She came out after a while.

“Stove,” said Sabe.

There wasn’t one. There was a hearth, a box of river stones with clay round it, but no fire in it, and no wood. Ice started to say something about wood.

Ina went up the ladder.

He didn’t ask. He went up past Sabe, into the house, and Ice saw through the door him kneel at the hearth with his pink raw hands, and look at them. Then he held them over the empty stones. Palms down.

And a little fire came out of them.

Small. Orange. Ordinary. Not the white-blue, not the mask. Just fire, a handful of it, the kind you’d make with a flint on a wet evening. It dropped out of his hands onto the stones and sat there, burning on nothing, and Ina sat back on his heels and looked at it.

Kei, at the bottom of the ladder, said, “Ina.”

“A little,” said Ina. From inside. “Just for the house.”

Kei looked at Ice. Ice looked at Kei. Kei sighed, and wrote something in his little book, and didn’t go up.

Sabe stood in the door of her house, on her porch, on her twelve new legs over the river, with the smoke from Ina’s fire starting to come up out of the reed roof behind her. Below her, in the mud, thirty-six people and eighty-seven people and a dog. She looked down at all of them with her milky eyes.

“Next spring,” said Sabe.

Nobody said anything.

“Next spring she’ll take something,” said Sabe. “She always does. She’ll wake up in the dark and take something.” She put her pole down on the porch. Tock. “And next summer we’ll put it back.”

She looked at Hako.

“Now you know,” said Sabe. “How.”

Hako looked up at her for a long time.

“Legs first,” said Hako.

Sabe laughed. The same laugh as on the jam, when the corner went. Like ice settling. Then she went inside and shut the door, and they heard her moving about in there, an old woman in her new house, finding where things went.

They went back down to the Narrows on the tenth day.

Because Maigo said so. He’d said so on the night of the Breaker, by the fire, shaking with the rest of them, with his bare feet on the rock. There’s a hollow. Behind the place the last arm had held. In the crack in the left cliff where the fingertips had gripped. There’s a hollow, going up. I felt it. When it let go.

They stood at the foot of the left cliff, on the rock where the pan had bumped, and looked at it.

The crack where arm 1 had gripped the cliff all spring was a door.

It had always been a door. You could see that now, with the arm gone out of it and the water down. A narrow tall opening in the black rock, a man’s width, just under the ledge where Chris’s party had camped. Steps went up inside it. Cut into the rock. Very old. Wet still, at the bottom, with the river’s line on them. Dry higher up. Going up into the dark inside the cliff, turning, out of sight.

The arm had been holding the door.

All spring. All the springs. With all of itself. Not to keep anybody out. Just because it was a place to hold. A crack in a cliff the right size for a hand. And nobody had ever got close enough to see what it was holding on to.

“Huh,” said Chris. He had his notebook out. One-handed. His arm was out of the strap now, but still stiff. He wrote something down. “The stair was under its hand the whole time.”

“It’s always the last place you look,” said Calvin.

“That’s not a real saying, Calvin.”

“It’s a very real saying.”

Ice stood at the bottom of the steps.

He didn’t go up. He stood there with Tuli’s pole in his hand and looked at the stair going up into the dark, and didn’t know what was at the top of it. Sixteen. He didn’t know anything about sixteen. He didn’t know what it was or who lived there or what it would ask of them. Alea’s logs were grey. They’d be grey for the rest of the way up, now. Every floor. He’d known that since the trapdoor.

He’d thought it would frighten him. Not knowing.

It did, a bit.

“Ice,” said Higu.

She was beside him. She had the rock. The new one, from floor 1, with the two columns. She held it out. On the right-hand side, under A FOREST and A RIVER and HE DOESN’T KNOW and HE ASKED HOW and THERE, there was one more line. Small. In chalk. Not underlined.

HELP.

He looked at it.

“You said it,” said Higu. “Down the thread. On the jam. To all of us.” She looked at the rock. “I wrote it down. While I was holding it. I had the chalk in my hand.” She was quiet. “It’s not a where.”

“No.”

“It’s a how.” She looked at him. “It’s the best how you’ve ever told me.”

He didn’t say anything. He couldn’t.

Higu took the rock back. She put it in her bag. Then she looked up the stair, into the dark, and said what she always said, at the foot of every stair since the Long Acre. Except it wasn’t what she always said.

“Where?” said Higu. And then, before he could answer: “Never mind. You don’t know. Nobody does.” She hitched her bag up. “Up, then. All of us. We’ll find out.”

There was a light at the top of the stair.

They saw it after the first turn. Small, a long way up, in the dark inside the cliff. Not a lamp. Not a torch. Pale, and round, and about the size of an apple, and moving. Not like a flame moves. Like something drifting. Coming down the stair toward them, slowly, from the top, turning a little in the air, the way a leaf turns going down a well.

Everybody stopped.

It came down three turns of the stair and stopped too. A few steps above them. Hanging in the dark. Pale and round. It didn’t belong to anybody. Ice knew that the moment he saw it, the way he’d known the river wasn’t frightened. He’d seen lights like it for two years, thirty-five of them, one at every one of his friends’ shoulders, the way Alea sat on his. Every one belonging to somebody. This one didn’t have anybody. It was just there. A ball of light, on its own, in the dark, with nobody to belong to.

At the back of the line, Aleaka’s goat stopped dead and looked at it.

And Hana, beside Milli, three people behind Ice, made a small sound.

He turned round.

Hana was standing very still. Her own light, the pale one that had gone at her shoulder for two years, the one that had said Yet, had gone very bright beside her ear. She was looking at the pale thing on the stair above them, and her face was doing something he’d never seen it do. Not frightened. Listening.

“It’s saying something,” said Hana. Very quietly. “I can’t—“ She shook her head. “Not words. I can’t hear it. But it’s saying something.”

The light hung there a moment longer.

Then it turned, and drifted back up the stair, slowly, into the dark, round the next turn, and was gone.

Nobody moved for a long moment.

“Well,” said Milli. Brightly. “That’s new.”

“Alice,” said Ice.

Hana looked at him.

“Do you want to go first?” said Ice.

Everybody went very quiet.

He didn’t know why he’d said it. He’d never in two lives said it. To anybody. Do you want to go first. He’d always been first. On every floor, on every stair, a floor ahead, alone, in the dark.

Hana looked at him for a long moment. Then up the stair, where the light had gone.

“Yes,” said Hana. “I think I do.”

She went past him. Milli went with her, holding her hand. Then Jared, and Newish, and Aleaka with the goat, which went up the stair after Hana without being pulled for the first time in its life.

Then the rest of them.

He went up somewhere in the middle. With Tuli’s pole in one hand.

And Thea’s hand in the other. On every stair.
