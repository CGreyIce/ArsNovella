Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 4: The Second Row

Chapter 124

Deep Water

7,284 words · 32 min read

The window had opened on its own, the first time the song came.

Aleaka hadn’t told anybody that yet. She’d been standing at the stern rail at the end of the first day, with the wax in her palm, trying to decide. Not deciding. Just holding it. Feeling the four small soft yellow balls go warm in her hand while the sun went down behind the islands and the water under the ship turned from green to black. And then the note had come up through the deck into the soles of her feet, very low, a long way down, and her window had opened in front of her face without her asking.

The same one. The one that had been sitting in her quest log since the top of the Great Fall on floor 8, a hundred and eighteen days ago, under the old falls-wren with the white feather.

[ The Call (2 of 3)
Answer one that calls you. ]

And under it, for the first time, a new line. Faint. Pulsing very slowly, in time with nothing, the way the song was pulsing under the hull.

[ Something is calling. ]

She’d stood there and read it four times.

Then she’d put the wax in her pocket.

“You took it out,” said Hana.

It was the morning. Grey and windy and bright, with the islands going past on the right a long way off and the Patience leaning over in the wind so that everything on deck that wasn’t tied down slid gently to the left and had to be fetched. They were in the bow, the five of them. Hana’s party. Where Hana had taken them, after breakfast, with a look on her face that Aleaka had last seen on the shelf above the Cataract, when Jared’s arm broke.

Hana wasn’t looking at Aleaka.

She was looking at Milli.

“One ear,” said Milli. Calm. Sitting on the anchor cable with his tail round his feet. “The left.”

“You took it out. Over deep water. At night. After she told us.” Hana’s voice was very smooth and very level and her tail was three times its normal size. “Everybody going over, one after another, in the dark. She said that. And you took it out.”

“Leaka hadn’t put hers in at all.”

“I know Leaka hadn’t put hers in at all, I’m getting to Leaka—“

“It didn’t pull,” said Milli.

Hana stopped.

“I waited for it to,” said Milli. “I stood there with one ear open, and I waited to feel something take hold of me and draw me to the rail. Rationally. I was ready for it.” He looked at his hands. “Nothing did. It just went on. It’s not that kind of thing.”

“You don’t know what kind of thing it is.”

“No,” Milli agreed. “But I know what it isn’t.” He looked at Aleaka. “And she knows more than me.”

They all looked at her.

Aleaka had been sitting on the bow rail with Tuppence on her shoulder and Sleet lying on her feet and her hands in her lap, trying to find the words. She always had to find the words. They never came the right way round the first time. Hana said she thought in pictures and had to translate, and Aleaka had never been sure if that was a compliment.

“It’s,” she said. “It’s not.”

They waited. They were good at waiting for her. That was one of the best things about them.

“Marrit thinks it’s the Undertow,” said Aleaka slowly. “That it sings, and calls you down. And I can see why. I can. Because it’s—“ She stopped. Tried again. “It’s big. It’s very, very big. You can feel it in the boards. And it’s sad.” She looked at her hands. “It’s so sad, Hana. I’ve never heard anything so sad. Not even the Cataract. The Cataract was just hungry. This is—“

She stopped again.

“It’s not pulling,” she said. “It’s asking.”

“Asking what?” said Newish.

“I don’t know.” Aleaka looked out at the sea. At the long grey swell going past, and the black water under it, going down. “That’s the bit I don’t know. It’s not words. It’s like when Sleet wants something. When she puts her head on your knee. You know she wants something, and you know it’s important to her, and you just have to—“ She waved a hand. “Work it out.”

“And the window,” said Jared.

She looked at him.

Jared was leaning on the foremast with his arms folded and his heavy-lidded eyes on her. He hadn’t said anything yet. He hadn’t needed to. He’d been watching her face since breakfast, the way Jared watched things, without hurry.

“You’ve got a window,” said Jared. “You’ve had it since last night. You keep looking at the air in front of you like there’s something written on it.” He tilted his head. “The Call. Two of three. Right?”

She looked at him for a long moment.

Then she opened it, and turned her hand over, the way you share a window, so that they could all see.

[ The Call (2 of 3)
Answer one that calls you.
Something is calling. ]

Nobody said anything for a while.

“Answer one that calls you,” said Newish eventually. “Brilliant. Great. And the thing that’s calling is the size of an island and lives at the bottom of the sea and the tide-folk have been putting wax in their ears against it since before they had boats.” She looked at Aleaka. “That’s a lot, Leaka. For a two of three.”

“I know.”

“The first one was a wren.”

“I know,” said Aleaka, and laughed, a bit, in spite of herself, because Newish always did that. Said the worst thing in the rudest voice, so that you could laugh at it.

Hana was looking at the window.

Not at Aleaka. At the window. At the faint new line at the bottom, pulsing slowly, in time with nothing. Something is calling. And her face had gone from frightened to something else. Calm. Smooth. The face she had when she was thinking, really thinking, the face that had looked at the Great Fall on floor 8 and seen its pulse.

“Why you?” said Hana.

“What?”

“Why is it calling you?” Hana looked up at her. “Not Marrit. Not the tide-folk. Not us. There’s forty-five people on this ship. Thirty-six of us, and Marrit’s eight, and Corran. And it’s been singing to ships for hundreds of years, they said. Singing to everybody.” She tapped the window, very lightly, with one finger. “But the window’s yours.”

Aleaka didn’t say anything.

“Because you’re the only one who didn’t put the wax in,” said Milli.

“Because she’s the only one who listens,” said Jared.

Hana looked from one of them to the other. Then back at Aleaka.

“Okay,” said Hana. “Then we find out what’s true.”

Marrit was not having it.

Aleaka had known she wouldn’t. She’d seen Marrit’s face on the first night, at the wheel, when she saw the wax still in Aleaka’s palm. It had gone very still and very hard, the way the Steward’s face had been on the high side: like a thing that had only one shape and wasn’t going to change.

“No,” said Marrit.

They were on the quarterdeck. Hana and Aleaka, and Higu, because it was Higu’s day with the rock, and Deston, because Deston was Deston. Marrit at the wheel. Her tide-cord wound round her fingers so tight the knots had gone white.

“You don’t take it out,” said Marrit. “You don’t listen. You don’t go near it. You don’t.” She wasn’t shouting. She’d never shouted. “My mother’s brother took his out. When I was six. On the Kittiwake. He said he wanted to hear it once. Just once.” She looked at the sea. “We found his shirt on the rail in the morning. Folded.”

Nobody said anything.

“So you put your wax in tonight,” said Marrit, to Aleaka. “Or I’ll put it in for you. And then I’ll tie you to the mast.”

“Marrit,” said Hana. Very gently.

Aleaka felt it before it happened. She’d known Hana a long time. She knew what Hana’s voice did when Hana was about to make somebody want something. The smooth went smoother. The calm went warm. Like honey going into tea. Hana didn’t always do it. But she wasn’t against it, when it was the quickest way, and Aleaka had seen her do it a hundred times, on every floor, to guards and clerks and anybody with a price.

She felt Hana’s voice start to go warm.

And then stop.

Hana had stopped. In the middle of the word. Aleaka looked at her. Hana was standing on the quarterdeck in the wind with her crown on and her mouth slightly open and her one visible eye fixed on Marrit’s hands. On the tide-cord, wound so tight round the brown fingers that the knots had gone white. On the left hand. Smooth across the back.

Are you real? Marrit had said, in the Kittiwake, at the Teeth. Hana hadn’t been there. But Thea had told it, on the porch, and Hana had listened, and gone very quiet.

Hana closed her mouth.

She stood there for a moment. Then she said, in her own voice, her ordinary voice, calm and smooth and not warm at all:

“I’m sorry about your uncle.”

Marrit looked at her.

“I’m not going to ask you to let her take it out,” said Hana. “It’s your ship. It’s your sea. You’ve lived on it your whole life and we’ve been here a week.” She paused. “I’m going to ask you to let her tell you what she heard. That’s all. And then you decide.”

Marrit stared at her for a long time.

Aleaka watched them. The captain at the wheel with her white knuckles and her folded-shirt uncle, and Hana with her crown on and no charm in her voice at all. Just asking. A caller doesn’t make anything come, the old man at the top of the falls had said. A caller asks.

“Fine,” said Marrit eventually. “Fine. Tell me.”

So Aleaka told her.

She told it badly. She always did. Out of order, with her hands, stopping and starting, the words coming round the wrong way, Hana helping now and then with a word when she got stuck, the way Hana always had. She told Marrit about the note coming up through the deck. How low it was. How long. How it rose and fell and stopped and came back in a different shape, like somebody saying the same thing over and over in different ways because nobody had understood the first time. How it didn’t pull. How Sleet’s ruff had frosted, not in anger, but the way it frosted when she was very alert, listening to something far off. How Tuppence had stopped bobbing, and Tuppence never stopped bobbing.

How sad it was.

“It doesn’t want us,” said Aleaka. “That’s the thing. I’d know. If it wanted us. Like the Cataract. The Cataract wanted Newish. You could feel it, it was like—“ She stopped. “This doesn’t want anybody. It wants us to hear.”

Marrit had listened to all of it without moving.

“Hear what?” she said.

“I don’t know,” said Aleaka. “Not yet.”

Marrit looked at the sea for a long time.

“My uncle said that,” she said. Quietly. “The night he took his out. I just want to hear what it’s saying. That’s what he said.” She turned the wheel a fraction. “I was six. I remember.”

Aleaka didn’t say anything. There wasn’t anything to say.

And then, from below the quarterdeck, from the main deck, from the nest of sailcloth by the mainmast where he’d been sitting all morning on his bucket with Maigo beside him, somebody said:

“My grandmother said that too.”

Corran had heard all of it.

Aleaka didn’t know how. He was deaf as a post half the time, Marrit said, and asleep the other half. But he’d been sitting on his bucket at the foot of the mainmast with his blanket round his shoulders and his mug in his hands and his milky eyes half shut, and he’d heard every word, and now he was looking up at the quarterdeck.

At Aleaka.

“The one who sang the song,” said Corran. “About you. Stair-folk.” He drank from his mug. “She said that. About the deep voice. She said it wasn’t the Undertow. Never had been. She said it was something old, that lived down there, and it was asking.” He said it exactly the way Aleaka had said it, slowly, with weight on the word. “Everybody said she was touched. She talked to gulls. She talked to the tide.” He smiled. “She’d have liked you, fox-girl.”

“Grandad,” said Marrit.

“Seventy years I’ve put wax in,” said the old man. “Every night over deep water. Because my father did. Because his did. Because your uncle took his out, and we found his shirt.” He looked at his mug. “And I never once asked if she was right. My grandmother. I never once asked.” He was quiet. “Because I was frightened. That’s all. Because it’s big, and it’s down there, and it’s sad, and I didn’t want to know.”

Nobody said anything on the whole ship.

The sails creaked. The sea went past. A gull went over, shouting.

“I’m eighty-nine,” said Corran. “I built this ship. I watched her swim. I’ve done everything I wanted to do.” He looked up at Marrit at the wheel, and his face was very gentle. “I’d like to know, love. Before I go. I’d like to know if she was right.”

Marrit’s hands were white on the wheel.

“It’s your ship,” said Corran. “You’re captain. I said so on the quay, and I meant it.” He put his mug down on the deck. “But if you’ll take an old man’s advice.”

“Grandad—“

“Turn her,” said Corran. “Tonight. Toward it. And let the fox-girl listen.”

She went to find Ice in the galley, because she always went to find Ice in the kitchen.

It was an old rhythm. Older than she could explain to anybody who hadn’t been there. On Earth it had been calls, every once in a while, and her telling him off for something, and him going meek, and then turning it. Here it had been a kitchen step on floor 8 in Wendmere, and before that, a hundred other places. She’d go and find him where he was cooking. She’d tell him off for something. And then they’d talk.

The Patience’s galley was a narrow box below the forward deck with a black iron stove and a rail round it so that pans didn’t slide off in a swell. Ice was at the stove with a pan of something and a face of extreme concentration, and the ship’s cook, a vast silent tide-folk woman called Ottery, was standing in the doorway with her arms folded, watching him do it, with the expression of somebody watching a burglar.

“She doesn’t like me,” said Ice, without looking round.

“I’m not surprised,” said Aleaka. “You’ve taken her stove.”

“I asked.”

“You asked after,” said Ottery, from the doorway, the only thing Aleaka had ever heard her say, and went away.

Aleaka came in and sat on the flour barrel, the way she’d sat on the step in Wendmere. Sleet lay down across the doorway. Tuppence hopped off her shoulder onto the rail round the stove and looked at the pan with deep interest.

“You heard,” said Ice.

“Corran?”

“The whole ship heard.” He stirred the pan. “He’s never said that. About his grandmother. Marrit’s never heard it.” He was quiet. “I’d like to know, love. Before I go.”

Aleaka looked at the back of his head.

She’d known him a long time. Not as long as some of them. Less than a year, on Earth, before the white room. But she’d known him well, in that year. Better than people who’d known him longer. And she’d been watching him, since the cliff path above Hythe, look up the slip every evening at an old man on a bucket. She didn’t know why. She knew it mattered.

“You wore the wax,” she said. “Didn’t you.”

He stopped stirring.

She hadn’t meant to say it. It had just come out, the wrong way round, the way her words did. But she’d seen it. Milli had told her, at breakfast, and she’d seen it in her head anyway. Ice put his in without being shown. Like he’d done it a hundred times.

He didn’t turn round.

“Two months,” he said. Very quietly. “Every night. I never once took it out.”

She didn’t ask when. She didn’t ask what two months. Nobody asked him that. She just sat on the flour barrel and looked at the back of his head, at the teal streak in the black hair, and waited.

“Marrit said,” said Ice. “Marrit said everybody, and I didn’t want anybody going over the side. So I put it in. Every night. And made sure everybody else did.” He started stirring again. Slowly. “I never heard it. Not once. Not one note. I lay in a hammock for two months over the deepest water there is, and I never heard it.” He was quiet. “There’s a lot on this floor I never listened to, Leaka.”

“You said that about floor 8.”

“I say it about every floor.” He turned round at last, with the spoon in his hand. His face was doing something she’d seen it do on the kitchen step in Wendmere. “It’s been true about every floor.”

Aleaka looked at him. And then, because it was the rhythm, because it was what they did, because his face needed it:

“That’s very stupid,” she said.

He blinked.

“You put wax in your ears for two months,” said Aleaka, “because a sailor told you to, and you never once wondered what you were missing. That’s very stupid. That’s the most stupid thing I’ve ever heard. And I’ve heard you explain how you make gravy.”

Something in his face let go.

“Sorry, Leaka,” he said. Meekly. The way he always said it.

“You should be.”

“I am.”

“Good.”

He looked at her for a moment. Then he put the spoon down, and leaned back against the rail round the stove with his arms folded, and Tuppence hopped up onto his shoulder, beside where Alea would be, and he didn’t seem to notice.

“You’re very cute when you’re cross,” he said.

“Shut up,” said Aleaka, and threw a potato at him, and he caught it, and they both laughed, and Ottery’s voice came down the companionway from somewhere above saying something in tide-folk that was very clearly about potatoes.

When they’d stopped laughing, he said, quietly, “Take it out tonight.”

She looked at him.

“Take it out,” said Ice. “Don’t put it in. Go and listen. You’re the only one who’s ever going to.” He looked at the potato in his hand. “I’d like to know too. What I missed.”

They turned her at dusk.

Marrit did it herself. She stood at the wheel with her face like stone and her tide-cord round her fingers and brought the Patience round off the line of the islands, away from the route every tide-folk ship had sailed for three hundred years, south and west into the open. Into the deep. The water under them went from grey to black. The islands fell away behind. Nothing ahead but the sea and the night coming up out of the east.

Everybody put their wax in.

Everybody but her.

Hana had made her a promise first. On the deck, in the last of the light, with the whole party round her. You put a rope on. Round your middle. Jared holds the other end. And if you go anywhere near the rail without telling us, he pulls. Aleaka had said yes. She’d have said yes to anything. She stood at the stern rail with a rope round her waist, tied with one of Marrit’s knots that Marrit had checked twice, and the other end of it in Jared’s hands behind her, wrapped twice round his fist and once round the mast.

Jared had his wax in. So did Hana. Milli had his in, both ears, this time, because Hana had looked at him.

Newish had hers in and was sitting on the deck by Jared’s feet with her arms round her knees, watching Aleaka’s face. If you need us, Newish had said, if anything’s wrong, anything, you say a name. Out loud. Anybody’s. And I’ll hear your mouth move and I’ll throw it.

Sleet was against her legs. Tuppence was on her shoulder.

Corran was on his bucket by the mainmast, with Maigo. No wax.

She saw that. Everybody saw it. The old man sitting in his blanket with his ears open for the first time in seventy years, and his grandmother’s song in him somewhere, and his hands round his mug. Marrit had seen it, and her face had done something, and then she’d turned back to the wheel and not looked again.

Ice was at the rail amidships, with Thea. Both with their wax in. Ice had his hand on the rail and he was looking aft, at her, and when she looked back at him he lifted his chin, slightly. Go on, Leaka.

The sun went down.

The stars came out.

And the song came up through the deck.

It was so much louder.

That was the first thing. Last night it had been a long way down, a deep note coming up through the planks into the soles of her feet. Tonight it was close. It was in the hull. In the rail under her hands. In the rope round her waist and the ribs of the ship and her own ribs, a long, low, enormous sound rising out of the black water off the stern, so low that it wasn’t a sound so much as a pressure, a vast slow weight pressing on her chest and then lifting off it.

Behind her, she felt the deck change. Everybody felt it, wax or no wax. You couldn’t not. Through her feet she felt forty-four people go still.

It rose. Over a whole minute. Higher, and higher, and then it hung there at the top, wavering, and came down again in a long fall like somebody letting out a breath they’d held for a hundred years.

And it was sad.

Oh, it was so sad.

She stood at the rail with her hands on the wood and the tears coming down her face behind her glasses, and listened to it, and reached for it the way she reached for everything. Kindred. The warm open feeling. I won’t hurt you. The thing she’d been doing since the first morning in Hearthstead, with Sleet, and the gulls on five, and the fish at the end of the last jetty in Wendmere.

She reached down into the dark for it.

And it was too big.

She felt Kindred go out of her and down into the black water like a rope thrown off a cliff, and keep going, and keep going, and not find the bottom. She felt the edges of something. The shape of something. Huge. Old. Slow. Not hungry. Not angry. Just—there, down in the dark, singing up at the ship, the way it had sung up at every ship for longer than there’d been ships, and every one of them going past with wax in its ears.

It wasn’t enough. Kindred wasn’t enough. That was the thing she understood, standing at the rail with the song pressing on her chest. Kindred isn’t calling, she’d said, at the top of the Great Fall. That’s just being nice. Being safe. And this wasn’t calling either. This wasn’t her asking for help.

This was something asking her.

Answer one that calls you.

Not reach. Not ask. Answer.

“Leaka,” she said. Out loud. To herself. The way Hana said it, when she’d gone too far into her own head. Leaka. Come back.

She opened her mouth, and answered.

She didn’t sing. She couldn’t sing. She’d never been able to sing, not like Thea, not like Chi. She just made a sound. Back down into the dark. As low as she could. A long low sound from the bottom of her chest, wavering, nothing like the enormous note coming up out of the sea, nothing like anything.

I’m here.

That was all it meant.

I’m here. I heard you. I’m listening.

Tuppence sang. On her shoulder. All at once, very loud, the falls-wren song, high and bright and bubbling, like water over stones. And Sleet, against her legs, threw back her head and howled. A long thin wavering wolf-howl into the starlight, with her ruff gone white as frost.

And the sea beside the ship came up.

She’d thought it was an island.

That was her first thought, honestly. That they’d hit something. A shoal, a reef, a rock. Because the black water off the starboard quarter, twenty metres from the hull, simply rose. In a long smooth swelling hump, rising and rising, with the sea pouring off it white in the starlight in sheets, and kept rising, until it was higher than the rail. Higher than the quarterdeck. Long. Longer than the ship.

Pale. Grey-white, like old bone. Ridged. Crusted all over with barnacles and shells and long trailing weed, so thick that it looked like a reef, like the Shoals, like a whole stretch of seabed lifted up into the air. Scarred. Old scars, long pale grooves, and old wounds, round and puckered, the size of cartwheels, all along its side.

And along the top of it, from one end to the other, a fin.

Not like a fish’s fin. Like a sail. A long ragged pale sail of skin, ribbed, torn in places, rising out of its back three times the height of a man and running its whole length, with the water pouring off it and the starlight shining through it, faintly, the way light shines through a hand held up to a lamp.

Somebody on the deck behind her screamed. One of the tide-folk. She heard it even with her ears full of the song.

Then the near end of it turned.

Toward the ship. Slowly. The head. If it was a head. Long and blunt and pale, with the barnacles thick on it, and ridges, and long pale trailing things hanging from under it like a beard of weed, swaying in the water. And in the side of it, near the ship, so close she could have touched it with the end of her bow, an eye.

The size of a door.

Dark. Very dark. Old as the Calling Stone. Old as the Great Fall. Old as anything. One half of it clouded white, scarred across, the way an old dog’s eye goes. The other half clear. Wet. Black and deep, with the stars in it.

Looking at her.

Not at the ship. At her. At the fox-girl at the stern rail with a rope round her middle and tears on her glasses and a wren singing on her shoulder and a wolf howling at her feet.

The song stopped.

The whole sea went quiet. She heard the water pouring off its sides. She heard the sails flap, because the wind had dropped, or because everybody on deck had stopped breathing at once. She heard Jared, behind her, take one hard step forward and plant his feet, and the rope round her waist go tight.

The eye looked at her.

And she knew what it wanted.

She didn’t decide. She’d tell Hana that afterwards, and Hana would say you never decide anything, you just do it and then decide, and that would be true. She put one leg over the stern rail.

“Leaka—“ Jared’s voice. Muffled. He couldn’t hear himself through the wax. The rope jerked.

She turned round on the rail and looked at him. Straight at him, so he could read her mouth. She said it slowly, picking her way over the words.

“It’s all right. Let it out. Slowly. Please.”

Jared looked at her for a long moment, his heavy-lidded eyes on hers in the starlight, with the rope wrapped round his fist.

Then he let it out. Slowly.

She went down the side of the ship on the rope, with her boots against the new paint and Jared paying out above her a hand’s breadth at a time, and Tuppence clinging to her collar, still singing, and the black water coming up. And then not the water. Its side. The barnacled, weed-hung, scarred pale side of it, rising up out of the sea right against the Patience’s hull, close enough to step onto.

She stepped onto it.

It was warm.

That was the thing. She’d thought it would be cold. It came up out of the deepest water there was, out of the dark at the bottom of the sea. But under her boots, under the barnacles, it was warm, the way a horse is warm, the way Sleet was warm when she slept on your feet. And it moved. Very slowly. Breathing. A long slow rise and fall under her, like the swell.

She knelt down on it.

She put her hands on it. Flat. And then, without thinking, cupped. The way the hollow had been on top of the Calling Stone at the top of the Great Fall. Two cupped hands. Worn into the stone by six hundred years of callers asking.

She wasn’t asking.

“I’m here,” she said. Out loud. Her voice was shaking. “I came. I heard you. I’m—“ The words went the wrong way round. She let them. “I’m sorry nobody listened. I’m sorry. I’m sorry it was so long. I’m listening now. Tell me.”

And it told her.

It wasn’t words.

She’d known it wouldn’t be. It came up through her cupped hands and into her arms and her chest the way the song had come up through the deck, but closer, inside, so close it was like remembering something rather than being told it. Not words. Pictures. Feelings. The way Sleet’s head on your knee was a feeling. Except it was so big she couldn’t hold all of it, and kept dropping pieces, and catching them again.

Down.

That was the first thing. The deep. Going down. Darker and colder and further than she’d known anything could go. The weight of all the water above. Things down there that had no eyes. Silence.

Turning.

Somewhere in the deep, a long way off, to the west, something turning. Slowly. The whole sea, turning. A great slow circle of water, miles across, going round and round, and in the middle of it, going down. A hole in the sea. A throat. Everything that went into the circle went round, and round, and in, and down, and didn’t come up. Weed. Fish. Wood. Ships. Bones. Round and round and down.

Not that way.

That was what the song had been. All this time. Over and over, for hundreds of years, to every ship that went past with wax in its ears. Not that way. Not that way. Go round. Warning. Calling them off. Calling them away. And the ships had heard a big sad voice in the dark and been frightened and called it the Undertow and stopped their ears against it.

And sailed straight on.

And then the last piece. The one she nearly dropped. The one that came up through her hands so heavy that she cried out, kneeling on its back in the dark, and felt Jared’s rope jerk tight above her.

Mine.

Mine is down there.

Small. Pale. A shape like this shape, but small. Turning in the circle. Round and round. Going in. Going down. A long, long time ago. So long ago that the scars on its side had been new.

And it had sung ever since.

She didn’t know how long she knelt there.

When she came back to herself, she was crying so hard she couldn’t see through her glasses, and her hands were still cupped on its warm barnacled side, and the song had started again. Quietly. Not the enormous note. Something lower, gentler, almost nothing, coming up through her palms. Like somebody humming to themselves in another room.

And there was a window in front of her face.

She hadn’t asked for it. She could barely read it. She took her glasses off and wiped them on her sleeve with shaking hands and put them back on, and it was still there, glowing pale and steady in the starlight over the black water.

She read it twice. Then a third window opened under it.

[ The Call (3 of 3)
Call one that cannot come. ]

She looked at that one for a long time.

One that cannot come. She didn’t know what it meant. She didn’t know what couldn’t come, or why, or where from. She looked at it, kneeling on the back of something older than the Calling Stone, with its small pale one turning in the dark somewhere to the west, and thought: I’ll find out.

Then she closed it. Carefully. And put her cupped hands back on the warm grey side.

“Thank you,” she said. “For telling me. I’m sorry. I’m so sorry.” She swallowed. “I’ll tell them. All of them. I’ll tell them not that way. I promise.”

Under her hands, the humming changed. Very slightly. She felt it. With Heed, now, she felt what it meant, the way you feel somebody squeeze your hand.

You came.

That was all it meant. Just that.

You came.

Jared pulled her up.

She didn’t remember most of it. She remembered his face over the rail, very close, with the wax still in his ears and his jaw tight, and his good hand taking hold of the back of her capelet and hauling her over the stern rail onto the deck in a heap, the way Azac had hauled Calvin off the quay. She remembered Hana’s arms round her, so tight she couldn’t breathe, and Hana’s tail wrapped round both of them, and Hana saying something into her hair over and over that she couldn’t hear and didn’t need to.

She remembered Sleet licking her face.

She remembered sitting on the deck against the stern rail with everybody round her, and looking up, and seeing the whole length of the ship. Forty-four faces in the starlight. Everybody. Every one of the thirty-six, and the seven tide-folk crew, and Marrit at the wheel. All looking at her.

And one by one, starting at the back, the tide-folk taking their wax out.

The night helmsman first. A young man with a scar on his chin. He’d been at the wheel beside Marrit the whole time. He looked at the vast pale shape still lying in the water off the starboard quarter, with its sail-fin dripping in the starlight and its one clear eye still on the ship. And then he reached up, slowly, with a shaking hand, and took the wax out of his left ear.

And listened.

She saw his face when the humming reached him. She’d see it for a long time. A tide-folk sailor who’d put wax in every night over deep water since he was a child, because his father did, because his father’s father did, hearing it for the first time. Not the enormous note. Just the low gentle hum. Coming up through the deck. You came.

He started to cry.

Then the next one. And the next. Pell the bosun, who was the size of Gabe and Azac together. Kip, the ship’s boy, who was twelve. Ottery, in the galley door, with her arms still folded. One by one, all along the deck, the tide-folk took their wax out and stood very still and listened, and their faces did what the helmsman’s face had done.

And then the climbers did too.

She watched it go down the deck. Hana, beside her, taking hers out with her eyes shut. Milli. Newish, swearing softly in Bosnian, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand. Jared, who had to let go of the rope to do it. Deston. Rachael. Gabe, enormous, standing at the rail amidships with his hand over his mouth.

And Ice.

Ice took his out slowly. Both ears. He did it the way he’d put them in, without looking, rolling the four small balls of wax in his palm. And then he stood at the rail beside Thea and listened to the thing he’d sailed over for two months and never heard, and she watched his face, from the stern, in the starlight, and it was the face she’d seen on the kitchen step in Wendmere when he said there’s a lot on this floor I never looked at.

Except worse. Much worse. And better.

Thea had taken her wax out too. She had her hand on his arm. She wasn’t looking at the creature. She was looking at him.

Aleaka looked away. It wasn’t hers.

She looked at the mainmast instead.

At the old man on his bucket.

Corran hadn’t moved. He was sitting exactly where he’d been all night, with his blanket round him and his mug in his hands and Maigo beside him, and no wax, the only one on the whole ship who’d never had any in. He was looking at the pale thing in the water. At its sail. At its eye. At the old scars on its side.

His face was wet.

“She was right,” said Corran.

Very quietly. Aleaka heard it anyway. She had fox ears. And Heed. And it was a thing that was calling out, a little, the way the song had: an old man on a bucket saying something to nobody, to the sea, to a woman who’d been dead for seventy years.

“She was right,” said Corran. “Seventy years. Gran. You were right.” He laughed. Thin. Cracked. It turned into a cough, and Maigo held him, and he finished laughing. He made sure he finished. “Touched, they said. Touched.” He shook his head slowly, looking at the eye in the water. “Well. I’m touched too, then. I’m touched. And I don’t care.”

Marrit had left the wheel.

Aleaka hadn’t seen her do it. The helmsman had it now, the young man with the scar, crying silently, steering. Marrit had come down off the quarterdeck and walked along the deck to the mainmast and was standing in front of her grandfather. She’d taken her wax out. She was holding it in her palm.

She was looking at the creature in the water. With the hum coming up through the deck. With her uncle’s folded shirt somewhere in her face.

“He just wanted to hear it,” she said. “He said. Just once.”

“I know, love,” said Corran.

“He went over the side.”

“I know.”

“It wasn’t it,” said Marrit. Her voice broke. “Was it. It didn’t take him. It never took anybody. He went over the side in the dark because it was there, and it was big, and it was singing, and he just wanted to—“ She stopped.

“He wanted to hear what it was saying,” said Corran gently. “So did I. So did Gran.” He looked at Aleaka, at the stern, past Marrit, with his milky eyes. “Fox-girl. What was it saying?”

Everybody looked at her.

Aleaka stood up. Slowly. With Hana’s hand under her elbow, and Tuppence back on her shoulder, and Sleet against her legs with her ruff going soft again, grey, the frost going out of it. She looked at all of them. Forty-four faces in the starlight.

She found the words. They came out the right way round, for once.

“It was saying not that way,” said Aleaka. “That’s all. It’s been saying it to every ship for hundreds of years. Not that way. Go round.” She looked out at the vast pale shape in the water, its fin dripping, its eye on her. “There’s something to the west. Something that turns. A whole circle of the sea, going round and down. And everything that goes in doesn’t come out.” She swallowed. “That’s the Undertow. That’s where it is. It’s not this. It never was this.”

The hum came up through the deck.

“And it lost one,” said Aleaka. “A long time ago. A small one. Like it. It went into the circle.” Her voice wobbled. She let it. “And it’s been singing ever since. So nobody else would.”

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then Corran, on his bucket, very slowly, put down his mug. And stood up. By himself. With both hands. The way he’d stood up on the quay when the Patience swam. He stood at the foot of the mainmast of his own ship in his blanket, and looked out over the rail at the thing his grandmother had sung about, and he lifted one old brown hand, and held it up.

The way you’d wave to somebody on a quay.

The great eye in the water looked at him.

And then, very slowly, the whole long pale shape of it began to sink. Down. Back into the black water. The barnacled back. The scars. The ragged sail-fin, last, sliding under with the starlight shining through it, until there was nothing but a long smooth swell rolling away across the sea, and the hum, coming up through the deck, fainter, and fainter, going down.

Not gone. She could feel it. With Heed, she’d always be able to feel it now, a little. Down there. In the dark. Going south.

Go round.

“South,” said Aleaka. “It’s going south. It wants us to follow it.”

Marrit looked at her grandfather. He was still standing with his hand up.

Then she turned round, and walked back along the deck, and climbed up to the quarterdeck, and took the wheel from the crying helmsman, and put her tide-cord round her fingers.

“South,” said Marrit. Her voice was perfectly steady. “Everybody. Wax out. We’re following it.”
