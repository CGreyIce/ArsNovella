Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 5: Everyone Comes Back Down

Chapter 163

Two Rings

7,957 words · 35 min read

The forge walked with the harvest.

Jared hadn’t known that. He’d thought, the first week, that Bettris’s smithy at the corner of Haverley’s green was where Bettris lived and worked and that was that: a stone shed with a chimney, an anvil, a hearth, a bucket. And it was. But on the afternoon they went up the valley, when Haverley was in and everybody was walking out of the village on the lane with their blades on their shoulders, Bettris walked out too. And behind her, pulled by a mule with one white ear, came the forge.

A cart. Two big wheels, iron-rimmed. On the bed of it, an iron hearth the size of a tin bath, with a hood over it and a short chimney sticking up out of the hood at an angle. A pair of leather bellows as long as a man, folded, strapped to one side. An anvil slung underneath, between the wheels, on chains, swinging. Racks of tongs and hammers and files down the other side, rattling. And a box of coal, and a barrel of water, and a little bench folded flat, and a lantern on a hook.

“Man,” said Jared.

Bettris didn’t look round. She was a short square woman of about forty with forearms like hams and a burn up the inside of one of them that had healed white and shiny, and she didn’t talk much. She’d let him and Gabe and Salty into her smithy the first week because they hadn’t asked to be let. They’d just picked up files. She respected that. You could tell. She’d never once said so.

“Blades break,” she said now. Walking. To the lane in front of her. “Every day. In every field. Somebody hits a stone. Somebody’s handle cracks. The edge goes.” She walked on. “You can’t send a blade back down to Haverley to be mended when the field’s three days up the valley.” She jerked her head at the cart. “So the forge goes up with them.”

“Every year?”

“Every year since there were blades.”

He walked beside her for a while, with his thorn-shield on his back, watching the forge go up the lane behind the mule, swaying, the chimney nodding.

He thought about Corran’s forge, on floor 11. Cold, for eleven years, at the top of the slip. He thought about Ressa’s under the second arch on floor 8, which never moved and never would, with the fall going past the door like a wall. He thought about the Breath. The crack in the floor of the world. Gabe’s anvil nine and Salty’s fourteen.

A forge on wheels. Going wherever it was needed.

He liked that more than he could have said.

The Acre went on.

He’d known it was long. Everybody had. You couldn’t stand at the edge of the first field and look up it and not know it was long. But you didn’t know until you walked it, with a harvest, village by village, and the walking took days. They went up it like a tide coming in. That was how it felt. Haverley came up the lane to Ashwell, which was three miles on, a village of orchards. And Ashwell, which had been bringing its own harvest in on its own for three weeks and was two days behind its stick, looked down the lane in the evening and saw a hundred and forty people coming up it with blades on their shoulders, and carts, and a forge, and thirty-six passers, and an old man in a chair roped to a wagon. And Ashwell’s Reeve, a tall bald man called Osric with a stick even longer than Annet’s, stood at the top of his lane and lifted it over his head with both hands, and Ashwell cheered.

They brought Ashwell in in four days.

Then Ashwell came too. Two hundred and some. Up the valley to Middle Acre, which was bigger. And Middle Acre, when it was in, came too. Every village they brought in, the crowd going up the valley got bigger, and the next village got brought in faster. That was the whole idea. Cobb had told Calvin: the harvest starts at the bottom of the valley and every village helps the one above it, all the way up, so the last field at the top gets the whole Acre.

Jared had never seen so many people do one thing.

The Bastion had been close. On the Long Night, with everybody on the walls. But the Bastion had been a wall, and a wall was about holding. This was about bringing in. Five hundred people in one field by the time they got to Middle Acre’s last piece, a great wide slope of barley under the western hills, and the whole slope moving at once: the mowers in three lines, staggered, swinging on Newish’s one; the binders behind them in a long bent row; the stooks going up behind the binders like a tent city going up in a morning; the carts waiting at the bottom with their horses steaming. You could stand at the top of the field and watch a whole slope of gold become a whole slope of stubble between one meal and the next.

And behind them, always, down the valley, the Grey.

It was hungrier now. Two fields a night, since the rain. Sometimes three. It came up the valley behind them field by field, over the harvested ground, over stubble with nothing left in it to take, and it took the stubble. It took the hedges. Then it started taking the orchards.

That was Ashwell. The night after Ashwell was in. In the morning, Jared walked out to the edge of the village with Gabe to wash at the trough, and looked down the slope, and the bottom row of Ashwell’s orchard was grey.

Apple trees. Old ones, broad, low, with the apples on them still: Ashwell picked its apples after the corn, not before, because the corn couldn’t wait and the apples could. And the bottom row was grey. Every tree. Every leaf, every twig, every apple still hanging on every branch, grey as stone and perfectly still in the morning wind. A whole row of grey trees, like the drawing of an orchard somebody had done in pencil and then forgot to colour.

Osric stood at the edge of it with his long stick and didn’t say anything at all.

An old woman from Ashwell went down the row and put her hand up and took hold of an apple, the way you’d take an apple off a tree, to see if it was ready. And it came apart in her hand. Grey dust, running down her wrist. She stood there with her hand up in the grey branches and her face doing nothing.

“Pick them,” said Annet.

She’d come up behind them. She was looking at the orchard. Not at the grey row. At the next one up, and the one above that, and all the rows going up the slope above them, green, heavy, red, with the dew on them.

“All of them,” said Annet. “Today. Before the corn. The corn’ll keep a day. The apples won’t.” She turned to the crowd, five hundred people standing in the lane behind her, waiting to go up to Middle Acre’s barley. “Ashwell’s apples. Under a roof. Before tonight. Everybody.”

So they picked apples. All day. Five hundred people in an orchard, on ladders and on shoulders and on the backs of carts, and the passers among them, Hako on Salty’s shoulders, Calvin on a ladder nobody was holding, shouting, Hyvred in the top of the biggest tree because she could see where all the apples were. Baskets and sacks and aprons. The carts went down to Ashwell’s barns full to the boards and came back empty and went down full again. Jared picked till his arms ached and his fingers were sticky and he smelled of apples all the way through, and at the bell every tree on that slope was bare, and every apple was under a roof.

In the morning the Grey had taken the whole orchard. Every tree.

Bare grey trees, on a bare grey slope, perfectly still. Nothing on them. Nothing taken. Not one apple left for it.

Osric looked at it for a long time. Then he lifted his stick, the long one, over his head, with both hands, at the grey trees.

Not a cheer. Something else.

The whole of Ashwell, behind him, lifted their hands.

He decided about the rings at Middle Acre.

He didn’t decide, really. He found out he’d decided. He was sitting at the forge cart on the third night, in the yard of Middle Acre’s big barn, with Bettris’s lantern on its hook and the hearth going and a scythe blade across the anvil with a crack in the heel. Gabe was on the bellows. Salty was holding. The nail guild. It had been the three of them at every forge on every floor since Ulla Dray’s on floor 1, when Salty made a hundred nails in a night and Jared made a rose.

And across the yard, by the barn door, a crowd of old women from Haverley were sitting on a bench in the light of another lantern with their laps full of wheat.

Ears of wheat. Hundreds of them. The best ears from every field they’d cut, picked out by hand, the fattest, the longest, the ones with the beard on them still. They were plaiting them. Their old brown fingers going and going in the lamplight, quick, sure, the way they bound sheaves. Plaiting the stalks together under the ears, three by three, into a long braid that got longer and longer in their laps and went down onto the ground and lay there in a gold coil. And every so often one of them would hold it up, and look at it, and the others would look at it, and somebody would say something, and they’d all laugh, and go on.

Hana was sitting in the middle of them.

She’d been sitting there every night since Haverley. Annet had put her there. I’ll need the women, Annet had said, and the women had come, and Hana had been sitting among them in the evenings ever since, with her ears up and her tail round her feet and her own crown, the little purple-and-gold one, in her lap. Not plaiting. They wouldn’t let her plait. She just sat there and let them measure her head with lengths of straw, and argue about it, and measure it again.

She looked happy. Jared hadn’t seen her look like that since a white room.

He looked at her for a long time across the yard with the hammer in his hand. Then he looked down at the cracked scythe blade on the anvil. Then at Salty.

“Salty,” he said.

“Mm.”

“You still got that nail?”

Salty stopped holding. Gabe stopped pumping. The fire in the hearth went from white to orange and settled.

“What nail?” said Salty.

“You know what nail.”

Salty looked at him for a long moment, across the anvil, in the firelight. Then he let go of the tongs, very carefully, so the blade stayed where it was, and reached round behind him for his pack, which was where it always was, on the ground by his feet. He opened the pocket on the bottom. The little one, inside, with the button. The one nobody ever saw him open.

He took out a nail.

It was nothing. A nail. Square, black, hand-forged, about as long as Jared’s finger, with a flat round head that had been hit slightly crooked. The kind of nail you’d use to hang a gate. A bit rusty at the point.

“Number a hundred,” said Salty.

Jared didn’t say anything.

“Floor 1,” said Salty. He was looking at the nail in his palm. “Ulla’s. The night I did the hundred. Yan’s hands had gone white. I made him the hand-warmer, and then I couldn’t stop, so I did nails. Ninety-nine of them went in the barrel for Ulla to sell.” He turned it over. “That one was the last one. I hit the head crooked. It wasn’t good enough to sell. And I was going to throw it back in the scrap, and I didn’t.” He shrugged. “I don’t know why. I’ve had it in that pocket ever since. Fourteen floors. It’s the first thing I ever made up here.” He looked up. “Well. The hundredth.”

“Man,” said Jared.

“Why?”

Jared looked across the yard. At the women. At the gold coil in their laps. At Hana in the middle of them, laughing at something, behind her hand.

Salty followed his eyes.

“Oh,” said Salty.

He didn’t say anything else for a while. He looked at the nail in his palm, and then at Hana across the yard, and then at Jared, and then back at the nail. His face did something. Salty’s face always did things. It was the most honest face on any floor.

Then he held it out.

“It’s not good enough,” he said. “It’s crooked. I told you. It’s the worst one.”

“It’s the first one,” said Jared. “That’s the one.”

He took it.

It was warm from Salty’s hand. He held it up to the lantern and looked at it. At the crooked head. At the little line of grain down the shank where Salty had drawn it out in Ulla Dray’s forge, four hundred and something days ago, which felt like years.

“Two,” said Gabe.

Jared looked at him. Gabe was leaning on the bellows with his arms folded on the top, watching.

“You’ll want two,” said Gabe. “Out of one nail. That’s the hard bit.” He nodded at it. “It’s not very much iron.”

“I know.”

“So you’ll want to draw it out first. Long. Thin. Then cut it in half. Then each half’s a ring.” He thought about it. “You’ll lose a bit, cutting. And in the scale. You’ll want to fold the ends, so they don’t open.” He looked at the nail. “You’ll have about enough. Just.”

“I know, Gabe.”

“I know you know. I’m saying it out loud.” He almost smiled. “Declan’s rule. Higu’s. Whatever.” He put his hands back on the bellows handle. “When?”

“Tonight.”

“Okay,” said Gabe, and pumped.

He’d never made a ring.

He’d made a rose. Two roses, on floor 1. A thorn, on floor 8, one-handed, in the gaps between Ressa’s hammer. Rudder irons on floor 11 that had held against the Undertow when the rudder they were holding did nothing at all. Rims. Hinges. Nails. A thousand mends on a thousand blades. He’d never once made anything that small, that had to be that round.

Bettris watched him for about a minute. Then she came over and stood at his elbow.

She didn’t say anything. She took a rod off the rack on the side of the cart. Thin, iron, about the thickness of a finger, tapered: thick at one end, thin at the other. She laid it across the anvil beside him and tapped it with one fingernail.

A mandrel. For rings. For the iron rings on the ends of scythe handles that held the blade on, which she made every week of her life.

“Thanks,” said Jared.

Bettris went back to her bench.

He drew the nail out. Slowly. Ressa’s way. You could tell, when you’d sat under the second arch, the difference between iron that was hot and iron that was ready. He waited for ready. He let it sit in the fire until it was the colour Ressa called the inside of a cherry, and took it out, and drew it, a few blows, and put it back. Salty holding it in the little tongs. Gabe on the bellows, slow, steady, the way he’d been on the bellows for Gabe’s own shield at the Breath.

Nobody hurried him.

It took an hour. The nail got longer. Thinner. A black square rod, then a round one, the length of his hand, then longer, as thin as a stalk of wheat and as long as his forearm. He cut it, in the middle, with the hot chisel, one blow, clean. Two. Then he took the first one and turned it round Bettris’s mandrel, a quarter at a time, tap, turn, tap, until the ends met.

That was the part he’d been afraid of. The ends meeting.

They met.

He folded them, the way Gabe had said. A tiny lap, the end over the end, hammered flat, so they wouldn’t open. Then back in the fire. Then the second one.

And then, when they were both round, both closed, both cooling on the edge of the anvil in the lamplight, two thin black rings no wider than a stalk of straw, he picked up the smallest punch in Bettris’s rack and turned the first one over and put the punch to the inside of it.

“What’s that?” said Salty.

“Mine,” said Jared.

A rose. Five petals, and a dot. The smallest he’d ever done. So small you’d only find it if you knew it was there, if you ran your thumb round the inside of the band where it sat against the finger. The same as the one on the inside of the lamp shield’s rim, where Ice’s hand went. The same as the one on Hana’s crown, the iron one, ugly, from floor 1. So he knows which bit’s mine.

He did the second one.

When he’d done, he sat back on the little folding bench and looked at them, side by side, on the anvil. Two rings. Black iron. Slightly uneven, both of them, if you looked close, the way handmade things are. One a bit bigger than the other.

Salty was looking at them too. Salty’s face was doing the thing.

“That’s my nail,” said Salty. Thickly.

“That’s your nail.”

“Number a hundred.” Salty wiped his face with the back of his wrist, which left a black streak. “The crooked one.”

“It’s not crooked now.”

“It’s still in there,” said Salty. “The crooked. You can’t get it out. It’s in the grain. I’d know.” He sniffed. “Good.”

Gabe didn’t say anything. He took his hands off the bellows and reached over and put one of them on the back of Jared’s neck, briefly, heavy and warm, the way he did with Ice, and took it away.

“How big?” said Jared.

It was the next morning. On the lane between Middle Acre and Lammas, walking, in the long line of the harvest going up the valley. Milli was beside him. He’d drifted back through the crowd to walk beside Jared the way he did when he wanted to say something and was working up to it, and then hadn’t said anything for a mile.

Milli looked at him.

“How big what?”

“Your finger,” said Jared. “And hers. I’ve got to size them.”

Milli stopped walking.

People went round him. A cart went round him. He stood in the middle of the lane with his ears straight up and his tail gone very still and looked at Jared.

“You’ve—“ said Milli. “Already?”

“Last night. Salty’s nail.” Jared stopped too. “They’re round. They’re closed. I’ve just got to size them. They’re on the small side. I’ll open them out on the mandrel if I need to. I can’t make them smaller.”

Milli didn’t say anything for a long time.

Then he said, very precisely, the way he said things when he’d measured them: “Seventeen point three millimetres. Mine’s nineteen point one.”

Jared looked at him.

“Inside diameter,” said Milli. “I measured. In the spring. At home. For the—“ He stopped. “For the real ones. The ones we’d ordered. I measured both of us four times with a piece of thread and a ruler because I didn’t trust the shop.” His mouth did something. “I’ve had them in my head ever since. I didn’t know I had. You said how big and they were just there.”

“Man,” said Jared.

“Is that close?”

Jared took the rings out of his pocket. He’d wrapped them in a bit of clean rag. He unwrapped them, there, in the lane, with the harvest going past on both sides, and held them out on his palm, and Milli looked at them.

He didn’t touch them.

“Those are hers,” said Jared. “The little one. And yours. I’ll check them against Bettris’s gauge tonight. They’ll be close. I’ll get them right.”

Milli was still looking at them.

“They’re iron,” he said.

“Yeah.”

“From a nail.”

“Salty’s. From floor 1. The first thing he ever made here.” Jared turned the little one over, so the inside showed. “And there’s a—“ He stopped. “There’s a thing in them. You’ll find it.”

Milli looked at the rings on Jared’s palm for a long time. In the lane, in the morning, with the dust going up from the carts and the mowers singing something up ahead, badly, on the one.

“Better than the ones we ordered,” he said. Very quietly. “Those were from a shop.”

He walked on. Quickly. So Jared couldn’t see his face.

Hana asked him at Lammas.

Lammas was the half. Annet’s ring for it was halfway down the stick, and when they came up the lane into it in the evening, the whole crowd going up the valley, Annet stopped at the edge of Lammas’s first field and looked at the stick and then up at the sky and then at the stick again, and said, to nobody, “On the stick,” and kept walking. It was the first time she’d said it since the rain.

Lammas was a big village round a crossroads, with a stone cross in the middle of it that had lost its arms a long time ago, and a barn nearly as big as Haverley’s tithe barn, and that night everybody slept in it, all of them, six hundred people in the straw. You couldn’t move. You couldn’t hear yourself think. Somebody was singing at the far end. Somebody was always singing at the far end now.

Jared found a place by the door, where there was air, and sat down with his back to the doorpost and his shield across his knees, and Hana came and sat down next to him.

She didn’t say anything for a while. Neither did he. That was how they’d always been, the two of them, as long as he’d known her. Years, on Earth. Half a world apart. Different countries, different times of day. Her at one end of a call at some hour of the morning and him at the other end at some hour of the night, and neither of them saying anything for ten minutes at a time sometimes, just there, the way you’re there with somebody in a room. He’d never once been in a room with her, on Earth.

He’d been in a room with her every night for four hundred and thirty-something days, now.

“Jared,” said Hana.

“Mm.”

“The binders.” She was looking at her hands. “Annet says. At a wedding. In the Acre.” She turned her own crown over in her lap. “You don’t have somebody give you away. They don’t do that here. They think it’s strange. Annet asked me why anybody would give a person, like a basket.” She almost smiled. “What you have is two binders. One each. And you and him take one band, both of you, and twist it together, both hands, round a sheaf from the last field. And the binders hold the sheaf for you while you do it. One each side. So it doesn’t fall.” She stopped. “That’s the whole thing. Then the Reeve says the words.”

“Okay.”

“So I need somebody to hold my side.”

Jared didn’t say anything.

“I’m asking you,” said Hana. She still wasn’t looking at him. “If you want. You don’t have to. It’s just holding a sheaf. You’ve held a thousand this month.”

He looked out of the door. At the crossroads of Lammas in the dark, and the armless stone cross, and the lamps.

He thought about half a world. He thought about the hours. He thought about every time on Earth he’d heard something in her voice, at the other end of a call, at some terrible hour, and there’d been nothing he could do about it but stay on the call. He thought about a white room. About waking up on a white floor in a circle next to her, next to her, and his first thought, before where, before what, before anything at all: oh. I can reach her.

He’d been the shield ever since. On every floor. He’d stood in front of her on floor 1 against a sheep, which had been stupid, and on floor 8 against the Cataract, which hadn’t. He’d made her a rose. He’d made a thorn. He’d made a rudder the Undertow couldn’t take. He’d stood between her and anything for four hundred days, because he’d spent all the years before that unable to stand anywhere near her at all.

Holding a sheaf. So it didn’t fall.

“Yeah,” said Jared.

Hana looked at him then.

“Yeah,” he said again. “Alice. Course I will.”

She didn’t say anything. She leaned sideways until her head was on his shoulder, crown and ears and all. And then, because she was Hana, and couldn’t let a moment sit for very long without doing something about it, she said, into his shirt:

“Milli’s going to ask you as well.”

“What?”

“He’s going to ask you to be his. I know he is. I can see him working up to it. He’s been working up to it for two days.” She lifted her head. “He doesn’t know I’ve asked you. He’s going to come over here, in about an hour, when he thinks I’m asleep, and stand in front of you with his ears up, and ask you very formally, and you’ll have to say no.”

“Man,” said Jared.

“I know. I’m sorry. I’m telling you so you’ve got time to think of something nice.”

He came in about forty minutes.

Hana was asleep, or pretending, against the doorpost, with her crown on her chest. Jared was still sitting up. And Milli came along the wall of the barn in the dark, stepping over people, carefully, with his ears up and his tail very straight behind him, and stopped in front of Jared, and stood there.

“Jared,” said Milli.

“Milli.”

“I wanted to ask you something.” Very formal. Very rational. He’d clearly practised it. “At the harvest-home. The binders. One each side. Annet says. I know it’s only holding a sheaf, but it’s the—“ He stopped. “It’s who you’d want standing next to you. That’s what it means. That’s what she said. Who you’d want standing next to you when you do it.” He swallowed. “So I wanted to ask you. If you would. For me.”

Jared looked up at him.

He’d had forty minutes. He hadn’t thought of anything nice. He’d thought about it the whole time and every single thing he’d thought of had been worse than the last.

“Milli,” he said. “Man. I’m sorry. She already asked me.”

Milli’s ears went down.

Not all the way. A bit. He stood there. Jared watched him take it, the way Milli took everything, by working it out: you could see him working out that of course she had, she’d known Jared longer, much longer, and Jared was hers, really, everybody knew Jared was hers, and of course she’d asked, she’d asked first, that was only—

“Ask Leaka,” said Jared.

Milli stopped working it out.

“Aleaka,” said Jared. “Ask her.” He nodded down the barn, into the dark, where Aleaka was asleep in the straw with Sleet wrapped round her like a frost-grey blanket and Tuppence a small dark lump on her shoulder. “She’s been yours as long as she’s been Alice’s. Longer, maybe. You two used to gang up on Alice on calls. I used to hear you.” He looked at Milli. “She’d hold your side. She’d hold it like it was the most important thing she ever held.”

Milli looked down the barn at Aleaka for a long time.

“She doesn’t—“ he said. And stopped. “She’s never wanted that. Any of that. For herself. She’s always said. She doesn’t feel it like that.”

“I know,” said Jared. “That’s not what you’re asking her for.”

Milli was quiet.

Then he turned round, without saying anything else, and went down the barn in the dark, between the sleeping people, and knelt down in the straw beside Aleaka. And put his hand on her shoulder. Not the one with Tuppence on it. And said something, very quietly, that Jared couldn’t hear.

He saw Aleaka wake up.

He saw her sit up, slowly, in the straw, with Sleet lifting her head beside her and her ruff going stiff and white, the way it did when she was surprised. He saw her look at Milli. He saw Milli say it again, his ears very straight, his hands in his lap.

And then he saw Aleaka put both her hands over her face.

She didn’t say anything for a long time. Then she took her hands away, and said something, and it carried. In the quiet, at that end of the barn. Slow, the way Aleaka talked, stumbling over the words a bit, the way she did when she meant them.

“I don’t want it,” said Aleaka. “Any of it. I never have. Not for me. I’ve never once wanted that.” She stopped. Started again. “I want it for you two so much it’s stupid. So much it hurts. I’ve wanted it for you since before you had the date.” Her voice went. “Yes. Yes, you idiot. Course I’ll hold your stupid sheaf.”

And she grabbed him by the front of his shirt and pulled him down and put her forehead against his, hard, and Sleet, not understanding any of it, got up and put her whole frost-grey head between them and licked both their faces at once.

Somebody nearby told them to shut up. It was Calvin, who’d been woken. Then he saw what it was, and sat up, and didn’t say anything else.

Beside Jared, against the doorpost, with her eyes still shut, Hana smiled.

The frost came at Brightwater.

The first real one. Not dew. Not nearly. Frost. Jared woke up in Brightwater’s barn on the thirty-fourth morning on the floor and went out to the trough to wash and the trough had ice on it. A skin of it, thin as a window, that broke under his fingers with a sound like a thin glass breaking. And the grass was white. And the stubble was white. And the hedges and the walls and the backs of the sheep in the field by the river, all white, all sparkling in the first low sun, every blade of grass with its own fur of ice. His breath went up in front of him in a cloud.

The whole valley was white. Every field. As far down as he could see.

Except the bottom.

At the bottom, a long way down, past Lammas and Middle Acre and Ashwell, past where he could see Haverley’s tower, small, by the river, the white stopped. In a line. And under the line, everything was grey. The grey didn’t sparkle. Nothing settled on it. The frost had come down the whole valley in the night, every field, every hedge, and where it met the Grey it had stopped, as if it had come to the edge of a page.

Annet was standing at the gate of Brightwater’s first field with her stick.

She looked at it for a long time. Then she ran her thumb down the stick to the frost rings at the bottom. To the high one she’d cut under the oak at Haverley, the second one, the one she’d cut a long way higher than her mother’s.

She’d cut it too low.

He watched her see it. She didn’t say anything. She took her knife out and cut a third ring, higher again, almost at Brightwater’s own notch. Very small. Very neat.

“How many fields left?” said Drew. Quietly. He’d come out behind them.

Annet didn’t look round.

“Brightwater,” she said. “Then Hollin. Then the barn fields.” She put the knife away. “Eleven. If the frost doesn’t bite in the day. If it’s only nights.” She looked up at the sky, which was clear, pale, blue, cold. “Four days. Five.”

“And the Grey?”

Annet looked down the valley. At the line between white and grey.

“Two a night,” she said. “Three, some nights. It’s in Lammas’s stubble.” She did the sum. You could see her do it. “Six nights to the barn fields. Maybe seven.”

Nobody said anything for a while. Jared’s breath went up in front of him in the cold.

“Then it’s five days,” said Annet Sallow. “And we’re done in four.” She put the stick under her arm. “Everybody. The frost’s here. It’s harvest.”

They did it in four.

He didn’t remember much of it afterwards. He remembered being cold in the mornings and hot by noon. He remembered his hands, which bled, both of them, across the palms, from the forks, and Kei putting something on them at night that smelled of honey and pine, and Newish putting something on them in the morning on the one, and them bleeding again by noon. He remembered the frost every morning, thicker each time, and the mowers going into it at first light with their blades leaving dark green lines across the white. He remembered that you could see your breath in the fields until mid-morning and then the sun would come round the eastern hills and the frost would go off the stubble in a long low mist, all at once, like the whole valley breathing out.

He remembered Gaffer Tobin.

On his cart, in his chair, roped in. Every morning the lads drove him up to whatever field was being cut and stopped the cart at the top of the headland and he sat there in his blanket with his stick across his knees in the frost and watched it come in. Brightwater’s first field. Brightwater’s second. Hollin’s long field by the river, where the barley was so heavy it lay down on its own. He didn’t sleep any more. He didn’t eat any more, much. He watched. And when a field was in, he lifted his stick an inch. And held it.

Longer every time. Jared counted, the way Newish would have. Ten. Twelve. Fifteen.

He remembered Ice.

Ice. Coming down off the ridge in the evenings, some evenings, before the bell, with Thea and Xola, from wherever there were still bulls on the tops. Limping, some evenings. And Xola, every single evening, walking beside him with her hands already up, already shining, before anybody had said anything. And Ice letting her. Saying, when Calvin asked him at supper, with his mouth full, does it hurt, just: yeah. The ribs. The shoulder. Whatever it was. Yeah, it hurts. Like it was nothing. Like he’d been saying it his whole life.

“He says it hurts now,” said Jared to Hana. At Hollin. In the dark. “Have you noticed that? Ice. Every time anybody asks.”

“I noticed,” said Hana.

“He never used to say that. Not once. Not on the calls. Not on any floor. You’d ask him and he’d say fine and you’d know he was lying and he’d know you knew.” Jared looked at his bleeding hands. “Now he just says it.”

“Thea,” said Hana.

“I know it’s Thea.”

“Then what are you asking?”

Jared thought about it.

“I’m not asking,” he said. “I’m just saying. I like it.” He was quiet. “Man. I really like it.”

They saw the Long Barn on the thirty-seventh day.

They came up out of Hollin in the morning, the whole of the Acre, every village, seven hundred people, maybe more, Jared had stopped being able to count, in a long slow line up the last of the valley with the carts and the forge and the blades on their shoulders and the frost going off the fields in mist round their boots. And the hills closed in. That was the first thing. The two long lines of hills that had gone all the way up the valley on either side, blue, low, a long way off, came in. Closer. Steeper. Until they were on either side of you like the walls of a room, and the valley was narrow, and the river was a stream, fast, clear, cold, coming down over stones.

And then the lane turned a corner round a shoulder of the hill, and there it was.

The barn fields. Three of them, in the very head of the valley, where the hills met. Gold. The last gold in the Acre. Barley and wheat, standing, heavy, rippling, the whisper going over them. And at the top of the last field, against the hill, where the valley ended:

The Long Barn.

It wasn’t a barn. It couldn’t be. Jared stood in the lane and looked at it and his brain tried to make it into a barn and couldn’t. It was a hill. Somebody had built a hill out of stone. Grey walls longer than a street, longer than two streets, with buttresses down the side like the ribs of something enormous lying asleep. A roof of stone slates that went up and up into the morning, green with moss, gold with lichen, so high and so long that the far end of it was blue with distance. And doors. Not two. Four, along the long side. Each of them big enough to drive a cart through with another cart on top and a third on top of that. All of them shut.

The tithe barn at Haverley would have fitted inside it four times.

“Man,” said Jared.

Beside him, Ice had stopped walking.

He was looking at it too. Not the way Jared was. Not with his mouth open. He was looking at it the way he’d looked at the tithe barn on the first evening, on Haverley’s green, when he’d stopped dead fifty metres from the doors and Gabe had had to come back for him. Very still. With his face doing nothing.

Thea was on his left, with the lantern. Gabe was on his right. Neither of them said anything.

“You’ve seen it,” said Jared.

He didn’t know why he said it. It came out. Quietly. The way I believe Ice had come out, on the slip at Hythe, when Milli had asked him something else.

Ice didn’t look round.

“In the snow,” he said. “Once.”

That was all. Jared didn’t ask anything else. He’d never asked Ice anything. Not on the calls, not on any floor. You didn’t. Not because there was a rule. Because if Ice wanted you to know a thing, he’d make a joke about it, sooner or later, and you’d get it from the joke. And if he never made the joke, you’d never needed to know.

He’d never made a joke about this.

They walked on up the lane, toward the barn fields, the whole of the Acre, slow, with the carts. And as they came closer, Jared saw that one of the four great doors, the one at the far end, the furthest up the hill, wasn’t quite shut. There was a gap. A dark one, a hand’s width, between the door and the post.

And through it, a long way in, in the dark at the end of the barn, something tall.

Not a thing. A frame. An iron frame, standing on its own at the far end of the barn floor, against the end wall. Tall. As tall as the roof. The shape of a person, almost: a long upright, a crosspiece high up like shoulders, a round iron hoop above that where a head would go. Bound with old rope. Empty.

Waiting.

“What’s that?” said Calvin, behind them.

Cobb, walking beside the cart, looked at the gap in the door.

“That’s where he goes,” said Cobb. “After. The straw man. We build him on that, at harvest-home, out of the last sheaf, and stand him at the end of the barn, to keep the winter.” He spat. “He’s not in there yet.”

“He’s not in there yet,” said Ice. Very quietly. To nobody.

Jared looked at him.

Ice was still looking at the gap in the door. At the empty frame. And his face, for one second, before it went back to doing nothing, did something Jared had seen it do exactly once before, on the slip at Hythe, on the morning of the launch, when the old man stood up off his bucket by himself.

Then it was gone. Then he was walking again.

“Man,” said Jared. Under his breath. To himself.

That night, in the last of the light, in the lane under the Long Barn, with the whole of the Acre camped round its walls in carts and tents and wagons because there was one field left and nobody was going down the valley to sleep, Hana sat on the tail of the forge cart with her legs hanging and Jared sat next to her.

The frost was coming. You could feel it. The stars were coming out, hard and white, over the hills that had closed in round them, and the air smelled of cold and woodsmoke and the barley in the last field, standing, waiting for the morning. All round them, the camp. Fires. Voices. Somebody singing. Somebody always singing now. And up the hill, against the stars, the Long Barn, black, enormous, its four great doors shut.

The women had finished the crown. Hana had it in her lap. He’d seen it. They’d shown it to the whole camp at supper, the old women from Haverley, holding it up between them on a stick so everybody could see. A plait of wheat as thick as his wrist, gold, round, with the ears all facing out and up, so it looked like a crown of sun. Woven through it, poppies, the last ones, from the edges of the barn fields, red as anything. And cornflowers, blue, from Hollin. And at the front, where the plait met itself, a space. Empty.

Hana was holding something in her other hand.

Her own crown. The little purple-and-gold one, with the bead chains. She’d unpinned the thing from the front of it. The ugly iron rose. The first one he’d ever made, on floor 1, in Ulla Dray’s forge, the night Salty made the nails. It’s ugly. I know. She’d worn it on the front of her crown every day since floor 1.

She held it out to him.

“Put it on the front,” she said. “Of this one. Where they’ve left the space.” She looked at him. “I asked them to leave the space.”

He took it.

He looked at it for a long time, on his palm, in the firelight. It was terrible. It was the worst rose anybody had ever made. The petals were all different sizes. One of them had cracked, a little, at the edge, from four hundred days on the front of a crown on fourteen floors.

“Alice,” he said.

“Mm.”

“Am I—“ He didn’t know how to say it. “Is this the right place? Here? For you to do it?” He looked up the hill at the Long Barn. “Not at home. Not with everybody. In a barn at the top of a valley at the end of the world, with a bunch of farmers and a straw man.”

Hana didn’t answer for a while.

“I asked Newish the same thing,” she said. “In the rain. At the Thorne barn.” She looked at the crown in her lap. “She said everybody who can be there is. Thirty-five of us. And a valley.” She almost laughed. “And then she said don’t leave it in the field. And then she cried and said she was counting.”

“Sounds like Newish.”

“It was very Newish.” She was quiet. “So I’m asking you. Is it?”

He looked at her. Sitting on the tail of a forge cart in the frost with a wheat crown in her lap and her own crown in her hand, and her ears up, and her eyes very bright in the firelight.

He’d been half a world away from her for years. Different countries. Different times of day. He’d never once, on Earth, been able to be in the place she was.

He was in the place she was.

“You’re doing it in the right place,” said Jared. “Wherever you are, Alice. That’s the right place.” He closed his hand round the rose. “That’s always been the right place.”

She didn’t say anything.

She leaned her head on his shoulder. And they sat there on the back of the forge cart, the two of them, the way they’d sat on a thousand calls, not saying anything, just there. With the camp all round them and the frost coming down and the stars, and the Long Barn up the hill against the sky, and in the dark at the far end of it, behind a door a hand’s width open, an empty iron frame, waiting to be filled.

In the morning he put the rose on the crown.

He used a single thread of iron wire, from Bettris’s rack, the finest she had. Twisted it twice round the stem of the rose and through the plait, at the front, in the space. It held. It sat there in the middle of all that gold and red and blue like a small black ugly thing that somebody loved, which was exactly what it was.
