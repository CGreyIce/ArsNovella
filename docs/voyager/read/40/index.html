Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 1: Thirty-Six Circles

Chapter 40

Train Longer

3,122 words · 14 min read

Rachael had already read the note, so she spent the morning watching everybody else’s faces instead.

It was the twenty-third day. The smoking hall in Reedmoor had been cleared of everybody who wasn’t a Climber; the eel-woman had shooed out her own people with a broom and a look that said I know what this is, I’ve seen it before. Thirty-five people sat at the long tables under the reed roof, with the smoke drifting up through the hole in the middle and the Fen steaming outside the open windows. Nobody was talking. They’d all been told, at breakfast, quietly, party head to party head, that Deston had something to say.

Deston stood at the end of the middle table. He didn’t stand on anything. He didn’t need to; he was the tallest person in the room. He just stood there with his hand flat on his shirt pocket for a long moment, the way Rachael had watched him stand on and off for twenty-three days. Then he unbuttoned the pocket and took out the card.

Rachael watched the room see it.

A small white card, the size of a playing card. Thirty-four people leaned forward very slightly at once, the way a field of grass leans in a wind.

“On the first morning,” said Deston, “in the white room. Before any of our lights came on. Before anybody was awake. Somebody put this in my hand.”

He turned it round so the room could see the front. DESTON, in clean black capitals.

Then he turned it over.

“Be careful,” he read. His voice was very level. “Train longer on the third floor. D.”

Nobody moved.

He put the card down flat on the table in front of him, writing up, and stepped back from it. As if it were something that had been heavy for a long time and he’d finally been allowed to set it down.

It was very quiet in the smoking hall for what felt like a long time.

Then Calvin said, “Third floor.”

Everybody looked at him. He was sitting near the front with his mouth slightly open, looking at the card.

“Third floor,” said Calvin again. “That was the first morning, Dest. Before we knew there were floors. Before the lights told us anything.” He was working it out out loud, the way Calvin worked everything out. “He wrote third floor before anybody knew there’d be a third floor. And here we are, on the third floor. And there’s a window outside saying four’s an eight-level jump.” He swallowed. “Train longer.”

“Yeah,” said Deston.

“D,” said someone else. Rachael thought it was Tiger.

“It’s his,” said Gabe.

He’d been sitting at the end of the table nearest Deston, in his shirtsleeves, the wolf coat folded on the bench beside him. He hadn’t looked at the card once since Deston put it down. He didn’t need to. He’d read it on the first morning, sitting in an empty circle.

“It’s his handwriting,” said Gabe. “He does his bs with the loop open at the top. Always has. He wrote my birthday card like that every year since I was thirteen.” He looked round the room. “Dest copied his chemistry notes for three years. We both know that b. It’s him.”

The room let out a breath, all at once.

And Rachael watched it go round. She’d known it would, once it started. The way a thing goes round a room once one person says it out loud and everybody else realises they’ve been holding a piece of it.

Chris went next. He didn’t stand up; he just opened his notebook to the back page and turned it round on the table so the people near him could see. The little graph. A flat line of dots at ten, for a week, and then one going straight up.

“He kept fighting at the cap,” said Chris quietly. “For a week. Before anybody knew about the padlock. Winter pointed it out. You don’t do that unless you know it’s being saved.”

“He knew when we’d get to the class hall,” said Winter, from the end of her table, bluntly. “Mother Crane said. About an hour. He was right to the minute.”

“He knew which door,” said Xav.

Everybody turned. Xav hadn’t said a word since he sat down. He was sitting very still, the way he always sat, with his hands on the table.

“At the class hall,” said Xav. “Quill said he knew exactly which door. Didn’t look at the other two.” He paused, and Rachael watched him decide something. “And on the second day, we went east. To the old chapel. Where Quill said nobody goes.” He looked at Gabe, and Gabe nodded, once. “There’s a statue there with a lantern. It’s lit. It’s been lit since the first day. Gabe touched it and a window opened.” He said the next part very carefully. “This vigil has already been kept.”

The room went very still.

“We didn’t put it in World,” said Xav. “It felt like his. But it’s everyone’s now, I think. So.” He sat back.

“He’s got something with him,” said Hyvred, from her table, quickly, before she could stop herself. “Something small. That sleeps next to him. I’ve seen where it lay. Twice.” She looked at Deston, and Deston nodded. “And I can see where he’s been. His light leaves a trail. I’ve been following it since floor 1. It goes out there.” She pointed out of the window, at the Fen, at the amber light in the middle of it. “To the tower. The night the lights went out was him.”

The smoking hall erupted.

It took a while to calm down. Rachael didn’t try to help. She sat at her table with her arms folded and watched it, the way she’d watched it in the white room. Everybody talking at once. Everybody turning to the person next to them and saying did you know. Everybody finding out that everybody else had been carrying a piece of it.

Yan told them about Suzu. She saw him. She says he looked back at me like I look at my mother’s door. Hana told them about three metres in the white room, and the iron rose, and the onion skin in her crown, and Hana’s crown went round the hall from hand to hand while everyone looked at a frozen onion skin. Tiger told them about the pine. Newish told them about the tower door on the pass, and the claw marks, and Hana sitting in his footprints. Maigo, very quietly, told them about the tree remembering someone warm.

And Aleaka, at the very end, from her bench with Sleet asleep in her lap, said something nobody expected.

“The little thing,” she said, slowly, stumbling a bit, the way she did when it mattered. “That sleeps next to him. I think I know what it’s called.”

Everybody looked at her.

“He named everything Alea,” said Aleaka. “In every game. Every pet, every companion, every horse. Always the same name. Gabe knows. Deston knows. Xav knows.” She looked down at Sleet. “So whatever it is. Whatever he’s got with him, curled up next to him on the bench, I’d bet you anything it’s called Alea.”

Gabe made a sound that wasn’t a laugh and wasn’t anything else.

“Yeah,” he said. His voice cracked. “Yeah. It’s called Alea. Course it is.”

Then Calvin stood up.

Rachael had known he would. She’d been watching him since Deston turned the card over. She’d watched his face go from shocked to working-it-out to something she knew very well, because she’d seen it at the top of a valley on floor 1. Right before he went over a wall and got his arm broken.

“So he’s on four,” said Calvin. “On his own.”

The room quietened.

“He’s been on four for five days,” said Calvin. “On his own. Eight levels above us. And he wrote us a note saying be careful, because he knows what’s up there.” He looked round the room, and he wasn’t grinning. “So shouldn’t we go help him? If it’s that bad up there? Shouldn’t we be going up, not sitting down here, waiting?”

There was a murmur. Rachael heard it go round the hall: some people agreeing, some not. She saw Tiger nod. She saw Hako sit up, grinning, already halfway to yes. She saw Gabe’s hands close on the edge of the table.

“We’re ten,” said Malcolm, from Xav’s table, flatly. “Four’s eighteen. We’d die.”

“We don’t know that—“

“We do know that,” said Malcolm. “There’s a window. It says eighteen. I’m a pessimist, Calvin, but I can read.”

“So we just leave him up there?”

“He’s not asking us to come.”

That was Thea.

She hadn’t said anything all morning. She’d been sitting at the end of her party’s table, very quiet, looking at the card on Deston’s table. She hadn’t touched it. Rachael had been watching her too, because Thea was one of the people she didn’t know. She’d wondered what was going on behind that very still face.

“Read it again,” said Thea. “He didn’t write come and find me. He didn’t write help. He wrote be careful. And train longer. He wrote it to the person he knew would make us do it.” She looked at Deston. “He’s not asking us to come. He’s asking us to wait.”

Calvin looked at her.

“Why?” he said. It came out raw. “Why would he want us to wait? If he’s up there on his own, and it’s that bad—“

“I don’t know,” said Thea.

She said it very simply. But Rachael saw something go across her face as she said it, and go away again. Something private, held very carefully. A thing Thea knew, or thought she knew, and wasn’t going to say.

Drew backed Deston. Rachael had known he would; she’d seen them write the line on the board together. He stood up at his table, with the little slate under his arm, and said it in his easy, calming voice, the one that made people listen.

“I’ve got a feeling about four,” said Drew. “I can’t explain it. When I was running a tournament, I used to get feelings like this. Something’s going to go wrong, right here, at this exact point. I could never explain them, and people used to talk me out of them, and then it’d go wrong exactly there.” He looked round the hall. “I’ve got one now. About four. I’m not getting talked out of it.”

“That’s not a reason,” said Calvin.

“No,” said Drew. “But the card is. I’m just telling you I agree with it.”

The room was quiet again. Rachael looked at Calvin. He was standing at his bench, with everybody looking at him, his jaw working. He was looking at the card. And then, very briefly, without meaning to, he looked down at his own left arm.

The one that had been broken at the top of the valley on floor 1. Both bones. Where she’d held them straight in the dark of his arm and felt them fit.

Rachael stood up.

She hadn’t meant to. She wasn’t a stand-up-in-a-room person. But she stood up anyway, at her table, with her arms folded, and everybody turned and looked at her, and she said it the way she said everything. Directly. She didn’t have time to waste.

“He was scared,” she said.

The room went quiet.

“When he wrote that. On the first morning. He was scared.” She nodded at the card. “Not of us. For us. You don’t write be careful to people you’re running away from. You write it to people you think are going to get hurt.” She looked at Calvin. “And you don’t write train longer on the third floor unless you know something’s waiting on the fourth one. Something bad enough that he spent his first minute here, before any of us were even awake, writing it down. Instead of saying goodbye. Instead of anything.”

Calvin didn’t say anything.

“I don’t know how he knows,” said Rachael. “I don’t care. I’ve known Declan since I was seventeen. He was the one you could ask anything. He never made you feel stupid for asking. And he never, ever told anybody to be careful unless he meant it.” She sat back down. “So we do what it says. That’s all.”

Calvin stood there for a long time.

Then he looked at Rachael, across the hall. She looked back. And something went between them, something about the top of a valley, and a ram, and two halves of a bone fitting in the dark.

“Okay,” said Calvin.

The room breathed.

“Okay. I’ll wait.” He sat down, slowly. “But when we go up, I’m going first. I’m the scout. That’s my job.”

“No, you’re not,” said Rachael.

“I’m—“

“No,” said Rachael, “you’re not.”

And half the hall laughed, not because it was funny, but because it was the first thing anybody had said in an hour that sounded like an ordinary day. Calvin put his face in his hands. Even Deston, standing at the end of the table with his hand nowhere near his pocket for the first time in twenty-three days, laughed.

They voted. It wasn’t really a vote. Drew asked who wanted to stay on floor 3 and train, properly, until everybody had done their tier trial and was well past eighteen. Thirty-five hands went up. Calvin’s went up last, slowly, but it went up.

Deston picked up the card off the table and looked at it for a moment. Then, instead of putting it back in his pocket, he walked over to the wall of the smoking hall and pinned it there. On a nail, by the door, where everyone would see it every time they came in or went out.

be careful, train longer on the 3rd floor - D.

He stepped back and looked at it.

“No more pocket,” he said.

Dennis was the one who asked the practical question, because Dennis always was.

“So how long?” he said, when the room had settled. He was sitting beside Rachael with his inventory window open in front of him, reading it like a bank statement, the way he had since the second day. “Because I’m the treasurer. I made myself treasurer and nobody argued, so I’m the treasurer. And if thirty-five people are going to sit in a swamp for months, somebody’s going to have to pay for the eels.”

“Until everyone’s done their trial,” said Drew. “And past eighteen. Well past.”

“That’s not a number, Drew. That’s a feeling.”

“Then I’ll make it a number. Twenty. Everyone at twenty, everyone through tier two.” Drew looked round the room. “Then we talk about four.”

Dennis did some sums in the air with one finger. His face went through several expressions, none of them happy.

“Right,” he said. “Right. So that’s a lot of eels.” He closed the window. “I’ll talk to her.”

He got up and went out of the smoking hall, toward the walkway where the eel-woman was waiting with her broom. Rachael watched him through the window. He stood very straight, in front of a small brown woman with her arms folded. He talked with his hands, the way he always did when he was being charming and wanted you to know it.

He was out there for twenty minutes. When he came back in, he looked like a man who’d been through a war.

“Well?” said Deston.

“She’s going to feed us,” said Dennis. “On a tab. Like Mother Crane.” He sat down heavily. “She says Climbers always stay a while at the end of the Threshold, and Climbers always come back down eventually, and she’s never been short-changed by one yet. And her grandmother said the same.” He paused. “And she wants the penguin.”

There was a short silence.

“She wants what?” said PenguChi.

“Not to keep. To borrow. For fishing. Mornings only.” Dennis spread his hands. “She says he caught more eels last night in ten minutes than her best boy catches in a week. She says that bird’s worth more than the lot of us.” He looked at Chi apologetically. “I said I’d ask.”

Chi looked down at Waddles, who was sitting at her feet in a small damp puddle, looking pleased with himself. Waddles looked back up at her. Then at the door, where the eel-woman was standing with her broom, watching him with great professional interest.

“Mornings only,” said Chi slowly.

“Mornings only.”

“And he can come back if he doesn’t like it.”

“Obviously.”

Chi looked at Waddles for a long moment. Then, with enormous dignity, she said: “I’ll have to ask him.”

Waddles stood up, shook himself, and waddled straight across the smoking hall to the door. He stopped at the eel-woman’s feet and looked up at her.

“Well,” said the eel-woman. “That’s settled, then.”

The whole hall laughed. Even Calvin. Dennis put his face in his hands and said, muffled, that he’d negotiated a three-month food deal for thirty-five people and the penguin had closed it.

Rachael found him afterwards, on the walkway outside, looking at the Fen.

She stood next to him with her hands in her pockets, a polite distance away. She didn’t say anything for a while. Neither did he.

“No more pocket,” she said finally.

“No more pocket.”

“How does it feel?”

Deston thought about it.

“Light,” he said. “I didn’t know how heavy it was until I put it down.”

She nodded. She knew about that. She’d carried a list in her body for twenty-six years, of things she couldn’t do and couldn’t eat and couldn’t risk. On the third morning in this tower, she’d woken up and the list had been gone. She knew exactly what it felt like when something heavy you’d carried so long you’d stopped noticing it was suddenly just not there.

She looked out at the amber light in the middle of the Fen.

“Whatever he saw up there,” she said, mostly to herself. “On four. Whatever made him write that.”

“Yeah.”

“We’re not going to walk into it.”

“No,” said Deston. “We’re not.”

They stood on the walkway in the heat, side by side, a polite distance apart, and looked at the light.
