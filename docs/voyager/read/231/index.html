Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 6: Unwritten

Chapter 231

Under the Road

5,169 words · 22 min read

It was summer, and Maigo could hear the road.

He hadn’t meant to. He never meant to. On every floor it happened the same way: he took his boots off, the first morning, because it was summer, because his root-foot wanted to touch whatever was under it, and he put his bare feet down on the ground, and the ground started talking.

Not in words. It never was. On nine it had been the Grow, a sound like something wearing a plant. On fifteen it had been the ice, tock, and the arm in the cliff going slack a finger at a time. On sixteen, the moss, which had been very quiet and very old and mostly said dark. On seventeen he’d kept his boots on, because the cobbles of Turnstead wanted to turn and he didn’t want to hear them want it.

On eighteen he took his boots off at the 56 stone, on the morning after the Willow Bridge, and put his feet down on the Long Road.

It was warm.

That was the first thing. Not the sun. The sun was on it, hot, white, the dust soft as flour between his toes. Under that. Down. In the packed hard road itself, under the dust, under the stones they’d laid it on however long ago. Warm, the way the ground under a house is warm where people have lived in it a long time.

And it was full.

He stood on the road with his eyes shut and the leaves on his antlers moving in the hot wind, and felt it. Footsteps. Thousands of them. Hundreds of thousands. Pilgrims, going one way, for longer than the oldest tree on the floor had been alive, and every step pressed into the road and kept there. He could feel them. Light ones and heavy ones. Old ones and children. A man with a limp. A woman who’d walked the whole of one stretch barefoot, like him, in a summer a very long time ago, crying.

And under the footsteps, something else.

Words.

Not spoken. Muttered. Under the breath. The way you’d say a thing over and over to yourself while you walked, to see how it sounded, before you had to say it out loud. Practising. Every pilgrim who’d ever walked the Long Road toward a bridge had walked it saying their toll under their breath, again and again, to the road, to their feet, to nobody. And the road had kept every one.

He stood there with his feet in it and couldn’t move.

“Maigo,” said somebody.

Chris. Beside him. Not loud.

He opened his eyes.

“You’ve stopped,” said Chris. “In the road. For about five minutes.” He was quiet. “Is it bad?”

Maigo thought about it. He always thought about things before he said them. People thought it was because he was slow. It wasn’t. It was because words, once you’d said them, stayed said, and he’d rather they were the right ones.

“No,” said Maigo. “It’s full.”

“Of what?”

He looked down at his feet. At the white dust between his toes.

“Everything people practised,” said Maigo. “Before the bridges. All the way along.” He looked up the road, at the pilgrims ahead, small, with their staffs. “They say it to the road first. Over and over. Before they say it to the river.” He was quiet. “The road kept all of it.”

Chris looked at him for a long time.

Then he looked down at the road. At the dust. As if he could see it.

“All of it?”

“All of it.”

Chris didn’t write anything down. Maigo noticed that. Chris always wrote everything down. He stood in the road and looked at the dust for a while, and then he put his hands in his pockets, and they walked on.

Maigo kept his boots off.

The second bridge was at the 48 stone, by a mill.

They came to it on the fifth morning after the Willow Bridge, down a long slope between barley fields with poppies in them. A river, slower than the first, wider, brown, with a mill on the near bank, an old timber one with a great wheel going round in a race of white water that came off the river through a sluice. The noise of it. The wheel creaking, and the race roaring, and the river under all of it. And over the river, a bridge. Two arches this time, wide and low, of yellow stone, with the road going over them in a gentle hump. A little stone house at the near end.

And a keeper on a bench. A woman this time. Sixty, maybe. Grey hair in a long plait. Deaf, like the other; Sorrel had said they all were. She had a bundle of brown cord on her knee and a little knife, and she watched them come down the slope between the barley.

Sorrel had come with them.

She’d walked out of the Lark at Fallow the morning after the Willow Bridge, with her staff and her six knots, and fallen in at the back of the line without asking, next to Aleaka and the goat. Nobody had told her she could. Nobody had told her she couldn’t. She’d walked with them for five days, and she talked to the goat, and she’d told Calvin to shut up twice, which had made Rachael look at her with something like respect.

“Mill Bridge,” said Sorrel. At the top of the slope. “The second one.”

“What’s the toll?” said Chris.

Sorrel looked at the bridge for a long time. Then at them. All of them. The long line of them, coming down between the barley.

“Something true,” she said. “To somebody who needs to hear it.”

Nobody said anything.

“You go up in twos,” said Sorrel. “On your own, the two of you. To the crown. And you each say a true thing to the other one. Something they need to hear.” She touched her cord. “And if it’s true, and they needed it, the bridge holds for both of you. You cross together.”

“And if they didn’t need it?”

“Then it doesn’t.” Sorrel looked at Chris. “Same as the first one. The far side’s water.” She was quiet. “Most people find it harder than the first. The first, nobody hears. This one, somebody does.”

Maigo looked at the bridge.

At the crown of it. Wide and low, the yellow stone, with the mill race roaring beside it. Two people could stand up there and say anything to each other and nobody on either bank would hear a word.

But one of them would.

They paired up on the slope.

Nobody organised it. Higu didn’t draw a rota; Xav didn’t stand up. It just happened, the way water finds its way downhill. People drifted. Toward somebody. And stood next to them. And didn’t say anything, because what you said up there was the toll and you didn’t spend it on the slope.

Maigo watched it happen.

Hana drifted toward Milli and stopped, and Milli’s tail went round her ankle. Yan stood very still with Working On It upright beside him, and Wendy came and stood at his elbow with her hand on the back of his coat, the way she always did, and he didn’t look at her. Calvin went toward Rachael and then stopped, halfway, and looked at Dennis, and then went to Rachael anyway, and Rachael didn’t look surprised. Gigi went to Thea. Xola watched her go and something happened in Xola’s face, and then she went to Kei, who blinked.

Winter stood where she was.

Ina came to her. Slowly. Not close. About the width of his mask. And stood there.

And Ice.

He was at the back of the line. With his boots in the dust and Tuli’s pole in his hand. Thea had gone to Gigi. He was standing on his own, at the top of the slope, looking down at the bridge. And Maigo saw him look round. At all of them. At the pairs forming. And he saw Ice’s face do a thing he’d seen it do on the Grey, at the line, a long time ago. Before he’d learned to ask.

Somebody will need a partner. Somebody always ends up on their own. It’ll be me. That’s fine.

And then Gabe was there.

He hadn’t drifted. He’d walked. Straight down the line from wherever he’d been, past everybody, with Hearthwall on his back, and stopped next to Ice at the top of the slope, on his right, where he always stood.

He didn’t say anything.

Ice looked at him. And his face did the other thing. The thing it did now.

Maigo stood in the dust and felt the road under his feet, warm, full, and looked round for his own.

He didn’t have to look far.

“Me,” said Salty.

He was standing next to Maigo. He’d been standing next to Maigo, Maigo realised, since the top of the slope. Not drifted. Just there. The way he’d been there on floor 2, in the snow, when Maigo’s root-foot had gone cold and his whole body had said shh and he’d stopped talking, stopped moving, stopped everything, and somebody had picked him up.

Salty had picked him up.

On floor 2. For two weeks. Carried him. Through the snow, up the pass, on his back, in his arms, everywhere. And wouldn’t put him down. Yan had told him afterwards. He wouldn’t put you down. We tried. He wouldn’t.

They’d never talked about it.

“Me,” said Salty again. Red in the face. Looking at the bridge, not at Maigo. “If you— If there isn’t anybody else. I mean. If you want.”

Maigo looked at him.

He thought about it, the way he thought about everything. Before he said it. So it would be the right word.

“Yes,” said Maigo.

They went up eleventh.

He’d watched the ten before. From the slope. Not their mouths. Their faces, after. Hana and Milli came down the far side holding hands so hard their knuckles were white, and Hana’s ears were flat, and she was laughing, or crying, or both. Yan and Wendy came down with Yan’s face doing nothing at all and Wendy’s doing everything, and Wendy had her hand on the back of his coat still, but tighter.

Gigi and Thea stood on the crown for a long time. Then Gigi put both her arms round Thea and held on. And Thea held on back, with the lantern hanging from one hand, the amber swinging. And the bridge held. And on the slope, Xola made a sound and turned her face into Kei’s shoulder, and Kei, who’d never been hugged by Xola in his life, stood very still and patted her back.

Ice and Gabe went sixth.

Maigo watched them go up. Gabe on Ice’s right. They stood on the crown, the two of them, with the mill race roaring and the wheel going round, and Ice said something. Short. And Gabe’s face—

Gabe sat down.

On the crown of the bridge. On the warm yellow stone. All at once, the way your legs go when somebody tells you a thing you’ve been waiting to hear for a very long time. He sat down with his back against the parapet and Hearthwall in his lap and put his hand over his eyes.

And then he laughed.

Maigo couldn’t hear it. He could see it. Gabe sitting on the crown of the Mill Bridge with his hand over his eyes, laughing, his shoulders going.

Ice stood over him. Looking down. Then he crouched. And said something else.

Gabe took his hand off his eyes. And said something back. Not long.

And Ice went very still.

Then he sat down too. On the stone. Next to Gabe. Shoulder to shoulder. The way they’d sat, Gabe had told everybody once, on a wall when they were thirteen and fourteen, after something had gone wrong.

They sat there for a long time.

The keeper, on her bench, watched them. Then she got up and went across the bridge and stood at the far end, at the bottom of the far arch, and waited. And they got up, eventually, both of them, and walked down the far side together, and the far side was stone, and she tied them each a knot.

On the slope, nobody said anything.

Maigo didn’t know what either of them had said. Nobody did. Nobody would.

He didn’t need to.

“Come on,” said Salty.

They went up.

The crown of the Mill Bridge was wide enough for four people to stand side by side. Yellow stone, warm from the sun, worn smooth in the middle. The mill race was right there on the left, over the parapet, white and roaring, and the wheel creaking round, and the river under the arches brown and slow. You couldn’t hear anything else. You couldn’t hear the slope or the bank or your own feet.

Maigo stood on it barefoot.

The bridge was talking too. Not like the road. Stone, not dust. Older. It had been told things. Two at a time, for as long as there’d been a bridge, two people standing exactly here and saying something true to each other. It had kept them, the way the road kept the practising. Warm, in the stone.

He could feel them under his feet. All of them. People who’d stood here.

I forgive you.

It wasn’t your fault.

I was there. I saw. I’m sorry.

I love you. I never said.

He stood on all of it.

Salty was looking at him. Red. Big hands hanging at his sides. Ressa’s small hammer in his coat pocket; Maigo could see the shape of it.

“You first,” said Salty. “If you want. Or me. I don’t—“

“Me,” said Maigo.

He’d thought about it for five days. Since Sorrel said seven bridges. He’d known, the moment she said it, that the second would be something like this, and he’d known who, and what. He’d walked five days barefoot on a road full of everything everybody had ever practised, practising.

He didn’t say any of the practised words.

“Every winter,” said Maigo, “I’m frightened I won’t wake up.”

Salty went still.

“Every one.” Maigo looked at the stone. “On two most. A bit every winter since. It comes up through my feet. The cold. And my body says not now. And I go quiet. And I can’t move, and I can’t talk, and I can hear everything, and I can’t do anything.” He was quiet. “And every time, I think: this is the one. This is the winter I don’t come back. I’ll just be a tree. In a field. With leaves on my head, and nobody inside.”

He’d never said it. Not to Yan. Not to Rachael, who’d asked, once, on floor 4, very carefully, and he’d said it’s fine. Not to anybody.

“On two,” said Maigo. “You carried me.”

Salty didn’t say anything.

“Two weeks. Up the pass. In the snow. I could feel you. The whole time. I couldn’t move. I couldn’t tell you. But I could feel you carrying me.” He looked up at Salty. “Yan said you wouldn’t put me down. They tried. You wouldn’t.”

“No,” said Salty. Very quietly.

“I woke up,” said Maigo, “because somebody was carrying me.”

He stopped. That was it. That was the whole of it. He’d thought there’d be more, and there wasn’t.

“You needed to know,” said Maigo. “That it was you. That’s why I woke up.”

The mill race roared.

Salty stood on the crown of the bridge with his big hands at his sides and his face going from red to white to something else, and his eyes going, the way Salty’s always went. He cried at everything. Everybody knew. He’d cried at the wedding, at the Long Acre, at Pask’s forge.

He didn’t cry now. He looked at Maigo.

“I didn’t put you down,” said Salty, “because I was frightened too.”

Maigo looked at him.

“If I put you down,” said Salty. His voice was very rough. “On two. In the snow. If I put you down, you’d be a tree. That’s what I thought. In a field. With leaves on your head. And you’d stay there. And we’d go up the pass and leave you.” He swallowed. “So I didn’t put you down. Not for two weeks. Not once. Not to sleep. Yan said I was being stupid. I was being stupid. I didn’t care. If I didn’t put you down, you weren’t a tree. You were Maigo. Being carried.”

He stopped.

“I’ve been frightened every winter since,” said Salty. “Every one. That you won’t wake up. I carry you in my head. Every winter. All the way through.” He looked at the stone. “I never said. I didn’t think you’d want to know somebody was that frightened about you.”

Maigo didn’t say anything for a long time.

Then he stepped forward, on the warm yellow stone, and put his forehead against Salty’s chest. Leaves and all. The little branches of his antlers going either side of Salty’s chin. The way he’d been carried. Except standing up.

Salty’s arms came round him.

They stood on the crown of the Mill Bridge with the race roaring and nobody hearing anything.

He didn’t look at the far side of the bridge. He didn’t need to. He could feel it through his feet.

Stone.

He’d had it since the Hollow. Like most of them. He’d looked at it once and thought when I know what it’s for, and shut it.

He opened it that night, at the inn below the Mill Bridge. The Barley Mow at Stile, a low white house with roses up the front and a yard full of pilgrims sitting on the warm stones in the long summer dusk with their new knots. He was sitting on the step with his feet in the dust of the road, because he couldn’t stop listening to it, and Salty was asleep against the wall beside him with his mouth open, and the window opened in the corner of his eye.

He read it twice.

Where nothing grows.

He looked down at his feet. At the white dust of the Long Road between his toes.

Nothing grew on a road. That was the whole point of a road. A thousand years of feet, packing it down, so nothing could. Not a weed. Not moss. Grass at the edges, in the hedges, but not on the road. He’d felt that the first morning, under the warm and the full: nothing alive. Just kept.

Hear what is kept there. He’d been hearing it for six days.

Say it for it.

He looked up from the window. Across the yard. At the end of the long bench by the door of the Barley Mow, where an old woman was sitting on her own with a cup of something going cold in her hands and a staff across her knees and a brown cord round her neck.

Six knots.

Sorrel had crossed the Mill Bridge with Aleaka. He didn’t know what they’d said. Aleaka had come down the far side with her face wet and the goat butting her hip. Sorrel had come down with her face doing nothing at all. She’d crossed it eleven times. She’d crossed every bridge eleven times. She’d never once crossed the seventh.

He got up. He didn’t wake Salty. He walked across the yard, barefoot, in the dusk, and out of the gate, onto the road.

And stood on it. In the middle. Where nothing grew.

And put his feet down.

He’d been listening to the road for six days with his feet. The way you listen to rain on a roof. All of it at once, a sound.

He listened properly.

The way he’d listened to the arm in the cliff on fifteen. Not to all of it. To one place. Down. Through the dust, through the packed white road, through the stones it was laid on, into the old hard ground under all of it. The way a root goes. Undergrowth. Root anywhere, however deep. What is waiting under the ground will know you’re there.

The road knew he was there.

He felt it notice. The way a big old dog notices you’ve sat down next to it. And then, all at once, everything it had kept came up through his feet.

He nearly fell over.

Thousands. Hundreds of thousands. Every pilgrim who’d ever walked from the Barley Mow toward the third bridge, practising. I was wrong. I was wrong about her. I was wrong. I’m frightened of the dark, I’ve always been, I’m forty and I sleep with a candle. I took it. I took the money. It was me. I love him. I love him and he’s my brother’s. Muttered. Under the breath. Again and again. A thousand miles of it. The road had kept every one, pressed down into the dust with the footsteps, warm.

He stood in it and let it go past him. All of it. Like standing in a river.

And then he went looking.

He didn’t know how. He just did. The way you’d look for one voice in a crowded room. Through all of it. For one set of footsteps. Small. Light. A woman’s. Walking this road eleven times in thirty years. Past this inn eleven times. Stopping at this gate, every time, the way you stop at a place you know. Standing here. Where he was standing.

There.

He found it.

Not words. He’d thought it would be words. Thirty years of practising the toll for the seventh bridge, what you’ve kept, muttered to the road eleven times over every mile. He’d thought he’d hear what she’d kept.

He didn’t.

He heard the road carrying it.

That was the thing. That was what came up through his feet. Not the secret. He didn’t want the secret; it wasn’t his, it would never be his, he’d have stepped off the road if it had come up as words. It wasn’t words. It was the weight of it. Thirty years of something kept, pressed into the dust at the gate of the Barley Mow eleven times, a little deeper each time. And the road under it.

Holding it up.

He stood very still.

The road had been carrying it. For thirty years. Every time she walked it. Every time she stopped at this gate and stood here muttering the thing she couldn’t say, the road had taken a bit of it. Into the dust. Into the stones. Into the warm old ground. Kept it. The way Salty had kept him. Not putting it down.

It wasn’t heavy any more.

He could feel that too. That was what the road knew, and she didn’t. Thirty years she’d been carrying it up and down the Long Road, thinking it was the heaviest thing in the world. And the road had been carrying it with her the whole time. Every step. And between the two of them, it had got light.

Light enough.

Say it for it.

He went back through the gate.

Across the yard. In the dusk. To the end of the long bench by the door, where an old woman was sitting on her own with a cold cup.

He sat down beside her. Not close. About the width of a staff.

Sorrel looked at him. Sideways. With her bright black eyes. Then at his bare feet, white with the road’s dust to the ankle.

“You’re the quiet one,” said Sorrel. “With the leaves.”

“Yes.”

“You listen to the ground. Your big friend said. The one who cries at everything.”

“Yes.”

Sorrel looked at the cup in her hands.

“Don’t tell me,” she said. Very quietly. Not looking at him. “If you heard it. Whatever it is. Don’t you dare tell me what I’ve—“

“I didn’t hear it,” said Maigo.

Sorrel stopped.

“I didn’t listen for it. It’s not mine.” He looked down at his feet. “I heard the road.”

She didn’t say anything.

“It’s been carrying it,” said Maigo. “With you. Every time you walked it. Every time you stood at the gate.” He was quiet. He thought about the words. He wanted them to be the right ones. He’d only get to say them once. “You think it’s the heaviest thing. You’ve been carrying it thirty years, and you think it’s the heaviest thing in the world, and that’s why you can’t put it down on the last bridge. Because it’s too heavy to lift up and say.”

Sorrel had gone completely still.

“It isn’t,” said Maigo. “Not any more. The road’s had it too. Eleven times. All the way. It’s been holding it up with you.” He looked at the road, through the gate, white in the dusk. “It’s light now. It’s light enough. You could say it.”

He stopped.

That was what the road would have said. If it had a mouth. If it could have stood up out of the dust at the gate the eleventh time she stopped there and told her. It’s light now. You’ve carried it long enough. So have I.

He’d said it for it.

Sorrel sat on the bench with the cold cup in her hands and didn’t move for a very long time.

Then she put the cup down. On the bench beside her. Carefully. And put both her old brown hands over her face.

She didn’t make a sound.

Maigo sat beside her, the width of a staff away, and didn’t touch her, and didn’t say anything else. In the yard, the pilgrims were going in, one by one, in the last of the light, with their new knots. Swallows going over. Across the yard, against the wall, Salty, awake now, looking at them, not coming over. Knowing not to.

After a long time, Sorrel took her hands down.

“Thirty years,” she said. Her voice was very small. “Light.”

“Light enough.”

She looked at him. Sideways. Her bright eyes very wet.

“You’re a strange one,” said Sorrel. “With your leaves.”

“Yes.”

“Thank you,” said Sorrel.

She picked up her staff and went inside.

He read it on the bench in the dark.

He thought about the road. All of it. A thousand miles of practising. Warm. Kept. And under every bridge, he supposed, more. The stone of the Mill Bridge, with I forgive you and it wasn’t your fault pressed into it two at a time.

And at the end of the road, the last bridge. Where everything went.

He’d felt it, standing at the gate. Not the bridge. The way. The way the warmth in the road went, under all the kept things, the way water goes downhill. All of it going one way. Forward. Down the road. Toward the end. Toward the last bridge, and whatever was there, that asked for what you’d kept.

He didn’t know what it did with it all.

He’d find out. They all would.

He went across the yard in the dark and sat down against the wall next to Salty. Barefoot. With the road’s dust on his feet to the ankle.

“What did you say to her?” said Salty.

Maigo thought about it.

“What the road would have,” said Maigo.

Salty looked at him for a long time. Then he nodded, as if that made sense, which it did, to Salty, who’d carried a tree up a mountain for two weeks because he was frightened it would stay a tree.

They sat against the wall. After a while, Salty put his arm round Maigo’s shoulders, leaves and all, and left it there.

At the window above them, Chris’s lamp was still lit. Maigo could see the shape of him, against it, at a little table, with a notebook open. Not writing. Just sitting there. With it open.

The light went out very late.
