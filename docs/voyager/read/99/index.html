Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 3: Party of One

Chapter 99

Kindling

5,716 words · 25 min read

Ina had been told no by a great many people in his life, and he’d learned a long time ago that there were two kinds.

There was the no that meant I haven’t thought about it. That one you could talk round. It was easy, if you had a voice people liked listening to, and he’d always had one. You found the thing they hadn’t thought about, and you put it in front of them, gently, as if they’d found it themselves, and the no turned into a maybe and the maybe turned into a yes, and nobody felt they’d lost anything. He’d done it a hundred times on Earth. He wasn’t proud of all of them.

And there was the other kind. The no that meant I have thought about it. All my life. And I’m frightened.

You didn’t talk that one round. You couldn’t. If you tried, you were just a man with a nice voice telling someone their fear was wrong. And it usually wasn’t. It was usually the most right thing about them.

Burr’s was the second kind.

So Ina didn’t talk to him. Not for two days. He went to the drum.

He turned up on the morning of the two hundred and nineteenth day, with his sleeves rolled and his mask round his neck, and stood at the edge of the drum platform until Burr looked round.

“I’d like to learn the ropes,” said Ina.

Burr looked at him for a long time.

“No fire,” said Burr.

“No fire,” said Ina. “Ropes.”

Burr grunted, and turned back to the drum, and after a moment, without looking round, held out a coil.

It was harder than it looked. Everything up here was. The ropes were made of a bark-fibre the bough-folk twisted themselves, grey and rough and stronger than anything Ina had ever handled, and they had to be coiled a particular way or they kinked, and hung a particular way or they wore, and checked every morning, every one, from end to end, with your fingers, for the soft place that meant a strand was going. Burr checked them himself. All of them. He went along them with his two-fingered hand, fast, never looking, and stopped dead at a soft place Ina couldn’t have found with a lamp and a week.

“How long have you been the ropes?” said Ina.

“Thirty-one years,” said Burr. “My father before me. His mother before him.” He went on along the rope. “Nobody falls. Not on my ropes. Thirty-one years.” He stopped. He looked at his own left hand, with its two fingers and a thumb, as if he’d forgotten it was there. “Nearly nobody.”

Ina didn’t ask. He coiled.

It was on the second afternoon that he found the cones.

He’d been watching the drum for two days, and he’d thought he understood it. A great wooden barrel on an axle, with handles, and the ropes wound round it, and when you hauled on the handles the ropes came up. But it didn’t work like that. Six people couldn’t have hauled up a basket with Salty in it on muscle alone. There was a frame behind the drum, going down off the edge of the platform into the leaves, and in the frame, on their own ropes, hung the counterweights. Great heavy nets of them. When the drum turned one way, the weights went down and the baskets came up.

He leaned over the edge and looked at them.

They were cones.

Hundreds of them, in the nets. Each about the size of a man’s head. Grey-brown and scaled all over in thick, tight, overlapping plates, shut so hard and so close that you couldn’t have got a knife in between them. They hung in their nets in great clusters, swaying very slightly, heavy as stone.

“What are these?” he said.

“Stone-cones,” said Burr, without looking. “From the north tree. They fall out of the crown in the autumn. They never open. Hard as rock. Heavier.” He hauled a line. “We gather the fallen ones out of the Down in the daytime. Always have. Every weight in Highcrook’s a stone-cone. Every drum. Every lift.” He jerked his chin at the doorway of the nearest hanging house, where a cone sat on the floor holding the door open. “Doorstops. Hammers. The children roll them. They last forever. My grandmother’s grandmother’s weights are on that drum.”

Ina looked at the nets for a long time.

“So the north tree,” he said slowly, “has been holding up your ropes.”

Burr didn’t answer.

“For three hundred years,” said Ina. “With its children.”

Burr stopped hauling.

He stood there at the drum with the line in his hands and didn’t move. He didn’t look at Ina. He looked down at the nets of grey cones hanging off the edge of his platform, the way you look at a thing you’ve seen every day of your life and never once looked at.

Then he went back to hauling.

He didn’t say anything else that afternoon. But that evening, when Ina was leaving, Burr said, without turning round:

“Tomorrow. At the drum. At dusk. Bring the little tree. And the old woman. And say what you’ve come to say.”

He said it at dusk on the two hundred and twenty-first day, on the drum platform, to rather more people than he’d expected.

Burr had brought the elders. Six of them, very old, very still, sitting on the edge of the platform in the last gold light with their long hands in their laps and their eyes shining. Old Linden was there, against the trunk of the north tree, with her cheek on the bark the way she always was. Maigo sat beside her with his hand flat on the wood. Tansy was on the drum handle. And behind them all, along the railings and the bridges and the branches, Highcrook had come to watch. Quietly. Hundreds of them. Big dark eyes in the dusk, beginning to shine as the light went.

And the thirty-five, a little way off, at the far end of the platform, where Burr had told them to stand. And Ice, among them, sitting on a coil of rope with his bad leg out in front of him. Ina saw him, and saw that he was watching. Not anxious. Just watching. I can tell you where. The how’s yours.

Winter was at the front. Arms folded. One blue eye and one pink. She didn’t nod at him. She didn’t need to.

Ina stood in the middle of the platform with his hands empty, and the mask at his throat, and looked at Burr.

“I’m not going to tell you you’re wrong,” he said.

Burr’s eyes narrowed.

“You’re not,” said Ina. “I want to say that first. You’re right. Fire up here is the end of everything. One spark, and there’s no Highcrook by dark. You told us that, and you meant it, and you were right. I believe you.” He paused. “I’m also the last person in this town you should trust with one. Look at me.”

They looked at him. The horn. The white hair going purple at the ends. The mask, hanging at his throat on its red cords, grey, with its fanged grin and its red-rimmed eyes.

“Where I come from,” said Ina, “people are frightened of me. Not because of anything I’ve done. Because of what I look like, when I make fire.” He touched the mask. “On another floor, a long way down, I sat in a hut made of turf and feathers, with seven people who were made of feathers too. Who’d built their whole lives round keeping fire small, the way you’ve built yours round keeping it out. Their hearths had drowned in a storm, and they were dying of cold. And I made a fire for them. With this on. All night.” He was quiet. “They screamed. At me, and at it. They were right to. It was the most frightening thing they’d ever seen. I know, because I was frightened of it too.”

Nobody on the platform moved.

“And it burned all night,” said Ina. “In a hut made of turf and feathers. The storm took half the roof off. And not one feather caught. Not one strand of turf. It burned exactly where I put it, and nowhere else, and in the morning I put it out. And it went out.” He looked at Burr. “That’s what it is. That fire. It’s the one thing on any floor I’ve seen that wind can’t take, and rain can’t take. And nothing can put it out but me.” He paused. “Which means nothing can spread it but me, either.”

“You say,” said Burr.

“I say,” Ina agreed. “And you’ve no reason on any floor to take my word for it. So I’m not asking you to.” He reached down to his belt, and unhooked the coil of rope he’d brought. Grey, rough, bark-fibre. One of Burr’s. He’d checked it himself that morning, end to end, with his fingers, for soft places.

He tied one end round his own waist. A plain knot. Tight.

He held out the other end. To Burr.

“You’re the ropes,” said Ina. “So you hold this. At the foot of the north tree, in the daylight, the whole time. You stand there with it in your hand. And if you see one thing you don’t like, one spark, one wisp of smoke going anywhere it shouldn’t, one moment where you’re frightened, you pull.” He looked at Burr steadily. “You don’t have to say anything. You don’t have to explain. You pull, and it’s out. Before you’ve finished pulling. And I come up the rope, and I never ask again. Not you, not anyone in this town, not ever.”

Burr looked at the rope in Ina’s hand.

“Every second of it,” said Ina, “is yours. You decide. Not me. Not the tree. You.”

It was very quiet on the platform for a long time.

Then Old Linden spoke. From the trunk of the north tree, with her cheek against the bark and her blind eyes open.

“Burr,” she said. Very soft. Dry, like leaves.

He turned his head.

“I’ve listened to him for seventy years,” said Old Linden. “My grandmother listened to him for sixty. Hers before that. All my life, he’s been saying the same thing, and I thought it was help. I thought he was hurting. I thought it was the sawyers.” She moved her hand on the bark. “The little tree says it’s not help. The little tree says he’s been asking. And I can hear it now. Now I know what it is. I can hear it in him. Under everything. Asking.”

She turned her blind face toward Burr.

“He’s held your ropes up for three hundred years,” said Old Linden. “With every child he ever had. Don’t you think he’s owed one?”

Burr stood there on his platform in the dusk, with the whole of Highcrook watching him, and the foxfire coming on in the jars along the rails one by one, blue-green.

He looked at Maigo. At the little brown shape against the trunk in its orange hoodie, with the leaves on its antlers.

“Is that what he says?” said Burr. Rough. “Truly?”

“Truly,” said Maigo.

Burr looked down at the nets of stone-cones hanging off the edge of his platform.

Then he reached out with his two-fingered hand, and took the end of the rope.

They did it the next morning, in the full of the light, when the Down was as quiet as the Down ever got.

The whole of Highcrook came to the branches to watch. Ina could see them when he looked up, which he did once, from the moss at the foot of the north tree, and then didn’t again. Every branch of the seven. Every bridge. Every railing. Hundreds and hundreds of small grey shapes, very still, with their big eyes all turned down, all looking at the same place. At him. It was like being at the bottom of a well, with the whole sky leaning over the top of it, made of faces.

Burr stood five metres from him on the moss, holding the rope.

He hated it. You could see it. He hadn’t stood on the Down for any time at all since he was a boy, and he stood on it now the way you’d stand on thin ice. Very still. Knees soft. His big eyes going round and round the ferns at the edge of the cleared ground, as if something might come out of them. He had the rope in both hands. The whole one and the two-fingered one. He’d wound it twice round the two-fingered one, so it couldn’t slip.

And he’d brought the cone.

That was the thing Ina hadn’t expected. They’d come down the ropes in the grey early light, Chris and Niko and Winter and Maigo and him, with Burr last. And Burr had come down with something in a net slung over his shoulder. Heavy. And when he’d landed on the moss he’d taken it out, and held it in both hands, and looked at it for a long time, and then held it out. To Maigo.

A stone-cone. Old. Grey as ash. Its plates worn smooth at the edges, almost polished, the way a stone gets polished by a thousand hands.

“Off the drum,” Burr had said. “The oldest weight we’ve got. My grandmother’s grandmother’s. It’s held up more walkers than anything in Highcrook.” He’d pushed it into Maigo’s arms. “If we’re doing this, we’re doing it with one of ours.”

Maigo had stood there holding it, with his sunglasses on, and not said anything for a while.

“It’s awake,” he’d said, at last. Very quietly. “It’s still in there. Asleep. It’s been asleep for—“ He stopped. “It’s still in there.”

Chris and Niko had cleared the ground. They’d done it before dawn, from a rope, in the half dark, which had made Burr furious and then grudgingly impressed. A patch of moss at the foot of the north tree, not far from the mouth of the cave, scraped right down to the black earth underneath, a few metres across. Winter had found stones. There were stones, if you knew where to look, in the root-walls of the great trees that had fallen, where the roots had torn them up out of the deep. She’d dragged a dozen of them across in a net and ringed the bare earth with them.

A hearth. In the Down. At the foot of the north tree. The first in three hundred years.

Ina knelt by it.

He put his hands on the stones. They were cold and damp. He looked at the bare earth in the middle of the ring. Then he looked at Burr.

Burr looked back. Holding the rope.

Ina put the mask on.

He heard it before he saw it. Above him. The sound the whole of Highcrook made when the mask went down over his face. Not a scream. They were too far up for that, and too afraid. A sigh. Hundreds of people breathing in at once, and holding it. Like wind through leaves, all at once, and then stopping.

He didn’t look up.

He put his hands out over the bare earth inside the ring of stones, and let it come.

It came the way it always came now. Not lit. Came. Up out of his chest and down his arms and out through his palms, and white. White as a star, with blue at the edges, standing up out of the black earth like a flower, not flickering, not bending, so bright that the whole foot of the north tree leapt up out of the green dusk. Every ridge of the bark. Every fern at the edge of the clearing. Every face, up there, in every branch, all turned down, white in its light.

The ferns at the edge of the cleared ground leaned away from it.

He saw that. He didn’t know what it meant yet. He filed it.

Burr’s hands had gone tight on the rope.

Ina knelt there with the white fire in front of him and the mask on, and the red-rimmed eyes of it looking out, and the fangs, and the horn. And he could feel them. All of them. Up there. Frightened of him. The way the kite-folk had been frightened, and Larchwick’s dogs, and a child on floor 2 who’d screamed and run. He knew that feeling. He’d known it all his life, since long before any tower. He’d spent most of it being charming so nobody would have to feel it.

On floor 5, he’d held the fire all night without a word. Because his voice through the mask had made it worse.

He thought about that. Kneeling there. He thought about it for quite a long time.

And then he spoke.

“Three hundred years ago,” said Ina.

His voice came out through the mask the way it always did. Lower. Rougher. With something in it that wasn’t quite his, a growl at the bottom, like a big animal behind a door. He heard the whole of Highcrook flinch. He kept going.

“Three hundred years ago,” he said, not loudly, because under the leaves you didn’t need to be loud, “some people came up your ropes. Like us. Walkers. With lights at their shoulders. And they counted themselves every morning at the bottom of the ropes, and said nobody left down. You know that. You still say it.”

Silence. Up there. Absolute.

“And one night,” said Ina, “they made a fire. Down here. At the foot of the north tree. Just an ordinary one. Small. Orange. The kind you sit by.” He looked at the white fire in front of him. “And your grandmothers’ grandmothers’ grandmothers sat up all night, up there, where you are now, and watched it. Frightened. The way you’re frightened now. Waiting for the end of everything.”

He paused.

“And nothing burned,” said Ina. “And in the morning the walkers went away up. And in the spring, where their fire had been, something came up out of the ground.” He didn’t point. He didn’t need to. Every one of them up there knew which way it was. “You’ve been calling it the Youngest for three hundred years. You never knew where it came from.” His voice through the mask was very quiet. “It came from here.”

Somebody up there made a sound. A small one. A child.

“The north tree’s been asking for another one ever since,” said Ina. “That’s all. That’s all this is. That’s all I’m doing. I’m not doing anything to your town. I’m doing something for your tree. The one that’s been holding up your ropes.”

He held out his hand to Maigo, without looking round.

Maigo put the cone in it.

He set it in the fire.

Right in the heart of it. Where the white was whitest. He put it down in the middle of the flame with his bare hand, which didn’t burn, because it never did, not his own fire, and he took his hand away, and the cone sat there in the white light, grey and old and shut.

Nothing happened.

For a long time, nothing happened. The fire burned. The cone sat in it. Ina knelt in front of it with the mask on, and Burr stood with the rope, and the whole of Highcrook held its breath up in the leaves, and the cone did nothing at all. Five minutes. Ten.

“Ina,” said Chris, very quietly, behind him.

“I know,” said Ina.

He fed it.

Not with wood. There wasn’t any. With himself. The way he’d learned on the Kitecliff, kneeling by a drowned hearth. He leaned into it, and gave it more. The white went whiter. The blue at its edges went up. The heat came off it in a wall, and the cleared earth round the stones began to smoke, and Chris took a step back, and Niko took two, and Burr—

Burr didn’t pull. Ina felt the rope at his waist go tight, and stay tight, and not pull.

The cone began to sweat.

Something came out between the plates. Pale. Gold. Shining. It came out in beads, along every seam where one plate lay over another, all over the cone, at once, and ran down, and hissed in the white. Resin. Three hundred years of it. Sealing the cone shut. Melting.

And then it cracked.

A small sound. Like a knuckle. One plate, near the top, lifting. A hair’s width. Then another. Crack. And another. Like somebody cracking their knuckles very slowly, one by one, in a quiet room. Crack. Crack. All up the cone, round and round it, plate after plate, opening. Lifting. Like a hand unclenching after a very long time.

Up in the leaves, all of Highcrook breathed out at once.

Ina heard it. A long soft sound, from everywhere, like wind. And under it, from somewhere very high, a single whistle. Two notes. High, then low. Tansy.

The cone stood open in the white fire, every plate lifted, like a grey flower.

And out of the gaps between them, the seeds came.

They were small. Smaller than a fingernail. Each one dark, with a single long thin wing, gold, like a sliver of mica. They came out of the cone in the heat and lifted. Up. On the air off the fire. Dozens of them, spinning, gold, going round and round slowly in the white light, rising, the way sycamore seeds spin on Earth when you throw them off a bridge.

And then falling. Down, past the fire. Into the black smoking earth inside the ring of stones.

“Ina,” said Maigo.

Ina put it out.

It went out the way it always did. Gently. Like a lamp turned down. The white sank into the earth and was gone, and the clearing went back to green dusk all at once, and the heat went out of the air, and there was nothing in the ring of stones but black earth and grey ash and the open cone sitting in the middle of it, smoking, and the seeds lying in the ash all round it like gold dust.

He pushed the mask up. His face was wet under it. He didn’t know when that had happened.

Maigo was already kneeling at the edge of the stones.

He didn’t take one at random. He looked at them for a long time with his head on one side, the way he’d looked at the cone. And then he reached into the warm ash with one small bark-brown hand, and picked up a single seed, and held it.

“This one’s awake,” he said.

He dug a hole in the ash with his finger. Not deep. He put the seed in it and closed the ash over it. Then he sat down, cross-legged, at the edge of the ring, and took off his one brown shoe. And put both feet in the ash. The shoe foot and the root-foot. Flat.

Put your feet down. Drink from what’s under you. Give it away.

His roots went out into the ash. Ina saw them go. Fine white threads out from his toes, through the grey, toward the place where the seed was. And Maigo shut his eyes behind his sunglasses and went very still. And gave it away.

They waited.

Floor 9 didn’t make you wait long. Not for growing. Ina had watched a fern unroll on the first day while he stood there. And in the ash, in the middle of the ring of stones, after what was perhaps a minute, the grey moved.

A root came out first. Pale. Thin as a hair. It came up out of the ash and turned, and went back down, the way roots do. Then the ash heaved, a little, the size of a thumbnail. And out of it came a stem. A thin red-brown stem, bent over like a hook, like a neck. It straightened. Slowly. While they watched. And at the top of it, folded tight together like two hands pressed in prayer, two leaves.

They opened.

Two small green leaves, in the grey ash, at the foot of the north tree. And then two more, from between them, smaller, paler, unfolding.

Maigo opened his eyes.

Ina saw Maigo read the third window. He saw him read it twice. And he saw Maigo’s face, under the sunglasses, do something he didn’t have a name for, and then put it away for later, the way Maigo put things away.

Then Maigo looked at the seedling and grinned. The old grin. The cocky sapling’s grin, from before any tower.

“Hi,” said Maigo. To the seedling. “I’m Maigo. You’re going to be enormous.”

Old Linden had come down.

Nobody had seen her do it. She’d made Burr’s people let her down in a basket after the fire went out, a blind old woman who hadn’t been on the Down since she was a child, and she was standing at the foot of the north tree now, a little way from the stones, with both hands flat on the bark and her cheek pressed against it.

She stood there a long time.

“Linden?” said Maigo.

“He’s stopped,” said Old Linden.

Her voice had gone strange. Ina looked at her. She was crying, without any sound, with her blind eyes open and her cheek against the bark of the north tree.

“Seventy years,” said Old Linden. “Every day. He’s never stopped. Not once. Not one day in my life, or my grandmother’s.” She pressed her hand flat. “He’s stopped asking.”

“What’s he saying?” said Maigo. “Now?”

She listened. For a long time.

“Nothing,” she said. “He’s just—“ She stopped. “He’s just looking at it.”

The ferns didn’t come back.

Chris noticed. Chris noticed everything. The Grow had been ticking all round the cleared ground the whole time, the way it always did, faint in the day. Leaning in. And it leaned in now, while they sat round the ring of stones watching a seedling grow. Fronds unrolling at the edge of the black earth. Reaching. Briar feeling its way across the moss toward the stones.

And at the edge of the ash, it stopped.

Not at the stones. At the ash. Where the earth had been scorched by the white fire, black and grey, a hand’s width out from the ring all round, the Grow came up to the edge of it and stopped. The fern-tips touched it and curled back. The briar went along the edge, round, and wouldn’t go across.

“Huh,” said Chris.

He watched it for a long time. Then he took out his book and wrote one word in it, in capitals, and underlined it twice, and showed nobody what it was.

Ina didn’t need him to.

He remembered the night of the split. Seven rooms of five, and walls of briar going up round them in the full of the afternoon. He’d stood in his room with the white fire already halfway up his arms, and Chris had put a hand on his shoulder and said no. You don’t light a fire in a forest you can’t see the edge of. And he’d been right. He’d been absolutely right.

But they could see the edge of this one.

Cut it, it grows back twice.

Burn it, though.

Burr hadn’t let go of the rope.

He was standing exactly where he’d been standing at the start, five metres off on the moss, with the rope wound twice round his two-fingered hand. He’d been standing there the whole time. He was looking at the seedling. Then at Ina.

“I didn’t pull,” said Burr.

“I noticed,” said Ina.

Burr looked at the rope in his hand as if he didn’t know how it had got there. Then he walked across the moss, slowly, to where Ina was sitting by the stones, and held out the end of it.

Ina untied the knot at his waist and took it.

“Three hundred years,” said Burr. “My grandmother’s grandmother’s grandmother. They used to say the walkers left the Youngest. Like a gift. On purpose.” He looked away to the north, through the trunks, toward it. “We thought it was magic. We thought walkers could make trees.” He shook his head. “We never knew the tree did it. We never knew they’d only made a fire.”

“I don’t think they knew either,” said Ina.

“No,” said Burr. He looked at Maigo, sitting in the ash with his feet in it, talking quietly to a seedling. “No. I don’t suppose they did.”

He was quiet for a while.

“There’s a lot of cones,” said Burr, eventually. Rough. Not looking at anyone. “On the drums. In the doorways. In the Down. Three hundred years of them.”

“There are,” said Ina.

“Not all at once,” said Burr.

“No.”

“And not up there. Never up there.”

“Never,” said Ina.

“And I hold the rope.”

“You always hold the rope,” said Ina.

Burr grunted. He looked at the seedling one more time. Then he turned round, and went back across the moss to the foot of the rope he’d come down, and stood there, waiting to be pulled up, with his back very straight and his arms folded.

Winter sat down next to him on the stones.

She didn’t say anything for a while. She’d been standing at the edge of the clearing the whole time with her bow strung, watching the ferns, in case. She put it down now across her knees.

“That’s a fact,” said Winter.

He looked at her.

“For the page,” she said. “They made a fire at the foot of the north tree, and the Youngest came up out of it. Not a theory. Not a guess. Burr’s story, and Maigo’s rings, and that.” She nodded at the seedling. “Three things that say the same thing. That’s a fact.” She almost smiled. “We’ve got one.”

“Congratulations,” said Ina.

“Thank you.”

They sat there. Across the clearing, at the foot of the rope, somebody else had come down. Ice. Slowly, the way he did everything at the moment, with his bad leg. He came across the moss with Gabe a step behind him, not helping, just there. And he stopped at the edge of the ring of stones, and looked down at the seedling in the ash.

He looked at it for a long time.

Ina watched him. He’d been watching Ice look at things on this floor since the top of the stair. Everyone had. The way he looked at the trees, and the light, and the bough-folk, and the foxfire, as if each of them was something he’d walked past a hundred times without seeing, and was seeing now for the first time, and couldn’t get over it.

“I never saw one of these,” said Ice. Very quietly. To nobody. “Not once.”

He crouched down, carefully, with his leg out to one side, and looked at the four small leaves at the level of his eyes.

Not once. Ina turned that over. Never saw one. Not I’ve never seen one. As if there’d been a time, a long time, a lot of chances, to see one. And he hadn’t.

He looked at Winter.

Winter was looking at the seedling. Very carefully. Not at Ice. Not at him.

And Ina understood, the way you understand the shape of a room in the dark from the sound of your own voice in it, that there was something there. Right there. In those two words. That if he picked at them, they’d come apart in his hands, and he’d know.

He didn’t pick at them.

He sat on the stones in the green dusk at the foot of the north tree, with the mask pushed up on his head and the end of Burr’s rope in his lap, and put the two words down, very gently, the way you’d put down something that wasn’t yours. And left them there.

You wait on the step.

“It’s going to be enormous,” said Maigo, to the seedling, from the ash.

“Yeah,” said Ice. And he laughed, very quietly, under his breath, crouching in the Down beside it. “Yeah, Maigotron. It is.”

Up in the leaves, a long way above, Highcrook had started whistling. All of it. Every branch. High and low and high, from tree to tree to tree, the way they did at dusk when the last ropes came up. Except that it wasn’t dusk. It was the middle of the morning, and the ropes were all still down, and nobody had told them to.

Ina learned the ropes for two days. No fire. The counterweights are stone-cones. “The north tree’s been holding up your ropes. With its children.”
Ina asked. He tied a rope round his waist and gave Burr the end. “Pull, and it’s out.”
Linden: “He’s held your ropes up for three hundred years. Don’t you think he’s owed one?” Burr took the rope.
Burr brought the cone. His grandmother’s grandmother’s. The oldest weight on the drum.
Ina put the mask on, and talked through it. The whole of Highcrook listened.
The cone opened. Gold seeds. Maigo planted the one that was awake. Four leaves.
The Old Wood (1 of 3), complete. Maigo: Heartwood. Next: something grows here that was never planted. Find its root.
Linden: “He’s stopped asking. He’s just looking at it.”
The Grow won’t cross the ash. Chris wrote one word.
Burr didn’t pull. “Not all at once. Never up there. And I hold the rope.”
Winter: “That’s a fact.”
