Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 2: One Floor Ahead

Chapter 79

Ok

4,359 words · 19 min read

The window came up on its own, the way they did. Small. Quiet. Right in front of his face, between his eyes and the blue door, at the top of everything.

[ Whisper · Voyager: ok ]

Gabe read it.

Two letters. Lowercase. No full stop.

He read it again. His hand was still flat on the blue wood of the door. He could feel the grain of it under his palm, and the warmth coming through from the oven on the other side, and his own heart going in his wrist.

ok.

dec. i know its you. im not mad. ok im a bit mad. That was what he’d written, on the second day, in a stone room in Hearthstead, sitting on the end of a bed he couldn’t sleep in. And then, further down, after a long time staring at the wall: just tell me youre ok. one word. please.

One word.

He’d asked for one word. On the second day. A hundred and fifty-one days ago.

And here it was.

His legs did something. He felt them go, a little, at the knees. He put his other hand on the door too, both hands flat on the blue wood. He stood there on the stone step outside a bakery on Lantern Street with his forehead nearly touching the door, with thirty-four people behind him in the narrow street holding their breath. And Deston’s gold thread, he could feel it, warm, running from his back to wherever Deston was standing. And the window floating in front of his face, two letters, ok.

He thought he might be sick. He thought he might laugh. He didn’t know which.

He pushed the door open.

The bell went over his head. A little brass bell on a curl of iron, ringing. And the smell came out at him, all of it, all at once. Bread and rosemary and an oven roaring and flour in the air. And warmth. After the cool shadow of the street, it was like walking into a hug.

It was a small shop. A counter across the middle. Shelves behind it full of loaves. A bow window full of light. And behind the counter, a woman with flour on her arms and her hair tied up in a cloth and a lot of freckles, standing very still, looking at him.

She didn’t say anything. She looked at him for a moment. Then she smiled, quick and sideways, and stepped back. Out of the way. Away from the door behind her, at the back of the shop. The kitchen door.

It was open.

Gabe hadn’t seen it open. It must have been, just now, while he was reading. He was looking at it now. Through the gap behind the counter, at a doorway into a kitchen full of orange light from the oven, and flour hanging in the air in the light like dust in a sunbeam.

And somebody standing in it.

He’d thought about this moment for a hundred and fifty-three days. He’d thought about it in Hearthstead, and Larchwick, and Reedmoor, and the Red City, and on a lip of a pit in a desert. He’d thought about what he’d say. What he’d do. What Declan would look like. He’d imagined it a thousand times.

He hadn’t imagined the flour.

Declan was standing in the doorway of the kitchen with flour on him. On his hands, up to the wrists. On the front of a black vest with teal tabs. In his hair, a little, a white streak of it in the black, next to the teal one. On his face, a smudge of it, on one cheek. As if he’d been kneading bread when the bell went, and had just stopped, and stood up, and come to the door, and hadn’t thought to wipe his hands.

Black hair, messy, falling over his eyes. The teal streak through the fringe on the right side. Teal eyes. A white shirt with the sleeves rolled to the elbow, under the vest. Black trousers, straps round the thighs, tall boots. It was the outfit from his streams. The character from his emotes. Gabe had seen it a thousand times in the corner of a screen, in a little square, waving.

It was the face that got him.

It was Declan’s face. Of course it was. The face in the white room, for one second, before the lights came up between them. Dec—

But it was thinner. A lot thinner. There were shadows under the teal eyes that Gabe had never seen there, not once in eleven years. There was something round the mouth, too. Something tight, and careful, and held. As if he’d been holding his face very still for a very long time and had forgotten how to let it go.

And he was older.

Gabe couldn’t have said how. It wasn’t lines, or grey hair, or anything you could point at. He was twenty-six. He looked twenty-six. But the way he was standing in the kitchen doorway, very still, with his floury hands at his sides, looking at Gabe. The way he was looking. It was the way old men looked at photographs. At people in photographs. As if he was seeing someone he’d lost a very long time ago, and couldn’t believe was standing in front of him.

Declan didn’t say anything.

He just stood there in the doorway and looked at Gabe. And Gabe saw him do something. Something small. He saw Declan’s mouth open, and then close again. He saw him take a breath. He saw him stop. For one second, two, standing in the doorway, before he spoke. Like a man at the edge of cold water.

Declan had never paused before talking in his life. Not once. Not in eleven years. He’d always talked first and thought about it afterwards. He’d always been the first in any room to say anything.

He paused now.

And then he said it.

“It’s me,” said Declan. His voice was rough. “Declan.”

Gabe hit him.

He didn’t decide to. He’d decided a hundred times, on a hundred floors, that he would, and then decided a hundred times that he wouldn’t. In the end his body decided for him, the way it decided on the Kitecliff when to lean. He went across the shop in three strides, round the end of the counter, past the baker, who didn’t move to stop him. And he put his right fist into the side of Declan’s face with everything he had.

He saw Declan see it coming.

That was the thing. That was the thing he’d think about afterwards, for a long time. Declan saw it. Gabe saw his eyes go to the fist the moment it started. He saw something in his whole body know, the way Gabe’s body knew now how to lean on the wind. The tank in him. The thing that had stood in the Kiln’s fire twice, and walked through the beams of the Prism at noon, and put one thrust into a speck of glass in a hole in the air. Gabe saw all of it see the punch coming, and know exactly how to turn it, or catch it, or step inside it.

And not move.

He just stood there in the kitchen doorway with his floury hands at his sides and let it land.

It landed. Gabe felt it go into his knuckles, all the way up his arm. Declan’s head went sideways. And then all of him went sideways. Backward, off his feet, into the kitchen, into a stack of flour sacks against the wall. The sacks went over. The flour came up. A great white cloud of it, filling the kitchen, billowing out through the doorway into the shop. And for a second Gabe couldn’t see anything at all.

He stood in the doorway in a cloud of flour, with his fist still up, breathing hard.

The flour settled.

Declan was sitting on the kitchen floor, among the burst sacks, with his back against the wall. White from head to foot. His hair white, his vest white, his face white except for a red mark coming up on the left side of his jaw. He had one hand up to it. He was looking up at Gabe through the flour.

And he started to laugh.

It came out of him the way it always had. Sudden. Helpless. Before he could stop it. Gabe had heard it in a hundred calls, across a hundred tables. In a second row, with a teacher looking. In Malacca, on a doorstep. He sat on the floor of a bakery kitchen covered in flour with his jaw going red and laughed. With his head back against the wall and his eyes shut, and tears coming out of them and making tracks in the flour.

“ONE WORD,” said Gabe.

His voice came out of him so loud it rattled the loaves on the shelves.

“You said one word,” said Declan, through the laughing. “You asked for one word.”

“A hundred and fifty-three days.” Gabe was shaking. All of him. “A hundred and fifty-three days, you absolute—you idiot, you stupid—for ONE WORD—“

“It was the word you asked for.”

“I KNOW it was the word I asked for!”

“Then I don’t see what you’re—“

Gabe went down on his knees in the flour.

He didn’t decide that either. He just went down, in the burst flour sacks, in front of Declan. He grabbed him. Both arms, round him, round the floury vest, and pulled him off the wall into his chest. And held on. Hard. So hard he felt something in Declan’s back give a little, and didn’t care. He held on with his face in Declan’s floury hair, and his shoulders heaving, and the flour going up round both of them again in a cloud.

And after a second, Declan’s arms came up round him too.

Slowly. Like the heaviest thing he’d ever lifted.

They knelt there on the floor of the kitchen in the flour and held on to each other, and neither of them said anything at all for a very long time.

“You’re not ok,” said Gabe, eventually. Into his hair. His voice was wrecked.

“No,” said Declan.

“You said ok.”

“I know.”

“You’re not even a bit ok. Look at you. You’re—“ Gabe pulled back, enough to look at him. At the shadows under his eyes, and the thinness, and the red mark on his jaw coming up through the flour. “You’re skin and bone, Dec. You look like you haven’t slept since the white room.”

“I haven’t, much.”

“Why—“

“I’m here,” said Declan.

He said it very quietly. With his floury hands on Gabe’s arms.

“I’m not ok,” he said. “You’re right. I’m not. But I’m here.” He swallowed. “That’s what the word meant. That’s all it meant. I’m here. I couldn’t—I couldn’t say the rest yet. But I could say that.”

Gabe looked at him.

It’s still going, you know. It’s still sending messages back. Every day. Just numbers. Just “I’m still here.”

“Still here,” said Gabe.

Declan’s face did something. “Xav told you.”

“Xav told me. On four.” Gabe wiped his face with the back of his wrist and got flour in his eyes and didn’t care. “Still here. Every floor. The coat and the lantern and the thing on the roof and the—the onion skins, Dec, on the corks, Tiger cried—“

“I heard.” Declan’s mouth twitched. “Alea told me. She said Tiger was going to cry. Before you found them.”

“Who’s Alea?”

Declan looked at him for a second. Then, very slightly, he looked at his own right shoulder. At the empty air there.

Gabe didn’t see anything. But he saw Declan look. And he saw Declan’s face go soft, the way it used to go soft over a game character, a pet, a horse, anything he’d named.

“It’s called Alea,” said Gabe. “Course it is. Leaka said. Course it is.”

And Declan laughed again. Wetly. With his forehead on Gabe’s shoulder.

The bell went over the shop door.

And again. And again. And again.

Gabe heard it from the floor of the kitchen, with Declan’s forehead on his shoulder. The little brass bell going and going, without stopping, as if somebody had tied it to something. And footsteps. Lots of them. The shop filling up. The counter getting pushed. Somebody saying sorry, sorry to the baker, who was saying it’s fine, mind the rolls. And voices. All of them, at once, piling into a shop that was built for six.

Calvin was first. Of course Calvin was first. He came round the end of the counter and into the kitchen doorway and stopped, and looked at the two of them on the floor in the flour. Then he made a noise like a kettle and sat down, right where he was, in the doorway, and put his hands over his face.

Then Rachael. She stood over them with her frown. She looked at Declan for a long, long time. Then, without saying anything, she knelt down in the flour and took his left hand, and turned it over, and looked at the palm. At the long raised line across it.

“Six?” she said. Very quietly.

“Six,” said Declan.

Rachael didn’t say anything else. She just put her own hand over it, flat, and Gabe saw something cold and blue go into the scar. It didn’t go away. It didn’t change. But Declan’s whole face did, a little, as if something that had been aching for a long time had stopped.

Then Deston. Deston didn’t say anything either. He came into the kitchen and knelt down behind Declan and put his hand on the top of Declan’s head, in the floury hair. Heavy and warm. And left it there. Gabe saw the gold come out of his palm, thin and fine. And he saw Declan feel it, and close his eyes.

“Dest,” said Declan.

“Second row,” said Deston. His voice was barely there.

Then everybody.

Gabe couldn’t keep track of it. Nobody could. Thirty-four people trying to get into a kitchen built for two. Flour going everywhere. People crying, people laughing, people saying his name. Declan, from the ones who’d known him in person. Ice, from the others. Over and over, in thirty different voices.

Dennis, from the counter: “She charged me double.”

“I told her to.”

Hyvred, in capitals, from somewhere: “YOUR LIGHT. YOUR LIGHT’S SO BIG. IT’S SO BIG RIGHT NOW—”

Hana, at the edge of the doorway, with her crown on and her hand over her mouth. And Declan looked up at her from the floor, and said one word. “Alice.” And Hana’s face broke completely. Milli caught her before she sat down.

Jared, behind Hana, steady: “You owe me a postcard.”

“I know. I’ve owed you a postcard for years.”

“Years, man.”

Aleaka pushed through, with Sleet at her heels. She knelt down in the flour in front of him with her two fox ears flat. She looked at him for a long time. Then at his right shoulder, at the empty air.

“You made it a fox,” she said.

Declan looked at her. “Leaka—“

“It’s called Alea,” said Aleaka. “Isn’t it.”

He was quiet for a second.

“Yeah, Leaka,” he said. “It’s called Alea.”

Aleaka put her hands over her face and laughed and cried at the same time.

Niko, at the back, small and still. Declan saw him over all their heads. And his face did something, and he said, quite clearly, across the whole crowded kitchen: “Mashi.” And Niko stood very still, and then turned round and went out into the shop, and Gabe heard him crying out there, quietly, by the window.

“Maigotron,” said Declan, to a tree person in an orange hoodie by the oven. Maigo, who never said anything, said “Hi,” and his voice cracked in the middle.

“RX.” Rui Xiang, leaning on the doorframe, knife going over his knuckles. The knife stopped.

Malcolm, from somewhere: “Is anything broken? I’ll do two.”

“My jaw, a bit.”

“Good,” said Gabe.

Tiger came through the crowd sideways with something in his hand, and held it up in front of Declan’s face. A thin brown curl. Papery. An onion skin, peeled all the way round in one go. Tiger didn’t say anything. He didn’t have to. Declan looked at it and had to look away.

Newish, from the back, very loudly: “You’re rude.”

And Declan, on the floor, covered in flour, with his jaw going red, said: “Love you guys.” He paused. “Except Newish.”

And the whole kitchen laughed. All of them. Thirty-four people at once, in a space built for two, so loud the loaves rattled on the shelves again.

Gabe laughed too. He couldn’t help it. He was kneeling in the flour with his arm still round Declan’s shoulders and he couldn’t stop.

And then it went quiet.

Not all at once. In a wave. From the back of the shop forward. People stopping talking, one after another, and turning round. Gabe felt it come through the crowd before he saw why. A sort of hush.

Thea was in the doorway.

Not the kitchen doorway. The shop door. The blue one, onto Lantern Street. She was standing on the step with the morning shadow behind her and the lantern in her hand. She was the last one. She hadn’t come in. She was just standing there in the doorway, looking through the shop, past the counter, past the crowd, into the kitchen. At him.

The crowd moved. Gabe didn’t see anybody decide. It just opened, the way it had opened at the inn door that morning for him and Deston. A gap, from the shop door to the kitchen, through thirty-three people. So that there was nothing between her and Declan but a counter and some flour in the air.

She didn’t come in.

She stood in the doorway with the lantern. Declan, on the floor of the kitchen with Gabe’s arm round him, looked up at her.

Nobody said anything.

Gabe watched Declan’s eyes go to her shoulder. Her left shoulder, under the jersey. Where Gabe knew there was a scar, long and pale, that Xola couldn’t heal. He watched Declan look at it, through the jersey, as if he could see it. And he felt Declan’s whole body go very still against his arm.

And he watched Thea’s eyes go to Declan’s hand. His left hand, open on his knee in the flour. The palm turned up. The long raised line across it.

Neither of them said anything.

Thea looked at him for a long time. Her face was very calm. The calm she had when she was reading something nobody else could read. And then she nodded. Once. Very slightly.

Declan nodded back.

That was all. Thea stood in the doorway, and didn’t come in. And after a moment the crowd closed again, slowly, and the noise came back up. But Gabe felt Declan’s shoulders, under his arm. They didn’t come down for a long time.

When it had got a bit quieter, Declan put his hand into the air in front of him. Into his inventory, the way you did. And took something out.

It was an ingot. About the size of a hand. Dark red. Heavy. Gabe saw that from the way Declan held it, with both hands, as if it might fall. And it was warm. Gabe could feel the warmth coming off it from where he knelt. And inside it, deep inside, something was moving. Slowly. Like a coal at the bottom of a grate.

Declan held it out to him.

“Floor 4,” he said. “The Kiln. First clear. Day forty-four.” He was looking at the ingot, not at Gabe. “Kiln-heart Iron. It’s a smith’s metal. You spend years looking for one piece.” He swallowed. “I’ve had it in my pack since day forty-four. Right at the back. I wasn’t putting this one on a bench.” He looked up. “I wanted to give it to you by hand.”

Gabe looked at it. At the dark red metal and the coal moving inside it.

He took it. It was heavier than it looked. Warm all the way through, like something alive.

“You stood in the fire for this,” he said.

Declan didn’t answer.

“Twice,” said Gabe. “We know. We saw.” His voice had gone rough again. “There’s a shape on the floor. In the furnace hall. In front of the dead ones. Where you stood. Everything else round it went black, like glass. And there’s a shape in the middle that didn’t burn. A man. With a shield.” He looked at the ingot. “Two of them. One on top of the other. You stood in the same place both times.”

Declan was looking at him very strangely.

“I stood in it,” said Gabe. “When we did it. All of us. I asked Drew if I could stand where you stood. I fitted. Nearly.” He almost laughed. “You’re skinnier.”

Declan didn’t say anything at all. He’d put his floury hand over his mouth.

So Gabe put the Kiln-heart Iron down on the floor beside him, very carefully. And he reached back over his shoulder, and undid the straps. The straps he’d had across his back since floor 3, since a forge in Reedmoor. He’d carried them through the Red City and the Reaches and the Glass. And he swung it round, off his back, into his hands. And held it out.

Round. Dark grey Fen iron, with a sheen like a puddle at dusk. A lamp in the middle, a little round boss shaped like a lamp. Lines going out from it, fine as hair, like the lines you draw round a sun. And round the edge, a rim of dark red iron with something in it still, faint, like the last ember in a grate.

“This was never mine,” said Gabe.

Declan looked at it.

“I started it on three,” said Gabe. “In Reedmoor. The night after the Lanternjaw, when we found your lantern on the bench. I didn’t know what I was making. I just wanted to make something.” He held it out further. “Azac said it was a shield for somebody who wasn’t there yet. Otta told us on five you’d lost yours. To a bird. That you’d been using barrel lids.” His voice cracked. “So we put the rim on. Me and Jared. At Otta’s forge. Kiln iron. From the thing you stood in front of.”

Declan reached out and took it.

Gabe watched him hold it. Watched his floury hands go round the rim, and turn it over, slowly, and look at the inside. And stop. At a place on the inside of the rim, right where a hand would grip. A tiny shape, worked into the dark red iron. Five petals and a dot.

“That bit’s mine,” said Jared, from the doorway.

Declan laughed. It came out of him cracked and wet and helpless. He put his arm through the straps, and his hand round the grip, over the little iron rose. And the shield sat on his arm.

It sat the way a shield sits when it’s been made for an arm. Gabe saw it. He saw Declan feel it. He saw his whole face change, as if something he’d lost a long time ago had come back.

“It fits,” said Declan. Very quietly.

“Course it fits,” said Gabe. “I know your arm.” He wiped his face. “I’ve known your arm since I was thirteen.”

Declan looked down at the shield on his arm. At the lamp in the middle. At the sun-lines going out from it.

“I lost the first one,” he said. “On five. The bird took it off the Spindle. I’d had it since the first day.” He swallowed. “I thought I’d never have one that fitted like that again.”

“Well,” said Gabe. “Now you’ve got one.”

Declan looked at him for a long time.

Then he leaned forward, with the shield on his arm, and put his forehead against Gabe’s. The way they’d done once, when they were fifteen and thirteen, in a second row, the night of a concert, because they were both terrified and neither of them would say so.

“Thank you,” said Declan.

“Shut up,” said Gabe.

They stayed like that.

“I owe you a thousand words,” said Declan, eventually. He’d sat back against the wall, with the shield across his knees and the Kiln-heart Iron in Gabe’s lap. “All of you.” He looked round the kitchen, at the crowd in the doorway, at thirty-four faces and the baker behind the counter. “I said I would. On floor 2, under a pine. Ok, and then a thousand other words.” He swallowed. “Tonight. In the square. I’ll tell you everything I can.”

Everything I can. Gabe heard it. He didn’t know what it meant. He saw Thea’s face, though, in the shop door, at can. And Xav’s, beside her.

“You owe me a thousand and one,” said Gabe.

“Why a thousand and one?”

“Because I’m going to make you say sorry separately.”

Declan laughed. “That’s fair.”

And from behind the counter, very calmly, over all of it, the baker said:

“The rolls are burning.”
