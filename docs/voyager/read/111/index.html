Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 3: Party of One

Chapter 111

The Count

3,876 words · 17 min read

Chi had never been the brave one. She’d been the calm one for so long now that she’d almost stopped noticing the difference.

On floor 10, she’d started noticing it again.

It was the cold. Not the cold itself; she didn’t mind cold. Waddles loved it. He’d belly-slid down every snowy street in the Bastion on the first day, shrieking, with hold-folk children running after him in a crowd, and now he had a following. Every morning a line of small grey-eyed children waited outside the barracks door for him to come out. No. It wasn’t the cold. It was what came out of it. Every night, for twenty-two nights, the deep bell on the fifth wall, and the cracking up the pass, and the Rimed coming down out of the dark. And every night Chi standing in the line with her sword and her shield and her heart going so fast she could feel it in her teeth.

She was frightened every night. She’d never told anybody. Nobody would have believed her. On the wall she was the one who said breathe to the hold-folk boy beside her, slowly, the way she’d said it to chat when chat got ugly, years ago, in another life. Breathe. Slowly. It’s only coming. And he breathed. And so, after a while, did she.

That was the trick. It had always been the trick. You were calm at somebody, and then you were calm.

She’d never had to do it at the tower before.

The Long Night had been five nights ago. Nobody had fallen. The hold-folk were still talking about it in the long halls, in low wondering voices, the way you talk about weather nobody’s ever seen. Not one. On the Long Night. Not one. Brenna had walked the length of the fifth wall the morning after with her shield on her back, very slowly, looking at every face in the line, and hadn’t said anything at all.

And then the Winter stopped.

That was the strange thing. Nobody understood it. The snow kept falling. The wind kept coming down the pass from the high side, hard and cold, every night. But the Rimed didn’t come. Not on the first night after the Long Night, or the second, or the third, or the fourth. The deep bell on the fifth wall stayed silent. The line stood on the parapet all night in the snow with its shields up and nothing came, and in the morning the slope below the wall was clean.

“It’s never stopped,” said Brenna. “Not before spring. Not once. Not in nine hundred years.”

“Maybe it’s tired,” said Calvin.

Brenna looked at him for a long time.

“The Winter,” she said, “is not tired.”

On the fifth morning, Chi was on the fifth wall at dawn with the end of the night watch, leaning on her shield, with Waddles asleep on her feet to keep them warm. Thea was beside her with the lantern. Drew was further along, talking to a hold-folk sergeant, low. The sky over the low end of the pass was going from grey to gold.

And something came down the pass.

Chi saw it first. She’d think about that afterwards. She was the one looking up the pass, at the high side, because she’d been looking at it all night out of habit, the way you keep looking at the door after somebody’s left.

It came down the middle of the pass, out of the throat of rock where the high side went up into the mountains. In daylight. Alone. Walking.

It was tall. Taller than the hold-folk. Taller than the Rimed. Thin, and very straight, the way a person stands when they’ve been told to stand straight and have never once forgotten. It wore a long grey coat that came to its feet, buttoned all the way down, with a high collar, and over the collar a hood. And it carried something in its arms, against its chest. A book. Big and flat and bound in grey.

It walked down the pass through the snow. And Chi watched it, and after a while she understood what was wrong with it.

It wasn’t leaving footprints.

The snow on the high side was deep. Knee deep, where the wind had piled it. The Rimed broke through it like boats through water. This walked on top. Not floating. Walking. Its feet came down on the snow and went up again and the snow under them stayed smooth and white and unbroken, as if nothing had touched it.

“Thea,” said Chi. Very slowly.

Thea looked up. She went still.

Further along the wall, Drew had stopped talking to the sergeant. All along the line, one by one, people were turning their heads. Hold-folk. Climbers. Looking up the pass. At the tall grey thing walking down it in the dawn with its book, leaving nothing behind it in the snow.

It stopped at the foot of the slope below the fifth wall. Thirty metres down. Right in the middle of the pass, where the Rimed always came. It stood there with its book against its chest, very straight.

Then it lifted its head.

Under the hood there was no face.

Not a hollow, like the Rimed’s helmets. Not a dark. A face-shaped smoothness. White. Like the Quiet, on floor 8, in the trial, the white thing that had come for Calvin first. Like the Sower, kneeling in its circle. Smooth and pale and calm and nothing.

A window came up in front of Chi’s face.

[ The Steward ]

Beside her, very quietly, Thea said: “Oh.”

Then it spoke.

Chi had heard a great many voices in her life. She’d spent years listening to people, in calls, in chat, in her own ear at three in the morning when somebody needed somebody. She knew what voices did. Loud ones and quiet ones. Kind ones and cruel ones. She’d never heard anything like this.

It wasn’t loud. It wasn’t anything. It was calm, and level, and very clear, and it didn’t come from the bottom of the slope. It came from right beside her. From right beside everybody. As if it were standing at her shoulder, reading something over it. Neither high nor low. Neither a man’s nor a woman’s. The voice of somebody filling in a form.

“The count,” said the Steward.

And Chi’s whole body went cold.

Not the Rimed’s cold. Not the wind. Something inside. It came up from the middle of her, from her stomach, and went out into her arms and her legs and her hands and her face, all at once, and locked. She couldn’t move. She tried. She tried to lift her hand, the one on her shield, and it didn’t lift. She tried to turn her head to Thea and it didn’t turn. She stood on the parapet with her shield in front of her and her mouth open and every single part of her held still, the way you’re held still in a dream.

She knew this. She’d felt it once before. On floor 2, on the eighth day, in a narrow place called the Fold, when a thing with a bell round its neck had screamed.

[ Afraid ]

Afraid, Thea had read, on floor 2, under the window. Cannot act until it passes. And under that, in grey, in the words under the words: It lasts as long as you hold on to it.

All along the wall, nobody was moving.

She could see them. From the corners of her eyes, which was all she could move. The hold-folk in the line, frozen with their shields half-raised. Brenna, at the head of the stair, with one foot on the top step. Drew, further along, with his hand still out toward the sergeant, his face gone grey and young and stuck. Thea, beside her, with the lantern up and her eyes wide.

Everybody. The whole wall. Held.

And at the bottom of the slope, the Steward opened its book.

It counted.

“One,” said the Steward.

Chi felt it land. Not on her. On somebody. Somewhere along the wall. She didn’t know who. She felt it go past her like a hand reaching past your face in the dark to touch the person beside you.

“Two.”

Another one. Further along.

“Three. Four. Five.”

It went along the wall. Slowly. Not looking up from its book. It didn’t need to look. Each number went out from it and found somebody and landed, like a stamp coming down on a page, and Chi could feel each one go past her, closer, closer.

It was counting the climbers.

Not the hold-folk. Just them. She could feel it skip the hold-folk. Go past them as if they were furniture. And land on Azac, three along, nine. On Jared, eleven. On Drew, fourteen.

“Seventeen,” said the Steward.

And it landed on her.

It was like being picked up. Not her body. Something else. As if somebody had reached into the middle of her, where she kept herself, and picked it up between finger and thumb and looked at it and written a number on it and put it down again. Seventeen. She felt it go into her and stay.

She’d never been so frightened in her life.

“Eighteen,” said the Steward. And Thea, beside her, made a tiny sound.

It went on. Twenty. Twenty-five. Not just the wall. Further. Down. Into the city. Chi felt the numbers go past the wall, down the pass, to the people who weren’t here. Rachael and Gabe at the house for the wounded. Maigo on the cliff. Calvin in the barracks, asleep. Thirty. Thirty-one. As if it didn’t matter where they were. As if it knew exactly where every one of them was, and was just reading them off a list.

“Thirty-five,” said the Steward.

It stopped.

Chi felt it stop. The whole wall felt it stop. The pause went on for a long time. A breath. Two. The Steward stood at the bottom of the slope with its book open and its white face bent over it, very still.

“Thirty-six,” said the Steward.

And somewhere along the wall, past Drew, past the sergeant, Chi felt it land on somebody like a hammer.

The windows came up.

All of them. All at once. In front of every face on the wall. And she knew, without being able to look, in front of every face in the city too. Small and blue and very plain.

[ Irregular ]

She’d heard about this. Chris had told the club, on floor 7, in a bakery. Irregular. In front of all of us. For half a heartbeat. At the fourth stroke of the bell. It had been there and gone so fast that most of them hadn’t seen it.

It didn’t go this time.

It stayed. In front of her face. Irregular. One word. And it stayed, and stayed, and she was frozen with her eyes open and couldn’t look away from it.

And beside her, Thea moved.

Chi didn’t know how. Nobody else could move. Chi couldn’t move her own eyes. But at the edge of her vision she saw Thea’s head turn. A tiny bit. Toward her own window. And Thea’s eyes narrowed, the way they narrowed when she was reading the words under the words. The way they’d narrowed on floor 2, on the walkway, with the lines of light going across it like code, and a nosebleed.

Source. Every window has words under the words.

Thea was reading it.

She was reading it while she was Afraid. Chi could see what it cost. Thea’s face had gone white. Her lips had gone white. A thin line of red came out of her left nostril and ran down over her lip, and she didn’t wipe it, because she couldn’t move her hand, and she didn’t stop reading.

And then she said it. Out loud. Very quietly. In a voice that barely worked.

“One record does not match.”

At the bottom of the slope, the Steward closed its book.

“The count will be reconciled,” it said. In the same calm voice, at everyone’s shoulder. “At the Gate. At the dark of the moon.”

And then, very slightly, its white face turned. Not toward the wall. Toward one place on the wall. Past Drew. Past the sergeant. Toward the place where the last number had landed like a hammer.

It looked at that place for a long time.

Then it turned round, and walked back up the pass, on top of the snow, leaving nothing behind it. Slowly. Very straight. With its book against its chest.

And everybody on the wall was still frozen. And it was walking away. And Chi stood there with her shield up and her body locked and the word Irregular in front of her eyes, and thought, very clearly, the way you think in a dream:

It lasts as long as you hold on to it.

She was holding on to it. She could feel herself holding on. The cold in her middle, locking her arms. She was holding it the way you hold your breath when you’re frightened, because you think if you let it go something worse will happen.

Let it go.

She did the only thing she’d ever been any good at.

She said it slowly.

“Breathe,” said Chi.

It came out of her in a whisper. Barely a sound. Her mouth could only just move. But it came out, slow and even and level, the way she’d said it a thousand times, to chat, to friends, to a hold-folk boy on the wall at night. Like someone pouring water.

Hush. Say it slowly. They’ll hear you through anything.

“Breathe,” said Chi again. A little louder. “Slowly. It’s going. It’s only talking. It’s going away.”

She felt it go out of her. Along the wall. Left and right. Not warmth, the way Azac’s stories went. Something quieter. Like a hand on the back of your neck, on a bad night. She felt it reach Thea, beside her. She felt Thea’s breath go in, ragged. And out.

“Let it go,” said Chi. Slowly. To everyone. To herself. “It lasts as long as you hold it. So put it down. That’s all. Just put it down. It’s only a word.”

Her hand moved.

On the strap of her shield. Her fingers. She felt them move. And then her arm. And then she could turn her head, and she turned it, and looked along the wall.

Thea was breathing. Hard. With blood on her lip and the lantern shaking in her hand. Beyond her, the hold-folk were moving, one by one, slowly, like people waking up. A sergeant lowering his shield. Brenna, at the top of the stair, putting her foot down on the step. Azac, three along, letting out a breath like a man coming up from under water.

“Breathe,” said Chi. Still slowly. She didn’t dare speed up. “That’s it. It’s gone. It’s walking away. Look. It’s going.”

They looked. Up the pass. The tall grey shape, getting smaller, walking on top of the snow into the throat of rock on the high side, very straight. Not looking back.

“It’s only a word,” said Chi.

And then she looked further along the wall. Past Drew, who was moving now, very slowly, his hand coming down, his face still grey. Past the sergeant. To the place where the last number had landed.

Declan was standing there.

He wasn’t frozen. That was the first thing she saw. He’d never been frozen. He was standing at the parapet with his lamp shield on his arm, looking up the pass at the Steward walking away, and he wasn’t Afraid. He wasn’t anything. His face was perfectly still. White. The white Chi had heard about, from the big pool on floor 8, when the Great Fall stopped. From a rope over the Down. From the Breath.

And the window in front of his face hadn’t gone.

Everybody else’s had. Chi’s had, when she let go. Irregular, and then nothing. But in front of Declan’s face, small and blue in the gold morning, it was still there. She could see it from here. One word.

He was looking straight through it. Up the pass.

Beside him, Gabe had his shield up. Hearthwall. Its edge across Declan’s right side. Gabe was moving, slowly, coming out of it like everybody else, and his face was doing something Chi had never seen it do. And he turned his head, and looked at Declan, and at the window still hanging in front of Declan’s face.

And then Gabe looked along the wall, straight at Thea.

Nobody said anything on the wall for a long time.

Brenna came along the parapet. Slowly. With her shield on her back. She stopped beside Chi, because Chi was nearest, and looked up the pass at the empty snow where the Steward had been. There wasn’t a mark on it. Not one.

“We have it written,” said Brenna.

Chi looked at her.

“In the old books. Under the first wall.” Brenna’s voice was very flat. “The Steward. Comes down from the Gate. Counts. It’s in there. Only once.” She was quiet. “Three hundred and twelve years ago.”

Thea turned her head.

“When the last climbers came,” said Brenna. “Thirty of them. It came down, and stood where it stood, and counted them. And everybody on the wall stood frozen while it did. The books say that.” She looked at the place at the foot of the slope. “And when it had counted thirty, it said: the count is correct. And went back up. And in the spring they went up through the Gate.”

Nobody said anything.

“The count is correct,” said Thea. Very quietly. With the blood drying on her lip.

“That’s what the books say.”

“Not one record does not match.”

“No,” said Brenna. She looked at Thea for a long time with her grey eyes. “Not that.”

Chi looked along the wall. At Declan. The window in front of his face had gone, finally. He was still looking up the pass. He hadn’t moved. Gabe was beside him with his hand flat between Declan’s shoulder blades, the way Chi had seen him do on a stair once, and not saying anything.

Thirty-six, she thought. Like a hammer.

She looked down at Waddles, who’d slept through all of it on her feet, and was only now waking up, and looking round, and making a small cross sound because everything had gone strange and nobody was paying attention to him.

She picked him up. She held him against her chest. He was warm.

She didn’t feel calm at all.

Thea sat down on the steps of the fifth-wall stair afterwards, with the lantern beside her and a cloth from Xola pressed to her nose, and didn’t say anything for an hour.

Chi sat beside her. She didn’t say anything either. That was the other half of the trick, the half nobody knew about. Sometimes you were calm at somebody by not saying anything at all.

“I read it,” said Thea, eventually. Through the cloth.

“I know.”

“I couldn’t move. I couldn’t move my hand. And I read it anyway.” She took the cloth away and looked at the blood on it. “I’ve never read anything that hard. It didn’t want to be read. It was like reading something with the light behind it, through the back of the page.” She was quiet. “It was the first time it’s ever let me see what’s under that word.”

“One record does not match,” said Chi.

“Yeah.”

They sat there. Down below them the city was waking up. Smoke going up from the long halls. Bells for breakfast. Somewhere a line of small children was waiting outside the barracks for a penguin.

“Thea,” said Chi. “What does it mean?”

Thea didn’t answer for a long time. She was looking along the parapet. To where Declan had finally sat down, with his back to the wall, and his shield across his knees, and Gabe on his right. Not talking. Both of them looking at nothing.

“It means the tower keeps records,” said Thea at last. “Of us. All thirty-six. And it’s got thirty-six records, and thirty-six of us. And one of them’s wrong.” She swallowed. “That’s all it means. That’s all I can say it means. That’s a fact. That’s what it said.”

Chi looked at her.

She’d been in the club since floor 6. She’d been at the top of a pit under the moon when Thea said the word, regression, and Hako said yeah, and Winter said is that a fact. She’d held it, the way they all held it, like a hot cup. She knew exactly what Thea was not saying.

One record does not match.

Because he’s been here before. And the tower’s got a record of him from last time. And he doesn’t match it any more.

She didn’t say it. Neither did Thea. They sat on the steps of the fifth wall with a penguin and a lantern and didn’t say it, together, the way you can sit with somebody and both not look at the same thing.

“Drew’s going to put it on the slate,” said Chi, after a while. “Facts go to everybody the same day. That’s the rule.”

“I know.”

“All thirty-five are going to read it.”

“I know, Chi.” Thea pressed the cloth to her nose again. “It’s a fact. It goes on the slate.” She shut her eyes. “And then everybody’s going to look at him the way the Steward did. And he’s going to have to stand there and let them.”

Chi didn’t say anything.

She thought about the last number. Thirty-six. Landing along the wall like a hammer. And the window that stayed in front of his face after everybody else’s had gone.

At the dark of the moon, the Steward had said. The count will be reconciled.

She knew what reconciled meant. Everybody did.

It meant you found the number that didn’t match. And you fixed it.
