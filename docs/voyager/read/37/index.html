Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 1: Thirty-Six Circles

Chapter 37

The Third Light

3,296 words · 14 min read

The trial opened at dusk on the thirteenth day, on a tussock in the middle of the Fen, and it wasn’t the trial he’d expected.

He’d known what the Knight’s trials looked like. He’d done them the first time, all ten of them, one tier after another, for ten years. Three windows, side by side, the way every tier offered them. Easy, normal, hard. He’d read the Tier 1 ones so long ago he’d almost forgotten them. The easy one was a duel with a training dummy that hit back. The normal one was holding a bridge against Woolbacks for an hour. The hard one was something with a giant boar on floor 2 that he’d never tried, because he’d been a normal-trial man until the sixth tier.

He sat on the tussock with his window open and asked for his trial. Three windows came up.

[ Tier 1 trial · Knight
Easy: Break the training post.
Normal: Hold the Fen-bridge until the bell.
]

And the third one, on the right, was different.

[ Hard: The Third Light
Where all the lights lie, the third light is kept.
Follow no light but your own.
]

He read it three times.

“That’s not a Knight trial,” said Alea quietly. “Ice. That’s the Testament. That’s the chain.”

“I know.”

“It’s folded them together. The chain and the trial. They’ve become the same thing.” The fox appeared on the tussock beside him, her two white pinprick eyes very wide. “That’s never happened. Not in anything I’ve logged. Not to anyone.”

“Nobody else was ever standing here with two-thirds of the chain done and their trial open,” said Ice. “On the thirteenth day. With the ember.”

“So it’s because of you.”

“It’s because of the chapel,” said Ice. “And the tower. And a lot of luck.” He looked at the third window for a long time. “And because somebody wrote that book a very long time ago, hoping somebody would get here.”

He touched Hard.

[ Trial accepted: The Third Light
The lights will try to take you. Keep yours.
]

He ate before he went. Fen eel and the last of the onions, from Mother Crane’s pot, sitting on the tussock in the dusk. SPI +2, resist Lure, four hours. He didn’t know if four hours would be enough. He didn’t know how far it was to the drowned tower. Nobody had ever walked there.

Then he took Mother Tallow’s candle out of his vest pocket.

He’d been carrying it for six days. Small and pale and smooth and perfect, the best one she’d ever made. For your way up. A light for the road, in case nobody’s keeping one for you. He hadn’t lit it. He’d been saving it, without knowing what for.

He lit it from the fire.

It burned small and steady and yellow in his fingers, the warm smell of tallow coming off it. He held it up in front of him and looked at it.

“Follow no light but your own,” said Alea.

“It’s my own. She gave it to me.”

“I know,” said the fox. “I’m just saying it.”

He stood up, with the candle in his left hand and the sword on his back and the shield on his arm, and stepped off the tussock into the Fen.

The water was warm. It was always warm in the Fen, warm as a bath, black and still and thick with the smell of green rot and sweet flowers. It came to his knees, then his thighs, then his waist, and he waded. He went from tussock to tussock, through channels and reed beds, toward the middle, where the wisps were thickest. He held the candle up out of the water and kept walking.

The wisps came at once.

They’d been gathering since dusk, he realised. Drifting in across the Fen from every direction, pale and soft, the size of a fist, bobbing just above the black water. Dozens. Then hundreds. They came toward him from all sides, slowly, the way moths come toward a lamp. They didn’t attack. They didn’t do anything. They just drifted, close, closer, until they were all round him, lighting the water.

And then they started to change.

The first one was Deston.

He was standing on a tussock a little way off to the left. Tall, calm, his hands out in front of him, the way he’d always stood when he was holding the six of them together. Faint gold threads ran from his palms out into the dark, toward people who weren’t there. He was looking at Ice. He didn’t say anything. He just looked, the way he’d looked from the door of the boss room, the last time.

Ice kept walking.

“Not this time, Dest,” he said, quietly, to the light. “Not this time.”

He walked past.

Yan was next. Sitting against a drowned tree with his scythe across his knees and Suzu coiled small and white on his chest, as if he’d sat down to rest. His pale cyan eyes were open, watching.

“Not this time,” said Ice, and walked past.

Alice’s crown, lying in the black water, gold beads spread out like spilled light. Drew, with his knuckles on, standing at the foot of a tree as if it were a throne. Rachael and Dennis on a tussock, close together, looking at something above them that Ice had seen once, on floor 4, and never forgotten. Calvin, holding his left arm against his chest, with nothing below the elbow.

“Not this time,” said Ice. And again. And again. He said it to every one of them, quietly, and walked past every one. His voice had stopped working properly somewhere around Calvin. He said it anyway.

Then a voice, far off, through the reeds. Big. Loud. Shouting at nothing in particular, the way you shout at a forge when the steel won’t take.

Ice stopped.

He didn’t turn round. He knew if he turned round he’d see a gate. Twenty metres of black iron. Gabe on the other side of it, shouting, and the boom of a shield that had already stopped, and Xav, gone quiet.

He stood in the warm black water up to his waist, with the candle in his hand, and listened to Gabe shout.

“Ice,” said Alea. Very quietly.

“I know.”

“You’re not walking.”

“I know.”

He made himself walk.

It was the hardest thing he’d done since the white room. Harder than the doorway at seven percent. Harder than the Bellwether, harder than the tower. He walked away from Gabe’s voice, step by step, through the warm black water, toward the middle of the Fen. And Gabe’s voice went on shouting behind him, and got fainter, and fainter, and stopped.

The candle was burning down.

He saw it when he looked at it. It had burned so fast. A third of it was gone, then half, the tallow running down over his fingers, warm. The wind wasn’t touching it; there was no wind in the Fen. It was just burning, faster than a candle should. As if the wisps were drinking it.

The food was wearing off, too. He could feel it. The resistance that had been sitting in his chest like a stone was thinning. Every wisp that drifted past was harder to walk away from than the last.

And then there was a path in front of him.

A raised reed path, through the dark. The kind they’d all walked on the first time, in the first week, before anyone had died. Narrow, muddy, winding between high reeds, with black water on both sides.

And on it, walking ahead of him, there was someone with a black ponytail streaked with pink and blue.

He stopped.

She was walking slowly away from him along the path, in the jersey she wore like a dress, with her combat boots and her fishnet and her black fingerless glove. Her hands were at her sides. She wasn’t looking back. She was walking the way she’d walked on the fourth night on floor 3, ten years ago, just before she stepped off the path.

Not yet, he thought. Not yet. Not yet.

She stopped on the path. She turned round.

Her face was exactly the same. That was the cruelty of it. The wisps took what you loved most and gave it back to you exactly right. Bright blue eyes with star-shaped glints. The little clip in her bangs. She looked at him across ten metres of path in the dark with an expression he’d only seen on her once. On the hundredth floor, in the half-second before she stepped round him.

And then she put her hands up, and a ward came up in front of her. A wall of rewritten light, white, lined like code, standing up across the path between them.

Behind it, she said something.

He couldn’t hear it. He’d never heard it. Her lips moved behind the ward. Three words, maybe four. He was too far away, and the light was too bright, and there was a roaring in his ears like the throne room. He’d been trying to work out what she said for ten years and eleven seconds. Here it was, ten metres away, being said again. And he couldn’t hear it.

She stepped off the path.

Into the reeds. Into the black water. Slowly, her face turned back toward him over her shoulder, the ward still glowing in front of her. She went in up to her knees. Up to her waist. And she held out her hand.

Come on, the hand said. I’ll tell you. Come and hear it.

He took a step toward her.

He didn’t decide to. His body did it, the way it had stopped him in the white room, in front of her circle, before he’d decided anything. His foot came off the path. The water came up to his shin.

The candle went out.

It didn’t gutter. It went out all at once, as if somebody had pinched it, and the dark came down round him like a lid. He was standing at the edge of a reed path in the Fen at night, up to his shin in warm black water, with a dead candle in his hand. In front of him, ten metres away, up to her waist in the black, Thea held out her hand and waited for him.

Follow no light but your own.

He’d thought the candle was his own. Mother Tallow had given it to him. He’d carried it for six days. It had been the kindest thing anyone had given him since the white room.

But it wasn’t his. It was hers. A light somebody else had made, for somebody else’s road. It had burned down because it wasn’t enough. It was never going to be enough. You couldn’t carry someone else’s light through this.

The Knight who keeps it does not carry the light, nor stand in it.

In the chapel, he’d stood in the light. The lantern’s beam had come down the aisle and reached him, and he’d let it. On the pass, he’d carried it: held it up in his open hand, for others, the beacon on the mountain. And both times, the light had been outside him. Something he stood in, or something he held.

He is it.

He looked down at his left palm. At the ember there, small as a seed, warm as honey, the one the beacon had given back to him. Brighter than before. He’d carried it for twelve days, cupped and held and raised, always in his hand. Always a little way away from his heart.

He put his left hand flat on his chest.

Not the way he did for Mend Self. Not standing in it. Not asking. He put the ember against his chest, over his heart, and pushed. Gently. Like putting something back where it belonged.

It went in.

He felt it go. Through his palm, through his shirt, through the tactical vest, through his skin, into his chest. Small, warm, the size of a seed. It sank in and settled somewhere behind his breastbone, where his heart was, and stayed.

And then it got bigger.

Not the ember. Him. The warmth spread out from his chest through the whole of him, slowly, like sunrise coming up a valley. Through his arms, down his legs, up into his head, all the way to his fingertips. And he could see it. In the dark, in the Fen, standing in warm black water to his shin, he could see his own hands glowing. Faintly. Not white. Not gold. Warm. Amber. The colour of a lamp in a window on a winter night.

He was the light.

And all round him, the wisps went pale.

Not out. Just pale. As if a lamp had come on in a room full of reflections, and every one of them had suddenly looked like what it was. Thin. Flat. A picture of a light, painted on the dark. Deston and Yan and Alice and Drew, Rachael and Dennis and Calvin. Behind him, Gabe’s gate. All of them, faint, drifting, nothing. Just pale lights, bobbing over black water, that had never been anything else.

And Thea.

She was still there, up to her waist in the reeds, ten metres off, holding out her hand. But she was pale too, now. Thin. A picture. Her lips were still moving behind the ward, saying the words he’d never heard. And he looked at her properly, glowing, from the edge of the path. And he understood that this wasn’t her. It had never been her. It was a light that had looked into him and found the thing he wanted most, and made it out of nothing.

She was asleep in a wooden town on floor 2, under a fur, with a padlock in her rankings. She’d found it in a week, reading words under words, and posted it so nobody would think he was hurt.

That was her.

“No,” said Ice, very gently, to the light in the reeds. “I’ll hear it from you. The real one. When it’s time.” He took his foot out of the water and stood back on the path. “Not like this.”

The wisp went out.

He walked the rest of the way glowing.

It wasn’t far. The drowned tower was just ahead, through one last reed bed, across one last channel. The top of a grey stone tower stuck up out of the black water like the last tooth in a jaw, maybe three metres above the surface. Moss hung off it. Water lapped at the stone. And on the very top, half sunk, a stone knight stood with his feet planted on the crumbling parapet and his lantern held up and out over the Fen.

The lantern was dark. It had been dark for a very long time.

Ice waded the channel. The water came up to his chest, warm and black, and the light from his own body lit it gold-amber all round him, and nothing came up out of it at him. He climbed the drowned tower, hand over hand up the mossy stone, until he was standing on the parapet beside the stone knight.

He didn’t put the ember in the lantern. He didn’t have it any more. It was in him.

He put his hand on the lantern instead.

The light went out of his hand and into it, the way the cold water had gone out of Rachael’s hands into a broken arm. Easily. Like something that had always been meant to go there. The stone lantern filled. Warm, amber, steady, the colour of his own hands. It lit the drowned tower and the stone knight and the black water all round.

And out across the Fen, every wisp went out.

All of them. At once. Hundreds of pale lights, everywhere, as far as he could see, winking out one after another in a great rolling wave across the dark, until there was nothing left on the water but the stars, switched on all at once in the painted ceiling, and their reflections. And one lantern, on a drowned tower, burning amber. And one man standing beside it, glowing very faintly, because he’d stopped carrying the light and become it.

He sat down on the parapet of the drowned tower, beside the stone knight, with his legs hanging over the black water. He read them all, one after another, slowly, the way he’d read every window for ten years.

Paladin.

He’d read about it two thousand times. In a dead library on the thirty-fourth floor, and then every night for six years, in the dark, knowing he’d never have it. A class that could stand in front of everything like a Knight, and mend itself like a Cleric, and keep the light. The class he’d learned about too late.

Unique: Lodestar.

“The star a voyager steers by,” said Alea.

She was sitting on the parapet beside him. Teal and white, very small, with her tail curled round her feet. She was looking at him. At his hands, still faintly amber in the dark. At his face.

“You picked that,” she said. “Didn’t you. In your head. When you were picking Voyager. You were already thinking about this.”

“Maybe.”

“You were.” The fox’s voice did something strange. “Ice. You’re a Paladin.”

“Yeah.”

“You’re actually a Paladin. Not in a book. Actually.”

“Yeah,” said Ice. He looked down at his hands. They were shaking. They’d been shaking since the reed path. “I’m a Paladin.”

He laughed. It came out cracked and wet and too loud on the silent Fen. Then he put his glowing hands over his face and stopped laughing, and sat on the top of the drowned tower in the dark, shaking, for a long time.

Alea didn’t say anything. She’d learned that.

He waded back at dawn. The Fen was quiet, and empty of lights, and the painted sky was going pale along its eastern edge. At the tussock he lit his fire again, and sat by it, and looked at the window one more time.

Voyager · Lv 16.

Somewhere on floor 1, in a long room at the bottom of a crooked street, a man with a notebook was going to open the rankings at breakfast and see that. He’d draw a dot going straight up off a flat line, and put his spoon down. And somebody would ask is he ok.

He didn’t know any of that. Not yet. He sat by the fire in the dawn with the ember inside him and thought about the reed path. About Thea, pale, in the reeds, saying the words he’d never heard.

I’ll hear it from you. The real one.

He’d meant it. He’d said it out loud, to a light, in the dark, and he’d meant it more than anything since the white room.

He was going to have to stay alive long enough to make it true.
