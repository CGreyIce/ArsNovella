Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 6: Unwritten

Chapter 221

Forty Paces

5,116 words · 22 min read

Higu woke up to a bell being rung wrong.

That was the first thing she knew, before she knew where she was. Somebody was ringing a bell, hard, with both hands, the way you’d ring one at a school sports day to bring everybody in off the field. Not quick and quiet. Not the Lanthorn way. And somebody was shouting.

Plan B.

She was on her feet with her bow before she’d opened her eyes properly. Drew was already up beside her, bare-handed, his face doing the thing it did when he was counting people. He didn’t say anything. He didn’t need to. He looked at her and she looked at him, and they went.

Out of the hollow. Along the walkway. Round the last root to the pool.

And stopped.

Thea was at the edge of the water with the bundle in her arms. Ice on her right. Gabe on her left. And people coming out of the roots behind them, out of every hollow, down every ladder, grey with the swarm’s dust, half asleep, with their lights in their little iron cages, coming to stand next to them. Malcolm coming round the far side of the pool at a walk with his slate under his arm. The whole of Lanthorn in the doors of the roots, two hundred faces, every glim silent.

And down the Hollow, past the far ladders, the dark was standing up.

Higu looked at it.

She’d been measuring things since she was small. Not counting; measuring. Newish counted so everybody would know how far. Higu measured so she’d know where. Where a thing was, and where it was going, and where you had to be when it got there. She’d drawn maps of every floor on the back of anything she could find. She’d drawn floor 2 in the snow with a stick. She’d drawn the Down on nine and had it be wrong every morning and drawn it again.

She looked down the Hollow at the dark coming up it, and she measured.

Wide. Wider than the slope. She couldn’t see the edges; it went out past the white trees on either side into the dark. Grey-brown, soft, coming up over the terraces the way a tide comes up a beach, not fast, steady, the near edge lifting over the walls and the dead villages and the split tree as it came. The rings on it, dark, the size of houses. Under it, through it, faint, the lights. Thousands. Moving like fish under ice.

And at the front, high up, two eyes. Round. Dull red-black. Wide open.

She looked at where the near edge was. The split tree. She knew where the split tree was; she’d drawn it from Rui Xiang’s description, on the rock, in the left-hand column. She looked at where it had been a breath ago, and where it was now.

She did the sum.

“About a drip,” said Higu.

Out loud. She hadn’t meant to. Everybody near her turned round.

“At that speed,” said Higu. “From the split tree to the far ladders. About a drip. Maybe a bit less.” She looked at it. “It’s slowing down as it comes up. The slope’s steeper. It’s big. It doesn’t like steep.”

Nobody said anything.

“It’s coming here,” said somebody. Calvin, she thought. Very quietly, for Calvin.

“Yes,” said Higu.

She looked at the pool. At the roots round it. Seven great white trees in a ring, with the town in their roots, walkways and ladders and hollows and two hundred people in the doors. And the Moth coming up the Hollow toward a girl standing at the edge of the black water with a lantern in her arms.

Plan B. It comes to the town first. We meet it at the pool.

She looked at the size of the thing coming. Then at the ring of trees. Then at the pool, which was as wide as the green at Haverley.

It wouldn’t fit.

It would come to the lantern. It would come over the far roots to get to the pool, because the pool was where the lantern was. Over the walkways. Over the hollows. Over the doors with two hundred people in them. It wouldn’t mean to. It was just very big, and it was coming to a point, and everything between it and the point was in the way.

It ate a street, Corwen had said. There isn’t a street there now.

“Not here,” said Higu.

She didn’t know who she said it to.

To all of them. To nobody. She said it out loud, the way she’d said about a drip, because it was the next true thing and her mouth said it before she’d decided to.

Everybody looked at her.

Thea, at the edge of the pool, turned her head. Gabe. Malcolm, with his slate. Xav, who’d come out of the roots and was standing very still near the forge, the way Xav stood. And Ice.

Ice was looking at her.

She knew what she’d always done at this moment. Every stair, every floor, for two years. At the bottom of nine stairs, she’d turned round to him and asked. Where? And he’d known. On every floor up to fourteen, he’d known. He’d say there, or the west gate, or don’t go left, and she’d draw it, and it would be right.

And on fifteen, at the top of the stair, she’d asked, and he’d said I don’t know.

And on the jam, she’d asked, and he’d said there, and she’d known it wasn’t memory. He’d decided.

And at the foot of the stair to sixteen she’d started to ask and then said never mind, you don’t know, nobody does. Up, then. All of us. We’ll find out.

She opened her mouth to ask him. Where? She was already turning.

Ice spoke first.

“Higu,” said Ice. “Where?”

She stopped.

The whole pool stopped. She felt it. Thirty-five people and the town of Lanthorn and the dark coming up the Hollow, and for a moment none of it mattered, because he’d asked her.

He was standing on Thea’s right with Tuli’s pole in one hand and his sword in the other. His face was doing nothing at all. He was just looking at her. Waiting. The way she’d waited at the bottom of every stair since the white room.

Where.

She’d never once in her life been asked that. She’d asked it. She’d spent two years asking it. She had a rock in her bag with the answers on it, his answers, in two columns, and the right-hand side had A FOREST and A RIVER and HE DOESN’T KNOW and HE ASKED HOW and THERE and HELP.

Nobody had ever asked her.

She looked down the Hollow at the dark coming up. At the slope. At the place where the path came up out of the white trees and the last roots of Lanthorn began. She’d measured it. She’d drawn it. She’d walked it every fall for thirty falls with her feet and her eyes, the way she walked every place, until she knew it.

Open ground. Pale moss. Between the last root and the first white trees. Where the strays used to drift round the town in a ring. Where John had practised. A wide flat apron of moss on the slope, below the town, with nothing on it at all.

“There,” said Higu.

She pointed.

“The edge. Where the path comes in. Below the far ladders.” She was talking fast. She never talked fast. “It’s open. Nothing on it. It’s below the town, so it’s coming up to it; it has to stop there to reach her, it doesn’t have to come over the roots. If she stands there, it comes to her and stops. On the slope. Not on the town.”

She looked at the open ground. She measured.

“Forty paces,” said Higu. “Out from the last root. Not thirty. Thirty and its wings’ll be on the roots when it comes down. Not fifty. Fifty and it’s too far for the archers on the roots. Forty.”

She stopped. She was out of breath.

Ice looked at her for a long moment.

“Forty,” said Ice.

He turned to Thea. Thea was already looking down the Hollow, at the open ground, at the place Higu had pointed. Her face was doing nothing at all.

“Forty paces,” said Thea. Quietly. “Okay.”

And she walked.

It took Higu about a breath to realise everybody was waiting for her.

Not for Xav. Xav was the one who laid things out. Xav was standing by the forge with his sword down, and he wasn’t laying anything out. He was looking at her. Waiting. Malcolm with his slate, looking at her. Chris with his notebook open, pen up, looking at her. Rui Xiang. Drew.

Where wasn’t just the place. It never had been. Where was where everybody stood.

She’d drawn it a hundred times. Two years of it. Every fight, every floor, on the back of the rock afterwards, the way it had gone: who’d stood where, who’d moved, the lines. She drew fights the way other people kept diaries. She knew where every single one of them stood best. She’d never once said it out loud before the fight, because there’d always been somebody else to say it. Drew. Xav. Ice.

She said it.

“Thea at forty,” said Higu. “Ice on her right. Gabe on her left. Malcolm behind them. That’s C. That’s the middle.” She was walking. Everybody was walking, down the walkways, along the roots, toward the far ladders, and she was talking as she went, and they were listening. “Archers on the roots. Me, Winter, Aleaka. Up high. The last roots, facing down. The eyes are the only thing we can hit from there. Go for the eyes.”

“Eyes,” said Winter. Behind her. With her forty new arrows on her back.

“Gigi. Maigo.” She found them. “When it comes down on the slope to reach her, the wings’ll come down on the ground either side. They’ll have to. It’s too big to hover that close. When they touch the ground—“

“Bramble,” said Gigi. Instantly. “It doesn’t let go.”

“Pin them. Both wings. Gigi left, Maigo right. Root them. Whatever you can.”

Maigo nodded. He was already taking his boots off.

“Hana. If it turns for the town. If it goes over the roots. Anything. Make a light. The biggest you can. Somewhere else.”

“Somewhere else,” said Hana. Her ears were flat. Her little lantern was humming on her crown.

“John.” John was off to one side, as always. She looked straight at him. “The small ones. They’ll come first. Before it. Like they always do. Take them away.”

“Beckon,” said John. Very quietly. “Yeah.”

“Everybody else.” She stopped at the bottom of the far ladders. At the last root. At the edge of the open ground. She turned round. Thirty-odd faces, grey with dust, with their lights in their cages. “Everybody else, in a ring. Round the middle. Twenty paces out from Thea. Not closer. Shields out. Facing in.” She was quiet a moment. “C.”

“Everybody,” said Malcolm. Under his breath.

“Xav,” said Higu.

Xav looked at her.

She didn’t tell him where to stand. She didn’t know. Nobody could tell Xav where to stand. He stood where the thing was going to be, a second before it was there, and he’d been doing it for twenty years.

“Wherever you like,” said Higu.

Xav almost smiled.

“Rui,” said Higu.

“I know where,” said Rui Xiang. He was already looking at a place on the slope, a little below and to the left of the forty-pace mark, where a white trunk stood alone at the edge of the open ground. He looked at it the way you’d look at a chair you’d sat in before. “I’ll be there first.”

“I know you will.”

And Drew.

She looked at him last. He was standing beside her, where he always was, with his bare hands, the knuckles scarred. Not counting. For once in his life, not counting anybody. Looking at her.

“Where do you want me?” said Drew.

She’d been asked twice in one night. By the two people she’d never in her life imagined asking her anything.

“Next to me,” said Higu. “On the root. Somebody has to stop me falling off it.”

Drew looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and something happened at the corner of his mouth that she’d seen maybe four times in two years.

“Yeah,” said Drew. “All right.”

Thea walked out to forty paces.

Higu counted them. She couldn’t help it. From the last root, out across the open moss, toward the white trees and the dark below. Not fast. Steady. With the bundle against her chest and her chin up. Ice on her right, a step behind and to the side, the way he walked on stairs with her hand in his except her hands were full. Gabe on her left with Hearthwall on his arm, turned outward now. Not inward, against his chest, the way he’d carried it on the terraces to keep the warmth in. Outward. The warmth going out. On purpose.

Hearthkeeper, Higu thought. Cover the one beside you. When they open the light.

Thirty-eight. Thirty-nine. Forty.

Thea stopped.

She stood on the pale moss, forty paces out from the last root of Lanthorn, in the open, with the town at her back and the dark in front of her. And Ice stopped on her right. And Gabe on her left. And Malcolm, a few paces behind them, sat down. Right there, on the moss, cross-legged, with his slate in his lap and his mace across his knees, like a man sitting down in a field to wait for a train.

And the ring came out round them. Thirty people, walking out from the roots, spreading, until there was a circle on the moss twenty paces out from the three in the middle, every one of them facing in. Shields. Swords. Staffs. Bare hands. Hako with his spear and the dog. Salty. Calvin with Rachael’s fist in his collar. Deston with his hand on his chest. Newish, with her lips already moving.

Higu went up the root.

The last root of Lanthorn, the great pale arch of it coming down out of the trunk above and going into the ground at the edge of the open moss. She went up it the way she went up everything, fast, without looking, to a place high on the curve where it was wide enough to stand, and turned round, and nocked an arrow. Drew came up after her. Slower. He sat down on the root behind her and put one hand flat on the back of her belt, and left it there.

From up here she could see all of it.

The ring on the moss. The three in the middle. The open ground going down to the white trees. Winter on the next root along, an arrow on the string. Aleaka on the one past that, with the goat at the bottom of it and the stair stray hanging over the goat like a lamp. Bee, higher, with her charcoal in her teeth and a strip of birch-bark, watching. Gigi and Maigo at the edges of the open ground, crouched, Maigo barefoot, Gigi with both hands flat on the moss.

And below it all, the dark, coming up.

She could see it now without trying. The near edge of it was at the bottom of the open slope, among the white trees, filling the spaces between the trunks like water filling the spaces between stones. The trunks went into it and didn’t come out. The rings on its wings went past between them, one, two, three, dark, enormous. And the lights under it, all those thousands of lights, moving.

It wasn’t hurrying.

She’d thought it would. All that hunger, Rui Xiang had said. It came up the slope as if it had all the time there had ever been.

It came for where the light had been.

The glims in Lanthorn behind her were silent. She could hear her own breath. Drew’s. Two hundred people in the doors of the roots, not breathing.

And then the wind came again. Warm. Soft. Smelling of dust and old candles. Up the slope and over the ring and over Thea and over the root where Higu stood, and lifting her hair off her face.

Off its wings.

The small ones came first. Like they always did.

Out of the dark, ahead of it, from between the white trunks where the near edge was. A cloud. Grey-brown. Silent. Hundreds. Coming up the slope over the moss toward the ring, toward the three in the middle, toward the bundle in Thea’s arms, the way smoke comes up a hillside.

“John,” said Higu.

She didn’t shout it. She didn’t need to. He was already doing it.

He was in the ring, on the downhill side, off to one side of where everybody else was standing. He had both hands out. Palms up. And out of them, one at a time, lights. Small and pale and white, the size of walnuts. Not sent. Let go. She could see the difference. A sent mote went out on a string; you could almost see the string. These went out free. Drifting. Off his palms and away, down the slope to the left, into the dark among the trees, one, three, eight, fifteen, a long loose line of lights going away from the ring, away from Thea, away from Lanthorn.

Nobody’s.

The cloud turned.

She watched it. All of them. Like iron filings. The whole grey-brown silent mass of them, halfway up the slope, turning in the air toward John’s line of lights, and following. Down and away. To the left. Into the trees. After the lights that weren’t anybody’s.

John stood in the ring with his hands still out and his eyes open. Not shut. Not looking through them. Just watching them go, with his own eyes, like anybody.

He didn’t flinch once. They weren’t his. There wasn’t any pinch.

What hunts will follow it away.

The cloud went into the dark after John’s lights, and the slope was clear.

And the Moth came up into the open.

Higu had seen its eyes from the pool. She’d heard Rui Xiang describe it lying in its bowl. She’d drawn it, from his words, on the rock: a long shape, folded wings, a head.

Her drawing had been the size of her thumbnail.

It came up out of the white trees onto the open ground at the bottom of the slope, and the trees didn’t come with it. It came out of them the way a ship comes out of fog. The head first. Low. Broad as a house, furred, grey, with the great round eyes on either side of it, each one as tall as Drew, dull red-black, and in each one a faint moving light far down. Two long feathered things curving up from the top of the head like the fronds of a fern, taller than the roots, moving slowly, tasting the air. And under the head, coiled, a thing like a rope of black leather as thick as Gabe’s body, wound round and round on itself.

Then the body. Behind the head. Long. Furred. The colour of dust. And on either side of it, coming up out of the trees, still folded half, the wings.

They went out and out. Past the edges of the open ground. Past the last trees she could see on either side. Grey-brown and soft and patterned with rings, each ring dark and round and the size of a house, like eyes painted on a sail. Half-folded, half-open, dragging on the moss behind it as it came. And through them, everywhere, faint and grey and moving: the lights.

She could see them properly now. Close. Under the wings, through the cloth of them, the way you see a lamp through a curtain. Thousands. Small, pale, every one of them the size of a stray. Moving. Pressing. All of them, all at once, toward the front of the wings. Toward the open ground. Toward forty paces out from the last root, where a girl was standing with something in her arms.

Whose, Rui Xiang had said. They’re all asking. It’s a crowd.

The Moth stopped.

At the bottom of the open ground. Thirty paces below the ring, maybe; she measured it without meaning to. Its head lowered, a little. The great feathered fronds turning. The eyes looking up the slope.

At Thea.

Not at the ring. Not at the thirty people round her with their shields facing in. Not at Lanthorn behind, with two hundred faces in the doors. At Thea. At the bundle.

It knew. It had seen it open for a count of ten, a long way off, from the bottom of everything. It had waited thirty falls.

It was here.

Higu drew.

She didn’t loose. Not yet. She held it, with the arrow on the string and the string at her cheek and her arms shaking, and looked down the shaft at the near eye, the left one, round and dull and red-black and as tall as Drew. And measured. Wind, none. Distance, sixty paces and down. Drop. She knew it. She’d shot down slopes on four and on nine.

Her arrows had always been moonlight. That was what she did. Pale and silver, on the string, out of nothing. On a floor with no moon she’d wondered, the first fall, if they’d come. They had. Thinner. Fainter. Like the moon through cloud.

The arrow on her string now was the faintest she’d ever drawn.

Go for the eyes.

Not yet.

On the next root along, Winter was holding too. She could see her out of the corner of her eye, very still, with her silver-blue frost arrow drawn and her jaw set. Waiting. Everybody was waiting.

For the middle.

Thea looked at the Moth.

Higu could see her face from the root. Small, at forty paces, in the middle of the ring. Calm. Doing nothing at all. Thea had a face for reading windows; Higu had seen it a thousand times. On floor 3 at a board. On ten in the dark of the moon. On thirteen in a grey hall in front of a book. A face like somebody looking at a page in a language they’d learned very young.

She was reading.

Higu saw the window open. Everybody’s did. In the corner of the eye, grey, pale, where they’d opened for two years.

That was all. Like the Breaker’s. Like the Last Sheaf’s. A name and what it was. No level. Floors above fourteen didn’t give levels, it seemed. Just names.

But Thea read under the words.

Source. Every window has words under the words. You can read them. She’d been doing it since floor 1. Asking the grey lines under the window lines what they were. Nobody else could. She’d read a whole book once, on thirteen, that wrote itself in faint amber only for her.

Higu watched her read. Watched her lips move.

Watched her face change.

It wasn’t fear. Higu knew Thea’s frightened face. This wasn’t it. This was something else. Something going out of her, slowly, all at once, the way the colour goes out of the sky at the end of a day.

“Thea,” said Ice. Quietly. On her right. He hadn’t looked at the window. He was looking at her. “What does it say?”

Thea didn’t answer for a long moment.

“It’s grey,” said Thea. Her voice carried. Higu didn’t know how. It wasn’t loud. It went out over the ring and the open ground and up to the roots, quiet and clear, the way Chi’s did when Chi said something slowly. “Under the name. One line. It’s very old. It’s been written over and over. In the same place. So many times the grey’s nearly worn through.”

She stopped.

“What does it say?” said Ice.

Thea looked at the Moth. At the great dull eyes, looking at her. At the lights pressing under its wings.

“Whose,” said Thea.

Nobody moved.

“It’s asking too,” said Thea. Very quietly. “Not just them. Inside. It. The whole thing. It’s the same word. It’s been asking it longer than anything.” She swallowed. “It’s not hungry. It never was. It’s—“ She stopped. Her arms were tight on the bundle. “It’s looking.”

Higu stood on the root with her arrow drawn and her arms shaking and her eyes stinging, and looked down at the thing at the bottom of the slope, and couldn’t make it into anything but what it was. A thing as big as the dark. With a street in its past and thousands of lights under its wings. Asking the same thing as a stray the size of an apple.

Whose. Whose are you.

She didn’t lower the bow.

She didn’t know what else to do.

Thea unwrapped the lantern.

Higu saw her decide. It was very quick. A breath. Thea looked at the Moth, and at the window, and at the ring round her, all of them facing in, and then she looked to her right, at Ice, for exactly one second.

He nodded.

He didn’t say anything. He didn’t step in front. He didn’t hold up his hand or his sword or his light. He just nodded, once, at her, with his face doing nothing at all, and stayed exactly where he was.

On her right. Where he always was.

Thea took one hand off the bundle and pulled the cloak away.

Not slowly. Not a hand’s width, for a count of ten, the way she’d done it for Edda’s empty cushion. All at once. The whole cloak, off, in one pull, and dropped on the moss at her feet.

And the light came out.

Amber. Gold. Warm. One light. Not a hundred points. Not scattered. One. Out of the lantern in her hands, across the open ground, up the slope to the roots, down the slope to the Moth. The brightest thing on that floor since there had been a floor. Brighter than Hana’s false light over the pool. Brighter than the fire that had taken a street. It lay across the pale moss and the ring of faces and the great furred head at the bottom of the slope like a sunrise coming in sideways through a door.

And it leaned.

Higu saw it lean. Everybody did. It always had, since the day Thea found it. It leaned now. Out of the lantern. Toward her right. Toward Ice.

When they open the light, Higu thought. Cover the one beside you.

On Thea’s left, Gabe lifted Hearthwall.

Not in front of her. Beside. He didn’t step. He turned. He turned his whole body toward her, and brought the shield up and across, sideways, so it was between the lantern and the bottom of the slope without him being between anything. His arm out straight across Thea’s front. His body beside hers. The shield where a shield goes.

Hearthwall was warm. It was always warm. Higu could see it now: in the amber light, the rim of it glowing, faintly, red, like a coal you blow on.

At the bottom of the slope, the Moth lifted its head.

The great fronds went still. The eyes, dull red-black, filled with the amber of the lantern, and something moved in them. Far down. A long way down. The way the light had moved in the Breaker’s eye on fifteen, Ice had told them. Coming up toward the top.

And under its head, the coil began to unwind.

Higu watched it go. Out. Down. Toward the moss. A rope of black leather as thick as a man, uncoiling from under the vast head the way a fern uncoils in spring, one loop, then the next, then the next, longer and longer, until the end of it lifted off the ground and hung there in the air at the bottom of the slope, swaying, and then began to come up.

Toward the ring. Toward forty paces. Toward the one light that wasn’t scattered.

Slowly. As if it had all the time there had ever been.

On the next root, Winter breathed out through her teeth.

Higu drew the arrow the last inch to her cheek.

“Eyes,” said Higu.

And loosed.
