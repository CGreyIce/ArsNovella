Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 6: Unwritten

Chapter 217

On the Drip

6,365 words · 28 min read

The drip fell into the pool.

That was the first thing Newish found out about Lanthorn that nobody had told her, and she found it out by standing at the edge of the black water with Isel on the morning after the moths, if it was morning, which it wasn’t, and looking up.

There was nothing up there. There never was. Dark, going up forever, with a few strays drifting in it very high, small as stars. And then, out of the dark, falling, a drop of water. She saw it come. A tiny bright point catching the light of the lanterns as it fell, from so high up it seemed to fall for a long time, a slow long time, longer than anything should take to fall, turning a little, glinting, all the way down out of the nothing above them.

Into the middle of the pool.

Plink.

A single ring went out across the black water. Slowly. All the way to the edges. To the roots. And stopped.

All round the pool, on every walkway and in every root, the people of Lanthorn looked up. Just for a moment. The way you look at a clock in a station when it chimes, even if you don’t need to know the time. Then they looked down again and went on with what they were doing.

“That’s one,” said Isel.

Newish looked at her.

“That’s one what?”

“One.” Isel lifted her empty lantern a little. “It’s the first drip after the long sleep. So that’s one. Then there’s two. Up to twenty-two. And then you sleep, and it’s one again.” She looked at the pool. “Everybody counts it. Gran keeps the count.”

Newish stood at the edge of the pool for a long time.

She’d been counting since the white room. Steps, spoons, Calvin’s boots, the Bellwether’s charges, the beat of a mowing line on floor 14 in the heat. She’d counted under everything, all her life, the way some people hum, and nobody had ever asked her to stop, and nobody, ever, on any floor, had ever handed her the count.

A whole town that counted.

“Oh,” said Newish. Very quietly. “Oh, I love it here.”

She told Milli. Milli had worked out the drip on the first night, by himself, by his own heartbeat, and he deserved to know he’d been right.

“An hour and six minutes,” said Milli. When she told him. He was sitting on the walkway outside the root-hollow with his tail round Hana’s ankle and a bit of wire in his hands that Jared had given him to practise the knot on, and he was terrible at it. “I said.”

“You said. It falls into the pool. The town’s pool. They built the whole town round it. It’s their clock. Twenty-two drips and you sleep. Seven to sleep. That’s a fall. That’s what they call it. A fall. A day’s a fall.” She sat down on the walkway. “Isel says it fell eleven when she means it’s the middle of the day. It fell eleven. Milli. It fell eleven.”

Milli looked at her for a long moment.

“You’re going to say that every drip now,” he said.

“I am going to say that every drip for the rest of my life.”

Hana laughed. It was the first time Newish had heard her laugh since the moths. She was sitting with her back to the root, with the little iron lantern on the front of her crown, beside the ugly rose, no bigger than a walnut, and her light inside it, scattered to a hundred points, humming. Every so often she lifted one hand and touched the lantern with one finger, very lightly, the way you’d check a sleeping baby was breathing. She didn’t seem to know she was doing it.

“It’s humming,” said Hana. When she saw Newish looking.

“I know. I can hear it.”

“It hasn’t stopped. Since it went in. It just hums.” Hana touched the lantern. “I asked it. Last night. When everybody was asleep. I asked it the thing. The thing I always ask.” She was quiet. “What are you?”

“And?”

“It didn’t say I am not able to answer that.” Hana’s ears went down. Then up. “It didn’t say anything. It just hummed louder. For a bit.” She looked at the pool. “I think that was the answer. I don’t know what it means.”

Newish looked at her own shoulder.

Her light was a spark. Since the arch. Since Milli’s please. She’d said please too, with everybody else, by the fire, and it had gone down to a spark, and it had stayed a spark. It didn’t hum. It didn’t do anything. It hung by her ear the way it always had, small and silent.

She hadn’t asked it anything since.

She didn’t know why. She asked everybody everything. It was the thing about her, the thing everybody said. Newish, shut up. Newish, stop talking. Love you guys. Except Newish. She’d yelled down a mountain pass once, at nobody, at a name at the top of a board, because of a thing he used to say on calls. She asked the sun things. She swore at it in the mornings.

She hadn’t asked the light on her shoulder anything for three falls.

She was a bit frightened of what it would say.

Jared and Gabe and Salty were making the lanterns.

In Corwen’s forge, all three of them, and Corwen, and Corwen’s sister, who’d turned out to be a weaver like their mother, a tiny fierce woman called Nesta who’d come in on the second fall and looked at what they were doing and said no and taken the wire out of Gabe’s hands. They worked in shifts. Three drips on, two off. The lanterns came out of the forge one at a time, walnut-sized, and went round the pool to whoever was next.

Gabe’s first one was terrible. Everybody agreed. Gabe agreed. It was lumpy and lopsided and the knot came undone at the bottom, and Nesta held it up and looked at it for a long time and said that’s the worst lantern I’ve ever seen, and Gabe said I know, and gave it to Ice.

Ice had looked at it in his hand for a long time.

He didn’t have a light. Not one anybody could see. There was nothing to put in it.

“It’s for if,” Gabe had said. Gruffly. “I don’t know. If. Just have it.”

And Ice had hung it on his belt. And left it there. Empty. And whenever Newish looked at him after that, at the edge of the pool, or in the door of a root-hollow, or coming back out of the dark with Thea and Xola behind him, grey with moth-dust, she saw it: a lopsided little lantern with nothing in it, swinging at his hip.

Milli’s light went into its lantern the moment Nesta hung it on his collar. Straight in. No waiting. Hummed at once.

“Of course it did,” said Hana.

Aleaka’s took three drips. It hung outside the cage, looking at it, the way the goat looked at a gate. Then it went in, and the goat butted the lantern, and it came out again, and Aleaka said with me to both of them and it went back in and stayed.

Jared’s went in and didn’t hum. Just sat. Jared said that was fine. Jared said that was the most Jared thing he’d ever seen.

Newish’s lantern came on the fifth fall. Salty brought it. He’d done it himself, mostly, with Nesta standing over him; it was a bit crooked at the top, where the ring was. He held it out on his big palm and went red.

“It’s crooked,” said Salty.

“It’s perfect.”

“It’s not. It’s crooked at the—“

“Salty. Shut up. It’s perfect.” She took it and hung it on her collar, by her ear. Her hands were shaking. She didn’t know why.

The spark at her shoulder looked at it.

She could feel it looking. She didn’t know how. It hung there by the little crooked cage and didn’t go in. Didn’t come out. Didn’t do anything at all.

“You don’t have to,” said Newish to it. Quietly. Which was strange, because she’d never in two years said anything to it quietly. “It’s not a door. Never shut a lantern. It’s just there. If you want.”

The spark didn’t move.

She left it.

Edda was dying.

Everybody knew. Nobody said it. That was the way in Lanthorn, Newish worked out: you didn’t say it, you just went and sat. And people went and sat. All the second fall and the third and the fourth, at the edge of the pool, by Edda’s root, in ones and twos. Brought her soup. Sat on the walkway near her and didn’t talk. And went away again.

She’d been out on her root the night of the moths. All night. At the edge of the pool, with her blanket, watching. Isel had tried to get her inside when the bells went and she’d said no, she’d said she kept the lamps and she’d see the lamps through, and she’d sat there with her tiny glim at her shoulder and her old lantern hanging over her head, empty, the whole time. While the moths came through the town. While Hana’s false light hung over the pool and they all went into it.

And after that, she’d got smaller.

Newish could see it. Everybody could. Edda was always small, but she got smaller, the way the light at her shoulder got smaller. Day by day. Fall by fall. She ate a bit of soup and then not soup. She talked a little and then less. She sat on her root at the edge of the pool and watched the drips come down, and her lips moved on every one, counting.

Newish went and sat with her on the third fall.

She didn’t decide to. She was going past, and Edda was there, and there was a gap on the walkway next to her root, and Newish sat down in it. Just like that.

She’d sat with a lot of people in two years. That was the job. Upbeat was a beat under everybody, but a healer was somebody who sat. She’d sat with the wounded on more floors than she could count. She’d sat on fourteen with old Ezra in the shade of a hedge at the end of the hottest day of the year, after he’d fallen behind the line, and counted his breathing back down, slowly, on the one, and he’d been fine. She’d sat in Mother Crane’s kitchen through three days of fever on floor 1, three hours at a time, on the stool, with everybody else, a hundred and eight shifts on Drew’s slate.

She’d never sat with somebody who was going and wasn’t going to come back.

She’d never had to.

Edda didn’t look round. She was watching the pool. Her lips were moving. Newish watched them, and counted with them without meaning to, under her breath, the way she always counted, and after a while she realised Edda had stopped counting and was listening to her.

“You count,” said Edda.

“Sorry. I always— It’s a thing. I don’t even—“

“Don’t be sorry.” Edda’s voice was so dry now it was hardly a voice. “You’re on it. Right on it. I’ve been listening to you since you came down the path. You counted the steps. Isel said.” She was quiet. “I’ve kept the count for sixty years. Since my mother. Nobody else can do it. They try. They drift. You can’t drift. Not on the count. If you drift on the count the whole town drifts.”

“I know,” said Newish. “I know. On fourteen there was a field. Twelve men in a line with blades. If one drifted—“

“—the whole line came apart.”

Newish stared at her.

Edda didn’t look round. Her mouth did something. “We had fields too,” she said. “Once. Before my gran. There was more light then. You could grow things.” She looked at the pool. “Nothing grows now but the moss.”

Plink. The drip. Fourteen.

“Fourteen,” said Edda and Newish. At the same time.

Edda turned her head, slowly, and looked at her for the first time. With her clouded enormous eyes.

“There,” she said. “You see.”

On the fifth fall, she asked Edda about the strays.

She’d been wanting to since the first night. Since Aleaka had come back out of the dark with the goat, white, and said they’re saying whose. Since Edda had said, at the pool, with all thirty-six of them round her, lights don’t talk. Since Hana’s light had gone into a lantern and started to hum.

She asked it straight. She asked everything straight. She’d never learned another way.

“Where do they come from? The strays. In the beginning. Before anybody had one.”

Edda didn’t answer for a long time. Newish thought she’d gone to sleep. Then:

“Nobody knows,” said Edda. “They were here before us. That’s what my gran said. The Hollow was full of them, and then people came, and the lights came to the children.” She was quiet. “People always ask where they come from. Nobody ever asks where they go.”

“Where do they go?”

“Out. When you die.” Edda’s thin hand lifted a little toward the dark. “And then to the next child. Mostly.” She was quiet again, a long time. “Not all of them.”

Newish waited. She was good at waiting. Nobody believed that about her, but she was. You couldn’t count if you couldn’t wait.

“My gran said,” said Edda, slowly, “that in her gran’s time, some springs, a stray would go up the stair.”

Newish went very still.

“Up the stair. The one you came down. Not down to the river. Up.” Edda’s eyes were half closed. “The stair goes up out of the Hollow, the other side, past the far roots. To somewhere else. Nobody goes. But the strays did. Some of them. Once in a long while. A stray would go to the bottom of the stair and stop there, the way they stop at a cradle. And hum. And then go up. And not come back.” She was quiet. “My gran’s gran said they’d been called. That somebody up there needed one more than we did. And they went to be theirs.”

Behind her, on the walkway, Newish heard Aleaka stop breathing.

She hadn’t known Aleaka was there. She looked round. Aleaka was standing at the end of the walkway with the goat, very still. And beside her, Hana. With the little lantern on her crown, humming.

“Leaka,” said Newish. Quietly.

“I know.” Aleaka’s voice was very strange. “I know. I’ve been— Hana. Can I—“

Hana looked at her. Then she knelt down on the walkway, by Edda’s root, and bowed her head, so that the little iron lantern on the front of her crown was in reach.

Aleaka put out one finger. Not touching. Just close. Her eyes shut.

Heed. When something calls you, you hear what it means.

The light in the lantern hummed.

Newish watched Aleaka’s face. It did a lot of things. Then it went still. Then her eyes opened, and they were wet, and she took her finger away from the lantern very slowly, as if it were hot.

“What,” said Hana. Without lifting her head. “Leaka. What did it—“

“It’s not whose,” said Aleaka. “Like theirs. It’s not asking.” She swallowed. “It’s—“ She stopped. She looked out at the dark beyond the roots, where the strays drifted, small and pale and far apart. “When it hums at them. At the strays. I’ve been listening for three falls. It’s the same thing every time. I didn’t— I didn’t want to say. Till I was sure.”

“Say,” said Newish.

Aleaka looked at Hana’s bowed head. At the little lantern.

“I was one,” said Aleaka. “That’s what it means. When it hums at them. I was one of you.”

Nobody said anything.

Out over the pool, a stray drifted past, high up, small and pale. Hana’s light hummed at it. Very softly. One low note.

And Edda, on her root, with her eyes shut and her tiny glim at her ear, said, so quietly Newish nearly didn’t hear it:

“Somebody taught it to talk.”

She told Chris. Chris wrote it down in THE LIGHTS, under Yet, under Don’t, under Milli asked it nicely. It did.

Day 754. Edda: some strays went UP the stair. “Called.” Didn’t come back.

Hana’s light, by Aleaka (Heed): “I was one.”

He looked at it for a long time. Then he drew a line under it, and an arrow going up the side of the page, off the top. And didn’t write anything at the end of the arrow.

“That’s a clue,” said Chris. “Not an answer.” He tapped the arrow with his pen. “Somebody up there. Took them. Taught them to talk.” He looked at the spark on his own shoulder, in its lantern. Not humming. “And put them on us.”

“Why?”

“I don’t know.” He shut the notebook. “That’s the first thing on this floor I’ve wanted to know more than how high it is.”

Higu, beside him, had her rock out. The new-new one. She wrote something on the left-hand side, the side for where they were, which on this floor had nothing on it but a drawing of a pool and some roots and a lot of question marks. She wrote it small, at the top, in the corner. An arrow. Going up.

She didn’t write anything at the end of it either.

Edda went on the seventh fall.

Newish knew before anybody. Before Isel. She was sitting on the walkway by the root the way she’d sat every fall since the third, counting with Edda under her breath, and on the ninth drip of the seventh fall Edda didn’t count.

Her lips didn’t move.

Newish counted alone. Nine. She looked up. Edda was looking at the pool. Her eyes were open. She was breathing. Slowly. Very slowly. Newish counted that too, without meaning to, the way she’d counted Ezra’s breathing in the shade on fourteen.

Too slow.

“Isel,” said Newish. Not loudly. “Go and get your gran’s people.”

Isel was sitting at the bottom of the root with her empty lantern in her lap. She looked up. She looked at her gran. And her face did a thing Newish had seen on a lot of faces, on a lot of floors, and never got used to.

She got up and ran.

They came. Not running. Lanthorn didn’t run. They came along the walkways and down the ladders and out of the roots, quietly, the whole town, two hundred people with their glims in their lanterns, humming, and stood round the pool. And the thirty-six came too. Ice with Thea and Xola. Gabe. Chris with his notebook shut. All of them. And stood at the back.

Corwen came and knelt at the bottom of the root. His sister Nesta knelt beside him. Isel climbed up into Edda’s lap, which was so small she nearly didn’t fit, and put her arms round her gran’s neck, and stayed there.

Edda’s glim was at her ear.

It was the smallest light Newish had ever seen. Smaller than Hana’s had been in her cupped hands. A grain. But it was humming. She could hear it. Under everything. Very faintly.

Corwen looked up at Newish. Then at the pool. Then back at her.

“She keeps the count,” said Corwen. Quietly. “When somebody goes. We sing their glim out on the drip. On the beat. So it goes out on the count and doesn’t get lost. And so the moths don’t hear it go.” He was quiet. “It goes out quiet, if it goes on the beat. If it goes off the beat it goes out loud, and they come.” He looked at his mother’s mother on her root, with her eyes open and her breath going slow. “She keeps the count. Nobody else can. Not on the drip. We all drift.”

Newish didn’t say anything.

“Isel’s learning,” said Corwen. “She’s not there yet.”

And Edda, on her root, with Isel’s arms round her neck and her eyes on the pool, said, in a voice that was hardly anything at all:

“The one with the mouth.”

Everybody looked at Newish.

She didn’t move. She couldn’t. She sat on the walkway with her hands in her lap and her crooked little lantern by her ear, empty, and her spark beside it, and everybody looking at her. Two hundred people with big dark eyes. And thirty-five people she’d known for years, who’d told her to shut up a thousand times.

On fourteen, at the gate of Bramble Piece, Annet Sallow had looked at her and said count, then. And she’d counted. Twelve men in a line in the heat and an old man falling behind and a boy who laughed. She’d been so frightened she’d been sick in a hedge afterwards. Nobody knew that. Not even Hana.

This was worse.

You didn’t get a second go. Not at this. On fourteen if you lost the count you said hold and started again. This was somebody’s whole life going out over a pool in the dark, and if she drifted, it’d go out loud, and they’d come.

“I can’t,” said Newish.

Edda turned her head on the root. It took a long time. She looked at Newish with her clouded eyes.

“You’ve been on it,” she said. “Since you came down the path. Every one.”

“That’s different. That’s just— That’s me. That’s just what I do. I don’t even—“

“That’s why,” said Edda.

Newish looked at her for a long time.

Then she looked round. She didn’t know what for. Somebody. Somebody to tell her she could stop. Hana. Milli. Jared with his smith’s hands. Aleaka. They were all looking at her, and none of them said anything, and none of them looked away.

And at the back, by the roots, Ice.

He was standing beside Thea with his pole and the empty lopsided lantern on his belt. He was looking at her. He didn’t nod. He didn’t do anything. He just looked at her the way he looked at all of them now, at the foot of a stair.

As if he were glad she was the one there.

Newish breathed in.

Plink.

The drip. Ten.

“Ten,” said Newish. Out loud. On it.

She counted Edda out.

It took four drips. It took four hours and twenty-four minutes, near enough, and Newish never once looked at Milli to check. She knew. She’d always known. She just hadn’t trusted it.

On every drip, she said the number. Out loud. On the drip, as the ring went out across the black water. Eleven. Twelve. And between the drips she counted under her breath, the long count, sixty-six minutes of it, so that she’d be on it when the next one came. And on every drip, the whole of Lanthorn hummed.

That was the song. She hadn’t known. Nobody had told her. Corwen had said we sing it out and she’d thought of words, a hymn, something like that. It wasn’t. It was a hum. Two hundred people round a pool, humming one low note, on the drip, on her number, and stopping. And their glims in their lanterns humming the same note. And then nothing, for sixty-six minutes. And then the drip, and her voice, thirteen, and the hum again.

And Hana’s light humming with them. She heard it. On the crown, in the walnut lantern, humming the same note on the drip like it had been doing it all its life.

And then Milli’s. And Aleaka’s. Then others. She heard them come in, round the pool, drip by drip. Not all of them. Some. A few more each time. The thirty-five sparks in their little lanterns, humming the Hollow’s one note on her count.

Not hers.

Hers sat by her ear, silent, outside its crooked lantern, and didn’t.

Fourteen.

Edda’s glim lifted.

Newish saw it go. On the drip. Exactly on it. As the ring went out across the black water, the grain of light by Edda’s ear came away from her, slowly, the way the spark had come up out of Hana’s hands into the lantern. It hung in the air above her face. Above Isel’s face, pressed into her neck.

Edda’s eyes were closed.

She’d stopped breathing. Newish hadn’t counted the last one. She’d been counting the drip.

The town hummed. Two hundred people. Thirty-six. The note went out across the pool and up into the dark.

And the glim went with it.

Up, slowly, from the root, out over the black water, on the hum, rising, getting no bigger and no smaller, a grain of pale light going up and out over the pool toward the dark where the strays were. Quietly. Exactly on the count. Not a sound.

Out in the dark beyond the roots, a dozen strays turned toward it. Whose. She didn’t need Heed to hear it. They turned to it the way they’d turned to the goat.

It went among them.

And then she couldn’t tell which one it was.

The hum stopped.

Nobody moved. The ring from the drip reached the edges of the pool, and the roots, and stopped. Isel was crying into her gran’s neck without any noise. Corwen had his hand on the root. Nesta had her hand on his back.

Newish sat on the walkway and didn’t count anything.

For the first time since the white room, she let the count go. She just let it go out of her, the way the glim had gone out. Sixty-six minutes of it. She didn’t know where the next drip was. She didn’t care. She sat there in the dark with her hands in her lap, not counting, and after a while she realised her face was wet.

“I’m not crying,” said Newish. To nobody. “I’m—“

She stopped. It wasn’t true. She wasn’t counting. There was nothing to count.

She was just crying.

Ice sat down beside her at some point.

She didn’t see him come. She looked round and he was there, on the walkway, not close, about a pole’s width away, with Tuli’s pole across his knees and Gabe’s terrible empty lantern hanging off his belt. He didn’t say anything. He was looking at the pool.

She cried for a bit longer. He let her.

Then she wiped her face on her sleeve and said, because somebody had to say something and it was always her:

“Go on, then.”

“Go on what?”

“Except Newish. You say it. Every time. On every call. For years. Love you guys, except Newish.” Her voice wobbled. “Go on. It’d help. Honestly. I’d love it if you said something rude right now.”

Ice looked at her for a long time.

“You counted her out,” he said.

“Somebody had to.”

“Nobody else could.” He looked at the pool. “Two hundred people and thirty-five of us and she asked for you. The one with the mouth.” His mouth did something. “She was right. You were on it. Every one.”

Newish didn’t say anything.

“I used to say it,” said Ice. Slowly. As if he’d only just thought of it. “Except Newish. Because you’d yell. And it was funny when you yelled. And because—“ He stopped. He looked at the empty lantern on his belt. “Because you were the one who’d always say the thing. Out loud. The thing everybody else was thinking. And I never said anything out loud. Ever. So I—“ He stopped again. “I don’t know. It was easier to say except Newish than to say I was glad somebody did.”

She stared at him.

“Love you,” said Ice. To the pool. “Including you.”

There was a long silence.

“Rude,” said Newish. Her voice cracked right down the middle. “That’s so rude. You can’t just— After years— You can’t just say that like a normal—“

“Mostly,” said Ice.

She hit him on the arm. Not hard. Then she put her face in her hands and laughed and cried at the same time, which she’d never done before and didn’t recommend, and Ice sat a pole’s width away and let her, and didn’t say anything else.

By her ear, something moved.

She felt it before she saw it. A warmth, close, on the side of her face, the way you feel somebody lean in. She took her hands away from her face and turned her head.

Her spark had gone into the lantern.

Salty’s crooked one. The little walnut cage hanging off her collar with its ring a bit wonky at the top. The spark was in the middle of it, scattered to a hundred points, faint, all round her ear.

And it was humming.

Not the Hollow’s note. Not the long low one. It was humming in a beat. A little one. Under its breath. One, two, three. One, two, three. Over and over. Quietly. Like somebody who’d been listening to somebody count for two years and just picked it up.

Newish sat very still.

“Oh,” she said. “Oh, you idiot. You’ve been listening the whole time.”

The light hummed. One, two, three.

“Two years,” said Newish. “You’ve been sitting there two years listening to me count and you never once— Not once—“ She put her hand up to the lantern. Not touching. Just close. The way Aleaka had. “You could’ve said.”

One, two, three.

Beside her, very quietly, Ice laughed.

The Moth came up at the end.

Not then. Later. Four drips later, at the end of the fall, when the town had gone back into the roots and Isel had gone to sleep in Nesta’s arms and Corwen had hung his grandmother’s old empty lantern, the one his mother had made, on the hook at the top of Isel’s ladder.

Thea did it.

Newish saw it all. She was still on the walkway. She hadn’t gone in. She didn’t want to yet.

Thea came down to the edge of the pool with the lantern. The big one. The amber one from the Fen, the one that leaned. She’d carried it wrapped in her cloak for ten falls, against her chest, everywhere, and slept with it under her and never once unwrapped it. And she came down to the pool where Edda’s root was, empty now, with the old woman’s cushion still on it, and stood there for a long time.

Then she knelt by the root.

Newish knew why. Everybody would know, later, because Thea told them. Edda had asked. On the fifth fall, in the gap between two drips, she’d looked at the bundle in Thea’s arms with her clouded eyes and said that one’s not a glim. That’s somebody’s whole light. And Thea had said yes. And Edda had said I’d like to see it. Before. And Thea had said it’ll bring them. And Edda had said I know. Before, then. Not now.

And then there’d been no before. There hadn’t been time.

So Thea knelt by the root, and unwrapped the cloak. Just for a moment. Just the top of it. Just enough that the old woman’s cushion could see.

Amber light came out over the pool.

Not much. A hand’s width of cloth folded back. But it was the brightest thing that had been on that floor since Hana’s false light, and it didn’t scatter. It didn’t come out in a hundred points. It came out in one. Warm and gold and steady, lying across the black water and the roots and Edda’s empty cushion. And leaning.

Toward the back of the pool. Toward the walkway. Toward Ice.

Thea knelt there with it for a slow count of ten. Newish counted. She couldn’t help it.

On ten, Thea folded the cloak back over it.

And far below them, past the edge of the roots, past the last white trunks, down the slope into the dark where the Hollow went down and down and no strays went at all, something opened its eyes.

Newish saw them. Everybody who was still at the pool saw them. Two lights. Far down. Very far. Round, and huge, and the colour of lamps that have nearly gone out, dull red-black, like the eyes of the moths, but so far away and so big that each one must have been the size of a house. Opening, in the dark at the bottom of everything.

Looking up.

Under the eyes, slowly, the dark moved. It wasn’t the dark. It was something in the dark, as big as the dark. Grey-brown and soft and patterned with rings. The edge of a wing. Lifting. Just a little. The way a sleeper turns over.

Every lantern in Lanthorn went quiet at once.

Two hundred glims. Thirty-five little iron cages. Every one of them stopped humming in the same breath, the way they’d stopped on the night of the moths, and the silence came down over the pool like snow.

Over the eyes, in the dark, in front of every face at the pool, a window opened. Grey. Pale. In the corner of the eye, where they’d been opening for two years.

The eyes looked up the Hollow for a long time. At the pool. At the roots. At the place where something warm and gold and bright had been, for a count of ten.

Then they closed.

The wing went down. The dark was just dark.

Nobody breathed. Newish counted to sixty under her breath before anybody did. Then, slowly, round the pool, the lanterns started humming again. One at a time. Hers too. One, two, three.

Thea was still kneeling by the root. She had the bundle in her arms. She was holding it so tight her knuckles were white. Her face was doing nothing at all.

Ice came down the walkway. He didn’t run. He walked, steadily, past Newish, down to the edge of the pool, and knelt beside Thea. Not in front of her. Beside. On her left. And put his hand over hers, on the bundle.

“It saw,” said Thea. Very quietly.

“I know.”

“She asked. Edda. She wanted to see it. Before.” Thea’s voice didn’t change. “I said it’ll bring them. I knew. I knew and I did it anyway, for a count of ten, for a woman who was already gone.”

“I know,” said Ice.

“It saw.”

“I know.” He didn’t take his hand off hers. “It was going to. Corwen said, the first night. That one’ll bring it up from the bottom. Sooner or later.” He looked down the Hollow, into the dark, where the eyes had been. “Better it’s for her.”

Thea didn’t say anything for a long time.

Then she leaned her head on his shoulder. Just for a moment. The way the lantern leaned.

“I’m not going to step in front of you,” she said. “When it comes.”

“I know.”

“You’re not going to hold it up and bring it all to you.”

Ice was quiet.

“I asked Corwen,” he said. “If I held up a light. And everything came to me. I asked if it would help.” He looked at the bundle in her arms. “He said it’d help tonight. And then the Moth would come up.” He was quiet. “I put it down.”

“Good,” said Thea.

“It’s coming anyway.”

“I know.” Thea lifted her head off his shoulder. She looked down the Hollow. Into the dark. “Then we’ll find out how. All of us.” She looked at him. “That’s what you said. On the stair. We’ll find out.”

“Higu said that.”

“Higu said it. You meant it.” She almost smiled. “That’s new too.”

Newish sat on the walkway above them with her crooked lantern humming one, two, three by her ear, and watched them kneel at the edge of the black pool, side by side, with the bundle between them, the amber light leaking out of the folds and leaning toward him.

Above them all, out of the dark, falling, a tiny bright point. Turning. Glinting. All the way down.

Plink.

A ring went out across the pool. All the way to the roots.

“One,” said Newish. Out loud. On it.

And all round the pool, very quietly, in their lanterns, the lights hummed.
