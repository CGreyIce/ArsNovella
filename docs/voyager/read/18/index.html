At the top of a hundred and twelve steps, with his hand on a wooden door and the cold already coming round its edges, Ice felt the name take.

It didn’t hurt. He’d half thought it might. It was more like the feeling of a coat settling on your shoulders when somebody helps you into it from behind: a small weight, arriving, fitting. The warm stone disc in his vest pocket went cool. Then it was gone, not lost, just finished, the way a candle’s finished when the wick’s burned down.

[ Name set: Voyager ]

He stood there in the dark at the top of the stair and read it twice.

“How does it feel?” said Alea.

“Like nothing.” He thought about it. “Like a jacket.”

“It suits you.”

“You can’t see it.”

“I can see your window. It suits your window.” A pause. “Voyager · Lv 6 · Knight. It looks like somebody who knows where he’s going.”

“I do know where I’m going.”

“I know. That’s why it suits you.”

He opened the door.

Floor 2 hit him in the face like a bucket of snow.

He’d forgotten. That was the stupid thing. Ten years, and he’d forgotten the exact quality of this cold. It had been the first shock, the first time: thirty-six people in summer clothes coming out of a door onto a mountainside in the middle of a snowfall, and every one of them gasping at once. Calvin had sworn so loudly an owl fell out of a tree.

There was nobody to swear now. Just him, in a white shirt with the sleeves rolled up and a tactical vest that was very good at holding flints and very bad at holding heat. In front of him, under a low grey sky that snowed, was a whole new world.

Pines. Pines going up and down and on forever, black-green and bowed with snow, filling a long steep valley. A frozen river at the bottom, winding like a dropped silver chain. Far down, where the river widened, there was a smudge of smoke and the dark shape of a wooden stockade. Larchwick. He wasn’t going there.

He rolled his sleeves down. It didn’t help at all.

“Hut first,” said Alea. “Then you can be miserable.”

“I’m not miserable.”

“Your lips are blue.”

“They’re my natural colour. It’s an avatar.”

“Your avatar has pink lips. I’ve been looking at them for ten years. They’re blue.” The fox appeared for a moment on the snow at his feet, made of teal and white light, and immediately sank in up to her chest, which she clearly hadn’t expected. She looked up at him with great dignity. “Hut,” she said.

The hut was where it had always been: two kilometres up the eastern slope, tucked into the lee of a rock outcrop, so buried in snow that you’d walk past it if you didn’t know. The first time, thirty-six of them had walked past it four times before Higu noticed the chimney.

Ice didn’t walk past it. He went straight to it, dug the door out with his shield, and shouldered it open. And there in the cold dark, hanging from a peg on the back wall where some hunter had left it years ago and never come back for it, was a coat.

It was enormous. Grey wolf-fur, inside and out, stiff with age, reaching his knees. It smelled of smoke and dead animal and a long time. He put it on over the vest and stood in the dark hut and shook, very hard, for nearly a minute, while the warmth slowly found him.

“The first time,” said Alea, “Gabe got this coat.”

“I remember.”

“He won it on a coin toss with Xav. Xav was furious for a week.”

“I remember, Alea.”

“I’m just saying. Now you’ve got it. Gabe’s going to have to buy one in town like everybody else.” A pause. “Xav will be pleased.”

He laughed. It came out as a cloud of steam.

The first Rimehounds found him at midday, on the slope below the hut, and he got it wrong.

He’d known he would. He’d known it the way he’d known about the goat. His head had ten years of these wolves in it. His body had a morning. And the coat, the wonderful, warm, enormous coat, weighed as much as a small child and hung off his shoulders like wet sailcloth, and every time he turned, it turned a quarter-second later.

There were three. One came out in front of him, low on the snow, snarling, ice-blue eyes fixed on his face. And he looked at it. He knew better, and he looked at it anyway, because something snarling at your face is very hard not to look at. The second one came round his left side without a sound and took him in the back of the leg.

He went down on one knee in the snow. The third one went for his throat.

The shield came up on its own. That was the ten years: his arm moved before he’d asked it to, and the wolf’s teeth closed on the iron rim instead of on him. He felt the shock all the way to his shoulder. He got the sword round and under and into something, and the wolf yelped and let go. The second one was still on his leg. He hit it with the pommel, twice, hard, between the ears, and it let go too.

“Clockwise,” said Alea. “Ice. Wendy’s drawing. They go round clockwise.”

“I know.”

“Then put your back to something.”

There was nothing to put his back to. He was on an open slope with the snow up to his shins. So he did the next best thing: he turned, so that the direction they were going round was the direction he was facing. He stopped looking at the one in front, and watched the gap to his right where the next one would come from.

It came from there. He was waiting for it.

It took a long time and it wasn’t pretty. Twice he had to stop in the middle of it and put his left hand flat on his chest and stand in the ember’s light, while the wolves circled and waited for him to fall over. But in the end there were three white shapes lying still in the snow, and one young man in a dead hunter’s coat standing over them, bleeding from the leg and breathing like a bellows.

He looked down at them for a while.

“Wendy worked that out,” he said. “The first time. With a pencil, on a log, at the back. She drew a terrible picture of a wolf going round a stick.”

“I know. I logged the picture.”

“She’s going to work it out again. Down there. In a day or two.” He wiped his sword on the snow. “She’s always the one who works out where things are coming from.”

“You could leave her a hint,” said Alea.

He thought about the cracked stone in the Fold. One’s a warning. Two’s a pattern.

“She doesn’t need one,” he said. “She’s Wendy.”

He didn’t open the logs until the afternoon.

He’d been not opening them since the Fold. All the way through the night in the ascension room, all the way up the stair, all morning in the snow, he’d felt them there, at the edge of his window. A little tab, marked with a number that kept going up. He’d moved through the day around them, the way you move through a house around a room you don’t go into.

But by the afternoon he’d killed three Rimehounds on the lower slopes, his first of this life. They were white wolves, waist-high, with ice-blue eyes, and they never came at you from the front if they could help it. His hands had finally stopped shaking from the cold. He was sitting on a rock in the wolf-fur coat, eating the last half of Mother Crane’s loaf, and there was nothing else to do.

“Alea,” he said.

“Yes.”

“The logs. From last night.”

She was quiet.

“All of them?” she said.

“Start with the whisper.”

He knew there was a whisper. He’d known since dawn. There’d been a small envelope in the corner of his window when he came out of the stair and the name took, the way a phone lights up when a message comes in. He’d looked at it, and looked away.

Alea didn’t say anything else. She just opened it.

Ice sat on the rock and read it.

Then he read it again.

It was sent at four in the morning. He could see the timestamp. Four in the morning, on the second day, in Hearthstead, while he’d been asleep in the ascension room with half a loaf next to a fox made of light. Gabe had been awake. Of course he had. Gabe had been sitting up in the dark somewhere with his window open, typing with two fingers, and he’d sent it.

One word.

Ice put the bread down on the rock.

He opened a reply.

He didn’t decide to. His hand did it, the way his feet had done things in the Fold: the reply box, a small white rectangle with a blinking line in it, under Gabe’s message. He looked at the line blinking.

He typed: ok

Two letters. Lower case. He looked at them.

One word, Gabe had said. You don’t have to say anything else. That was all. Two letters, and Gabe would sleep tonight. He’d stop sending DECLAN into World every hour. He’d stop sitting at the long table with his porridge going cold.

And then.

Ice knew Gabe. He’d known him since Gabe was a skinny thirteen-year-old in the second row of a school choir, and he knew exactly what Gabe would do with ok. He’d read it a hundred times. He’d show it to Deston. He’d know, absolutely and for certain, that the man at the top of the rankings was Declan. Then he’d stand up from the long table and go up the valley to the Fold, and kill the ram if he had to do it alone, and climb. He’d climb after him. Floor 2, floor 3. Faster than he was ready. Faster than any of them were ready. With the whole of Xav’s party behind him, because Xav would never let him go alone.

To floor 4.

Ice’s thumb was over send.

Alea didn’t say anything. She didn’t tell him not to. She’d never once in ten years told him not to do something he’d already decided, and she’d learned that he decided slowly, and she could wait. She just sat in the corner of his window, the little fox shape, and watched.

He took his thumb away.

He deleted the k. Then the o. The reply box sat there empty with the line blinking in it.

He closed it.

Then he sat on the rock in the snow in the dead hunter’s coat and put both his hands over his face, and stayed like that for a long time.

“There’s more,” said Alea eventually. Very gently. “You don’t have to.”

“Show me.”

“Ice—“

“I said show me.”

It went on. Gabe typing the same word every few minutes, between everybody else’s questions, like a man knocking on a door he knows somebody’s behind. Then a gap. A long gap, more than an hour, where Gabe didn’t type anything at all. Ice could see it in the timestamps, and he could see Deston in it. Deston would have sat down next to him and said something low and level and true, and Gabe would have stopped.

And then, at the very end, the last line before the night went quiet:

[ World · Gabe: ok. careful. we’re being careful. ]

Ice read that one for a very long time.

Careful. He’d written it on the back of a card with a fingertip, in a white room, before anyone’s eyes were open. Be careful. And sometime in the middle of the first night, sitting at a long table in the Copper Spoon, Deston had said something to Gabe. Gabe had stopped knocking. And he’d typed that word back, into nothing, to a dash at the top of a list.

We’re being careful.

“He read it,” Ice said. His voice didn’t work properly. “Deston. He read the note. He told Gabe.”

“Yes,” said Alea.

“They’re being careful.”

“Yes.”

“Then it was worth it.” He wiped his face on the sleeve of the dead hunter’s coat, which smelled of smoke and wolf. “The ok. Not sending it. If they’re being careful, then it was worth it.”

Alea was quiet for a moment.

“I’m going to log that you said that,” she said, “and I’m going to read it back to you the next time you’re sitting on a rock crying.”

“That’s fair.”

“It’s not fair. It’s just what’s going to happen.”

He picked up the last of the bread and ate it.

The first camp was four hundred metres east of the stair door, along the treeline, under the biggest pine he’d ever seen.

He’d slept there the first time too. Not alone. With all thirty-five of the others, packed in under its branches like sardines. Nobody had wanted to go down to Larchwick in the dark, with the snow coming down and wolves howling in the valley, and the great pine had kept the snow off them all night like a roof. In the morning there’d been frost in everyone’s hair. Maigo had complained loudly that it was cruel to make a tree sleep under a tree.

There was no one under it now. Just the needles, dry and brown and deep. And the snow all round, and the dark coming down.

He built a fire in a ring of stones. He went down the slope a little way, to a place where the snow lay thin over a south-facing bank, and dug with his gloved hands until he found them, just where they’d been ten years ago. Ten years from now. He’d stopped trying to say it right. Wild onions: small, hard, white, smelling sharply green even half-frozen in the earth.

He sat by the fire under the great pine and peeled them.

Top and tail. The cut down one side. The skin coming away in a single papery piece if you were lucky. He was lucky every time. He’d done this thousands of times, on a hundred floors, in caves and camps and the ruins of things. And every single time, somewhere at the back of his head, a short wide old woman in a stained apron was standing behind him with her arms folded, waiting to see if he was going to ruin anything.

He made broth in Mother Crane’s pot, with snow-water and onions and a strip of Rimehound he’d cut and didn’t think too hard about. It was terrible. It was much better than his first broth. He drank it all.

[ Onion Broth (yours) · warmth · cold resistance · six hours ]

“It’s not bad,” said Alea.

“It’s terrible.”

“It’s not as bad as the first one.”

“That’s a low bar.”

“That’s the bar,” said Alea, quoting him, and he laughed into the pot.

He left the onion skins in a little heap in the snow by the ring of stones, without thinking about it, the way you leave things when you’re tired and you’ve done something a thousand times. He didn’t notice he’d done it. He wouldn’t think about it again for days.

He lay under the great pine in the dead hunter’s coat, with his shield beside him and his sword beside that, and the fire burning down. Through the branches he could see the low grey ceiling, faintly lit from somewhere, still snowing.

Somewhere down there in the dark, a long way below, the valley went down to the Fold, and the Fold went down to Hearthstead, and in Hearthstead thirty-five people were asleep. Or not. One of them, at least, was probably not.

“Alea,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Will you keep the whisper?”

“I keep everything.”

“I know. I mean— keep it somewhere I can find it.” He looked up at the dark branches. “When this is over. When they’re all upstairs, and safe, and I can stop. I want to answer it.”

“With ok?”

“With ok,” said Ice. “And then about a thousand other words. And then I’ll let him hit me.”

The fox was quiet in the corner of his window for a while.

“I’ll keep it,” she said. “I’ll keep it at the top. Where you’ll see it every morning.”

“That’s going to hurt.”

“Yes,” said Alea. “I think it’s supposed to.”

He closed his eyes. Under the great pine, four hundred metres east of the stair, in the snow, with a heap of onion skins by the fire and a name on him that didn’t belong to anyone yet, he slept.
