Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 2: One Floor Ahead

Chapter 50

The Worn Place

3,759 words · 16 min read

Xav couldn’t see it. He sat in it anyway.

Hyvred had shown him on the sixty-seventh day, the morning after the furnace hall. She’d walked him up the long stair to the high terrace, all the way to the door at the top. Then she’d stopped by the wall just beside it and pointed at the red stone, without touching it. There. Back against the wall. Knees up. He sat there every evening. It’s gone right into the stone.

Xav had looked. He’d seen red stone. A bit of wall. A bit of terrace. Dust, swept, or blown clean by the wind. Nothing else.

“I can’t see it,” he’d said.

“I know.” Hyvred had wiped her eyes. “I just wanted somebody else to know where it was.”

So now, in the evenings, after the day’s work, Xav came up the long stair and sat in it.

He sat the way she’d said. Back against the wall, just to the left of the door. Knees up. Arms loose over them. He didn’t know if he had it exactly right. He didn’t suppose it mattered. He sat in the worn place in the red dusk and looked at what Declan had looked at, for three weeks, every evening, before any of them came.

The door was right beside him. That was the first thing he noticed. Close enough to touch. Anybody coming up the two hundred and four steps from floor 3 would have opened it and stepped out onto the terrace almost on top of whoever was sitting here. You’d see them before they saw you. You’d see their face in the first second, coming out of the dark of the stair into the red.

Then the terrace. Then the long stair going down from it to the first square. He could see the whole length of it from here. Every step. Then the square itself, twenty metres across, the dry fountain, the long grey shape beside it. Then the four storeys of empty windows round the square, and the roofs above them. Every roof. Every parapet. He could see them all from here, the way you’d want to, if you’d spent six weeks learning that something lived on them.

And beyond the square, down, the whole Red City going down in steps toward the dry river. And at the very bottom, small, black, the furnace hall, with its chimney going up through the rock.

Xav sat and looked at all of it, and read it the way he read a room.

Door. Stair. Square. Roofs. Furnace.

From here, a man could watch the door every evening, waiting for it to open. And see the stair the people would come down when it did. And the square where something had once been waiting for them. And the roofs where it had lived. And the hall at the bottom where the last thing on the floor stood in the dark, which he’d already killed.

Everything he’d have needed to see, if he’d been waiting for someone to come through a door. And everything he’d have needed to check, every single evening, to be sure that when they did, there’d be nothing left between them and the bottom of the floor.

He sat here and checked, Xav thought. Every night. Door, stair, square, roofs, hall. And then back to the door.

He knew that pattern. He’d watched a man do it at a table once, in their first year of poly, before a group presentation he was nervous about. Check the slides. Check the notes. Check the clock. Check the door. Back to the slides. Over and over, for an hour, with his leg jiggling under the table, until the lecturer came in and he stood up and was suddenly, completely fine, and funny, and made the whole room laugh.

Declan had always checked everything, right up until the moment he moved. And then he moved fast, without stopping, and never looked back. In every game, every round, every door. Check, check, check, go.

He’d never once, in all the years Xav had known him, waited.

On the eighty-ninth evening, Thea came up the stair while he was sitting there.

She didn’t say anything. She had the little lantern in one hand and a small cloth-bound book in the other, the one she’d bought in Reedmoor, and she came across the terrace and sat down against the wall a little way along from him. Not in the worn place. Next to it. Two metres off, the same as him. She put the lantern down on the stone between her feet. It glowed amber, soft, the only warm light on the whole of floor 4.

She opened the book on her knees and wrote something in it.

Xav didn’t ask. He’d learned on the first day, in the white room, that Thea told you things when she’d finished thinking them, and not before. He looked at the door, and the stair, and the square.

She’d been up here every evening since the House of Ash. He’d seen her. Sometimes before him, sometimes after. Always with the lantern. Always sitting a little way off from the worn place, never in it. Always writing in the book and closing it when somebody came near.

She didn’t close it now. She just wrote, and then stopped, and sat with the pen still on the page, looking at the door.

“He sat there for three weeks,” she said.

“I know.”

“Waiting for us.” She didn’t look at him. “Hyvred says it’s like a stain. Every evening, in the same place. She says you can see the shape of his back on the wall.”

“I know,” said Xav. “I can’t see it.”

“Neither can I.” She turned the lantern a little with her foot, so its light fell on the stone between them. “I just come up here because he did.”

They sat for a while.

“He learned it from me,” said Xav.

She looked at him then.

“Waiting.” Xav kept his eyes on the door. “He never used to. Not for anything. Every door, he went straight through, shield up. Every fight. You’d say wait and he’d already be gone.” He was quiet for a moment. “It took me about a year at poly to get him to wait half a second at a door. Half a second. That’s all I ever got. Wait, then move once. He used to say it drove him mad.”

“Rui Xiang said a week,” said Thea. “Behind a wall on the north terrace. Before he went into the square.”

“Yes.”

“And three weeks here.”

“Yes.”

Thea looked at the worn place beside her, the stone she couldn’t see anything on. Then at Xav.

“So who taught him the rest?” she said.

Xav didn’t answer. He didn’t know. He’d been asking himself since Rui Xiang came down off the roof.

Thea wrote something in her book. One line. She looked at it for a long time. Then she closed it, not hurriedly, just closed it, and put her hand flat on the cover.

“Calvin says you made a face,” she said. “On the first morning. At the Spoon. When the name came up on the board.”

Xav kept looking at the door.

“Calvin talks too much,” he said.

“Calvin talks exactly enough.” She didn’t push. She just sat with her hand on the book, waiting, the way he sat. He realised, after a while, that she’d learned that from somewhere too. “I’m not asking you to tell me. I’m just saying I noticed that Calvin noticed.”

“I know.”

“When you want to,” said Thea.

She picked up the lantern and stood. She looked down at the worn place one last time, as if she could see something there after all. Then she went back down the long stair toward the square, with the little amber light going down with her step by step until it was lost among the red.

Xav sat in the worn place and watched it go.

So who taught him the rest?

He let himself think about it that night.

He’d been not thinking about it for eighty-nine days. Since the first morning, at the long table at the Copper Spoon. Somebody had read the name off the rankings out loud, and he’d felt a door open in his head and very carefully closed it again. He’d been good at not thinking about it. He was good at waiting. He’d said could be anyone to Calvin, and gone back to his porridge, and not opened that door again.

He opened it now. On the high terrace, alone, with his back against the wall in the place where Declan had sat, in the middle of the night that looked exactly like the evening.

A classroom in Singapore that smelled of whiteboard markers. The third week of their first term. A lecturer with a slide that was mostly black, and in the middle of it, so small you had to stand right in front of the screen, a single pale blue dot. That’s us. That’s the Earth. That photo was taken from four billion miles away, by a probe that had been travelling for thirteen years. It turned its camera round and took a picture of home, and then it kept going.

And Xav, who’d been in that class three weeks and hadn’t said much to anyone, had leaned over to the boy sitting next to him and whispered. He didn’t know why. He’d never been someone who whispered in lectures. He’d just wanted somebody to know.

It’s still going, you know. They launched it in the seventies. It’s past everything now. Outside the whole solar system. It’s still sending messages back. Every day. Really faint. They need dishes the size of stadiums just to hear it.

And the boy next to him, who’d been talking to everyone in the class for three weeks until they all knew each other, had gone quiet. He’d looked at the dot on the screen for a long time. Then he’d whispered back:

What’s it saying?

Nothing, really. Just numbers. Just “I’m still here.”

That was all. That was the whole of it. A whisper in a lecture, nine years ago. Xav had told him about the golden record later, the greetings and the music and the heartbeat, out on the steps at lunch. Declan had listened to all of it with his chin on his fist. And then they’d never talked about it again, not once in nine years, because it wasn’t the kind of thing you talked about. It was just a thing Xav had told him once, in their third week, before they were friends.

The probe’s name was Voyager.

Xav sat very still in the worn place with his hands on his knees.

It’s a common word. Could be anyone.

It was. That was true. Half the internet had put that probe on a profile picture at some point. Somebody at the top of the rankings in a tower in another world, calling themselves Voyager, could be anyone.

But it wasn’t anyone.

He knew who’d peeled the onions at the Spoon on the first morning. Who’d kept the chapel’s vigil before any of them knew there was a chapel, and stood in the doorway, and held it, because he always tanks. Who’d left a wolf-fur coat folded on a bench on floor 2 that fitted Gabe exactly, and a lantern on a bench on floor 3 for whoever came next. Who’d gone into the first square of the Red City with a bell and his boots wrapped in cloth. Who’d stood in the Kiln’s fire, twice, in the same place, alone, and then walked up a stair on the day thirty-five people came down one, without stopping to be seen.

And he’d called himself Voyager.

A small thing, sent out ahead on its own. Further than anybody else had gone. Past every floor, into the dark, alone. Still going.

And still sending messages back. Every day. Really faint. Floor 1 has been cleared. Floor 2 has been cleared. A coat. A lantern. A dead thing by a fountain that didn’t go to light. Two dead Kilns and the shape of a man on the floor in front of them. Not words. Never words. He’d never answered Gabe’s whisper. Not once. He’d never posted in World. He’d never said a single thing to any of them.

But every floor, something came back down the stair.

I’m still here.

I’m still here.

I’m still here.

Xav put his hand over his eyes.

He’d been right in front of them the whole time. Shouting. Sending it back every day, as loud as he could, in the only way he’d let himself. And none of them had had a dish big enough to hear it. Except, maybe, the one person he’d picked the name for. The one person who’d remember a whisper in a lecture.

He picked it for me, Xav thought. So I’d know.

He sat in the worn place until the rankings said it was morning. He didn’t cry. Xav didn’t cry. But he sat there for a long time with his hand over his eyes, and when he took it away, the red dusk looked exactly the same.

He told Gabe on the ninety-third evening.

He hadn’t meant to. He’d meant to wait, the way he waited for everything, until the moment was right. But Gabe came up the stair at dusk carrying the big sacking-wrapped shape under his arm. He sat down on the other side of the worn place from Xav, the other side from where Thea sat, and started unwrapping it on his knees. And Xav looked at what was under the sacking, and stopped waiting.

It was a shield.

He’d known it was a shield. Everyone had. Gabe had been forging it every night on floor 3 at the eel-woman’s brother’s forge, for weeks, and carrying it wrapped on his back ever since, and never once used it. Azac had said it was a shield for somebody who wasn’t there yet. Nobody had asked anything else.

It was beautiful. Xav hadn’t expected that. Round, like Gabe’s, but bigger. Dark grey Fen iron, hammered smooth, with a dull sheen on it like a puddle at dusk. It had a boss in the middle shaped like a small round lamp. And all round the face, worked into the iron so finely you could only see it when the light caught it, there were little lines going outward from the lamp, like the lines you draw round a sun.

The rim was bare. Unfinished. A raw edge of iron, waiting for something.

“It needs a rim,” said Gabe. He ran his thumb along the raw edge. “I’ve been trying to work out what. Fen iron’s too soft for the rim. It’d fold the first time anything big hit it.” He reached into his pack and took out a bar of dark red metal from the Kiln. Kiln iron, heavy, still faintly warm. “This’ll do it. When I find a forge on five. Kiln iron. From the thing he stood in front of twice.” He turned the bar over. “Seems right.”

Xav looked at the shield on Gabe’s knees.

“It’s not for you,” he said.

“No,” said Gabe. “It’s not for me.”

He didn’t say who it was for. He didn’t have to.

They sat for a while, the two of them, on either side of an empty place against the wall, looking at the door.

“Gabe,” said Xav.

“Yeah.”

“When we were seventeen. In our first month at poly. Before he and I were even friends.” He kept his eyes on the door. “I told him about a space probe.”

He told it. All of it. The slide with the dot. The whisper. What’s it saying? Just “I’m still here.” The golden record on the steps at lunch. He told it in his calm voice, the one that made people trust him. He’d never been more grateful for that voice, because he wasn’t sure, the whole time, that it was going to hold.

The probe’s name was Voyager.

Gabe didn’t say anything when he’d finished. He sat with the unfinished shield on his knees and the bar of Kiln iron in his hand and looked at Xav. His face was doing something complicated.

“You’ve known,” he said. “Since the first day.”

“I’ve known since the first day,” said Xav. “I didn’t let myself think it. In case I was wrong.”

“You’re not wrong.”

“I know I’m not.” Xav looked at the worn place between them. “He picked it for me. Out of everything. So that if I saw it at the top of the rankings, I’d know. Not that it was him. We all knew it was him. So I’d know what he was doing.”

“Which is what?”

“Going on ahead,” said Xav. “On his own. Further than anybody. And sending messages back. Every floor.” His voice did something, at last, and he let it. “A coat. A lantern. That thing on the roof. The shape on the floor in the furnace hall. That’s him, Gabe. That’s him answering. Every floor, he’s been answering. Just not in words.”

Gabe sat very still.

Then he put the Kiln iron down on the shield and took the whisper window out. Xav watched him. dec. i know its you. im not mad. ok im a bit mad. just tell me youre ok. one word. please. And under it: sent. And nothing after. Ninety-three days of nothing.

Gabe looked at it for a long time.

“I’m still here,” he said quietly.

“Yes.”

“Every floor.”

“Yes.”

Gabe closed the window. He didn’t type anything. He picked up the Kiln iron and turned it over in his fingers, and then he laughed, very softly, and wiped his face with the back of his wrist.

“Course he did,” he said. “Course he did. The idiot. He couldn’t just say.”

“He’s never been able to just say,” said Xav. “Not about anything that mattered. He’d talk to a whole classroom about nothing for an hour, and then when it mattered he’d go and do something instead.”

“Peel onions,” said Gabe.

“Peel onions.”

They sat on either side of the worn place in the red dusk, and the bare-rimmed shield lay across Gabe’s knees.

They went up on the ninety-fifth day.

Everybody was twenty-three or twenty-four by then. Rui Xiang was twenty-five, and had a padlock, and was unbearable about it. Drew gave the word on the ninety-fourth evening in the hall on the first square, standing on the edge of the dry fountain so everybody could see him: tomorrow. All of us. Together. Calvin first, Rachael on his collar, me last. Same as before. And thirty-four people said yes at once, so quietly that it barely carried. Which on floor 4 meant it carried everywhere.

They had to kill the Kiln again first. It had come back the day after they’d killed it, the way bosses did, and it stood in the furnace hall between its three dead selves, glowing, waiting for them. They killed it in twenty minutes this time. They knew it now. Gabe stood in the shape. Six healers stood behind him. Deston’s threads ran gold between all of them, and when the fire stopped, Xav moved once.

There were four shapes on the floor afterwards. One on top of the other.

Then they went up the stair at the back of the hall, in a long line. Calvin first, talking. Rachael with her fist in his collar. Drew last.

Xav was near the back, with his party, behind Hyvred’s. He counted the steps. Everybody did. They’d started doing it on floor 3, all thirty-five of them, out loud together, because Hyvred had said once that he counted, and somehow after that so did they.

Thirty. Forty. Fifty. Their voices went up the stair ahead of them in the dark, low, together, bouncing off the rock.

At about a hundred, Xav felt someone fall into step beside him. Thea, with the lantern. She’d come back down the line from her party to walk with him. She didn’t say anything. She just walked beside him, counting under her breath, with the amber light going up the steps in front of both of them.

At about two hundred, Hyvred stopped counting for a moment, ahead of them. Xav heard her catch her breath. She didn’t say anything. She didn’t need to. On every step of the stair, he knew, without being able to see it, there was a mark. A warm mark, the colour of a lamp in a window. Fading now, after twenty-nine days. Somebody had gone up these stairs ahead of them on the sixty-sixth day, counting, alone.

Two hundred and thirty. Two hundred and forty.

Two hundred and forty-four.

There was a door.

Calvin stopped in front of it at the head of the line. Everybody stopped behind him. Thirty-five people standing on a stair in the dark, with Thea’s lantern and John’s little lights and nothing else.

“Okay,” said Calvin. His voice came back down the stair, quieter than it usually was. “Okay. Look at my back. Everybody. Whatever’s out there. Nobody runs.”

He put his hand on the door.

Beside Xav, Thea had stopped counting. She was standing very still on the two hundred and forty-third step, looking up the stair past everybody’s heads at the door. The lantern was held up a little in her hand, the way you hold a lamp at the top of a dark house, for somebody coming home.

Calvin opened the door.

And the wind came down the stair to meet them. Cold, and wet, and smelling of rain. The first wind any of them had felt since the white room that came from somewhere, and was going somewhere else.
