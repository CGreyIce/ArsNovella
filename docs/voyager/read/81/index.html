Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 2: One Floor Ahead

Chapter 81

The Thirty-Six

3,284 words · 14 min read

John had spent most of his first two years of poly in the corner of a screen.

That was how he thought of it, anyway. Off to one side. Declan had started streaming in their first year, with a cheap webcam clipped to the top of his monitor. He’d point it at himself and play whatever he was playing and talk. Endlessly, to anybody who’d turned up in the chat. And John had started turning up in his room on the evenings when nothing else was happening. Then most evenings. And because the room was small, and the chair was where it was, John ended up in the corner of the frame.

Off to one side. A shoulder, mostly. Sometimes half a face, if he leaned in to see the screen. A voice saying no, left, LEFT at the wrong moment. People in the chat started asking who the guy in the corner was. Declan started calling him the cameraman, even though John had never touched the camera. And for two years, nearly every hour Declan was live, John was there. In the corner. Watching. Laughing. Off to one side.

He’d liked it there. It was the best seat in the house.

And then Declan had moved to Malacca. And the Singapore friends had all drifted, at once, the way people do. Nobody said anything. Nobody decided. The calls just got further apart, and then they stopped, and then it was years.

John had thought about that a lot, on floor 2. Sitting by a cold fire under a pine tree with Gabe, looking at a little pile of onion skins. That’s a guy holding something together very carefully. With both hands. You don’t answer whispers when you’re doing that. You’d drop it. He’d said it to Gabe as if he knew. He hadn’t known. He’d just been the guy in the corner long enough to know what Declan’s face did when he was holding something.

Tonight, sitting on the cobbles in the square at dusk with everybody else, he’d watched that face pause before it spoke. And he’d known exactly what it was holding.

Drew did it at half past ten, in the long room of the inn.

He didn’t make a speech. That wasn’t Drew. He waited until everybody had come in from the square, all thirty-six of them, and Merle too, standing at the back by the door with her floury arms folded. Then he stood up on a bench at the end of the long table, so everybody could see him, and opened his window.

Not his own. The group’s.

It came up in the air above the bench, bigger than a normal window, the way group windows did. Blue, with a thin gold line round the edge. John had seen it once before, on floor 3, in the smoking hall in Reedmoor, on the fortieth day, when Drew had made it.

[ The Thirty-Five · General: Drew ]

And underneath, thirty-five names. In seven groups of five.

Everybody in the room went quiet.

“On three,” said Drew. “In Reedmoor. I made this. Because somebody had to.” He looked at the window. “And Gabe said it should be thirty-six. And I said, when he comes back, we’ll rename it. That’s a promise.” He was quiet for a moment. “And I wrote FOR NOW on the slate. In chalk. And it stayed there for six weeks, every time any of us walked into the smoking hall.”

He looked down the long table. At Declan, sitting halfway along it, between Gabe and Deston, with the shield leaning against the bench behind him and his purple jaw.

“So,” said Drew.

He touched the window. A smaller one came up, under it.

[ Invite to group: Voyager ]

Drew touched it.

And at the middle of the long table, John saw a window come up in front of Declan’s face. Small and blue. He couldn’t read it from where he was sitting. He didn’t need to.

Declan looked at it.

He didn’t touch it. Not straight away. He sat there between Gabe and Deston and looked at the little window in front of his face. John watched his hands. They were flat on the table. They’d gone very still.

He paused.

John had seen him pause four times today. In the kitchen, before it’s me. In the square, before sorry. Before not yet. Before let it look. Every time, the same. Like a man at the edge of cold water.

This one was longer.

“Dec,” said Gabe. Very quietly. Beside him.

Declan didn’t look at him. He was looking at the window.

“I’ve been a floor away,” he said. To the window, as much as anybody. “For a hundred and fifty-three days. I don’t—“ He stopped. “I don’t know how to be in one.”

Nobody said anything.

“You just press it,” said Calvin, from across the table. Very helpfully. “It’s the button on the left.”

The whole table laughed. Declan laughed too. Cracked, and sudden. He looked up at Calvin, and something in his face came loose.

He pressed it.

[ Voyager has joined the group. ]

And above Drew’s bench, the big window changed. The gold line round it flickered. The name at the top went out, letter by letter, as if somebody were rubbing out chalk. And came back.

[ The Thirty-Six · General: Drew ]

And underneath, the seven groups of five. And then, below them, on its own, an eighth line. One name. With a gap round it, where the other four should have been.

Voyager.

“A party of one,” said Hako, from somewhere. “Huh.”

Declan glanced at his right shoulder. At the empty air there. And his mouth did something.

“She says a party of one is still a party,” he said.

“Who says?”

“Alea,” said Aleaka, before Declan could. “Obviously.” She had the drawing of the fox in her lap. She’d had it in her lap all evening.

Drew stood on the bench under the window, looking up at it. At The Thirty-Six. For a long time.

Then he looked down at all of them. And grinned. The confident one, the one with the platinum streak falling over his eyes.

“Somebody thank me,” said Drew.

“Thank you, Drew,” said thirty-four people at once.

“Thank you, Drew (rude),” said Newish.

“You can’t say the brackets out loud, Newish.”

“I can say whatever I want, I’m rude.”

And then, under all of it, from halfway down the table, quietly, one more voice.

“Thank you, Drew.”

Drew stopped.

Declan was looking up at him. From the bench, between Gabe and Deston, with the shield behind him. Not laughing. Not joking. Just looking up at Drew on the bench, the way you’d look at somebody you’d wanted to say something to for a very long time.

“Nobody ever thanks the guy running it,” said Declan.

John didn’t know what it meant. He saw it hit Drew, though. He saw it go into him like a thrown stone. He saw Drew stand very still on the bench, under the window that said The Thirty-Six, and his face go through something. Something old.

Later, Higu told John what it was. A tournament. Years ago. Online. Drew had run it out of his bedroom over a long weekend, on his own, with a spreadsheet and no sleep. At the end of it, when everybody had logged off without a word, one person had stayed behind in the call. To say thank you. Nobody ever thanks the guy running it.

Drew got down off the bench.

He didn’t say anything. He walked down the long table, past everybody, to where Declan was sitting. He stood in front of him for a moment. Then he put his hand on top of Declan’s head, briefly, the way you would with a younger brother, and ruffled his hair. Hard. And walked out of the inn into the square without looking back.

Higu got up and followed him. Nobody else did.

John found Declan at the inn door at a quarter past eleven, looking out at the clocktower.

He’d come out for air. Everybody kept coming out for air, in ones and twos. The long room was too full of feeling. Declan was leaning on the doorframe with his arms folded, in the white cloak, with the shield on his back, looking up at the four lit faces across the square. Like moons. And at the dark belfry above them.

John stood beside him for a while. He didn’t say anything. He’d always been good at standing beside Declan without saying anything. It was what you did, in the corner of the frame.

Then he held up his hand.

A light came out of it. Small. Round. White. One of his motes, the little points of light he’d been sending ahead of himself since floor 1, to see what they saw. It came out of his palm and floated up into the dark of the doorway. And John nudged it, very slightly, with his mind, so that it drifted round in front of Declan’s face. And stopped there. About a foot away. A little to one side. Lighting him from the front, soft and white.

Like a ring light. Like the cheap webcam light, clipped to the top of a monitor, a long time ago.

Declan looked at it.

Then he looked at John.

“You’re on camera,” said John.

Declan laughed. It came out of him so suddenly he had to put his hand over his mouth. He stood in the inn doorway with a little white light floating in front of his face, and laughed, and his eyes were wet.

“Off to one side?” he said.

“Best seat in the house,” said John.

They stood there. The mote hung in the air between them. Across the square, the clocktower ticked.

“We drifted,” said John, after a while. He said it the way he said everything. Plainly, confidently, as if it were simply a fact you could put on a table. “After you moved. All of us. The Singapore lot. Nobody said anything. It just happened.” He looked at the clocktower. “I’ve thought about that a lot. Since floor 1. That nobody said anything.”

“I know,” said Declan. “Me neither.”

“I’m sorry.”

“Me too.” Declan was quiet for a moment. “I used to look at the corner of the screen. After. For a long time. When I streamed. I kept looking at where you used to sit.” He swallowed. “There was a lamp there, after. That’s all. A lamp.”

John didn’t say anything.

He let the mote drift a little closer. So that it lit the purple on Declan’s jaw, and the flour still in his hair, and the shadows under his eyes. All of it. Everything the camera would have seen.

“You look terrible,” said John.

“I know.”

“You look like you’ve been awake for a hundred and fifty days.”

“I have, mostly.”

“Okay,” said John. He let the mote go. It drifted up into the dark over the square and hung there, a little white point among the real stars. “Then after tonight,” he said, “you sleep. And somebody watches the door. And it’s not you.”

Declan looked at him.

“Gabe’s already called it,” said John. “He says he’s sitting outside whatever door you’re behind. All night.” He almost smiled. “I’m going to sit next to him. Off to one side.”

Declan didn’t answer. He looked up at the little white mote, hanging among the stars over Evermorrow. For a long time.

The clock struck the half hour. Half past eleven.

“Time,” said Declan.

Thirty-six people walked across the square to the clocktower at twenty to midnight. And a baker.

The old caretaker had gone home at dusk, the way he did every day. The door at the bottom was never locked. They went up the narrow stone stair in a long line. John went near the front, sending motes ahead of them, one after another, so that the whole spiral of the stair was lit with little floating points of white. Like fairy lights. Like a string of bulbs at a night market.

Past the clock room. The ticking was very loud now, very slow. Tock. Tock. Tock. Iron wheels turning. The great round window of the clock face from the inside, with the moon behind it and the shadows of the hands lying across the floor. Everybody looked. Nobody stopped.

Into the ringing chamber.

Thirty-six ropes. Hanging from the black beams in a circle, each with its soft woollen grip at the bottom, worn smooth by twenty-six nights of one person’s hands. And on the beam above them, in John’s light, the carved words. When every bell is rung at once, the Hour may be struck.

The bedroll was gone. Somebody had rolled it up and put it against the wall. John thought he knew who.

They stood in the doorway, all of them, looking at the ropes.

“Pick one,” said Declan.

Nobody moved.

“Any of them,” said Declan. “It doesn’t matter which.” He looked round at the circle. “I’ve had my hands on every one of them. Twenty-six nights. Trying.” He was quiet. “Pick any one you like. I’ll take whichever’s left.”

So they went in. One by one. Into the circle. Each to a rope.

Calvin first, of course, to the one nearest the door. Rachael next to him. Dennis. Deston. Azac. Then Xav, very calmly, to one across the circle, and his whole party round him. Yan’s party. Hana’s. Hyvred’s. Chris’s. Thea’s.

John took one between Malcolm and Rui Xiang. He put his hand on the woollen grip. It was soft. Warm, almost. Worn smooth.

When they’d all taken one, there was one left.

In the middle of one side of the circle. Between Gabe and an empty space where Gabe had obviously left a space on purpose.

And directly across the circle from it, holding a rope with her left hand, with her right hand holding the little glass lantern up so its amber light fell across all of them, was Thea.

Declan went to the last rope.

He put his hand on it. His left hand. The one with the scar across the palm. John saw him close it round the woollen grip. He saw him look at it, for a second, his own hand on a rope with thirty-five other hands on thirty-five other ropes round him. And then he looked up. Across the circle.

At Thea.

She was looking back at him. Over the ropes, through the amber light. Neither of them said anything.

Merle stood in the doorway with her floury arms folded and watched them all.

“If it works,” said Merle.

Everybody turned and looked at her. She hadn’t said anything all the way up the stair. She was standing in the doorway of the ringing chamber in her apron, with the light from John’s motes on her freckles, looking at Declan across the circle of ropes.

“If it works,” she said again. “If it’s tomorrow. Will I remember?” Her voice was very steady, the way it got when she was holding on to it with both hands. “All of it. The hundred and fifty-three. Will I wake up tomorrow and remember all of them? Or will it be like they never happened?”

Declan looked at her for a long time.

“I don’t know,” he said.

Merle didn’t say anything.

“I don’t know, Merle,” he said again. Quietly. “I’m sorry. Nobody’s ever done this before. I don’t know what happens after.”

John watched the baker’s face. He watched it go through something. And then, slowly, unexpectedly, he watched her smile.

“Good,” said Merle.

Declan stared at her.

“Good,” she said again. “I haven’t not known something in a hundred and fifty-three days. I’ve known every single thing that was going to happen. Every word. Every dog. Every rain.” She unfolded her arms. “I’d like to not know something. Just one thing. Just tomorrow.” She looked round the circle at all of them, thirty-six people with their hands on thirty-six ropes. “I’d like to wake up and not know what’s going to happen.”

Nobody said anything.

“Pull hard,” said Merle. “That’s all. Pull hard, and I’ll see you in the morning.” She almost laughed. “Whatever morning it is.”

She stepped back out of the doorway, onto the top step of the stair, where she could still see. And folded her arms again.

The clock room began to grind.

John felt it before he heard it. A shudder in the stone under his feet. Something enormous moving, below. Iron on iron. The slow loud ticking speeding up, getting louder, tock-tock-tock-tock.

“It’s coming out,” said Declan. Very calm. “The Hour. It’ll come out of the clock face on the first stroke. It won’t look at us. It’ll walk to the wheel.” He looked round the circle. “When the first stroke goes, we pull. All of us. At the same time. Exactly the same time.” He swallowed. “Two bells made it stop for a breath. Seven made it stop for three. Thirty-six—“ He stopped. “I don’t know. Nobody’s ever done thirty-six.”

“How do we pull at exactly the same time?” said Calvin. “There’s thirty-six of us. Somebody’s going to be late. I’m going to be late.”

Declan looked across the circle.

Not at Thea. At Newish.

She was holding her rope near Hana’s party, with her chin up, looking back at him. Newish, who’d yelled VOYAGER YOU’RE RUDE down a mountain pass on floor 2. Who’d counted ONE, two, three, GO at every fight since the first day, because it was the only thing she knew how to do. Upbeat. Put a beat under them. Everyone’s faster on the one.

“Newish,” said Declan. “Count us in.”

Newish stared at him.

“Me?”

“You.”

“You’re not rude any more? Suddenly I’m useful?”

“You were always useful,” said Declan. “I was always rude.” His mouth did something. “Count us in. On the one.”

Newish looked at him for a long moment. Her face did something complicated and then decided not to.

“Fine,” she said. Loudly. “Fine. Everybody shut up and listen to me. When I say one, you pull. Not on two. Not after one. On it. Calvin, that means you.”

“Why me—“

“Shut up, Calvin.”

The grinding below them got louder. The whole tower was shaking now, very slightly, under their feet. John held his rope with both hands. The wool was warm. Across the circle, the lantern’s light trembled.

And then, from the clock room below, through the floor, a sound like a great door opening.

The great bell rang. Above them, in the belfry. Huge. So loud John felt it in his teeth and his chest and the soles of his feet.

One.

“ONE,” said Newish.

And thirty-six people pulled.
