Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 1: Thirty-Six Circles

Chapter 2

Thirty-Five

3,806 words · 17 min read

The last thing Deston remembered was a dishwasher.

He’d been loading it. It was late, and he’d been tired enough that he’d put a wooden spoon in the bottom rack, which he knew you weren’t supposed to do, and he’d been standing there with one hand on the door, deciding whether he cared enough to take it out. He remembered deciding he didn’t. He remembered pushing the rack in.

Then he was lying on his back on something white, with a column of light standing over him like a lamp somebody had switched on in a dark room.

He lay there and looked at it. It was very bright, but it didn’t hurt. It hummed, faintly, a note too low to be sure of. As he watched, it started to come down: the whole tall pillar folding in on itself, shrinking and brightening, until what was left was a ball of soft white light about the size of an orange, hanging in the air above his left shoulder. It pulsed, slowly, the way a phone does when it’s still thinking.

Deston sat up.

The ball of light came with him, keeping its place by his shoulder like a dog that had already decided who it belonged to.

“Okay,” Deston said, to nobody, out loud. He’d always talked himself through things. “Okay. So that’s happening.”

Something in his right hand. He looked down. A card, white and stiff, about the size of a playing card. On the front, in clean black capitals that hadn’t been there a second ago:

DESTON

He turned it over.

be careful, train longer on the 3rd floor - D.

It was written by hand. Not printed. The letters were slightly uneven, as if someone had written them quickly with something that wasn’t quite a pen, and the ink still had a faint shine to it, like it had only just dried.

He read it twice.

3rd floor. Third floor of what?

Train for what?

D.

His own initial. For one strange, sideways second he wondered if he’d written it himself, half-asleep, and then he looked at the handwriting properly and knew he hadn’t. He’d never once in his life written a lower-case b like that, with the loop left open at the top. He knew somebody who did. He’d copied that somebody’s chemistry notes for three straight years of secondary school, because the somebody had a habit of actually paying attention in chemistry, and he’d learned to read those open-topped bs faster than his own.

No.

He looked at the card for a while longer. Then he put it in his shirt pocket and buttoned the pocket shut, and stood up to find out where he was.

At one-metre-eighty-five, Deston was used to seeing over rooms.

This one was round and white and much smaller than it felt. There was no door. There was no window. There was no light he could find a source for, and yet everything was evenly lit, the way a room is in a dream. A ring of faint circles ran around the floor, each with a glowing ball of light hovering over it, and in each circle there was a person waking up.

There were a lot of people waking up.

The noise was building. It was the particular noise of a crowd that hasn’t decided yet whether to panic: the same question asked in thirty different ways, people saying names. Somebody was crying. Somebody else was laughing, the high, breathless laugh of someone who had no idea what else to do with themselves.

He looked for his own people first. It was a reflex. Twenty-six years of being the one who knew everybody had left him with a mind that sorted every room into mine and not yet.

Rachael. Four circles down, sitting up with her knees drawn in and her arms round them, looking around the room with an expression that said she’d decided to be annoyed until she could work out what else to be. Dennis, next to her, already on his feet, turning slowly on the spot with both hands in his hair, saying, “No, because what is this,” to anybody who’d listen. Calvin, next to him, sprawled flat on his back and grinning up at the ceiling as if someone had just told him a very good joke.

“Bro,” Calvin said to the ceiling. “Bro. Bro.”

Further round: Xav, sitting very still with his hands on his knees, reading the room the way he’d read every room since they were seventeen. Rui Xiang next to him, quick eyes already on the move. Malcolm, who’d put his face in his hands. John, already standing, his chin up and his back straight as if he’d decided the best thing to do was look like someone in charge. Azac, flat out, still very slightly snoring.

That was everybody he’d known for a decade. Everybody from Singapore. That was—

He counted. He couldn’t help it.

That was everybody from Singapore but one. Gabe wasn’t next to him. Gabe should have been next to him; he could feel it, the way you can feel a missing tooth.

And there was a voice he’d know anywhere in the world, coming from the far side of the room, very loud, getting louder.

“Declan!”

Gabe was at the wall.

There was something there that might once have been a door: a round-topped shape in the white, very faint, like the outline of a doorway somebody had painted over. Gabe had both hands flat against it. He was pushing as if it might open if he just leaned hard enough.

“Declan! Hey! Declan!”

Deston got to him in six strides. He put a hand on Gabe’s shoulder, and Gabe turned so fast he nearly lost his balance.

“He was here,” Gabe said.

“Who?”

“Who do you think? Declan. He was right there.” Gabe pointed back across the ring at the circles. “He was standing by my— I woke up, and he was standing over there by this thing, and it was open, Dest. There was grass. And he looked at me.”

“Okay.”

“Don’t do the okay.”

“I’m not doing anything,” Deston said. “I’m listening. Breathe.”

Gabe breathed. Gabe always breathed when Deston told him to, which was one of the reasons they’d survived each other since Gabe was a skinny thirteen-year-old in the second row of the school choir. He was a big man now, broad and heavy and strong, and he was breathing like he’d run up a flight of stairs.

“He looked at me,” Gabe said again, quieter. “And I said his name, and he— He stopped. Like he heard me. Like he was going to say something back.” He swallowed. “And then all this light came up out of the floor, out of my— and I couldn’t see. And when it went down he was gone, and that—“ He slapped the white wall. “That was a wall.”

Deston looked at the wall. It was a wall.

He looked back at the ring of circles. They were spread round the floor with gaps between them, each a little bigger than a person, each with its little floating light and its person inside. Gabe’s was the one next to his, which was right. That was where it should be.

Except it wasn’t next to his. There was one circle between them.

It was empty. Its light was the faintest in the room, the barest glow on the white floor, and as Deston watched it was getting fainter, like the last warmth going out of a chair after someone stands up.

“Gabe,” he said slowly. “When you woke up. Which circle was he next to?”

Gabe followed his look. His face changed.

“That one,” he said.

“How many of us are there?” said Calvin, loudly, to the whole room.

He was standing up now, on his toes to see over people. Calvin had never in his life not asked a question because it might be awkward. It was one of his finest qualities. It was also why nobody had ever let him near a wedding speech.

“Like, actually. Has anybody counted? Because there are a lot of you I don’t know, no offence.”

“None taken,” somebody said, and then, after a pause, “Who are you?”

“Calvin. Hi. You?”

“Drew.”

“Nice to meet you, Drew. Nice necklace.” The man wore a nameplate on a chain that said 88EXP, and he’d glanced down at it as if surprised to find it there. “Okay, so who’s counting?”

“I’ll count,” said Deston.

He did, because it gave his hands something to do while his head worked. He went round the ring and counted heads, and in the process he met people. That was the other reason; he’d always met people the way some people breathe. He shook hands. He said his name. He tried, as fast as he could, to put faces and names together, which was harder than usual, because a lot of the faces weren’t the faces their owners had had yesterday.

A girl with golden horns and long dark hair streaked crimson was lying on her back in her circle, sobbing at the ceiling with her glasses pushed up into her hair, while her friend knelt next to her with a hand on her shoulder. The friend had antennae, and translucent wings, and two small fluffy yellow things bobbing anxiously round her head.

“She’s fine,” the friend told him bluntly, before he could ask. “She can see. She couldn’t before. Give her a minute.”

“Of course,” Deston said. “I’m Deston.”

“Bee. That’s Hyvred. Move.”

He moved.

A small, round, purple something was sitting on the head of a girl with very long lavender hair and feathery gills, making a high, worried noise. A penguin, a real, live, yellow penguin, was waddling determinedly between circles while a girl with a short brown bob and a heart-shaped tuft of hair on top of her head followed it at exactly the same speed, saying, “Waddles. Waddles. Please.” A man with long silver-white hair and black-and-crimson robes was sitting up very straight, his pointed ears pink at the tips, with a white snake wound round his neck like a scarf. A small green one lay coiled round the wrist of another man, in a green haori, who was looking down at it with a face of absolute wonder.

A short, broad, very muscular man had his arm round a girl in purple headphones and was saying, “Wendy. Wendy, it’s okay. I’ve got you. Whatever this is, I’ve got you.”

“You have no idea what this is,” said the girl in headphones.

“No,” said Salty. “But I’ve got you.”

A very small person who seemed to be made of wood was sitting cross-legged in an oversized orange hoodie with the hood up, wearing pixelated sunglasses, absolutely silent, watching everything.

“You okay?” Deston asked him.

The small wooden person considered the question for a long moment. “Yeah,” he said, and went back to watching.

Deston kept counting. A pair of short black horns curved back through very long silver hair; next to them, a single purple horn rose out of a mess of white and lavender, over an indigo coat embroidered with wisteria. A girl in a royal-blue jersey dress, ponytail streaked pink and blue, had opened a translucent window in the air in front of her and was poking at it with one finger like a mechanic poking an engine, completely absorbed.

Thirty-five.

He went round again to be sure. He counted the empty circle the second time, standing over it while its faint glow guttered and went out.

Thirty-five people. Thirty-six circles.

“Thirty-five,” he said, when he got back to Calvin.

“So that’s an odd number,” said Calvin. “Is that bad? Odd numbers feel bad.”

“I don’t know, Cal.”

“Thirty-six circles, though.”

“Yeah.”

“So someone’s missing.”

“Yeah,” said Deston. “Someone’s missing.”

Yan found them.

Deston knew Yan the way you know a friend’s friend you’ve played with for years: a voice on a call, deep and unhurried, who always seemed to arrive at a conclusion a few seconds before everybody else did and wait politely for them to catch up. He’d never seen Yan’s face before. He was fairly sure he still hadn’t. The face he was looking at was the one from Yan’s streams: silver-white hair in a high topknot with a gold ornament, pale cyan eyes, jade drops at the ears. Long ears. Pointed.

The white snake round his neck turned its head and looked at Deston with bright green eyes, and Deston had the clear and unsettling impression that it was deciding whether he was edible.

“Deston?” said Yan.

“Yan. Hey.”

“I heard someone shouting for Ice,” Yan said. He said it carefully, as if he were setting it down on a table between them. “I think I saw him.”

Gabe turned round so fast he nearly knocked Deston over.

“Where?”

“By the wall. Where you were pushing.” Yan’s eyes went to the faint, painted-over shape. “I woke up, and I turned my head, and there was someone standing in front of it. It was open. There was green behind him.” His mouth tightened slightly. “Then Suzu sat on my face.”

The snake made a small noise. Yan tilted his head, the way people do when they’re listening to something.

“She says he was there,” Yan said slowly. “She says he went out the door.”

There was a short silence.

“She says,” said Gabe.

“She says.” Yan’s expression didn’t change. “I’ve been hearing her since I woke up. In my head. I’ll explain later, if there’s a later. But she says someone was there, and he went out of the door, and he was sad. That’s all she’ll give me.”

“Was it him?” Gabe said. “Was it Declan?”

“I don’t know,” said Yan, honestly. “I saw black, and white, and a bit of teal. For maybe a second. And then a snake.” He hesitated. “It looked like his drawing. The one he puts on everything.”

“It was him.”

“It was.”

The voice came from behind them. It was smooth and calm and slightly unsteady at the bottom, the voice of someone who had decided to say a difficult thing evenly. Deston turned.

A woman with long, straight lavender hair was standing a little way off. A thick fringe covered her right eye; a little purple-and-gold crown sat crooked on her head, trailing bead chains. Two soft triangular ears stood up through her hair, and they were flat back with nerves. A man with short brown hair, a yellow streak and the same cat ears was holding her hand very tightly.

“I’m Hana,” she said. “That’s Milli. I saw him too. It was Ice.” She looked at Gabe, and then at Deston, as if she’d guessed who they were. “I was closest to the door. I sat up and he was right there. Three metres. Black vest, white shirt, the teal bit in his hair. He looked at me. He knew me.” Her ears went flatter. “Then Milli woke up and grabbed me, and I looked away for one second, and when I looked back the door was gone.”

“I’m sorry,” said Milli. “I didn’t— I didn’t know what was happening.”

“I know, honey.”

“Ice,” Deston said. It wasn’t a name he used, but he knew it. Half the people in this room had probably only ever known his oldest friend by it: a handle on a stream, a voice in a call, a little drawing of a black-haired boy with a teal streak on every emote and every profile. Declan to the people who’d known him at a dinner table. Ice to everybody else.

Gabe, Yan, Hana. Three people. Three different places in the ring.

“So he’s here,” said Gabe. “He’s here, and he ran.”

“Gabe—“

“He saw me, and he ran, Dest.”

“We don’t know that,” Deston said, but he heard how it sounded.

“He was standing right there. He looked right at me. Then he went out the only door in the room before it closed. What’s that, if it isn’t running?”

Deston didn’t answer. He was thinking about the card in his shirt pocket, buttoned shut. Be careful. Somebody who’d been standing over him while he slept, while he was still holding a blank card, before any of their lights had come down. Somebody who’d known there was going to be a third floor, and that the right answer was to stay on it longer, and who’d had time to write that down for Deston, and not to wait for any of them.

“I don’t know,” he said. “I don’t know what it is.”

Rachael didn’t come over.

He noticed it the way he noticed most things about Rachael: sideways, because she didn’t like being looked at directly when something was wrong. She’d opened the window in front of her face, like everyone else had by now, and she was sitting very still in her circle, reading it.

He went and sat down next to her without saying anything. She didn’t say anything either. After a minute she tipped the window a few degrees so he could see it too.

He read it. Then he read it again, more slowly, and understood.

She’d spent twenty-six years with something on that line. The illness had a long name and an endless list of things she couldn’t do, couldn’t eat, couldn’t risk: hospital appointments, and the special careful arithmetic of a person who knew exactly how many germs were in every room she walked into. She’d been cynical for as long as he’d known her. He’d always privately thought she’d earned it.

“It might be wrong,” she said, very quietly.

“It might.”

“It’s probably wrong. It’s a floating box. It said I’m level one.” She swallowed. “I can breathe, Dest. I didn’t notice until just now. I’ve been breathing this whole time and I didn’t notice. I always notice.”

He didn’t touch her. You didn’t, with Rachael. He just sat beside her with his shoulder a polite ten centimetres from hers and let her have it.

After a while she shut the window with a flick, like closing a tab she’d been caught reading, and said in her usual voice, “This place is stupid.”

“Extremely.”

“Who were you lot shouting about?”

He told her.

Rachael was quiet for a moment. Then she said, “If Declan’s here, he’s got a reason.” She was looking at the painted-over door. “He was always the one you could ask anything. Remember that? Not because he had answers. Because he’d never make you feel stupid for asking.” A pause. “So if he ran off without saying anything, it’s because he couldn’t, not because he didn’t want to. That’s all.”

Deston looked at her.

“What,” she said.

“Nothing.”

“Don’t make it a thing.”

“I’m not.”

He found Gabe again a few minutes later, sitting in the empty circle.

Not standing in it, or looking at it. Sitting in it, cross-legged, as if he could keep it warm. Its glow had gone out entirely. It was just a slightly paler ring on the white floor now, like the mark a glass leaves on a table.

Deston sat down on the edge of it.

“Don’t,” Gabe said.

“Don’t what?”

“Don’t tell me I imagined it.”

“I wasn’t going to.” Deston unbuttoned his shirt pocket and took out the card. He looked at it for a second more. Then he turned it over to the handwritten side and held it out.

Gabe took it. Deston watched him read it: the frown, the second read, his lips moving very slightly on 3rd floor. Then Gabe got to the end, and his whole face went still.

“D,” Gabe said.

“Could be me.”

“It’s not you.”

“Could be Dennis. Could be the guy with the necklace. His name’s Drew.”

“It’s not Drew.” Gabe looked up. His eyes were very bright. “That’s his b. Look at the b. He’s always done his bs like that. He wrote my birthday card like that every year since I was thirteen. He wrote Happy birthday, big man every single year with the stupid open b, and every year I told him that’s not how you write a b, and every year he said—“

“It’s a design choice,” said Deston.

“It’s a design choice,” said Gabe, at the same time, and then put his hand over his face.

They sat there.

“He wrote you a note,” Gabe said, through his hand, “and he didn’t write me one.”

“He wrote me a warning, Gabe. That’s not the same thing.”

“Feels the same from here.”

“He put it in my hand while I was asleep,” Deston said slowly. “Before anything. Before the lights. Before any of us could see him do it. He didn’t sign his name, he signed a letter. He didn’t say where he was going. He just said be careful.” He took the card back and looked at it again. “That’s not someone running away from us. That’s someone who knows something we don’t, and doesn’t want us to come after him.”

“Well, too bad,” said Gabe.

“Yeah,” said Deston. “I figured.”

He put the card back in his pocket and buttoned it. He didn’t show it to anyone else. He wasn’t sure why yet, except that thirty-three frightened people in a white room with no door didn’t need be careful from a stranger who’d vanished, and the two people who could read the b already had.

Across the ring, Xav had stood up. He hadn’t said a word since he’d woken. He walked over to the painted-over doorway, stood in front of it with his hands in his pockets, and looked at it for a long time. He didn’t push it, the way Gabe had. He just looked, the way he looked at a problem when he’d decided to solve it later.

Deston thought about Xav and Declan: three years in the same polytechnic class, then years of every game together, then Malacca and the long quiet drift neither of them had ever said anything about. He thought Xav probably had his own questions about why Declan might run, and didn’t like the answers.

Then the ball of light at Deston’s shoulder, which had been pulsing patiently all this time, went suddenly steady and bright.

So did every other ball of light in the room, all at once, all thirty-five of them, like a theatre’s house lights coming up before a show.

And thirty-five voices, perfectly in unison, perfectly calm, said:

“Welcome, Player.”
