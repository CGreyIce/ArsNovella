Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 6: Unwritten

Chapter 224

Keep Your Street

6,242 words · 27 min read

There was no hammer on seventeen either.

Salty listened for it at the top of the stair. He always did now. Four thousand and eighty steps up the far wall of the Hollow, Milli said, who’d counted every one out loud from about the two-thousandth because Newish had stopped to cry about Edda’s lantern and somebody had to keep it going. Four thousand and eighty steps, in the zigzag, with the lit Hollow falling away below them like a sky upside down. And a dozen strays coming up behind. And at the top, a door in the roof of the world.

He put his hand in his coat pocket on the last step, the way he did, and closed it round Ressa’s small old hammer. You’ll want something to listen to, little light. Where there isn’t a hammer.

He listened.

Wind. That was the first thing. After thirty falls in the still warm dark of the Hollow, wind, cold and grey and smelling of rain on stone. Somewhere a gull. And under it, a sound he couldn’t place for a long time. A sort of creak. Low, slow, everywhere at once. Like a ship at anchor. Like timbers settling in a house at night.

Then Tiger opened the door, and he saw where it came from.

The stair came out in a tower on a wall.

A round tower, grey stone, set into a town wall so long and high it curved away out of sight on both sides. And inside the wall, below them, a town. Grey too. Grey stone and black slate, under a grey sky that was low and moving and full of rain that wasn’t falling yet. Roofs, hundreds of them, steep and wet and crowded, going away from the foot of the wall in every direction, to a middle he couldn’t see because it was too far and too grey.

Streets between the roofs. Narrow. Crooked. Going every way at once.

And moving.

He didn’t see it at first. Nobody did. He stood at the top of the tower with Wendy beside him and Yan behind and the rest of the thirty-six coming up the last of the stair, and looked down at a town, and thought that’s a town. And then he blinked.

And it was a slightly different town.

Not much. A street that had gone left from the foot of the tower now went right. A little square that had been there had a house in it. A roof that had been slate was thatch.

He stared.

He didn’t blink. He made himself not blink. He looked at the street going right from the foot of the tower and didn’t take his eyes off it, and it stayed going right, and his eyes started to water, and it stayed, and his eyes stung, and he blinked—

Left.

“Mate,” said Salty.

“I saw,” said Wendy. Beside him. Very quietly. “I saw it go. When you blinked. I was looking at it too and it went when I blinked. Not when you did.” She frowned. “No. When we both had. I blinked first. It didn’t go. Then you blinked. And it went.”

“It goes when nobody’s looking,” said Higu.

She’d come up behind them. She had her rock out. She’d already drawn the tower and the wall and the street going left from it, and now she was looking down at the street with her chalk stopped halfway through a line, and her face was doing something Salty had never seen it do.

“It’s the Down,” said Higu. Very quietly. “On nine. Where the map was wrong every morning.” She looked at the chalk. “Except the Down did it at night. This does it every time nobody’s looking.”

Below them, at the foot of the tower, a door opened in a house, and a woman came out, and stood on her step, and looked up and down her street. Slowly. Carefully. The way you’d look at a dog you weren’t sure about. Then she sat down on the step with a cup of something in her hands and went on looking at it.

She didn’t take her eyes off it once.

The town was called Turnstead.

The woman on the step told them that. Her name was Ottilie, and she was about sixty, square and grey and red in the face with the cold, and she didn’t get up when thirty-six people and a goat and a dog and a Rimehound came down the tower stair and out into her street. She didn’t take her eyes off the street to look at them either. She looked at them out of the corner of her eye, sideways, the whole time she talked, with her face turned to the far end of her street where it went round a corner.

“Climbers,” said Ottilie. “From the wall. Haven’t had climbers in—“ She stopped. “Never had climbers. Heard of them. In stories. A long way back.” She took a sip of her cup. “You’ll want a street.”

“A street?” said Deston.

“You can’t stay in mine. I’ve only got the one house and it’s got me in it.” Ottilie pointed with her chin, without looking. “Two along. Past the pump. There’s one nobody’s kept since the Harrows went. Long thin one. Eleven houses. You can have that, if you keep it.”

“Keep it?”

Ottilie looked at Deston for the first time. Properly. Turned her whole head away from her street and looked at him, for about a second. Then back.

“You don’t know,” she said. “Do you.”

“Know what?”

“Keep your street,” said Ottilie. Very slowly, the way you’d explain to a child. “Watch it. Somebody’s got to be watching it. Day and night. All the time. If everybody stops watching it at once—“ She lifted her cup at the end of her street. “It goes. Turns. You look back and it’s somewhere else. Or it’s somebody else’s. Or it’s gone into the Loose.”

“The Loose?”

“The middle,” said Ottilie. “Where nobody watches. It’s been turning so long nobody knows what’s in there.” She took another sip. “Keep your street. That’s all. Never all blink.”

She didn’t say anything else. She sat on her step and drank her cup and watched her street, and after a while it was clear she’d finished, and they went.

They didn’t believe her.

Not properly. Salty would think about that later, for a long time. They’d seen it from the tower. Every one of them had stood at the top and blinked and seen a street change. And they still didn’t believe it, not in their bodies, not the way you believe a fire’s hot. Because they were tired. Four thousand and eighty steps, and two days across the Hollow before that, and the Moth before that. And because a street was a street. You’d been walking on streets your whole life. They didn’t move.

They found the Harrows’ street. Long and thin, past the pump, eleven tall narrow grey houses on either side with steep slate roofs and windows looking down onto the cobbles. Empty. Dusty. Doors unlocked. They split up into the houses the way they always did, by party, and Higu drew a rota on the back of her rock: two awake at all times, two hours each. And Chris wrote it in his notebook. And Xav said right.

And then Wendy and Salty sat on the front step of the first house for the first watch, two hours, looking up the street in the grey evening, and it didn’t move. Not once. They watched it the whole time.

And at the end of the two hours Calvin and Dennis came out and took over, and Salty went in and lay down on a bare wooden floor beside Tiger and went to sleep before his head was properly down.

And Calvin and Dennis sat on the step and watched the street.

And at some point in the second hour, in the dark, with the rain started, Calvin said something, and Dennis laughed, and they both turned their heads at the same moment to look at each other.

He woke up because Tiger had his hand over Salty’s mouth.

He didn’t know where he was. Bare wooden floor. Grey light through a window. Tiger kneeling over him with his hand over his mouth and his tail stiff as a stick behind him and his eyes very wide.

“Don’t shout,” said Tiger. In a whisper. “Wendy’s asleep. Don’t shout. Just look.”

Tiger took his hand away.

Salty sat up. He looked at the window.

It wasn’t the Harrows’ street.

It was a street. Grey stone, wet slate, cobbles. But wider. Shorter. Curving. With a pump in the middle of it that hadn’t been there and a house on the far side painted, of all things, yellow. And no other houses he recognised at all.

He got up. Quietly. He went to the window.

Their house was standing on its own in the middle of a row of houses he’d never seen. Neighbours either side. Strangers’ houses, with strangers’ washing on lines between them. And the other ten houses of the Harrows’ street, the ones with thirty-one of their friends asleep in them, weren’t anywhere.

“Where’s everybody,” said Salty.

“I don’t know,” said Tiger.

“Where’s Yan. Where’s—“ He looked round the room. Wendy, asleep on the floor in the corner with her bow beside her. Cursiffe, asleep sitting up against the wall with his ink book in his lap. Yan—

Not there.

Yan had been in the room. When Salty went to sleep. By the door. With Working On It upright against the wall beside him and Suzu round his neck.

The door was there. The wall was there. The scythe was there, leaning against the wall.

Yan wasn’t.

“He went out,” said Tiger. “Before. In the night. He said he wanted to look at the lights. The strays. He went out to the step.” Tiger swallowed. “He left the scythe.”

They found out later that it was the same for everybody.

Eleven houses. Each one somewhere else. Not far; that was what Ottilie said afterwards, in her flat voice, not looking at them. Never far. It doesn’t throw things. It just turns them. A street here, a house there. But the Harrows’ street was gone. Eleven houses with thirty-five people in them scattered across a quarter of Turnstead, in other people’s streets, among other people’s houses, every one of them waking up in grey light to a window full of somewhere else.

And one of them not in a house at all.

Salty stood in the strange yellow street in the rain with Wendy and Tiger and Cursiffe, and Working On It in his hands because somebody had to carry it, and didn’t know what to do. Not one thing. He looked up the street and down it and it was a street, and none of their friends were in it, and if he looked away it might stop being even that.

“Yan,” said Wendy. Up the street. Not loud. Then louder. “YAN—“

A window opened across the street. A man leaned out and looked at them, and then quickly back at his street, and shut the window.

Salty had never been so frightened in his life.

Not on the Grey. Not in the Down on nine. Not at the bottom of the Hollow with the Moth coming up. He’d been frightened then, with everybody beside him. He’d never been frightened like this. Standing in a street with no idea, none at all, where anybody was. Where anybody was.

Never all blink.

They’d blinked. Two people on a step had turned their heads to laugh at a joke, at the same time, in the dark, and every single person who should have been watching hadn’t been.

“Salty.”

He turned round.

Ice was at the end of the yellow street.

He’d come round the corner at a run, with Thea behind him and the lantern swinging in her hand and Tuli’s pole in his, and Gabe’s terrible lantern on his belt humming, and he stopped at the end of the street and looked at the four of them. Counted them. Salty watched him count. He always counted.

“Four,” said Ice. Breathing hard. “Five with— Where’s Yan?”

“We don’t know.” Wendy’s voice was very steady and very thin. “He went out. In the night. To look at the strays. We don’t—“

Ice stood very still.

His head tipped. A little. Toward his own shoulder. The way he did. Listening to something nobody else could hear.

“Bearing,” said Cursiffe. Very quietly. Behind Salty. “While you share a floor, you will always know which way they are.”

Ice turned on the spot. Slowly. The way a compass needle turns when you set it down. And stopped. Facing back the way he’d come. Past Thea. Past the corner. Across roofs.

“There,” said Ice. He pointed. “That way. Not far. Four streets. Five.” He was already moving. “He’s all right. He’s not hurt. He’s—“ He stopped. His face did something. “He’s sitting down.”

“How do you—“

“I always know where you are.” Ice didn’t look round. “All of you. I always have. Every floor.” He was walking fast. “I just never needed it in a town before.”

Yan was sitting on a step.

In a narrow street with a well at one end of it, four streets away, five, in front of a house that wasn’t anybody’s they knew. On the step, with his arms round his knees and Suzu round his neck. Looking up.

There was a stray over the street.

Salty saw it as they came round the corner. One of the dozen that had followed them up the stair. Small and pale, the size of an apple, hanging in the air over the middle of the narrow street at about the height of the roofs. Not drifting. Still. Turned down, the way strays turned, at the street below it.

And the street wasn’t moving.

That was the thing. Salty didn’t understand it until later. They came round the corner into it, the five of them, and Thea, and Ice. And the whole time, nobody was looking at the street. They were looking at Yan. And the street stayed.

Yan looked round at them.

“Oh,” said Yan. Cool and level. “There you are.”

“There you are?” Wendy was across the street before anybody else. She got Yan by the front of his coat with both hands and hauled him up off the step. “There you are? You left your scythe. You went out in the night. To look at lights. On a floor where the streets move—“

“I know.”

“—and the street moved, and you weren’t in it, and we woke up and you weren’t—“

“I know, Wendy.” Yan didn’t take her hands off his coat. “I watched it go.”

Wendy stopped.

“I was on the step,” said Yan. “Of the first house. Looking at the lights. Up. Over the roofs. There were three of them, over the street.” He looked up at the one over this street. “And Calvin and Dennis were on the step of the fourth house, on watch. And Calvin said something about Dennis’s mother. And they both looked round at each other and laughed.” He was quiet. “And I was looking up. At the lights. Not at the street. Nobody was.”

“And it went.”

“And it went. Not all at once. Like a—“ He thought about it. “Like a deck of cards when somebody shuffles it. The houses went past me in the dark. Every one of them. Very quietly. And then I was on a step in a different street, by myself, and the house behind me wasn’t ours.”

He looked up at the stray over the street.

“And this one came,” said Yan. “It followed me. From our street. It came and hung there. Over this one. And looked down.” He was quiet. “And the street stopped turning. I’ve been watching it. Since. It hasn’t moved once.” He tipped his head. “It’s watching it. Isn’t it. The light. It’s keeping it.”

Nobody said anything.

Salty looked up at the stray. Small and pale and very still, over the middle of the narrow street, turned down.

Keep your street, Ottilie had said. Somebody’s got to be watching it.

“Huh,” said Ice. Very quietly.

He was looking at the stray too. And then, slowly, down. At his own belt. At the light in Gabe’s terrible lantern. Turned up at his face, the way it always was.

He didn’t say anything else.

Higu made a new rota.

She drew it on the back of the rock, sitting on a step in the yellow street that afternoon, with all thirty-six of them crammed into it, found, every one, by Ice and Bearing and Tiger running. It took her an hour. It wasn’t two awake. It was six. Always six, on six different steps, facing six different ways. And never, ever, two of them looking at each other.

“Nobody laughs on watch,” said Higu.

“That’s a terrible rule,” said Calvin.

“Nobody laughs on watch, Calvin.”

“It was a really good joke about Dennis’s—“

“Calvin,” said everybody.

Ottilie came and looked at the rota, sideways, with her eyes on her own street two along. She didn’t say anything for a long time.

“Six is good,” she said finally. “Six’ll keep it.” She looked at Calvin, very briefly. “Laughing’s fine. Just not both of you at once.”

They took a new street. Not the Harrows’; the Harrows’ was gone. Another one Ottilie pointed at, two along from the yellow one, short and crooked, nine houses. They kept it. Six at a time. Every hour. Day and night.

It held.

He met Old Pask on the third day.

He heard him first. He’d been listening for a hammer since the top of the stair and there wasn’t one anywhere in Turnstead, not one forge going, in a town with doors and hinges and nails and pots in it. He’d thought that was odd. He’d thought about it on every watch.

On the third day, on his way back from the pump with two buckets, he heard a hammer.

Once. Tink. Thin and light and tired. A small hammer on a small anvil. Then nothing. Then, a long time later, tink.

He followed it.

Round two corners, keeping one eye on the street he was in the way everybody in Turnstead did, sideways, the way Ottilie did. Down a lane so narrow the roofs touched over his head. And at the end of the lane, a forge. Low, stone, with a black open front and a hearth inside gone down to almost nothing. And at the anvil, an old man.

Very old. Thin, bent, with white hair in a wisp and a leather apron that had been made for somebody twice his size, or for him, a long time ago. He had a hammer in his hand. A light one. He was looking at a bit of iron on the anvil, a hinge, half made. And then, every few breaths, he’d lift his head, slowly, and look out of the open front of the forge, up the lane. At his street. For a long time. And then back at the hinge. Tink.

Salty stood in the lane with his buckets.

The old man looked up. At him. Then quickly back at the lane, past him.

“You’ll be one of the climbers,” said the old man. His voice was dry as old leather. “Ottilie said. Big lot of you. Keeping the Crook.”

“Yeah.” Salty put the buckets down. “I’m a smith. Sort of. I heard your hammer.”

The old man looked at him again. Longer this time. At his hands. Then back at the lane.

“Pask,” said the old man. “I’m Pask.”

“Salty.”

“Salt-y.” Pask tried it. “Salt-y. Hm.” Tink. “You’ll want to know why there’s no forges.”

“I was wondering.”

“Can’t keep a street and work iron,” said Pask. “Not on your own. Can’t watch the lane and the fire both. Can’t watch the lane with your eyes on a hot bar.” Tink. “There were seven forges in Turnstead when I was a boy. All in one street. The Smithy. Seven families. Somebody always watching. You’d hear it from the wall.” He looked out at the lane. “Families went. One way and another. Streets turned. People moved. The Smithy got smaller. Then it was just me.”

“Where’s the rest of the street?”

Pask pointed with his hammer. Without looking. At the far end of the lane, where it went round a corner.

“Round there,” said Pask. “Was. It went into the Loose when my wife died. Eleven years. I was at the burying. Everybody was. Nobody watching.” Tink. “Came back and it was this. My forge and this lane and nothing past the corner.”

Salty looked at the corner.

“Who watches it now?” said Salty. “At night.”

Pask didn’t answer for a long time.

“Me,” he said.

“All night?”

“I sleep in the day. A bit. In the chair. With the door open.” Tink. “Ottilie looks in. Her girl, sometimes. It’s not much of a street. Lane and a forge. It doesn’t try very hard.”

Salty looked at him. At the old thin arms. At the eyes, red-rimmed, with dark under them like bruises.

“When did you last sleep?” said Salty. “At night. Properly.”

Pask lifted his head and looked at him. For a long time this time. Long enough that the lane might have gone.

It didn’t.

“Eleven years,” said Pask.

He read it three times.

Then he looked across the street, at the back of Wendy’s head on the opposite step, with her hood up against the rain and her bow across her knees and her face turned up the street, watching.

“Wend,” he said.

“I’m watching.”

“I know. I’ve got a window.”

“Read it to me. Don’t look at me. I’m watching.”

He read it to her. Out loud. In the rain. To the back of her head.

Wendy didn’t say anything for a long time.

“Pask’s,” she said.

“Yeah.”

“You’ve been to see him three times. You think I don’t notice. You come back from the pump smelling of a forge.” She was quiet. “Let the one it belongs to sleep.” She was quiet longer. “Eleven years, he said?”

“Eleven years.”

“Then go on,” said Wendy. Without turning round. “Go and let him.”

He went the next night.

He told Higu, so she’d move the rota. He told Yan, who looked at him for a long time with his head on one side and said your light’s bigger than it was on eight, which Salty didn’t know what to do with. He told Ice, who was sitting on the step of the last house in the Crook on his own watch with Thea’s head on his shoulder asleep and the little lantern humming on his belt. Ice listened, and looked at him, and didn’t say I’ll come or let me or anything at all except, after a moment:

“Hard?”

“Hard.”

“Course.” Ice looked up the street. “Tell me in the morning.”

He went down the narrow lane at dusk with Ressa’s small hammer in his pocket.

Pask was in the chair in the door of the forge, with a blanket over his knees and a cup in his hands, looking out at the lane. The fire was down to embers. He looked at Salty coming down the lane. Then back at the lane.

“You again,” said Pask.

“I’m keeping it tonight,” said Salty.

Pask didn’t move.

“The lane,” said Salty. “Your street. Tonight. All night. I’m watching it. You go to bed.” He was quiet. “A proper bed. Upstairs, or wherever it is. Not the chair. Shut your eyes. I’ve got it.”

Pask looked at him for a very long time. Long enough that, in any other town, the light would have changed. Long enough that Salty, standing in the lane, felt the lane behind him want to go. He didn’t know how he knew. A sort of lean. In the stones. Like a horse shifting its weight when the rider’s not paying attention.

He didn’t look away from Pask. And he didn’t look away from the lane. He did what Ottilie did. Sideways.

“Why,” said Pask.

Salty thought about it.

“Somebody did it for me,” he said. “Sort of. On eight. In a forge under a waterfall. I sat on a stool for forty nights and listened to a woman’s hammer, and she let me. And when I left she gave me this.” He took Ressa’s small hammer out of his pocket. “So I’d have something to listen to. Where there isn’t a hammer.” He turned it in his hand. “There isn’t one here. In the whole town. Not a proper one. I’ve been listening since the stair.”

Pask looked at the hammer.

“And on fourteen,” said Salty, “my mate went out on a field on his own and couldn’t hold it. And he asked. And all of us held it for him.” He shrugged. “That’s how you do it. Somebody holds it so you can put it down.” He looked at Pask. “Eleven years is a long time to hold a lane.”

Pask didn’t say anything.

Then, very slowly, he put down his cup. He folded the blanket off his knees. He got up out of the chair, an old man getting up a bit at a time, and stood in the door of his forge.

He looked at his lane for a long time.

Then he turned his back on it.

On purpose. All the way round. With his face to the dark of the forge. Salty saw his shoulders go stiff. Saw him wait for it. For the lane to go, the way it had gone eleven years ago when everybody was at a burying.

It didn’t go.

Salty was watching it.

Pask stood with his back to his street for a long breath. Then another. Then he made a sound, small and dry, that might have been a laugh or might not. And went into the dark at the back of the forge, and up a narrow stair Salty couldn’t see, slowly, one step at a time, and Salty heard a door, and a bed creak.

And then nothing.

It was the longest night of his life.

Longer than the Grey. Longer than forty nights on a stool under the second arch. Because on the Grey you could look at whatever you liked, and under the arch you could shut your eyes and listen. Here you couldn’t do either.

He sat in Pask’s chair in the door of the forge with the blanket over his knees and watched a lane.

Not hard. Not straight at it. Ottilie’s way. Sideways. With the edge of his eye. Because he’d found out in three days that if you stared straight at a street you blinked more, and your eyes watered, and you got tired faster. You had to sort of rest your eyes on it. Like resting your hand on a dog.

The lane leaned.

All night. He felt it. Not a moving; a wanting to move. The stones of it, the walls either side, the corner at the end, leaning. Every time his eyes got heavy. Every time his mind went somewhere else, to eight, to the Grey, to the stair. A lean. The way a ship leans on its anchor when the tide turns.

He didn’t look away.

The rain stopped at about midnight. Then it was very quiet. Turnstead was always quiet at night, the way Lanthorn had been quiet; everybody awake who was awake was watching, and you didn’t talk when you watched. Just the creak. The low slow creak of the whole town, everywhere, settling, wanting to turn.

At about two his eyes went.

He felt it coming. The heaviness. The lid of his eye coming down on its own. The lane going grey and soft at the edges. And the lean in the stones getting stronger, stronger, like a hand on his back, pushing—

He took out Ressa’s hammer.

He didn’t think about it. It was just in his hand. He got up out of the chair, still watching the lane, sideways, and went to the anvil, which was just inside the door, where he could see the lane from it. And he put his free hand flat on the anvil, cold. And he lifted the small old hammer.

THUD.

Not a tink. He didn’t do tinks. He hit Pask’s anvil the way Ressa had hit hers, with the whole of his arm, and the sound went out of the forge and up the lane and off the walls and away over the roofs of Turnstead in the dark.

His eyes came open.

He counted. One. Two. Three. Four.

THUD.

The lane stopped leaning.

He didn’t know why. He’d think about it later, for a long time, with Cursiffe. He just knew that when the hammer went, the lane stopped pushing. Like a horse that hears its rider’s voice. Like a ship that feels the anchor bite.

THUD.

Every four seconds. The way Ressa had done it. The way he’d heard it for forty nights, and in his sleep on nine, and on ten under the Breath, and on the Patience, and on the stair at Tell. Four seconds. THUD. He stood at Pask’s anvil with Ressa’s hammer and struck the empty anvil every four seconds, and watched the lane, sideways, and the lane stayed.

At about three, he heard a window open.

Up the lane somewhere. And then another. And then, faint, a voice. Calling something. He couldn’t hear what. He didn’t look. He struck.

THUD.

At about four, he heard singing.

He nearly looked then. Nearly. He knew the voice. He knew the song. He’d known it since floor 2, since the first nights, when she’d sing it last thing, quiet, at the end of every night, after everybody had stopped talking, the same one every time, so you’d know it was the end of the day. He’d hummed it in the dark on floor 2 when he couldn’t sleep, and never told her.

It was coming down the lane. Slowly. Wendy’s voice. Not loud. The last song of the night.

He didn’t look. He struck.

THUD.

And she came down the lane singing it, and sat down on the step of the forge, in the door, beside his feet, with her back to him and her face to the lane. Watching it. And kept singing. On his beat. The song fitting itself to the four seconds like it had always been meant to.

He didn’t say anything. He didn’t stop.

After a while, he started to hum it.

THUD.

The sky over the roofs went grey at about six.

He was still standing. His arm was dead. He’d switched hands four times and both of them were dead. Wendy had stopped singing an hour ago and was just sitting on the step with her bow across her knees, watching the lane, and every so often, very quietly, saying four, under her breath, on the beat, the way Newish would.

The lane was there.

Every stone of it. The corner at the end. The narrow walls. Pask’s forge. Exactly where it had been at dusk.

Behind him, in the dark of the forge, he heard a stair creak.

Pask came down. Slowly. One step at a time. In a nightshirt, with his white wisp of hair flat on one side from the pillow. He stood at the bottom of the stair and looked at Salty at the anvil with the small hammer, and Wendy on the step, and the lane beyond them both. Grey in the dawn. There.

He looked at it for a long, long time.

“I heard it,” said Pask.

“Sorry. I didn’t— I couldn’t keep my eyes open, so I—“

“No.” Pask came across the forge. He put his old thin hand flat on the anvil beside Salty’s, where it was warm now from the hammering. “I heard it. In my sleep. All night.
