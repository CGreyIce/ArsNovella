Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 4: The Second Row

Chapter 147

Plan B

4,307 words · 19 min read

Malcolm made three plans for the Reckoning, because two wasn’t enough.

He made them on the morning after the crier, sitting on his stool by the pump in the copy-room yard, before anybody came to be healed, with a slate on his knee that he’d borrowed from Drew and a stub of chalk. He’d always done it this way. On Earth it had been lists on the backs of envelopes, two copies, one in each pocket. Up here it was slates. He’d done it on floor 4 before the Kiln and on floor 8 before the Cataract and on floor 10 before every night on the wall. Plan A. Plan B. Plan C. In case.

He wrote A at the top, and looked at it for a long time.

The disputed man’s two hands shall be read in full. Every line. Before the Magistrate, and before Tell.

He didn’t know what was in the lines. That was the first problem. He didn’t know, and nearly everybody he could ask either didn’t know either, or did, and wasn’t saying.

There was a club. Malcolm had known that since floor 6.

Everybody had, more or less. You couldn’t have thirteen people go quiet every time you came into a room without noticing. On floor 10 they’d met in the hay hall over the stables on the fourth level, and on floor 11 in the Patience’s sail locker, and on floor 12 behind the Mast. On floor 13, since the crier, they met in the old tack room at the far end of the stables under the copy-room. Malcolm had seen them go in last night, one at a time, trying not to look like they were going anywhere. Hyvred, who was very bad at it. Chris, who was worse. Winter, who didn’t bother. Xav.

Xav had gone in last, and looked back once at the stable door, at Malcolm sitting on the bottom stair, and hadn’t said anything.

Malcolm hadn’t minded. Not really. He’d decided a long time ago, on the Breath stair on floor 10, sitting at the top of it all night in the snow while Xav went down to tell Gabe whatever it was he’d sworn to tell him, that he didn’t need to be in the room. He needed to be at the top of the stairs. In case.

But he’d been on the roof with Rui Xiang the night before the crier. And he’d heard Rui Xiang say it, with his back against the parapet and his hands shaking.

Thea already knows. Not what you think. What is. Somebody told her.

He hadn’t told anyone he’d heard. He’d sat by the chimney with his arms round his knees and not said anything, and when Xav looked at him afterwards, he’d looked back and nodded, once, and Xav had understood. I heard. I’m not going to do anything with it.

He still wasn’t. He was going to make three plans.

He went to see Askew about Plan A.

She was at her desk at the back of the Reading Hall, under the black door, reading something with her nose very close to it. She looked up when he came and stood in front of the desk, and her walnut face didn’t change, but she put her pen down.

“You’re the other healer,” she said. “The slow one. With the pump.”

“Malcolm.”

“Malcolm.” She folded her hands. “What do you want, Malcolm?”

“I want to know how it works,” he said. “The Assize. When it asks who writes him. Exactly. What we have to do. What counts. Where we stand. What we write on.” He held up Drew’s slate. “Do we bring our own slates? Is it chalk? How big does it have to be? What if the chalk breaks?”

Askew looked at him for a long moment.

“Nobody,” she said, “in forty years, has ever asked me what happens if the chalk breaks.”

“It does break,” said Malcolm. “Chalk breaks. Higu snaps hers all the time.”

Something moved at the corner of Askew’s mouth.

She told him. All of it. She took a long time over it, because he kept asking things. The Assize sat in the great hall above the Bench’s crypt. The Magistrate behind its screen at the far end. The disputed one stood in front of the screen. The two hands were read aloud by the Count’s clerk, the first, then the second. Then the Magistrate said who writes him. And anybody in the hall who was told by Tell, and not corded or held, could come forward to the long table in front of the screen and take a slate from the stack there, the Tellhouse’s slates, chalk provided, and write him. Their name at the top. Then him, as they knew him. As much or as little as they liked. And give it to a teller, who read it aloud. And put it on the table, face up. And the next.

“And then?” said Malcolm.

“And then the Magistrate reads all of them,” said Askew. “Every slate. And weighs them. Against the first hand, and against the second.”

“Weighs them how? Do they have to match? Word for word? Like the door-roll?”

“No.” Askew shook her head. “Not word for word. That’s for tellers. This is for people.” She was quiet. “True to. Whether what you’ve written could be the same man as the first hand. Or as the second. The Magistrate reads every slate and asks of each one: which of these two is the man you’re describing? And when it’s read them all, it says which hand stands.” She picked up her pen again. “That’s all.”

Malcolm stood there and thought about it.

“What if it’s both?”

Askew’s pen stopped.

“What?”

“What if everybody writes him, and what they write is true to the first hand and the second? Both at once?” He frowned at the slate in his hand. “Then the hands agree. Don’t they? If what we write is true to both of them. Then they’re the same man.”

Askew looked at him for a very long time.

“Two hands that disagree,” she said slowly, “can’t both be true to the same slate. That’s—“ She stopped. “That’s never happened.”

“Okay,” said Malcolm. He wrote something on his slate. “But if it did.”

Askew didn’t answer. She was looking at him the way she’d looked at Orlo when he struck through his own line.

“Plan A,” said Malcolm, writing. “We write him. All thirty-five. Chalk provided.” He looked up. “Thank you, Remembrancer. Can I have a slate? A spare. In case.”

Plan B was Hawise’s list.

Two sheets, both sides, and Orlo had started a third. Sixty-one names, the last time Malcolm counted. Citizens of Tell who’d asked to write the corded man and been told no, not till the Reckoning, and had put their names down for the Reckoning instead. Nell from the half-door. Joan Tull. Aldo, first. Wat and Edith. The porter. Bertha. Ivo Rook. Rafe Collier, who’d thrown the mud. The knife-grinder from the Shambles. The lamplighter. Two old sisters. A great many people Malcolm had never met, from the Low Town and the Hay Market and the coopers’ lanes, who’d been given something in the giving and hadn’t forgotten it.

If the Bench stops us, he wrote. Tell writes him.

He looked at that for a while.

Then he wrote C.

Declan came to the pump on the second evening.

Malcolm didn’t see him come in. He was busy. A little girl from the Tanners’ Row with a fever that had gone into her chest, and her mother holding her on her knee by the trough, and Malcolm with both hands on the girl’s back, agreeing with her lungs, the way Rachael had taught him, slowly, finding what they were trying to do and helping them do it. He gave her two. He always gave two. One for the job and one in case. The mother cried and kissed his hands, which nobody had ever done before, and took the girl home, and across the yard, on the corner by the stable door, the clerk that stood there now wrote something in its book. It always did, when he healed somebody. He’d stopped minding.

He turned round on his stool to wash his hands in the basin, and Declan was sitting on the edge of the trough.

He’d come in from the Fell Gate. You could tell. He had the lamp shield on his back, scratched, and his boots were grey with the chalk dust of the fells, and his left forearm was wrapped in a bit of torn shirt that had been white and wasn’t now. Thea and Xola weren’t with him. Malcolm looked past him at the gate and couldn’t see them.

“Xola’s at the Fell Gate,” said Declan. “There was a goatherd. On the way back. His leg. She did it and she’s got nothing left. Thea’s with her. They’re coming slowly.”

“Oh,” said Malcolm.

Declan held out his arm.

“Can you?” he said.

Malcolm sat on his stool and looked at the arm.

He’d known Declan for a long time. Since before the tower. Not as well as Gabe, or Xav, or Rui Xiang. But for years. And up here he’d watched him for three hundred and eighty-five days, a hundred and fifty of them from close enough to heal him. He’d been on the root on floor 9 the day Xav’s party watched, with his backup heal half-cast all day, waiting. He’d said it to Xav that night: When his hand goes gold, it was bad. That’s the only sign.

And in all that time, on all those floors, Declan had never once come to anybody and held out his arm and asked.

He’d healed himself. Or he’d left it. Or he’d let it be done to him, after, sitting very still with his face doing nothing, the way you’d let somebody brush your hair. But he’d never asked.

“Mal,” said Declan.

“Sorry.” Malcolm took the arm. Unwrapped the torn shirt. It was a long cut, down the outside of the forearm from the elbow, not deep, but dirty, with grey chalk in it. “What did this?”

“Fell-cat. Big one. Lv sixty-two.” Declan didn’t look at it. “It’s fine, it’s just—“ He stopped.

Malcolm looked up.

Declan’s mouth was shut. He was looking at the yard gate, at nothing. As if he’d heard himself start to say something, and caught it halfway, like a cup going off the edge of a table.

“It hurts,” said Declan. Quietly. “It’s not bad. But it hurts. And Xola’s got nothing left. So.” He looked down at his own arm. “Can you?”

Malcolm didn’t say anything for a moment.

Then he put both hands on the cut, and agreed with it.

It went in easily. Declan’s arm wanted to close. It always did. Malcolm had healed him maybe a dozen times, after, on a dozen floors, and every time it had been the same: like pushing at a door that was already open. Paladin. His body knew how. It just needed somebody to give it a reason not to wait.

The cut closed. Clean. The grey chalk came out of it in a fine dust onto the yard stones.

And Malcolm gave it a second one. In case.

Declan felt it go in. Malcolm saw him feel it. He looked at Malcolm.

“You did two.”

“I always do two.”

“I know.” Something happened at the corner of Declan’s mouth. “I just never stayed still long enough to notice.”

Malcolm took his hands away. He sat on his stool with the basin of chalky water at his feet and looked at Declan, and couldn’t think of a single thing to say.

“In thirteen floors,” he said at last, “you’ve never asked.”

“I know.”

“Not once. Not on four, not on nine, not on the wall. You’d do your gold thing. Or you’d just—“ Malcolm stopped. “What changed?”

Declan didn’t answer straight away. He looked at his arm. At the clean pink line where the cut had been. He turned it over in the last of the light.

“I said I’d try,” he said.

“Try what?”

“Heal sooner.” He was quiet. “Somebody asked me. I said I’d try.”

He didn’t say who. He didn’t need to. Malcolm had been on the root on floor 9 when Thea came up the rope last with the lantern and walked past everybody to stand in front of Declan, with the angriest face Malcolm had ever seen on anyone.

“Okay,” said Malcolm.

“Thanks, Mal.”

“You’re welcome.” He reached for the basin. “I’m putting it on the slate, you know. Declan asked. Somebody should write it down.”

Declan laughed. Short, surprised, through his nose.

And at the yard gate, coming in slowly, Xola, grey with tiredness, leaning on Thea, stopped dead. She looked at Declan sitting on the trough. At his arm. At Malcolm’s hands, wet. At the clean pink line.

“Did you—“ said Xola. “Did he just—“

“He asked,” said Malcolm.

Xola looked at Declan for a long time. Then she let go of Thea and came across the yard and sat down on the trough beside him, heavily, and put her head on his shoulder, and closed her eyes.

“Good,” said Xola. “Good, Icey.”

Thea stood in the gate with the lantern and didn’t say anything. But Malcolm saw her face. And he thought, he’d tell Xav afterwards, that it was the face of somebody who’d been carrying something up a stair for a long time and had just, for one step, felt somebody else take some of the weight.

The club met again that night. In the tack room. Malcolm sat on the bottom stair of the stable steps, where he always sat, and listened to them not being quiet enough.

He couldn’t hear words. Just voices. Winter’s, flat and hard. Hyvred’s, in capitals, then hushed. Chris’s, reasonable, going on for a long time. Drew’s. And then a long silence. And then one voice, very quiet, that he could only just hear. Thea’s. Saying something short.

And then the door opened, and Thea came out.

She came out on her own, with the lantern. She didn’t look at him. She went past him up the stable stairs, to the landing outside the copy-room door, where the stair turned, and stopped there, and sat down on the top step with the lantern beside her, and put her face in her hands.

Malcolm didn’t move.

He thought about going up. He thought about it for a long time. He was a healer. Somebody was sitting on a step with their face in their hands. That was what he did.

But he didn’t think it was the kind of thing that hands fixed. And he didn’t think she’d want the slow one with the pump, who wasn’t in her club, to see her like that.

So he stayed where he was. At the bottom of the stairs. In case.

After a while, the copy-room door opened above her, and Gabe came out.

He didn’t know Gabe had been waiting for her. He found out afterwards that he had. Gabe had been sitting just inside the copy-room door for an hour, with Hearthwall across his knees, listening to the voices from the tack room through the floor, the way Malcolm had listened to them from the stairs.

Gabe sat down on the step beside her. Not close. A little way off. The way you’d sit beside somebody on a bench in a hospital corridor.

Neither of them said anything for a long time.

Malcolm sat very still on the bottom step, in the dark, and thought about leaving, and didn’t, because he’d have had to walk past them.

“Thea,” said Gabe, at last.

“Gabe.”

“What they’re going to read.” Gabe’s voice was very low. Malcolm had to hold his breath to hear it. “At the Reckoning. Every line.” A pause. “Is it bad?”

Thea took her hands away from her face. She didn’t look at him. She looked at the lantern.

“I can’t,” she said.

“I know you can’t.” Gabe didn’t move. “I’m not asking what. I’d never ask you what. I know it’s not yours.” He was quiet. “I’m asking are you all right.”

Thea didn’t say anything for so long that Malcolm thought she wasn’t going to.

“No,” she said.

“Okay,” said Gabe.

That was all. He didn’t say anything else. He didn’t put his arm round her. He just moved, a little, along the step, until there was less of a gap. And then he sat there, with Hearthwall leaning against the wall beside him, warm, with its three flames glowing very faintly from inside in the dark of the landing, and she sat there with the lantern, amber, and neither of them said anything at all.

Malcolm sat on the bottom step.

He understood something, sitting there. He’d been trying to understand it for a long time, since floor 10, since Rui Xiang on the barracks roof said Xav’s holding something and he’d thought, so’s Gabe, so’s Thea. He understood it now. It came to him the way a diagnosis came, sometimes, all at once, after you’d been looking at the same symptoms for days.

They both knew. Gabe and Thea. Whatever it was. They both knew.

And neither of them could tell the other one. Not because they didn’t trust each other. Because it wasn’t theirs to tell. Thea’s was somebody else’s, given to her. And Gabe’s was something he’d worked out himself and been told was only a guess, and had decided to wait for. And so they were sitting on the same step, a hand’s width apart, holding the same thing, and neither of them could put their end of it down long enough to pick up the other end.

He’d never seen anything so lonely in his life. Or anything so kind.

“Gabe,” said Thea, after a long time.

“Mm.”

“At the Reckoning—“

“I’ll be on his right.”

“I know.” She stopped. “I know you will. I need—“ She stopped again. Malcolm heard her breathe in, and out. “Not tonight. I’ll tell you. What I need. Not tonight.”

“Okay,” said Gabe. “Whenever.”

She picked up the lantern. Stood. Went in through the copy-room door.

Gabe sat on the step a while longer, with Hearthwall.

Then he said, without turning round, quite quietly, “You can come up now, Mal.”

Malcolm went very red in the dark.

“How long have you known?”

“Since you sat down,” said Gabe. “You always sit at the bottom of stairs.” He still didn’t turn round. “It’s all right. I’m glad it was you.”

Malcolm came up the stairs. He sat down on the step below Gabe’s. Gabe moved Hearthwall a little, so the warm side of it was against Malcolm’s back.

“I’m not in the club,” said Malcolm.

“Neither am I.”

“I don’t want to be.”

“Me neither.” Gabe was quiet. “I just want to be on his right.”

“I want to be at the top of the stairs,” said Malcolm.

Gabe looked down at him then, finally, and something in his face eased.

“You are,” he said.

He found Xav on the roof, after. Of course he did. Xav was always on the roof when he couldn’t sleep, and Malcolm had been finding him there since floor 10.

“How was it?” said Malcolm. Meaning the tack room.

Xav didn’t answer straight away. He was sitting on the parapet with his arms folded, looking across the Wellgate at the Countinghouse. At its plain grey door, shut. At the clerk on the corner below it, under the dead lamp.

“Winter wants to go in,” he said. “And take the book. Or burn it. Hyvred says you can’t burn it, she’s looked at it from the street and it’s not paper, it’s the same as the clerks, the same nothing. Chris wants to tell everyone. All thirty-five. What we think. Before Tell hears it read out. So nobody finds out in front of the whole city. Winter’s way. Tell the club first, then the rest.”

“And?”

“And Thea didn’t say anything. For an hour. She sat in the corner with the lantern and let us argue.” Xav’s voice was very quiet. “And then she stood up and said: It’s not ours to tell. It’s not the club’s. It’s not mine. And she went out.”

Malcolm sat down on the lead beside him.

“So what’s the plan?”

Xav laughed. Not much. “I don’t know. You’re the plan man.”

“I’ve got three,” said Malcolm.

Xav turned his head.

“Plan A,” said Malcolm. He held up the slate. “We write him. All of us. At the Assize. Askew says it’s not word for word. It’s true to. The Magistrate reads what we write and asks which hand it’s true to. So we write him as we know him. Thirty-five slates. Chalk provided.”

“And if Tull stops us?”

“Plan B. Tell writes him. Hawise’s list. Sixty-one names. More by now.” Malcolm turned the slate over. “And plan C.”

“What’s C?”

Malcolm looked at the back of the slate. He’d written it on the bench that afternoon, after Declan had held out his arm, and he hadn’t shown it to anybody.

C. Whatever they read out. Whatever it says. We stand next to him while they read it. And we don’t move.

Xav read it over his shoulder. He read it twice.

“That’s not a plan,” said Xav.

“It’s the only one that always works,” said Malcolm. “Every floor. Every time.” He looked at it. “It worked on the fifth wall. Nobody moved. It worked on the heap on floor 9. It worked in the white room, the first day, before any of us knew anything. Thirty-five of us in a ring round an empty circle.”

Xav didn’t say anything for a long time.

“I heard what Rui said,” said Malcolm. “On the roof. About Thea.”

Xav went still.

“I’m not going to do anything with it,” said Malcolm. “I just wanted you to know I heard. So you’re not carrying that as well.” He put the slate down on the lead between them. “I’m not in your club, Xav. I don’t want to be. I don’t want to know what’s in the book. I want to be at the top of the stairs. That’s all I’ve ever wanted. Since the Breath.”

“You were,” said Xav. Hoarse. “On the Breath stair. All night. In the snow.”

“I’ll be at the top of the Assize stairs too,” said Malcolm. “In case.” He looked across at the Countinghouse. “With a spare slate.”

Xav laughed. Properly, this time. He put his hand over his face and laughed into it, quietly, so as not to wake the copy-room, and his shoulders shook.

“Malcolm,” he said. “Has a plan B for his plan B.”

“Plan C,” said Malcolm. “Keep up.”

On the twenty-ninth day in Tell, Calvin went back to Cooper’s Lane at noon for the third time. Malcolm went with him, because Rui Xiang had a hunt, and Calvin said he needed somebody who’d notice things and not say anything, and everybody agreed that was Malcolm.

Hawise was on her step. The clerk came round the corner.

“ALYS,” said Calvin. Not shouting. Calling. Across a field.

She stopped. She turned her head. Further than last time, Calvin said after; Malcolm hadn’t seen last time. Far enough that Malcolm could see her face. Plain. Pale. A face you’d forget while you were looking at it.

And her lips moved.

No sound. Nothing. Just the lips, once, very slightly, the way your lips move in your sleep when you’re dreaming you’re saying something.

Then she turned away, and walked on, and was gone.

Hawise didn’t put her hands over her face this time. She sat on her step and looked at the corner where the grey had gone, very steadily, with her chin up.

“What did she say?” said Calvin. “Mal. You were closer. What did she—“

Malcolm thought about it. He thought about it the way he thought about a patient: carefully, all the way through, without deciding what he wanted it to be.

“I don’t know,” he said honestly. “It was short. One word. Maybe the start of one.”

Hawise turned her head and looked at him.

“What did it look like?” she said. “The start.”

Malcolm looked at her. At her inky hands in her lap. At her face, which had sat on that step every week for twelve years.

“It looked like Haw,” he said.
