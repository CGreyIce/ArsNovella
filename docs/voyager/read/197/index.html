Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 5: Everyone Comes Back Down

Chapter 197

Agree With It

5,156 words · 22 min read

Malcolm had one plan, and he hated it.

He always had three. He’d had three for the Reckoning, because two wasn’t enough. He’d had three for the Kiln on floor 4, and three for the Gate on ten, and three for the Grey on fourteen, though the third one for the Grey had been mostly run. On Earth it had been lists on the backs of envelopes, two copies, one in each pocket, in case he lost one. He didn’t trust anything that only had one way of going right. Things that only had one way of going right went wrong.

For this, he had one.

A. The remedy works.

He’d written it on the back of Drew’s slate on the first morning on floor 1, sixty-three days ago, sitting on the step of the class hall in the painted snow, and then sat there with the chalk in his hand trying to write B. He’d tried every morning since. He’d tried on the twentieth day, after Kei put a grain of the blue flower on his own tongue and couldn’t talk. He’d tried on the fortieth, after the rota went up. He’d tried last night, on the first night, sitting against the wall of the long room in the dark with thirty-four other people not sleeping, listening to an old woman cough through the floorboards over the kitchen.

He couldn’t think of a B.

There wasn’t one. That was the thing. If the remedy didn’t work, there wasn’t anything else. There was no other flower. There was no other root. There was no other floor to go down to. This was the bottom. This was floor 1, and an old woman in her bed under the eaves, and thirty-six people in her cookhouse who’d come down thirteen floors to sit with her, and if it didn’t work then it didn’t work, and there was no B, and Malcolm had been a pessimist his whole life, on purpose, carefully, so that nothing could ever surprise him, and he had never once in his life been so frightened of being right.

On the morning of the second day, she was worse.

He knew before Kei said it. He knew from the stair. He came into the kitchen at first light and stood at the bottom of it and looked up at the line of light under the door at the top, yellow and amber, the candle and the lantern, and listened. You could hear her breathing from the kitchen. You hadn’t been able to, yesterday. Fast and shallow and wet at the bottom, like somebody breathing through cloth.

Kei came down at seven. He’d been up all night; he’d been up all night on the first night of the rota and on every night before it, as far as Malcolm could tell, since fourteen. His face was grey. He had his book under his arm and the jar in his coat and Dose round his neck with her head up, tasting the air.

“Second dose went in at midnight,” said Kei. To all of them. They’d all looked up when he came down; thirty-five people at the long table, not eating the porridge Thea and Gabe had made between them, which was, Hako said, technically porridge. “Third at six. Same amount. A grain.” He swallowed. “She’s hotter.”

Nobody said anything.

“It’s the second day,” said Kei. “Ma Yarrow said. The second day’s worse. It’s supposed to be worse. It goes up for two days and on the third night it—“ He stopped. “It turns. One way or the other.”

“Which way?” said Calvin.

Kei looked at him.

“I don’t know,” he said.

Malcolm looked at his porridge.

A. The remedy works.

He took out the slate. Drew’s. He’d kept it. He looked at the empty space under A where B should have been, and then he put it away again, and picked up his spoon, and ate the porridge, because Rachael was watching him and Rachael had told him once, on floor 4, that a healer who didn’t eat was a healer who’d fall over at the wrong moment, and he’d written that down.

Rachael went up at eight.

She wasn’t on the rota at eight. She was on it at nine the third night, with Kei and Xola and Maigo, the bad slot, the one Kei had saved for the healers. But she went up at eight anyway, with her gloves in her hand, and Malcolm went after her, because he was on at eight, and because Rachael going up a stair with her gloves off was a thing he’d learned to follow.

The room under the eaves was small and hot. Too hot. The candle in the window and the lantern beside it. Chi on the end of the chest, coming off her shift, with her cup. Dec in the chair.

He was always in the chair. Malcolm had stopped expecting anything else by the middle of the first night. Kei’s rule said three hours, and Dec did his three hours, at the beginning, and then he went down and sat on the bottom step with Thea, and then somebody would come down at the end of their three hours and say she asked for you. And he’d go back up. She did ask. In the night she’d started to ask, every hour or so, in a cracked hot voice, where’s the onion boy, and he’d go up, and sit in the chair, and she’d find his wrist with her hand on the quilt and hold it. And then she’d sleep, or not sleep, and he’d sit there, and the next person on the rota would come up and sit on the chest across from him. Nobody told him to go. Kei had stopped saying it at two in the morning.

He looked up when they came in. His face. Malcolm had known that face since poly, the first week, when Dec had talked to every single person in the class one after another until they’d all decided to like each other, out of what Malcolm had thought at the time was pure stubbornness. He’d thought he knew all its weathers. He’d seen it go white on fourteen. He’d seen it go grey, on the shield, in the crypt in Tell.

He’d never seen it like this. Like somebody holding something very heavy, very still, so it wouldn’t spill.

“She’s hotter,” said Dec.

“I know,” said Rachael.

She knelt down by the bed. Mother Crane was asleep, or nearly, with her face red and wet and her mouth open, her chest going up and down too fast under the patched quilt. Her hand was on Dec’s wrist. Even asleep. Rachael looked at her for a long moment, the way she looked at everything she was going to mend, frowning, measuring. Then she put her bare right hand flat on the old woman’s forehead.

“Rachael,” said Malcolm.

“I know.”

“You’re on at nine tomorrow. The third night. Kei said—“

“I know what Kei said.” She didn’t take her hand away. “She’s too hot. She’s going to cook. She’ll not get to the third night at this.” She shut her eyes. “I’m just taking some. An hour. To get her down.”

Malcolm knew what Burden was. Everybody did. He’d seen it on the Long Acre, at the straw man’s frozen foot, an hour a day, Rachael’s arms going white to the shoulder and Calvin holding her hands after without saying a word, the only time anybody had ever seen Calvin not say a word. He’d seen it in the crypt in Tell, when she pulled the grey out of Dec on the shield and her hands went white to the elbow. It’ll come out of me, she’d said, both times. That’s what it’s for.

He’d never seen her take heat.

It went into her slowly. He watched it. A flush, coming up her wrist from the hand on Mother Crane’s forehead, red, like a burn. Up her forearm. Her face went pink, then red. Sweat came out on her lip. She didn’t move. She knelt by the bed with her hand flat on the old woman’s head and her eyes shut and took it, a little at a time, the way you’d take water out of a boat with a cup.

Mother Crane’s breathing slowed. A little. Not much. Enough that you could hear the difference.

“Malcolm,” said Rachael. Without opening her eyes. Her voice was thick, like somebody with a fever. “Do your thing.”

He blinked. “What thing?”

“Your blessing. The one. On her.” Rachael swallowed. “I can take it out. I can’t put anything in. Put something in.”

He’d learned to heal on floor 4.

He’d never meant to. He’d been a Cleric because it was the thing in the class hall on the first morning that had sounded least likely to get him killed, and he’d done blessings, the light on the shoulder that made a hit hurt less or a step go further, because they were what the Cleric’s door gave you. He was good at them. He always put two. One and then another behind it, in case the first one slipped. Malcolm the backup, Rui Xiang called him. Not unkindly. It was just true.

And then on floor 3 he’d decided he should learn to heal, as well. As a backup. So that somebody could fix Dec when Gabe finally caught up with him and punched him, which everybody had known for forty days was going to happen. And on four he’d made Rachael teach him, every evening, in the Red City, and she’d been a terrible teacher, because she didn’t know how she did it. She just did it. She’d frowned at him and cut her own arm with a knife and held it out and said agree with it, over and over, until he did.

Agree with it. He’d never known what it meant. He’d just done it, eventually, by trying everything else first. You put your hand on the cut and you didn’t push. You didn’t try to close it. You found the part of the arm that already wanted to close, that was already trying, and you agreed with it. You said yes. And it closed. Faster. Not much faster; Rachael said he’d never be fast. But it closed.

He knelt down on the other side of the bed from Rachael. He put his hand out.

He didn’t know what to agree with.

That was the trouble. A cut wanted to close. You could feel it wanting to. A fever didn’t want anything. A fever was just heat, and wet, and too-fast breathing, and an old woman’s body burning up in a bed under the eaves. There was nothing to agree with. There was nothing in it that was trying.

He put a blessing on her anyway. The light on the shoulder. The way you’d put one on a shield before it took a hit. It went on, and sat there, and did nothing he could see. He put a second one behind it. In case.

Nothing.

“It’s not working,” he said.

“It’s not supposed to work,” said Rachael, through her teeth. Her arm was red to the elbow. “It’s supposed to help. Agree with it.”

“With what? There’s nothing—“

“Malcolm.” Rachael opened her eyes. They were wet and too bright, like Mother Crane’s. “What’s a fever?”

He stared at her.

“What is it?” said Rachael. “Not what does it do. What is it?”

He thought about it. He made himself think about it, properly, the way he made plans. A. B. C.

“It’s hot,” he said.

“Why?”

“Because—“ He stopped.

Because she was fighting. That was why. He’d known it on Earth; everybody knew it. A fever wasn’t the illness. A fever was the body, burning itself up to burn the illness out. It was the thing that was trying. The heat was the fight. The old woman in the bed wasn’t doing nothing. She was doing the hardest thing she’d ever done, with everything she had, in her sleep, and it was making her so hot she might cook.

Agree with it.

He put his hand on her chest. Flat, over the quilt, over her heart. He didn’t push. He didn’t try to cool anything. That was Rachael’s job, and Xola’s. He found the thing that was trying. It wasn’t hard, once he knew what he was looking for; it was everywhere. It was the whole of her. Every breath, too fast. Every beat of her heart under his hand, too hard. Fighting.

He agreed with it.

He put a blessing on that. Not on her. On the fight. The light on the shoulder, the one that made a step go further, on the thing in her that was trying. And a second one behind it.

In case.

Under his hand, Mother Crane’s heart went once, hard. And then steadier. Not slower. Steadier. Like somebody who’d been running in the dark and had just been handed a lamp.

Rachael looked at him across the bed.

“There,” she said. “That’s it.” She shut her eyes again. “That’s agreeing with it.”

Malcolm knelt by the bed with his hand on an old woman’s heart and felt it beat, steadier, and didn’t say anything at all. He looked up. Dec was looking at him. From the chair. With Mother Crane’s hand on his wrist and his face held so still.

Dec didn’t say anything either. He just nodded. Once.

Malcolm thought: I learned this so I could fix his face when Gabe hit him.

He’d thought, on floor 3, that that was what a backup was for. He’d thought it was for Dec.

He looked at the old woman in the bed. At Dec’s wrist, under her hand.

It was, he thought. It was for Dec. It just wasn’t his face.

Rachael did an hour. Then she stood up, and swayed, and Malcolm caught her, and she went down the stair with her arms red to the elbow and burning and her face grey, and at the bottom of it Calvin was waiting. He took both her hands. He didn’t say anything. Malcolm had seen him do it on fourteen, every day, and every day it had been the strangest thing he’d ever seen, Calvin not saying anything. Calvin held Rachael’s burning hands in his and stood there at the foot of the stair and looked at her, and she let him, and after a while she put her forehead against his shoulder. Just for a moment. Then she took it away.

“Ten minutes,” she said. “Then I’ll go back.”

“You won’t,” said Calvin. “Kei’ll stop you.”

“Kei can try.”

Xola went up with a bowl of water that wasn’t water, quite; it came out of her palms cold, the way it had on the Glass, and she put cloths in it and laid them on the old woman’s head and wrists and feet and changed them every few minutes, singing under her breath. Maigo went up with his one leaf. Malcolm didn’t know what Maigo did. He stood at the foot of the bed with his shoes off and his bare feet flat on the floorboards and his eyes shut, very still, the way he stood in the snow on two, and after a while Xola said oh, and put her hand on the floor beside the bed, and said it was warm. The boards were warm. Maigo was taking the heat down out of her, through his feet, through the floor, into the kitchen, into the ground under the Spoon, and giving it away.

“Like a lightning rod,” said Hako, at the bottom of the stair, watching. “But for being ill.”

“Classic,” said Hyvred, before he could.

Nobody laughed. But Hako’s face did something grateful.

The town came in the afternoon.

It started with the egg girl. Malcolm was at the long table with his slate, staring at the place where B should have been, when there was a knock at the front door. Nobody had knocked at the Copper Spoon’s door in sixty-four days. People just came in. Calvin got up and opened it.

The egg girl was standing on the step. In the painted snow. With her basket on her arm.

She looked past Calvin into the long room. She opened her mouth. And Malcolm, who had heard her say Eggs? Fresh eggs? on her corner by the well every day of the first two weeks and every day of the last sixty-four, the same three words to everybody and nobody, like a bell, watched her open her mouth to say it.

“Are they for her?” said the egg girl.

Calvin looked at her.

“The eggs,” she said. Slowly. Like she was finding the words one at a time on a shelf she’d never looked at before. “Are they for her? Mother Crane. My brother said she’s poorly. He said the goat’s in the street. He said there’s a candle in her window.” She held out the basket. “These are for her. Not for selling. For her.”

Calvin took the basket.

“What’s your name?” he said.

The egg girl looked at him as if nobody had ever asked her that. Malcolm thought it was quite possible nobody ever had.

“Elsie,” she said.

“Thanks, Elsie,” said Calvin. “I’ll tell her. I’ll tell her it was you.”

And the egg girl went red to the ears, and turned, and ran.

After that it didn’t stop. All afternoon. The miller, with a sack of flour, and his father behind him with his cough nearly gone, leaning on a stick. The woman from the tannery with the burned arm, with a jar of something that smelled of goose fat. The old smith, who’d sat on his anvil in astonishment on the first day, with a little iron trivet he’d made that morning, for the pot. Quill, from the stationer’s, with a notebook. A notebook. Blank. He held it out to Calvin with both hands and said, “For writing down. In case she wants to. When she’s better.” Children, in a little line, with nothing, who just wanted to stand on the step and look at the light in the window. A man nobody knew, with a cheese.

They didn’t come in. They stood on the step, one after another, or in the street in the snow, round the goat, which was lying on the cobbles where it had lain down last night when the candle went in, and they held out what they’d brought. And Calvin took every single thing, and asked every single name, and said thanks, and I’ll tell her, and I’ll tell her it was you.

By dusk there were people standing in Crooked Street all the way up to the corner. Not doing anything. Just standing. In the painted snow, under the painted sky, with their faces turned up to the small square window under the eaves where a candle burned and a lantern beside it.

Malcolm went up the stair at dusk with the notebook from Quill, because Calvin had asked him to.

Mother Crane was awake. Just. Her eyes were open, too bright, on the ceiling. Dec was in the chair. Thea was on the chest. Malcolm stood at the foot of the bed by the boots and held up the notebook.

“From Quill,” he said. “For writing down. When you’re better.”

Mother Crane’s eyes moved to it. Slowly.

“Quill,” she said. Her voice was cracked and hot. “Quill’s never given anybody anything. Forty years. Sells you a pencil and counts the shavings.”

“He’s on your step,” said Dec. Quietly. “Half the town’s on your step. In the snow. With eggs.”

Mother Crane looked at him. Then at the window. At the candle, and the lantern. She couldn’t see the street from the bed. She looked at the window for a long time anyway.

“What are they doing?” she said.

“Waiting,” said Dec. “For you.”

“I’m not going anywhere.”

“I know,” said Dec. “Tell them that.”

“Tell them I’m not dead,” said Mother Crane, and shut her eyes. “Tell them I’ll want my trivet back. Smith’s been making that trivet since his wife died and never finished it. Tell him it’s about time.” She was quiet. “Tell the egg girl thank you.”

“Elsie,” said Malcolm.

Mother Crane’s eyes opened again.

“Her name,” said Malcolm. “The egg girl. It’s Elsie. Calvin asked.”

Mother Crane looked at him for a long moment. Something moved in her face that Malcolm couldn’t read.

“Elsie,” she said. “Forty years she’s been on that corner. I never knew.” She shut her eyes. “Tell Elsie thank you.”

On the second night Gabe sat on Dec.

Malcolm saw it happen. It was nearly midnight, and Dec had been in the chair for nine hours of the last twelve, and down on the bottom step for the other three, and he hadn’t eaten anything since the morning of the first day except what Thea had put in his hand and watched him swallow. His face had gone past grey into something with no colour at all. His hands were shaking, a fine fast shake, all the time now, that he didn’t seem to notice.

Gabe came up the stair at the change of the rota with Hearthwall on his back and stood in the door and looked at him.

“Dec,” said Gabe.

“I’m fine.”

“You’re not. You’re shaking. You look like something I’d scrape off my boot.” Gabe ducked under the beam. “Up.”

“She asked for—“

“She’s asleep. She’s got Malcolm’s thing on her and Xola’s cloths and Maigo’s feet and Thea, and Chi on the chest, and she’s asleep, and you’re coming downstairs and you’re lying down for one hour, and I’m sitting on you.” Gabe put out his hand. “One hour. Then you can come back up and sit in your stupid chair till it falls apart.”

“Gabe—“

“You don’t get a vote on this kind, mate,” said Gabe. “I told you on fourteen. You never get a vote on this kind.”

Dec looked at him. At the hand. Then down at Mother Crane, asleep, with her hand on his wrist.

Thea, on the chest, leaned over and very gently lifted the old woman’s hand off his wrist, and held it herself.

“One hour,” said Thea. “I’ve got her.”

Dec stood up. He swayed, like Rachael had. Gabe caught him. And Malcolm, at the foot of the bed, watched the biggest man on floor 1 put his arm round the shoulders of a man who had once, a long time ago, in poly, talked to every single person in a class until they all decided to like each other, and walk him down a narrow stair behind a stove like you’d walk somebody home from a party.

Malcolm went down after them, at the end of his shift.

Dec was lying on the bench by the stove with his eyes shut. Gabe was sitting on the end of the bench. On his legs. Not all his weight; just enough. Just so he’d know. He had his arms folded and Hearthwall across his knees, warm, and he was looking at the stair.

Dec was asleep. Properly. For the first time in days, as far as Malcolm knew.

“He’ll kill me,” said Gabe, without looking round, “when he wakes up.”

“Probably,” said Malcolm.

“Worth it.” Gabe looked at the stair. “He’s done that his whole life, you know. Since he was thirteen. Sat up with people. He sits in the chair and he doesn’t get up.” He was quiet. “And I’d never once seen anybody make him.”

Malcolm sat down on the floor by the stove.

He got out the slate. He looked at it. A. The remedy works. And under it, the space where B should have been. He’d been looking at that space for sixty-four days.

He thought about the egg girl, Elsie, on the step with her basket. About Quill and the blank notebook. The smith and the trivet. The children in the snow. Rachael’s arms, red to the elbow. Xola’s cloths. Maigo’s bare feet on the warm boards. His own blessing, on the thing in an old woman that was trying. Calvin, saying I’ll tell her it was you. Gabe, sitting on Dec. Thea, holding her hand.

There was still no B. There wasn’t one. There’d never be one.

He wrote, under A, very small:

A is everybody.

And then he looked at it for a long time, and didn’t rub it out.

On the third day she was worse again.

Kei’s fourth dose went in at six in the morning, and the fifth at noon, a grain each, the edge. Rachael did another hour at ten, against Kei’s direct and furious orders, and came down white-faced and burning and let Calvin hold her hands. Malcolm did his blessings, two and two and two, on the fight, every time he was on, and between times when he wasn’t, from the chest, from the door. Xola changed the cloths. Maigo’s feet stayed on the boards. The town stood in the street. Dec woke up after an hour and forty minutes, and found Gabe on his legs, and looked at him, and Gabe looked back, and Dec didn’t kill him. He just got up and went back up the stair.

She was worse.

She didn’t wake much now. When she did, she didn’t know where she was. She talked to her husband. Tam. Tam, your boots. Get your boots off my table, I’ve told you. She talked to her gran, who’d been dead fifty years. One comes down alone, Gran. You said. What happens to them? You never said. She asked for the onion boy, over and over, and he was there, every time, in the chair, and she didn’t seem to know it. She’d find his wrist. And hold it. And ask for him again.

At dusk on the third day, Kei came down the stair and stood at the bottom of it and didn’t say anything for a long time.

Thirty-five people at the long table, in the kitchen, on the floor, against the walls, looking at him.

“The third night,” said Kei. “It’s now. It turns tonight.” He swallowed. His face was the colour of the snow on the step. “One way or the other.”

“Which way?” said Calvin. Like he had on the second morning. He knew the answer. He asked anyway.

Kei looked at him.

“I don’t know,” he said. “I’ve done everything. I’ve done every grain. I’ve measured every—“ His voice went. He stopped. Dose put her head against his cheek, and he shut his eyes. “It’s the right amount,” he said. “She says. It’s the right amount. It’s just not—“ He couldn’t finish it.

Malcolm heard himself say it.

He didn’t mean to. He was a pessimist. He’d been one on purpose all his life so that nothing could ever surprise him, and he’d been so frightened for three days of being right that he hadn’t said a single thing he thought out loud, not once, not to anybody, not even to the slate.

“I don’t think it’s enough,” said Malcolm.

It came out into the kitchen very quietly. Into all that silence. Thirty-five people heard it.

Nobody said anything.

And then, from the far side of the kitchen, from the floor by the pantry door, where he’d sat for three days with his back against WINTER WATCH and his scythe across his knees and his face tipped up at the boards of the ceiling, never once going near the stair, Yan said:

“It isn’t.”

Everybody looked at him.

Yan didn’t look down. He was looking at the ceiling. At something none of them could see, through the boards, above the kitchen, in a small hot room under the eaves.

“Her light’s lifting,” said Yan. Cool and level. The way he said everything. “Since the dusk. It was smaller. Now it’s not smaller. It’s—“ He tilted his head, a fraction, the way you’d tilt your head to listen to something a long way off. “It’s going up,” he said. “Slowly. The way they do.”

Nobody moved.

At the top of the narrow stair, behind the door, under the eaves, Malcolm heard a chair scrape. And then nothing.

Yan put his scythe down on the floor beside him. Carefully. He stood up.

He looked at the stair. The narrow stair behind the stove. Fourteen steps. The one he hadn’t gone near in three days, because wherever he stood there was an edge, and he wasn’t putting one by her bed.

“Wendy,” said Yan.

Wendy was already on her feet.

“Hold this,” said Yan. And he pointed at the scythe on the floor, and didn’t wait to see her pick it up, and crossed the kitchen, and went up the stair.
