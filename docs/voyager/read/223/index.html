Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 6: Unwritten

Chapter 223

Whose

5,449 words · 24 min read

It hummed all night.

He lay on his back in the root-hollow with Thea asleep against his side and the lantern on the floor beside his hip, and listened to it. He couldn’t do anything else. He’d tried. He’d shut his eyes and counted his breath and told himself sleep, the way he’d told himself on every floor in two lives, and his body had said no.

So he lay there and listened.

It wasn’t loud. You had to be close. A faint uneven sound, low, coming out of Gabe’s terrible lantern through the bad knot in forty little points of pale light. Not the Hollow’s note, the one Lanthorn sang its dead out on. Not Newish’s one, two, three. Something else. A tune, nearly. Like somebody humming a song they’d only heard once, a long time ago, through a wall, and were trying to put back together. Getting a bit of it. Losing it. Going back. Trying again.

He knew the song.

He hadn’t said. He hadn’t said to anybody. He’d lain on the moss in the falling dust with Xola’s hands round his and Gabe’s hand on his neck and Thea’s forehead against the back of his head, and the light had started to hum, and he’d heard the first four notes and gone completely still inside, and not said.

He’d heard it every night for a year. Through a phone. From the other end of a call, at two in the morning his time and two in the morning hers, while he played and she sang, quietly, so she wouldn’t wake anybody. Not a song anybody knew. Just one she sang. One she’d made up, or half made up, or picked up from somewhere and changed. He’d never asked. He’d known it so well he could have hummed it in his sleep.

He supposed he had. In two lives. Enough times that a light sitting on his hip for thirteen falls, turned toward his face, listening, could pick it up.

It’s trying to remember, he thought. It’s trying to remember a thing I remember.

Ice, said Alea.

Very quietly. On his shoulder. She’d been quiet all night. She’d been quiet, he realised, since the moss. Since mine. She hadn’t said a word.

“Yeah.”

Can I look at it?

He didn’t say anything for a moment. Then he turned his head on the blanket, carefully, so he didn’t wake Thea, and looked at his own shoulder.

He couldn’t see her. He never could. Nobody could, except Thea, once, for a second, a long time ago. A fox, Thea had said. Small. Red-gold. Sitting on his shoulder looking at her with its head on one side. And then not.

He felt her move. Down his arm. The way she did when she wanted to look at something close. A weight that wasn’t a weight, going down past his elbow, to his wrist, to his hand on the floor. To the lantern beside his hip.

Oh, said Alea.

He waited.

It’s not one of us, said Alea. After a long time. It’s not a system. It hasn’t got anything in it. No windows. No words. No voice. She was quiet. It’s just a light. A stray. Like the ones in the dark. It hasn’t got a single thing in it except the humming. A pause. And you.

“Me?”

It’s turned to you. It’s always turned to you. Even now. With you not looking. It’s turned to where your face is. Her voice went odd. The way the big one’s turned to you. Thea’s. The way that’s been turned to you since the day she found it.

He didn’t say anything.

I’m not jealous, said Alea.

He waited.

I’m a bit jealous, said Alea.

He laughed. He couldn’t help it. Very quietly, into the dark, with Thea asleep against his side. It came up out of his chest like something let out.

Don’t laugh. I’m being serious. But her voice was doing the thing it did when she was nearly laughing too. Two lives. Two lives I’ve been on your shoulder. Every floor. Every night. I’ve told you every number. I told you the numbers on the jam. I told you twenty-six. And not once, not once in two lives, have you ever put your hand over me and said— She stopped.

He stopped laughing.

“You never asked,” he said.

Silence.

“You never asked whose,” he said. Quietly. “You just were. From the first day. You were already mine. You came with the— You were just there, on my shoulder, telling me numbers. You never asked.” He looked at the dark ceiling of the hollow. “It asked.”

Alea didn’t say anything for a very long time.

No, she said finally. Very small. I didn’t, did I.

“Do you want to?”

No. Instantly. Then, slower: No. I know the answer. I’ve always known the answer. That’s why I never asked. A pause. It didn’t know. That’s the difference. It’s been in the dark asking everybody for as long as it’s been a light, and nobody ever said. And you said.

He felt her come back up his arm. Slowly. To his shoulder. And sit.

It’s a nice song, said Alea. The one it’s humming. It keeps getting the middle wrong.

“I know.”

Do you know what it is?

He didn’t answer.

Oh, said Alea. Very softly. Oh. You do.

He lay in the dark with Thea asleep against him and the light on the floor by his hip humming the middle wrong, and didn’t say anything, and Alea didn’t make him.

Lanthorn woke into light.

That was how he’d remember the first fall after. Not the drip. Not the count. Light. He came out of the root-hollow in the morning, if it was morning, which it wasn’t and was, with Thea’s hand in his, and stopped on the walkway, and so did she.

The Hollow had a sky.

Not a real one. Rock. Far up, grey and pale, hung with stone teeth the size of trees, and the crack in the middle of it with the thread of silver water coming down. But lit. Lit all over, softly, from underneath, by the lights.

Strays. Thousands. Everywhere. Drifting in the high air over the white forests, settling on the slopes, gathering in the valleys like mist in the hollows of a field at dawn. Pale and small and soft. So many that the whole Hollow was full of a sort of glow, faint and even, the way the sky’s full of light on a white winter morning before the sun’s come up. Nine kilometres of it. He could see the far wall. He could see the river Milli had seen, catching the light, winding away across the floor of the Hollow between the white forests. He could see villages. Terraces. Stone walls. Old roads.

Lanthorn had never seen any of it.

He watched them find out. All morning. People coming out of the roots onto the walkways and the open moss and standing there, looking out. Old people. Children. Corwen, in the door of his forge, with his hammer in his hand, not moving. Nesta beside him. They didn’t say anything. Nobody said anything. They just looked. The way you’d look at a room you’d lived in all your life in the dark, when somebody finally opened the curtains.

And the strays came in.

Not all of them. Some. Drifting in among the roots, along the walkways, one and two at a time, the way they’d come up the path behind John’s light. Into the empty lanterns. There were so many empty lanterns. Hundreds, on every root, every hook, every chain, empty for years, for Edda’s whole life, some of them. And the strays went into them, one by one, through the knot, and sat there, scattered to a hundred points, humming whose. And Lanthorn went from lace to lit.

He watched a child, a boy of about six, stand on a walkway under an empty lantern with his face turned up and his own glim at his shoulder, watching a stray drift down and go into the lantern above his head. The boy’s mouth was open.

“Is that one for my sister?” said the boy. To nobody. “When she comes?”

His mother, beside him, put her hand on his head and didn’t say anything.

Corwen came to find him at the pool.

He didn’t say anything at first. He stood beside Ice at the edge of the black water, with the drip falling into the middle of it, a thread of silver now that you could see where it came from, all the way down from the crack in the roof. They watched it.

“We’ll go back down,” said Corwen eventually. “To the terraces. Not this fall. Next. Or the one after.” He looked out across the Hollow. “There’s light enough. You can grow things in this. My grandmother said. Barley. Beans. In her gran’s time there were beans.” He was quiet. “We’ll see if the walls still hold.”

“They hold,” said Ice. “Rui Xiang walked them.”

“Then we’ll see if the soil’s any good.”

“Maigo can tell you.”

Corwen looked at him. Then he nodded slowly.

“Edda would have liked to see it,” said Corwen.

“I know.”

“She kept the count sixty years in the dark.” Corwen looked at the drip. “Sixty years of counting it in the dark and never once seeing where it came from.” He was quiet a long time. “Isel’ll see. Isel’ll keep it with the light on.”

Ice didn’t say anything.

Corwen looked down. At his belt. At Gabe’s terrible lantern. At the light in it, humming its wrong middle.

“That’s a bad lantern,” said Corwen.

“I know.”

“The knot’s all wrong. You can see the middle. A moth’d have it in a breath.”

“I know.”

“Nesta’ll make you a better one. She’s already started. She was up all night.” Corwen looked at the light. “It’d go in a better one. It’d be safer.”

Ice looked down at it too.

He thought about it. He did. He thought about Nesta’s lanterns, so fine you couldn’t see where the iron stopped, and the light in a cage like that, scattered to a hundred points, safe, nobody able to find the middle of it.

He thought about Gabe on a root at the edge of the dark with Hearthwall turned inward, handing him a lumpy lopsided thing that the knot came undone at the bottom of. It’s for if. I don’t know. If. Just have it.

“No,” said Ice. “Thank you. Tell her thank you.” He put his hand on the lantern. Lightly. Not over it. Just on the iron. The light hummed. “This one.”

Corwen looked at him for a long moment.

Then something happened in his face. A long way down. Like Sabe’s had on the ledge. Like Hobb’s had in the mud at Keddle.

“My mother would’ve said that,” said Corwen. “She’d have looked at that and said it’s the worst lantern she ever saw. And then she’d have said keep it.” He turned to go back to his forge. Stopped. “You don’t move a light out of the first lantern it chose. Not unless it asks.”

Isel found him in the afternoon.

He was sitting on the root at the bottom of the far ladders, where John had practised and where Higu had said forty paces. The moss below it was grey with the dust that had been the Moth. A long low drift of it, pale, going down the slope, with Gigi’s bramble and Maigo’s roots still going through it, already greening. Already, Maigo said, putting out shoots. Things would grow in it. Moth-dust is good for roots, Maigo had said, with his eyes shut and his feet in it, sounding pleased.

Isel sat down on the root beside him. Not close. About the width of an empty lantern. She had Edda’s in her lap. It had a stray in it now. One of the ones that came up behind John’s light. It hummed whose very quietly, scattered.

She looked at his belt for a long time.

“It came to you,” said Isel.

“It came to the lantern.”

“It came to you.” Isel looked at it. “It went in the lantern because the lantern was on you. It doesn’t care about the lantern. That’s a bad lantern.” She was quiet. “It came to you.”

He didn’t say anything.

“They come to the new ones,” said Isel. “That’s what they do. In a day, or three, or a week. A stray comes to the new one and stays.” She looked at him with her enormous dark eyes. “It’s never come to somebody grown. Not ever. Not in Gran’s time. Not in her gran’s.”

“I’m not new,” said Ice.

Isel looked at him. At the light. Back at him.

“It thinks you are,” said Isel.

He sat on the root and didn’t have anything to say to that at all.

He told Thea at the pool, at the end of the fall. What Isel said.

Not the song. He didn’t tell her about the song. He’d tell her, he thought, some night. On a stair, maybe. Or never. Some things you didn’t tell; you just let somebody find them.

He told her what Isel said. They come to the new ones. It thinks you are.

Thea was sitting on Edda’s old root at the edge of the black water, with her lantern in her lap. Open. She hadn’t wrapped it since the moss. Nobody needed her to. There was nothing at the bottom of the Hollow any more but a long pale drift of dust with green coming up through it. The amber light lay across the water, warm, steady.

Leaning at him.

She looked at him for a long time when he said it.

“You are,” said Thea. “A bit.”

“New?”

“A bit.” She looked at the pool. “You started again. Didn’t you. That’s what you told me. Under the aurora. You started again, from the white room, with everything you knew.” She was quiet. “And on fifteen you stopped knowing. And you’ve been new ever since. Starting things. Asking. Saying I don’t know. Letting Hana go first.” She almost smiled. “Saying mine.”

He didn’t say anything.

“It’s not wrong,” said Thea. “It’s a stray. It’s been in the dark its whole life. It knows a new one when it sees one.”

He sat down beside her on the root. Close. Their shoulders touching. The two lanterns between them, hers in her lap and his on his belt. The big amber one and the small pale one in the bad knot. Both of them turned toward him.

“Can you read it?” he said.

Thea looked at him.

“Its window,” he said. “If it’s got one. You read everything. Under the words.”

“I didn’t want to,” said Thea. “Without asking.”

“I’m asking.”

Thea looked at the little lantern on his belt for a long time. Then she leaned over, slowly, and looked at it the way she looked at windows. Close. Her lips moving.

“It hasn’t got much,” said Thea. “There’s no name. No level. It’s not one of ours. Not a system.” She was quiet. “Just a grey line. Under nothing. The way the Moth’s was. The way all the strays’ are, I think.” She looked closer. “I read the strays’ on the walkways this morning. Every one of them. It’s the same line. Whose. Over and over. Worn in.” She stopped.

“And this one?”

Thea didn’t answer for a moment.

“It was whose,” said Thea. “I can see where it was. The grey’s worn where it was written. Over and over, like the others.” She touched the lantern with one finger. Very lightly. “It’s been written over.”

“With what?”

Thea sat back. She looked at him. Her eyes were very bright in the amber light.

“His,” said Thea.

He looked at her.

“Not mine,” said Thea. “It’s not your window. It’s its. You said mine. It wrote his.” She looked at the lantern. “On the line where whose was. One word. His.”

He didn’t say anything for a long time.

The light on his belt hummed. Four notes. The middle wrong.

Thea went very still.

She turned her head and looked at the lantern. Then at him. Her face did something he’d never seen it do. Not in two lives. Like somebody hearing their own voice on a recording, from a long time ago, in a room they’d forgotten.

“That’s—“ said Thea.

“I know.”

“That’s mine. That’s the— I used to—“ She stopped. “On the calls. I never told anybody that. I never sang it for anybody but—“

“I know.”

“How does it know—“

“It’s been on my belt thirteen falls,” said Ice. “Turned to my face. Listening.” He looked at the pool. “I suppose I hum it. Sometimes. When I’m not thinking.” He was quiet. “I didn’t know I did.”

Thea looked at him for a long, long time.

Then she put the big lantern down on the root beside her, carefully, and put both arms round him, and her face in his neck, and didn’t say anything at all. And he held her. And between them, on his belt, the little light hummed her song, getting the middle wrong, trying again.

The 35’s lights hummed too, now. All of them.

He hadn’t noticed when. Somewhere in the fall after the Moth. He’d walk past somebody on a walkway, and there’d be a sound by their ear, from the little iron cage on their collar. Hana’s: low and steady, the Hollow’s note. Newish’s: one, two, three. Milli’s: something precise, the same short phrase over and over, like a ruler being laid down. Jared’s, which had sat silent since it went in: a single deep note, once in a while, like a hammer on an anvil far off.

Chris had started a new page in THE LIGHTS. Thirty-five names down the left side, and beside each one, how it hummed. He’d got as far as twenty-two before he put the pen down and said this is the best column I’ve ever had and went to sit by the pool for a while.

The voice didn’t come back. The flat calm one, the station announcement, I am not able to answer that. Nobody had heard it since the arch. Windows still opened. Numbers still showed. But nobody’s light read them out any more.

“I don’t miss it,” said Hana. At the pool. With the little lantern on her crown. “I thought I would. Seven hundred and forty days of I am not able.” She touched the lantern. “It’s nicer like this.”

“What does yours say?” said Ice. “When you ask it what it is.”

Hana looked at him with her ears up.

“It hums louder,” said Hana. “For a bit.” She was quiet. “And then it hums at the strays. The way it did on the first night. And Leaka says it means I was one.” She looked out across the lit Hollow, at all the thousands of pale lights settling on the slopes. “So I think it’s telling me. I think that’s the answer. It was one of them, once. Before it was mine.” She looked at him. “Somebody took it up the stair and taught it to talk and put it on my shoulder in a white room.”

“Who?”

“I don’t know.” Hana’s light hummed. “Yet.”

They found the far stair on the fourth fall.

They’d always known it was there. Edda had said. The stair goes up out of the Hollow, the other side, past the far roots. Nobody goes. But in the dark nobody could have found it; the other side of the Hollow had been nine kilometres of nothing.

Now you could see it from Lanthorn’s roots.

The far wall. Grey rock going up into the roof. And cut into it, climbing it, zigzagging up the face of the cliff toward the roof in long switchbacks, a stair. Narrow. Very old. Faint, from this far, like a scratch on a wall. Going up and up until it reached the roof, near the crack where the drip came out, and went into it.

“There,” said Higu. On the root. With her rock. She’d drawn it already.

“Two days,” said Milli. “Across. If the old roads hold. Then the stair.” He squinted at it. “Four thousand steps. About. I’ll tell you when we’re on it.”

They left on the eighth fall.

Lanthorn came to see them off. All of it. Not at the edge of the town; out on the open moss, below the far ladders, where the dust was greening. Two hundred people with their glims, and the strays in their lanterns, and the light on everything.

Corwen gave Jared his mother’s pliers. The ones she’d made half the lanterns in Lanthorn with. He didn’t say anything; he just put them in Jared’s hands and closed Jared’s fingers round them and went back to stand by Nesta. Jared stood there looking at his hands for a long time.

Nesta gave Gabe a lantern. A good one. The finest Ice had seen her make. And looked at the one on Ice’s belt, and then at Gabe, and said, flatly, so you’ll know what one’s supposed to look like, and Gabe laughed so hard he had to sit down.

Tegan came with the baby. Edda. Asleep, with her glim hanging over her face, humming. Tegan didn’t say anything to anybody. She walked along the line of thirty-six until she got to Malcolm, and stopped, and put her forehead against the top of his head, very briefly, the way she had on her step in the swarm. Then she went back.

Everybody looked at Malcolm. Malcolm went red and looked at his slate.

“What,” said Gabe. “What was that? What did you do?”

“Nothing,” said Malcolm. “I was just there.”

And Isel.

She came to Newish last. With Edda’s old lantern, with the stray in it. She stood in front of Newish on the moss, small and thin and flat-faced, and held it out.

“You keep the count,” said Isel. “Up there. Wherever it is.”

Newish looked at the lantern. Then at Isel.

“That’s your gran’s,” said Newish.

“I know.” Isel held it out. “She’d want somebody to count. Up there. Where there isn’t a drip.” She was quiet. “I’ve got the count here. I’ve got the drip. You haven’t got anything. You’ll need a lantern to count by.”

Newish took it.

She didn’t say anything. For once. She stood on the moss with Edda’s old lantern in her hands and the stray in it humming whose, and her own light in Salty’s crooked cage humming one, two, three, and her mouth open, and nothing coming out of it at all.

Then she knelt down and hugged Isel so hard Isel’s feet came off the ground.

“One,” said Newish. Into Isel’s hair. Wet.

“Two,” said Isel. Muffled. “Three.”

They crossed the Hollow in two days.

Through the white forests, lit now, the paper-white trunks going up into the soft glow and the strays drifting between them. Along old roads, stone-paved under the moss, that went from village to village across the floor of the Hollow. Through the villages, empty, stone, with turf roofs and open doors and lanterns hanging by every door. Empty lanterns.

The strays followed them.

Not all of them. Some. A few at first, then more, drifting along behind the line of thirty-six the way they’d drifted round the goat on the first night. Aleaka watched them, walking backwards half the time, with the goat at her side and the stair stray hanging over the goat’s head.

“They’re following us,” said Aleaka. On the first night, at a fire in a stone village. “Why are they following us?”

“Whose,” said Hana. Touching her lantern. “They’re asking. They’re always asking.”

“But why us?”

Nobody had an answer.

They came to the far wall on the second day.

It went up out of the floor of the Hollow like the side of the world. Grey rock. Wet. And at the bottom of it, cut into it, the first step. Worn in the middle, the way the steps up from the Narrows had been worn, as if a lot of feet had gone up it a very long time ago and then none at all.

And at the foot of the stair, there were strays.

Waiting.

A dozen. Hanging in the air at the bottom of the first step, very still, the way strays hung over a cradle. Not drifting. Humming. The way Edda had said. A stray would go to the bottom of the stair and stop there, the way they stop at a cradle. And hum. And then go up.

The line stopped.

Everybody looked at them. Thirty-six people and a goat and a dog and a Rimehound, at the foot of a stair, with a dozen strays hanging in front of it and more behind them, coming across the Hollow in a long loose drifting line.

Hana’s light, on her crown, began to hum louder.

Not at the strays. Up. At the stair. At the top of it, somewhere up there in the roof, a long way off, where it went into the rock near the crack.

“Leaka,” said Hana. Very quietly.

Aleaka put out one finger toward the little lantern. Not touching. Her eyes shut.

She listened for a long time.

“It’s not I was one,” said Aleaka. Slowly. “It’s different. It’s—“ She opened her eyes. She looked up the stair. “It’s I remember this. This stair. It’s been up this stair before.” She swallowed. “Up. Not down. It went up this.”

Nobody said anything.

Chris had his notebook out. He drew an arrow. Going up. Off the top of the page. He looked at it. Then, at the end of the arrow, for the first time, he wrote something.

This stair.

Higu, beside him, took out her rock. On the left-hand side, under the drawing of the pool and the roots and the question marks and the arrow going up, she drew a stair. Zigzag. Going up into the corner of the rock.

Then she turned round and looked at Ice.

He was standing at the back of the line, with Thea’s hand in his and Tuli’s pole in his other hand and Gabe’s terrible lantern on his belt, humming.

He knew what she was going to say. She’d said it at the bottom of every stair since the white room. Since the Long Acre. Since the river and the arch and the root at forty paces. He knew it the way you know the next step in the dark.

Higu opened her mouth.

“Where?” said Ice.

Higu stopped.

Then she laughed. Out loud. Higu, who hardly ever laughed, at the foot of a stair at the edge of the world, with her rock in her hands. It came out of her like a cough, surprised, and then she couldn’t stop, and Drew beside her was looking at her as if she’d grown wings, and she was laughing with her hand over her mouth.

“Up,” said Higu. Through it. “I don’t know. Up. All of us.” She wiped her eyes. “We’ll find out.”

“We’ll find out,” said Ice.

He went up in the middle.

Hana first, because she’d gone first up the stair from the Narrows and she’d said she’d keep doing it until she got tired, and she wasn’t tired. Milli with her. Then the rest. Higu and Drew. Xav. Rui Xiang, who’d have gone first if anybody let him, and didn’t ask. Newish, near the front, with Edda’s old lantern in one hand, counting.

He went up in the middle. With Thea.

Her hand in his. On every stair. The way it always was. The big amber lantern in her other hand, open, leaning at him. And on his belt, Gabe’s terrible lantern, with the light in it, humming her song. Getting the middle wrong. Trying again.

At the foot of the stair, behind them, the strays.

He looked back once. On the hundredth step. He couldn’t help it.

They were coming up.

Not all of them. A few. A dozen, maybe. Drifting up the stair behind the line of thirty-six, slowly, step by step, the way the goat had climbed the hundred and twelve on floor 1. Small and pale. Humming. Going up out of the Hollow, the way Edda’s gran’s gran had said they used to. Called.

Aleaka was looking back too. On the step below him. With the goat. She was looking at the strays coming up behind them, and her face was doing something he couldn’t read.

“Nothing leaves its floor,” said Aleaka. Very quietly. To nobody.

“He did,” said Ice. Nodding at the goat.

“He did,” said Aleaka. “And now they are.” She looked at him. “It’s not bending, Ice. It’s not breaking. It’s—“ She didn’t finish. “Somebody’s calling them. Up there. Like Edda said. Somebody’s been calling them up this stair for longer than Lanthorn’s been in the roots.” She looked up. At the stair going up the wall into the roof. “And now there’s something to follow.”

“Us?”

“Us,” said Aleaka. “Or him.” She looked at his belt. At the light in the lantern. “Or that.”

He looked down at it.

It hummed. Four notes. The middle wrong.

Whose, it had asked, in the dark, for as long as it had been a light.

His, it had written, where whose had been.

He put his hand on the lantern. Not over it. Just on the iron. Lightly. The way you’d put your hand on a dog’s head as you went past.

Then he went on up the stair, in the middle, with Thea’s hand in his.

And under it, in the grey, where nothing had been written on fifteen or on sixteen, where Alea said it was only watching:

Nothing. Still.

But Thea, on the step beside him, glanced at the corner of his window as it closed, the way she glanced at all of them. And her hand tightened in his. Just a little.

He didn’t ask what she’d seen.

She’d tell him. Some night. On a stair.

Or he’d find it.

The far stair up the far wall to the roof. Corwen gave Jared his mother’s pliers. Nesta gave Gabe a proper lantern “so you’ll know what one’s supposed to look like.” Tegan put her forehead on Malcolm’s head. Nobody knows why. “I was just there.”
Isel gave Newish Edda’s lantern. “You’ll need a lantern to count by.” One. Two. Three.
Strays waiting at the foot of the stair like at a cradle. Hana’s light: “I remember this. I went up this.” Chris wrote at the end of the arrow: This stair.
Higu turned round to ask. I asked first. “Where?” She laughed. “Up. I don’t know. We’ll find out.”
Strays coming up behind us. Aleaka: “Somebody’s calling them. And now there’s something to follow.”
I put my hand on the lantern. Up, in the middle. Thea’s hand.
Lv 80. In the grey, nothing. Thea saw something. She’ll tell me. Or I’ll find it.
