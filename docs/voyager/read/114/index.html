Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 3: Party of One

Chapter 114

The Dark of the Moon

3,747 words · 16 min read

Thea used to sing.

Not well. Not for anybody, at first. In the shower, in the car, the way everybody does. And then for about a year on Earth, every night, in a call with one other person in it, she’d sung. He’d play something, quietly, with his mic on, and she’d sing. Whatever came into her head. Old songs, new songs, half-remembered ones she made the rest of up for. Sometimes for an hour. Sometimes until one of them fell asleep.

She’d stopped when the calls thinned. She hadn’t decided to. There just hadn’t been anybody on the other end of the call, after a while, and you don’t sing into an empty room. Or she didn’t.

She hadn’t sung since the white room. Not once. Not on any floor. Not when Merle’s bread came out of the oven on floor 7 and the whole square smelled of rosemary, and not when Gigi put her forehead against hers in the ash on floor 9.

She thought about it now. On the barracks roof, with the lantern, on the night after the deal. Looking up the pass at the dark where the Gate was.

She didn’t know why she was thinking about it. She did.

The Steward didn’t come back.

Not on the morning after Drew stood in the snow with nothing in his hands, or the morning after that, or the one after. The thirty-six went up to the fifth wall at dawn every day anyway, out of habit, and stood in the line with the hold-folk, and watched the throat of the pass on the high side where it always came from. Nothing came. No tall grey shape on top of the snow. No book. No the count.

The Winter didn’t come either. The pass stayed silent. The deep bell on the fifth wall didn’t ring. Every night the hold-folk stood on the wall in the snow with their shields up, all night, waiting, and in the morning the slope was clean.

“It’s waiting,” said Brenna.

She said it on the sixth day, on the wall, looking up the pass. Thea was beside her with the lantern.

“For what?”

“For you.” Brenna didn’t look at her. “Nine hundred years, the Winter’s come down that pass every night from first snow to spring. And now it’s stopped. Since the Long Night. Since your general stood in front of the Steward with his hands open.” She was quiet. “It’s not coming down here any more. It’s waiting up there. For you to come up.”

Thea looked up the pass.

“At the dark of the moon,” she said.

“The day after tomorrow,” said Brenna. She looked at Thea then, with her grey eyes. “The books say the last climbers went up in spring. With the snow melting. And the Winter gone back up the pass behind the Gate for the year.” She paused. “You’re going up in the middle of it. With the Winter sitting up there waiting. Nobody’s ever gone up in winter.”

“No,” said Thea. “I know.”

Brenna looked at her for a long time.

“We’ll be on the walls,” she said. “All five. All of us. When you go.” She turned back to the pass. “That’s all we can do. We can’t go through the Gate. It doesn’t open for us. It never has.” She was quiet. “But we can watch you go.”

He cooked, the last two days.

Of course he did. Thea had known he would. He’d cooked on every floor since the first, for himself at first, alone, a floor ahead, and then for all of them. On floor 8 in the old kitchen of the house for lights, with Hako banned. On floor 9 in the long-hall ovens of Highcrook. He cooked the way some people pray.

On floor 10 he went to Hesk.

Thea saw him do it. She was in the stores with Dennis, who was trying to talk Hesk into giving them more salt for the house for the wounded and failing to make it a negotiation, because Hesk just kept saying yes. And Ice came down the stair and stood in the doorway and asked Hesk, very politely, if he could have the long hall’s ovens for two days, and whatever was in the stores that the wall wouldn’t need, and some onions.

“For the climbers,” said Hesk.

“For everyone,” said Ice. “The wall. The wounded. Whoever’s in the hall.” He paused. “We’re going up. I’d like to cook first.”

Hesk looked at him for a long time with his grey eyes.

“That’s not trading,” he said.

“No,” said Ice. “It’s not.”

He cooked for two days. Thea didn’t see much of it. Nobody did. He was in the long hall kitchen from before dawn to after dark with the ovens going and the doors shut and Gabe on a stool outside them, on guard, the way Gabe had sat outside Rachael’s house for the wounded with the shield. Salty went in once with a sack of something and came out with his eyes wet and wouldn’t say why. Tiger went in and didn’t come out for six hours; when he did, his hands were brown to the wrist with onion skin.

And on the evening of the second day, the night before the dark of the moon, he fed the Bastion.

Not all of it. You couldn’t. But the long hall on the fourth level, the biggest one, with the tables pulled back out from the walls where Sten’s children had been falling over all week, and every seat on every bench full. Hold-folk. Climbers. Soldiers off the wall and smiths up from the Breath and old women carried in on chairs and children in grey wool with their bare feet tucked under them. Brenna. Geir, with his one eye. Ingrith, small and gentle, with a soldier on either side of her. Hesk. Sten. Ansa, at the end of a bench near the door, with Azac beside her and Rui Xiang beside him.

And down the middle of every table, in the light of a hundred lanterns, food.

Thea didn’t know what half of it was. Salt meat from the stores, slow-cooked until it fell apart, with something dark and sweet in it. Bread, the hold-folk’s flat grey bread, but changed somehow, lighter, with herbs in it that smelled like floor 7. Roasted roots with the honey-pears from floor 9 that he must have been carrying since Highcrook. And onions. Onions everywhere. Roasted whole in their skins until they went soft and black on the outside and sweet as fruit inside, a dozen to a dish, down every table in the hall.

The hold-folk didn’t know what to do. Thea watched them not know. They ate at the same tables off the same pots every day of their lives, and they’d never once been cooked for. They sat there in front of it in their grey with their big work-roughened hands in their laps, and looked at it.

And then Sten, at the head of the first table, reached out with his bony old hand, and took an onion, and peeled it. Slowly. Layer by layer. All the way round. And ate it.

“Good,” said Sten, in his creaking voice.

And the hall ate.

Thea found him afterwards, behind the long hall, in the yard where the bins were, sitting on an upturned crate in the snow. With his sleeves rolled up and his forearms brown with onion skin and his hands hanging between his knees. Not doing anything.

She sat down on the crate next to him. She’d brought the lantern. She put it down in the snow between their feet.

“That was the best thing I’ve ever eaten,” she said.

“It was salt meat.”

“It was the best salt meat anybody’s ever eaten.” She looked at his hands. “You peeled every onion in the Bastion.”

“Tiger peeled about half.”

“Tiger cried about half.” She almost laughed. “He came out of that kitchen like he’d been to a funeral.”

Ice didn’t laugh. He was looking at his hands.

“Mother Crane taught me,” he said. Quietly. “On floor 1. The fourth morning. Peel it properly. All the way round. In one go. You don’t take shortcuts with the thing you’re feeding people.” He turned one hand over. “I’ve done it every floor since. For everybody. It’s the only thing I can do that’s just—“ He stopped. “That doesn’t cost anybody anything.”

Thea didn’t say anything.

The fourth morning, she thought. On floor 1. And then, very carefully, not letting herself think it the rest of the way: which first floor.

She put it down. The way you’d put something down that wasn’t yours.

“Tomorrow,” she said instead.

“Tomorrow.”

They sat there in the snow behind the long hall. Inside, through the wall, they could hear the hold-folk still at the tables. Somebody laughing. Somebody singing, badly, a hold-folk song with a lot of verses. The children shrieking at something. Waddles, probably.

“Higu put us on the two-till-four,” said Thea. “Tonight. The door.”

Ice turned his head and looked at her.

“She’s got the rock tonight,” said Thea. “She wrote the rota. She put us together.” She looked at the lantern. “I don’t think it was an accident. Higu doesn’t do accidents.”

“No,” said Ice, after a moment. “She doesn’t.”

The barracks step at two in the morning was the coldest place in the Bastion, and the quietest.

Everybody else was asleep. Even the hold-folk on the walls had gone quiet, standing their watch in the snow without talking, with nothing coming. The city was dark except for the fires on the five walls going up the pass, one above the other, small and orange in the black, and above the top one, nothing. The throat of the pass. The high side. Where the Gate was, in the dark of the moon.

There was no moon. That was the thing. The sky was enormous and black and absolutely full of stars, more than Thea had ever seen, so many that the sky looked dusty with them. And no moon at all.

They sat on the step. Side by side. A little apart. With the lantern between them, and her shoulder very nearly against his, the way it had been on the roof.

And something came and sat on the lantern.

Thea saw it land. Small. Pale. Glowing faintly, like moonlight through a curtain, on a night with no moon. A fox. Not much bigger than her hand. With neat pointed ears and a long brush tail and two white pinprick eyes. It sat on the top of the lantern, on the brass ring at the top, with its tail curled round its feet, and looked at her.

She’d seen it before. Twice. Once on floor 6, for one second, on his shoulder, in a bowl of gold glass, with blood running down her arm. And once on floor 7, when it had looked at her on purpose. As if it wanted her to know it was there.

“Hello, Alea,” said Thea.

The fox looked at her. Then it tipped its head on one side.

“She says hello,” said Ice. Very quietly. “She says she’s sorry she’s been rude. She’s been busy.”

“Busy?”

“Counting,” said Ice. He was looking at the fox, not at Thea. “Every time it counted us. She counted it back.” He paused. “She doesn’t like it counting us.”

Thea looked at the little pale fox sitting on her lantern, on his light, with its tail round its feet.

“Neither do I,” she said.

The fox blinked at her. Slowly. The way a cat blinks at you when it’s decided you’re all right.

She didn’t decide to sing. She thought about that afterwards, a lot. She didn’t decide. It was just that it was two in the morning, and cold, and quiet, and there was a lantern and a fox and a man sitting next to her on a step who’d peeled every onion in a city so that people who’d never been cooked for would know what it felt like. And tomorrow there was a Gate. And it was the time of night, on Earth, when she used to.

So she did.

Not loudly. Barely above a breath. She didn’t even start with words. Just the tune. An old one. One she’d sung at the end, most nights, when one of them was about to fall asleep. She didn’t know where she’d got it from. A song that didn’t go anywhere much, that went round and round, up and down, like somebody rocking a chair. She hummed it, on the step, in the cold, with her hands round her knees.

And then, after a while, the words. Quietly. Half of them half-remembered, the way they’d always been, the rest made up as she went.

She didn’t look at him. She looked at the stars. At the fires going up the pass. At the dark at the top of it.

She sang it all the way through. The way she used to. Once round, and then again, slower, the way she’d always done it at the end, when one of them was already asleep and the other one was keeping going so they’d stay that way.

When she stopped, the step was very quiet.

She turned her head.

He was crying.

Not the way he’d cried on the jetty, or on the heap of green in the Down, or with his forehead on Gabe’s shoulder. Not shaking. Not making any sound. Just sitting there on the barracks step in the cold with his hands round his knees and his face turned toward her and the tears running down it in the lantern light, very steadily, as if they’d been waiting a very long time and had finally been told they could.

On the lantern, the fox had shut its eyes.

“You used to sing that,” said Ice. “At the end.”

“I know.”

“Every night.”

“I know,” said Thea. “I’m surprised you remember.”

He didn’t say anything for a long time.

“I remember all of it,” he said.

She heard it. The way she’d heard the fourth morning in the snow behind the long hall. The way Ina had heard never saw one on floor 9, and Gabe had heard always at the Breath, and Rui Xiang had heard ours in a tunnel full of names. All of it. As if all of it was a very long time. As if he’d been carrying a song she sang on a call for a year on Earth, a long time ago, through something that went on a great deal longer than a year.

She put it down. Carefully. The way she’d promised.

“Good,” she said.

They didn’t say anything for a while after that. The stars went round. The fires on the walls burned low and somebody on each wall built them up again. The fox slept on the lantern.

“Thea,” said Ice, eventually.

“Mm.”

“When this is—“ He stopped.

She waited.

“When we’re through,” he said. “When it’s—“ He stopped again. She watched him try. She watched his hands lock round his knees. She watched him look up the pass, at the dark where the Gate was, and then down at the step between them, and then at her. “Not yet,” he said. Very quietly. “I’m sorry. I keep saying that. Not yet.”

“I didn’t ask,” said Thea.

“I know you didn’t.”

“I’m not going to.”

“I know.” He looked at the lantern. At the fox, asleep on it. At his own light, in the glass, that she’d carried up seven floors. “You’ll be the first,” he said. “I said that on the bridge. I meant it. I still mean it. Whatever happens tomorrow, whatever it—“ He stopped. “You’ll be the first.”

“I know,” said Thea.

She did. She’d known on the bridge. She’d known, she thought, for a lot longer than that. She didn’t know how. It didn’t matter.

She leaned. The last little bit. Until her shoulder was against his, properly, and stayed there. Not asking anything. Just there. The way you’d lean on somebody at the end of a long call, at three in the morning, when neither of you wants to be the one to hang up.

He leaned back.

They sat on the barracks step in the dark of the moon, shoulder to shoulder, with a lantern between their feet and a sleeping fox on top of it, until Gabe came out at four to take the next watch, and saw them, and stopped in the doorway, and went quietly back in again, and came out five minutes later making a great deal of noise with his boots.

They went up at first light.

All thirty-six. Out of the barracks in the grey, with their packs on and their weapons ready, up the stairs and the streets of the Bastion, through the tunnels of the second wall, and the third, and the fourth. And all the way up, the hold-folk were on the walls.

Every wall. Every level. In grey, in lines, with their shields on their backs. Standing on the parapets and the stairs and the roofs and the doorsteps as the climbers went past below them. Not saying anything. Not cheering. Just standing. And as each of the thirty-six went past, they put their fists to their chests. Once.

Thea saw Brenna at the first wall, at the head of the stair, with the scar white through her lip. Geir, at the door to the Breath, with his one eye, and his arms folded, and his chin up. Ingrith on the step of the house for the wounded, with her small gentle hands together, and a line of hold-folk behind her with pink fingers and pink ears and pink toes, all of them with their fists to their chests. Hesk at the door of the stores. Sten, barefoot in the snow on the fourth level, in front of forty children who were all standing very straight and very still, the way they’d stand on the wall one day.

And at the fifth wall, at the very top, in the line, Ansa. With her shield up. Covering the man on her left.

The gate of the fifth wall was open.

They stopped in front of it. All thirty-six. In the arch, in the grey, with the pass going up beyond it into the throat of rock and the mountains, white and black and silent, and the Gate somewhere up there in the dark of the moon. Waiting. With the Winter.

Nobody said anything.

And then Newish, at the front, with her staff in her hand, turned round and looked at all of them.

“Count,” she said.

She didn’t say one. That was the thing. Everybody waited for her to say one, the way she’d said it on the stair up from floor 7, and floor 8, and floor 9. One, and then everybody, and no gap.

She said: “Newish.”

And understood, a second later, what she’d done. Thea saw her understand it. Saw her face go from startled to something else. And then she grinned, the way she grinned when she’d been right about something and was going to be insufferable about it.

“Calvin,” said Calvin. At once. Loudly.

“Hana.”

“Milli.”

“Jared.”

One by one. Out loud. At the gate of the fifth wall, in the grey, with the hold-folk on the parapet above them and the Gate up the pass ahead. Not numbers. Names. The way they’d said them back at the Steward, down off the wall into the snow, five mornings ago. Except nobody was counting them this time. They were counting themselves.

“Aleaka.”

“Drew.”

“Higu.”

“Chi.”

“Xola.”

“Thea,” said Thea.

It went round all of them. Every one. Deston and Rachael and Dennis and Azac. Yan, very quietly. Salty. Wendy. Tiger. Cursiffe. Hyvred and Gigi and Bee and Hako and Kei. Chris and Niko and Maigo, small and brown with his leaves out, and Winter and Ina. Xav and Rui Xiang and Malcolm and John.

“Gabe,” said Gabe.

Thirty-five.

And then nobody said anything. And everybody waited. The way the wall had waited, every morning, for the Steward to say thirty-six like a hammer.

He was standing at the back. Where he always stood now. With Gabe on his right and the lamp shield on his arm. He looked at all of them. At thirty-five people standing in the arch of a gate at the top of a wall, in the grey, waiting for him.

He didn’t say thirty-six.

“Ice,” he said.

Clearly. Not loud. Not to them. Up the pass. At the dark at the top of it. At whatever was up there in the throat of the rock, with a book, keeping a count. As if he were telling it which one to write down.

Thea felt it go up the pass. She didn’t know how. She just felt it. A word, going up through the cold, into the dark, and landing.

On the parapet above them, Ansa put her fist to her chest.

“Thirty-six,” said Newish. Very quietly. “No gap.”

They went up.
