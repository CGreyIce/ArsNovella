Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 2: One Floor Ahead

Chapter 46

Come Down Talking

4,900 words · 21 min read

He moved out of the room on the high terrace on the fifty-ninth day.

He’d slept there for six weeks. A bare stone room with one window and no door, looking out over the canyon, three steps from the top of the stair. He’d liked it. He’d kept it swept. He’d built a ring of stones for a cooking fire in the corner under the window, and there was a black scorch on the ceiling above it now, and a pile of dust-hound bones he’d been meaning to take outside. He’d got used to waking up in it, and looking out of the window at the door, and checking.

He took the bones outside. He took his bedroll and his pot and his last bag of floor 3 herbs. He swept the floor. He couldn’t do anything about the scorch on the ceiling.

“They’ll know someone lived here,” said Alea.

“They’ll know someone lived somewhere.” He looked round the room one more time. “Everyone already knows I was on four. The rankings say so. I just don’t want to be in it when they open the door.”

He found a new place across the gap. A tall, narrow house on the other side of the street from the terrace, four storeys of red stone with a flat roof and a broken parapet. From the roof, through a crack in the parapet, he could see the door at the top of the stair, and the whole of the high terrace, and the first long stair going down from it to the first square below. And the square itself, twenty metres across, with the dry fountain in the middle and a long grey shape lying on the stones beside it that hadn’t moved in thirty-four days.

He went up to the roof the long way, every time. Down the stair on the far side of the terrace, across a bridge, along a lane, in through the back of the house and up the inside. Never across the street. He didn’t think about why. He’d spent ten years not leaving a trail where he was going to wait. It was just habit.

He sat on the roof with his back to the parapet and watched the door.

Day 60. Floor 3. Average nineteen.

Day 62. Floor 3. Nineteen. Rui Xiang twenty-one, John twenty, Gabe twenty.

Day 63. Floor 3. Nineteen. Calvin twenty. Calvin had posted it in World in capitals.

Day 64. Floor 3. Twenty, nearly all of them. Bee nineteen. Maigo nineteen. Hana nineteen, and Hana had posted i am RESTING. resting is a SKILL.

Day 65. Floor 3. Twenty.

All of them. Every name on the list, twenty, in a column, the way he’d watched them go ten, then fifteen, then seventeen, all through the long weeks in the Fen. Alea read them out one at a time, slowly, the way she knew he liked.

Then she was quiet.

“There’s a post,” she said. “From Drew. An hour ago.”

[ World · Drew: everyone’s 20. we go up tomorrow. all 35. together. calvin first, rachael on his collar, me last. nobody runs. ]

He read it four times.

Calvin first, Rachael on his collar.

He didn’t sleep that night. He didn’t try. He sat on the roof with his back against the parapet, in the red dusk that was the same at midnight as it was at noon, and looked at the door. Alea didn’t tell him to sleep. She sat on the parapet beside him with her tail round her feet, and every so often she said a name. Just a name. Malcolm. Bee. Chris. And he said yeah, and they sat a bit longer.

“Ice,” she said, around the time the rankings would have said dawn on a floor that had one.

“Yeah.”

“What are you going to do? When it opens.”

“Watch.”

“Just watch?”

“Just watch.” He looked at the door. “I’ve got Bearing now. If anything goes wrong, I’ll know before they do. I’ll be there in—“ He measured the gap with his eyes, the terrace, the long stair down. “Eight seconds. Maybe six.” He swallowed. “Nothing’s going to go wrong. There’s nothing left on this floor. I just want to see them.”

The fox looked at him for a long time.

“Okay,” she said. “Then we’ll watch.”

The door opened on the sixty-sixth day, a little before what would have been noon.

He knew before it opened. Not because he heard it. Because something woke up behind his breastbone.

It was like nothing he’d ever felt. Not a sound, not a light, not a window. A pull. Small and fine, like a single thread drawn tight, running from somewhere just behind the ember in his chest out across the street to the door at the top of the stair. Like the needle of a compass, if the compass were inside him, and had just swung round and settled, and pointed.

That way.

And he knew whose it was. He couldn’t have said how. He just knew, the way you know a voice before you see the face.

Calvin.

The door opened.

Calvin came out of it.

He was in front, the way Drew had said. He stepped out onto the red terrace and stopped dead, one hand still on the door frame, and looked out at the Red City with his mouth open. The lightweight leathers of a Rogue, with two daggers at his hips, and a Tracker’s cloak, and both his hands. Both his hands. One on the door frame. One out at his side, the fingers spread, the way he’d always held his hand out when he wanted people to stop behind him.

“Okay,” said Calvin.

His voice came across the gap clear as a bell. Floor 4 was silent, and silence carried everything.

“Okay. Okay. It’s red. Everybody, it’s red.” He laughed. It was the same laugh. Delighted. The way he laughed at anything new. “It’s a whole city. It’s a city in a canyon. Oh my days. Look at my back. Nobody touch anything. Look at my back.”

And right behind him, out of the door, came Rachael, with a hand fisted firmly in the back of his collar.

Rachael.

The second thread woke. It swung, and settled, and pointed, an inch to the left of the first.

She was frowning. She was always frowning. Her staff was in her other hand, and she was looking at the canyon past Calvin’s shoulder with an expression of profound distrust, as if the whole Red City had personally offended her.

“Calvin,” she said. “You’re talking.”

“I’m meant to talk. That’s the skill. They follow my voice.”

“Then talk quieter.”

“Okay,” said Calvin, at exactly the same volume.

Ice put his hand over his mouth.

“Three,” said Alea, very softly, beside him.

Dennis was through the door, arguing. Ice couldn’t hear what about. Something about the eel-woman’s tab, maybe, or money, or the stairs, the way Dennis was always arguing about something to keep from being frightened. His thread woke. Then Deston, tall and calm, stepping out and looking straight down the canyon at the first square the way he’d looked at everything since the white room, as if he could hold it still by looking at it. Four. Then Azac, with his shield and his sword and a look of enormous satisfaction, saying something to Deston that made Deston nearly smile. Five.

Ice counted.

He didn’t decide to. It came out of him, the way the steps had come out of him on every stair since the first one, in a whisper too low for anyone to hear across a street.

“Six,” he whispered.

Xav. Small, calm, still, his hands loose at his sides. He stepped out of the door and stopped, and did the thing Xav always did in new places: stood absolutely still and read the room. Ice watched him look at the terrace, the walls, the canyon. The roofs.

He held his breath.

Xav’s eyes went along the roofline on the far side of the street, slowly, the way they’d go round a map before a round. They passed over the broken parapet. They didn’t stop.

“Seven.”

Gabe.

Gabe came out of the door with his shield on his arm and the wolf-fur coat on his back. On floor 4. In the dry heat of the Red City, in the red dusk. He was wearing the coat. Big and broad and loud in the doorway, with something wrapped in sacking strapped across his back on top of the coat: a big round shape, heavy, the edge of it pulling the straps tight across his shoulders. He looked at the city. He looked at it for a long time. Then he said, loud enough to carry to the bottom of the canyon:

“Huh.”

The seventh thread woke, and swung, and settled. It pulled harder than the others. Ice didn’t know if that was the skill or if that was him.

“Eight,” he said. His voice had stopped working properly. He kept counting anyway.

Rui Xiang, spinning a knife across his knuckles, already looking at the roofs with the expression of a man who wanted to be up on them. Malcolm, behind him, with his staff and his face of quiet doom, saying something that was almost certainly I don’t like this. John, with his chin up, a small light already floating ahead of him over the terrace like a firefly. Nine. Ten.

Then Yan, with his pale cyan eyes, and Suzu round his neck, white and small. Salty, enormous, with a greatsword on his back and a smith’s hammer at his belt. Wendy, with her bow. Tiger, already halfway across the terrace before anyone told him to stop. Cursiffe, last of Yan’s five, with ink on his fingers and his nose in a little book he was writing in as he walked.

Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen. Fourteen. Fifteen.

Hana, and he had to stop counting for a second. She came out into the red dusk with her crown on and the rose tucked into it, gold beads swinging, and looked at the canyon. She said something to Milli that made Milli laugh. Milli, small and quick, with his tail up. Jared, steady as a wall, with a shield he’d clearly made himself. Newish, mouthing something rude at the canyon. And Aleaka, with a white wolf pup at her heels that was Sleet, grown to the height of her knee now, sniffing the red stone.

Sixteen. Seventeen. Eighteen. Nineteen. Twenty.

Then Hyvred.

Hyvred came out of the door, and took two steps onto the terrace, and stopped. Not to look at the canyon. She was looking at the ground.

At the stone just beside the door. Just there, where the wall met the terrace, where for three weeks a man had sat every evening with his back to the wall and his knees up, looking at the door.

She stood and looked at it. Then she went down on one knee and put her hand flat on the stone.

Ice went very still on the roof.

“Ice,” said Alea.

“I know.”

“What’s she—“

“I don’t know.”

Hyvred stayed there, kneeling, with her hand on the red stone, and didn’t move. Behind her Gigichi came out of the door, and Bee, and Hako, and Kei. Twenty-one. Twenty-two. Twenty-three. Twenty-four. Gabe had stopped at the top of the long stair. He’d turned round, and he was looking at Hyvred. He came back across the terrace and crouched down beside her. Ice could see his mouth move. He couldn’t hear what he said. For the first time since the door had opened, he couldn’t hear.

Hyvred said something back. Very quietly. Gabe looked at the stone under her hand for a long time.

Then he looked up.

Not at the roof. Down. Down the long stair, and down the canyon, toward the bottom of the Red City. Toward the dry river. As if he were looking for something that had gone that way.

Ice let out a breath he hadn’t known he was holding.

The long way round. Down the far stair, across the bridge, along the lane. Every time. Whatever Hyvred was seeing on that stone, it didn’t lead across the street. It led down.

“Twenty-five,” he whispered, as Kei came through the door with a green snake round his neck, and stopped behind Hyvred, and looked down at her hand too.

Chris’s party came next. Chris with his longsword and shield and a notebook sticking out of his belt pouch. Niko with a book open in one hand, as if he’d been reading on the stairs. Maigo, quiet, with leaves in his hair. Winter, with frost on the end of her bow, looking at everything. Ina, with his oni mask pushed up on the top of his head.

Twenty-six. Twenty-seven. Twenty-eight. Twenty-nine. Thirty.

Then Thea’s party. Higu, silver-haired and quiet. Xola, with water moving round her hands. PenguChi, with Waddles under one arm, the penguin looking out at the Red City with an expression of grave disapproval.

Thirty-one. Thirty-two. Thirty-three.

And then Thea.

She came out of the door into the red dusk with the lantern in her hand.

It was his lantern. The Lure-glass Lantern he’d taken out of the Lanternjaw’s mouth and left on a bench in the ascension room on floor 3. She was holding it up a little, the way you hold a lamp in a dark house, and it was lit. Amber. Warm. The only warm-coloured light on the whole of floor 4. It lit her face from below, and the black-and-pink-and-blue of her ponytail, and the star-shaped glints in her eyes.

The thirty-fourth thread woke. It didn’t swing. It went straight to her as if it had always been pointing there.

He couldn’t count her. He tried. His mouth made the shape of thirty-four and nothing came out.

She stopped on the terrace. Not to look at the canyon. She looked at Hyvred on one knee, and at Gabe crouched beside her. She went over to them, and Hyvred said something, and Thea looked down at the red stone by the door. She held the lantern over it, so the amber light fell on the place where he’d sat.

Then she did what Hyvred had done. She went down on one knee and put her free hand flat on the stone. And left it there.

He sat on the roof behind the broken parapet and couldn’t breathe.

“Thirty-four,” said Alea, very quietly, for him.

Drew came last. Of course Drew came last. He stepped out of the door with his buckler on his arm and his bladed knuckles on, and looked round once at his whole group, spread over the terrace. Calvin at the top of the stair, talking. Rachael on his collar. Gabe and Hyvred and Thea on their knees by the wall. Everyone else standing in the red dusk, staring.

“Right,” said Drew. His voice carried as well as Calvin’s. “Everyone’s through. That’s thirty-five. Calvin. Go.”

“Thirty-five,” said Ice. It came out as a whisper. It was the last thread. It woke and swung and settled, and they were all there, all thirty-five of them, threads running out of his chest across the street to every one of them. Thin and taut and fine. Not one of them was red.

He put his face in his hands.

They went down to the first square talking.

Calvin first. Rachael on his collar. Then everyone else, in a long line down the long red stair, the way they’d come the first time. Only now they were all level twenty. Only now there were thirty-five of them, not thirty-six. And now, at the bottom of the stair, there was nothing on the roof.

Ice watched the roofs.

He couldn’t help it. He’d spent six weeks watching the roofs over the first square. He’d spent ten years before that seeing them, every night, behind his eyelids. As Calvin went down the stair he looked at every roof edge round the square, one after another, the way he’d done on the day of the bell. Every parapet. Every gutter. Every place where a tall grey shape could come loose like a shadow and fall four storeys without a sound.

There was nothing there. There was a row of carrion birds on the north roof, and dust, and the red sky.

“Okay,” Calvin was saying, on the stair. “Okay. Nobody run. Look at my back. There’s a square. There’s a—“ He stopped for half a step. “Oh. There’s a fountain.”

Come on, come on, it’s amazing, there’s a fountain.

Ice’s hands were shaking.

“It’s dry,” said Calvin. “It’s a dry fountain. Rachael, can you see this? There’s a—“

He reached the bottom of the stair. He walked out into the first square, slowly, with his hand out at his side for the people behind him and Rachael’s fist in his collar. Toward the dry fountain in the middle. Toward the long grey shape lying on the stones beside it.

And he stopped talking.

Ice watched him stop. Twenty metres across a square. The same square, the same fountain, the same four storeys of empty windows. He watched Calvin stand by the dry fountain with both his arms and look down at the thing that had taken one of them, the first time, before he’d finished turning round.

It was smaller, lying down. It had been lying there for six weeks in the dry wind, and floor 4 didn’t take things away. It had dried. Its long grey too-jointed limbs had folded down onto the stones the way a crane folds, and stayed folded, and gone grey-brown and stiff, like old wood. The smooth faceless head lay on one side. There was a dark hole in the middle of its chest where a sword had gone in, and on the stones beside it, where he’d dropped it, lay a small bronze bell.

Calvin looked at it for a long time.

Rachael let go of his collar.

The others came down the stair behind them and spread out round the square and stopped, one by one, in a ring round the fountain. Thirty-five people standing in a square on floor 4, silent, for the first time since they’d come through the door. Looking at the thing by the fountain that hadn’t gone to light.

Ice couldn’t hear what they said. They weren’t saying anything. Bee turned away and put her face in Hako’s shoulder. Kei crouched down by the hole in its chest, very close, the way a doctor looks. Rui Xiang wasn’t looking at the body at all. He was looking up. At the roofs.

And Calvin turned round.

He turned round slowly, in a full circle, the way he had the first time. Looking up at all four storeys of empty windows. Only this time he wasn’t calling anybody. This time he was quiet. He was looking at the roofs the way Ice looked at roofs. The way you look at roofs when you’ve just understood what lives on them.

Rachael said something to him. Very low. He nodded without looking at her.

Then she did something Ice had never seen Rachael do, the first time or this time. She reached out, and took Calvin’s left hand in hers, and held it. Not his collar. His hand.

She didn’t know why. Ice could see it in her face, even from across the street. She didn’t know why she’d done it. She just stood in the first square of the Red City with her frown on, holding Calvin’s left hand at the end of his whole left arm, as if somebody had to.

Ice turned away from the crack in the parapet and sat with his back against it, and cried, very quietly, the way you do on a floor where everything listens.

Alea didn’t say anything. She’d learned that.

He went the long way.

Down through the back of the house, out into the lane, along the high terraces on the north side of the canyon, keeping to the roofs, with cloth on his boots. He didn’t look back. He didn’t need to. Thirty-five threads ran out of him behind him, up the canyon, to the first square. He could feel every one. He could have shut his eyes and pointed.

None of them was red.

He’d never felt anything like it. On every floor, for ten years, he’d lain awake not knowing. Where Thea was. Whether Gabe had come back from the far side of the floor. Whether the scream he’d heard in the dark was someone he knew. Now he walked down a silent red canyon with thirty-five threads running out of his chest, and every one of them said: here. I’m here. I’m fine.

“Ice,” said Alea. “You’re smiling.”

“I’m not.”

“You’re crying and smiling at the same time. It looks terrible.”

He laughed. It came out wet. He wiped his face on his sleeve and kept walking.

The furnace hall was at the very bottom, by the dry river. The great black barn of a building, cut into the canyon floor, with the chimney going up through four hundred metres of rock. He went in through the big doorway with his shield up.

The Kiln was there. It had come back the morning after he’d killed it, the first time, the way bosses did. It stood in the middle of the hall in the heat and the dark, iron and fired clay, three times his height, with the furnace glowing in its chest. Behind it, against the back wall, lay the thing he’d killed on the forty-fourth day: another Kiln, cold and dark and still, its chest plates open, its furnace heart black, with a long hole through the middle of it where his sword had gone in. Floor 4 didn’t take things away.

The live one turned its head toward him.

He didn’t make a speech about it. He’d done this before. It took eleven minutes. He stood in front of it with his shield up and waited for it to burn too hot to hold. When it opened its chest plates and breathed fire at him, he stood in the fire and let the ember mend him as fast as it burned. When the four seconds were up, he put his sword into the open heart.

[ The Kiln · defeated ]

No announcement. No first clear. Just the one line, and the second Kiln going down on its knees and then onto its face beside the first, and the heat going out of it slowly, the way a stove cools.

He stood in the furnace hall between two dead Kilns, smoking a little, with his health climbing back up.

“It’ll come back tomorrow,” said Alea. “For them.”

“I know. They’ll do it together.” He looked at the two dead shapes. “They’re ready. They’ll see these. They’ll know what it does.”

At the back of the hall, past the two cold bodies, there was a door in the rock. Behind it was a stair. He’d looked at it every day for three weeks, and not gone up it.

He went and stood at the bottom of it now.

He stood there for a long time with one foot on the first step.

Thirty-five threads ran out of his chest behind him, back up through the furnace hall door and up the canyon to the first square. He could feel them moving a little. People walking about. Somebody sitting down. Somebody, probably Calvin, going round and round the fountain. Somebody standing very still, a long way up, by the door at the top of the stair, as if they’d gone back up to it on their own. He thought he knew who.

None of them was red.

“You could stay a night,” said Alea. “Just one. On a roof. You’d still be one floor ahead tomorrow.”

“If I stay a night,” said Ice, “I’ll go back up that canyon in the morning.”

The fox didn’t argue. She knew it was true.

“I want to,” he said. “That’s the problem. I want to go up there and stand in the square and let Gabe hit me, and eat whatever Dennis has been arguing about, and sit with them. And I can’t. Not yet.” He looked up the dark stair. “Something heard me in that cell. Irregular. I don’t know what that means yet. I don’t know what’s going to be different up there, and I’m not walking them into it. I’m going first. I’m going to stand on five and find out what’s changed, and then when they come up—“

“You’ll be one floor ahead,” said Alea.

“I’ll be one floor ahead.”

He took his foot off the first step and put it on the second.

He counted. He always counted. Thirty, forty, fifty, up through the rock in the dark, with his own faint amber light going ahead of him on the stone. At a hundred he stopped and stood still and felt for the threads. Still there. Faint. Thirty-five of them, a long way below and behind, fine as hair. At two hundred they were still there, fainter.

At two hundred and forty-four, there was a door.

He stood in front of it with his hand on it. He could feel the threads behind him, so faint now he had to hold his breath to find them. Thirty-five. Here. I’m here. I’m fine.

“When I open it,” he said, “they’ll go.”

“Yes,” said Alea.

“None on this floor.”

“Yes.”

He stood there.

“Ice,” said Alea. “They were fine for sixty-six days without you knowing which way they were. They’ll be fine for thirty more.” Her voice was very gentle. “And then they’ll come up those stairs, all of them, talking. And you’ll feel every one of them come through the door.”

He opened the door.

The threads went. Not all at once. One by one, the way lights go out in a town at night, from the edges in, until there was only one left, somewhere at the very back of everything, the faintest pull, pointing down and back toward a woman on her knees by a wall with a lantern in her hand. Then that one went too.

[ Bearings held: 35
None on this floor.
]

He stood in the doorway with his hand on the frame, and felt nothing pulling at him at all, for the first time in a day. It was the loneliest thing he’d ever felt.

And then the wind hit him in the face.

It was cold. Not the dry, iron-smelling wind of floor 4, coming up a canyon on the same course every hour of every day. This was a wind that came from somewhere. It came at him sideways, then from the front, then dropped away and came back harder, gusting, the way wind does on a hill in winter. It smelled of rain. Of rain that had fallen a long way off and been carried here, and of salt, and of something green and wet he couldn’t name.

He stepped out of the door.

He was on a small flat-topped island of rock, about the size of a house, with a stone hut round the door behind him and nothing else on it but grass bent flat by the wind. At the edges of the island there was nothing at all. Just air. And below the air, a long way down, a floor of cloud, silver-grey in the dark, going out in every direction as far as he could see.

And above him, there were stars.

He looked up.

On floors 1 and 2 and 3 there’d been stars too. Switched on all at once at dusk, in neat rows, in the painted ceiling. He’d stopped seeing them years ago. On floor 4 there’d been nothing. Just the red lid.

These weren’t in rows. They were everywhere, thousands of them, too many, thrown across the whole sky without any order at all. Some were bright and some were faint and some were so faint he couldn’t tell if they were there. And while he stood and looked at them, the wind moved a long rag of cloud across a corner of the sky, and the stars behind it went out, and came back, one by one, as it passed.

Nobody had switched them on. They’d been there all along.

“Floor five,” said Alea, very quietly, sitting on his shoulder.

He nodded. He couldn’t say anything.

[ Floor 5 · the Windward Reaches ]

He stood on the little island in the dark, with the cold real wind in his face and the stars going on and off behind the clouds, and thirty-five threads gone quiet in his chest, and waited for the morning.
