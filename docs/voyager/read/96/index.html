Lodestar

The moths came first.

He didn’t hear them. That was what he remembered afterwards. Everything else in the Down made a sound: the ticking, the sawing, the slow wet creak of the ferns. The moths came out of the dark without any sound at all. One moment there was nothing at the edge of the light. The next there was something. Pale. Moving. Coming in very slowly, the way a leaf comes down.

It was as wide as a cloak.

Grey and gold, and soft, so soft it looked like smoke. Furred all over, thick, like the inside of a winter glove. Its wings went up and down, slowly, slowly, and made no sound, and in the middle of them, where its head was, two small round eyes shone dull red, like coals in a grate. It came into the amber and turned once round his head, close enough that he felt the air of it on his face. Warm. Dusty. Sweet.

Then it landed on his arm.

On his raised arm. On his left hand, held up over his head with the light pouring out of it. It settled there, wings open, the way a moth settles on a lamp, and the light went dim.

He shook it off. It went up into the dark and came round again, slowly, and settled again.

And there were more.

They came in out of the dark from everywhere, silent, pale, turning, dozens of them, as wide as cloaks, drawn to him the way moths are drawn to every light, with no harm in them at all. They landed on his shield. On his shoulders. On his hand. On the moss round his feet. Wherever they landed, the light went dim, the way a lamp goes dim with a cloth over it. And wherever they’d been, they left a dust. Fine and gold, soft as flour. It was on his arm. It was in the air. He breathed it in and it tasted of honey, and his eyelids went heavy.

Very heavy. All at once. As if somebody had put a hand on each of them.

“Don’t sleep,” said Alea.

“I’m not.”

“You blinked for two seconds.”

“I’m not.”

He shook the moths off his arm. They went up, slowly, and came round, slowly, and came back. He held the light up through them. Over his head, where it would go furthest. And far off between the trunks, the amber went out over the Down in every direction, gold on the bark, gold on the ferns, flickering where the great soft wings went across it.

He shook his arm. Up they went. Round they came.

And north, in the dark, the sawing got louder.

The first sawyer came at what Alea said was nine o’clock.

He heard it a long time before he saw it. The sawing came out of the north, and stopped, and came again, nearer, rrrrrrr, and stopped. And then it didn’t stop. It came on. Through the ferns. He could hear the ferns going down in front of it, and the scrape of six legs on root, and the long dry breath of it, and then the feelers came into the edge of the light, long and jointed and swinging, the way they’d come out of the holes in the log that morning. And then the head. Glossy. Black and brown. With the moth dust settling on it like gold.

It looked at the light.

Then it came.

He put the shield round. He didn’t move his feet. That was the whole of it, that night, from the first one to the last. Don’t move your feet. He set them in the moss and turned the shield and gave it the face, and it hit him like a cart and he went back a step against the root behind him and no further, and the jaws came round for the rim. Kiln iron. Sparks. That long screaming grind, side to side, through his arm into his teeth, while the moths went round them both in the sparks without a sound, as if nothing were happening at all.

He put his sword in behind its head. It died. Its legs kept going.

He kicked it off his shield and held the light up again.

He read it with a sawyer’s dust still settling on his shield.

Like a road. He felt it, too. Behind his breastbone, thirty-five threads, long and pale, a long way off, moving. Not wandering. Not bending round. Coming. Straight. Seven bundles in seven places in the Down, and every one of them coming at him in a line, the way a needle swings to north and then goes there.

He’d felt them come to him before. On floor 6, across the gold glass. On floor 7, up the hill road at half past eleven at night. He’d always known it was happening. He’d always been sitting there knowing, a floor away, or a room away, waiting, unable to do anything about it except be wherever he was.

He’d never once been the thing they were coming toward on purpose. Because he’d asked.

“Two,” said Alea, as the next one came out of the dark.

It got hard around midnight.

Not all at once. Things didn’t happen all at once in the Down. They happened slowly, the way the ferns unrolled, so slowly you didn’t see them happen until they had.

The sawyers kept coming. Two at a time now, sometimes three, out of the dark from all sides, drawn to the light the way the moths were drawn to it. Everything in the dark will come to you instead. And they did. He stood with his back to the root and killed them. He stopped counting. Alea didn’t. She said the numbers quietly every time, the way Newish had counted the stair, so that he’d always know there was a number, and that it was going up.

They got through to him, sometimes.

A jaw on the outside of his right thigh, from a sawyer that came in low under the shield while he was turned to the one in front. It didn’t saw. There wasn’t time. It just closed, and opened, and he felt the cloth go and then the skin go and then something under the skin, and a heat all down his leg into his boot.

He killed it. And he felt the ember go into the cut.

Mend Self. It went in the way it always went in, warm, fast, from the middle of him, like a hand going into a sleeve. And it closed. It closed, but not like it closed on other floors. It closed like a door being pushed against a drift of snow. A finger’s width. Then a stop. Then another finger’s width. Hot, like a needle sewing, the way it always was now, and then the echo on top of it, the whole of the hurt again, all at once, the way it had been since floor 5. And then not closed. Not all the way. Still open at the bottom, still wet, still hot, while the ember pushed and pushed.

“It’s slow,” he said, through his teeth.

“It’s floor 9,” said Alea. “Healing closes it. It doesn’t make it not have happened.”

“That’s not in the—“

“It’s in now.”

A jaw on his left forearm, above the shield strap, that took a strip off like peel. A foot, a heavy black hooked foot, that came down on his shoulder when one of them went up the root behind him and over the top, and that one he didn’t see until it was on him. He got the sword up into it from underneath, from his knees, and got back up. Mend Self. The door against the snow. The echo.

And he was tired.

That was the other thing. He’d been tired before. Ten years of tired, every kind. But on every floor below this one, when the ember went in, the tired went too. Not all of it. Some of it. You got up from a heal lighter than you went into it. Not here. Here the cuts closed, slowly, and the tired stayed exactly where it was. And got more. Every sawyer, every heal, every shake of his arm to get the moths off the light, put a little more on, like snow on a roof.

The moth dust didn’t help. It was on everything. On his face. In his mouth, sweet, honey. His eyes kept going heavy. Every time he blinked, it took longer to open them again.

“Talk to me,” he said.

“About what?”

“Anything.”

Alea was quiet for a moment.

Then she started to sing.

He didn’t know at first what it was. She’d never sung. Not in ten years and two hundred days. She had no idea how to do it, and it came out thin and wrong and off-key, in the dark, in his head, and it took him a whole sawyer to recognise it. A song about a lamp in a window. Something Mother Tallow had sung, on a pass on floor 2, all night, with the snow coming down and him in the doorway holding the light up for her.

He laughed. He couldn’t help it. It came out of him cracked and stupid, in the dark in the middle of the Down, with a sawyer’s jaws on his rim.

“You’re terrible,” he said.

“I’ve never done it before.” She didn’t stop. “Keep your eyes open.”

He kept his eyes open.

He could feel them coming, all the time.

That was what got him through the middle of the night, more than the song. Behind his breastbone, thirty-five threads, getting shorter. Getting thicker. Getting close.

And the bundles were joining up.

He felt the first two do it a little before midnight. Somewhere east and south, Deston’s five and Hana’s five, coming at him in two straight lines from two different places, and the lines meeting, and running together. Ten. And a while after that, from the west, Chris’s lot ran into Yan’s. And then, all at once, from the north, three bundles at the same moment, Hyvred’s and Xav’s and Thea’s, and he felt them hit each other in the dark and become one thing. Fifteen.

He’d thought, for ten years, that the Down took people apart. That that was what it did. That if you went into it, it walked you away from everybody, slowly, a path at a time, until you were on the far side alone, and the only way back was three days of cutting.

It didn’t, though.

He watched it on his threads in the dark, with a sawyer coming at him. Seven bundles in seven places, walking at one point. Not at each other. Not looking for each other at all. Walking at him. And because they were all walking at the same thing, from everywhere, they ran into each other on the way. They couldn’t not. Seven became five. Five became three.

The Down didn’t take people apart. It took people apart who were looking for each other.

It couldn’t do a thing to people who were all looking at the same light.

“Thirty,” said Alea. “Keep your eyes open.”

And then the Grow came for the light.

He felt it first in his feet. Somewhere past midnight. He’d been standing in his flat place of moss for hours, with his back to the root and the dead sawyers heaped round him in a ring, and he’d stopped noticing the ticking, the way you stop noticing rain. And then his foot wouldn’t move.

He looked down.

Briar. Dark red. Up round his boot. Round the ankle. Climbing.

Not just there. Everywhere. All round his flat place, the Grow was coming in. It had been coming in all night, he thought, slowly, the way it did. But it wasn’t slow now. It was up over the dead sawyers. It was in them. Coming up through them, round them, out of them, green, and the nearest one, the first one, from nine o’clock, wasn’t a sawyer any more. It was a low green mound with a shape in it. In the morning it’d be a hill.

And it was coming up round him.

Up his legs. Over the face of the shield, where it rested on the moss between fights. Fern unrolling against his chest. A pale sapling, whippy and green, going up beside him, past his shoulder, past his raised arm. Leaning over. Toward the light.

It was trying to cover it.

He understood that all at once, and went cold. Not the moths. The moths were just moths. This was the floor. This was the thing that had put a wall between him and Gabe in the full of the afternoon. It was putting a roof over him now. It was going to grow over the top of him and close, like a hand closing over a candle, and then nobody would be able to see.

Anyone who can see you can find the way.

He tore his foot out of the briar.

It hurt. The thorns came out of his boot and out of the leather and out of his ankle. He put the foot down again, higher. On top of the briar. On the Grow itself. And it held him. And he tore the other foot out and put it up there too, and stood, a hand’s height higher than before, on a tangle of green, with his arm up.

It grew. He climbed it.

That was the rest of the night. Every few minutes the Grow came up round his boots, and he tore them out, and put them on top of it. Every few minutes the light was a little higher. The heap under him grew, and he stood on the heap, and the heap grew, and the dead sawyers went under it, and the root at his back went under it, and he went up. Slowly. One foot at a time. With his arm up and the light pouring out of it and the sawyers coming up the side of the heap at him through the moths, and his thigh not closing, and his eyes going heavy.

He didn’t move his feet. Not anywhere but up.

“Lodestar,” said Alea, softly, between verses. “It doesn’t go anywhere. It just goes up.”

He couldn’t answer. He didn’t have anything left to answer with.

Far above him, in the dark, there were lights.

He saw them around two. He didn’t know what they were at first. He’d been looking down for so long, at feet and jaws and briar, that when he lifted his head to get the moths off his face, he thought he’d gone mad.

High up. Very high. In the branches, fifty metres up, sixty, where the great limbs of the trees went out across the dark from trunk to trunk. Small lights. Blue-green and cold. A line of them, moving. Along a branch, and down, and along another branch, and over a long dark gap on what must have been a bridge. Coming toward him from the east, along the high ways, very slowly, one after another, swinging a little as they came.

Foxfire. In jars.

We look down all night. We’ll see it.

Burr’s people had seen. And they hadn’t waited for first light. They’d come out along the high ways in the middle of the night, through the dark, over the bridges, from Highcrook. Toward the light. A line of small cold lights in the top of the forest, the only other lights in the world, coming.

He stood on his heap of green with his arm up and watched them come, and the tears ran down his face through the gold dust, and he didn’t stop them.

“Keep your eyes open,” said Alea.

“They’re open.”

“Keep them open, then.”

The Grow closed over him at a little after four.

He’d known it would. He’d been climbing it for hours, and it had been climbing faster than him for the last one, and he’d been too slow. His legs had stopped doing what he told them. He’d tear a foot out of the briar, and lift it, and it wouldn’t go as high as last time. And then it wouldn’t go high enough. And then the briar came up over his knees, and his waist, and the sapling beside him, whippy and pale, bent over his head and met another one coming from the other side, and tangled with it, and they grew together. And the fern came up round them. And the briar came over the top.

And it was dark.

Not all the way. There was still the light in his hand. But it went nowhere now. It lit the inside of a small round green room, a ball of briar and leaf and stem, closed all round him, a hand’s width from his face, so close he could see the hairs on the stems. And the moths outside, he could see their shadows on it, going round, trying to find the way in to the light, and not finding it.

He was inside the Grow. In the light. And nobody outside could see it at all.

He held the window in front of him. His hand was shaking so badly he could hardly read it.

He could feel the threads, very close now. So close. Thirty-five of them. Two bundles. Twenty on one side and fifteen on the other, a few hundred metres off, coming, and slowing. And stopping. His hand went to his sword.

He felt it go. He didn’t decide it. He was on the far side of the floor in the dark with three nights behind him and a wall of briar a hand’s width from his face, and his right hand went to his hip and found the hilt and pulled, and the blade came out, a hand’s width, two—

“Don’t,” said Alea.

He knew. He knew. If he cut, it would grow back twice, and then four times, and he’d cut it again, because he wouldn’t be able to stop himself, and it would close in on him tighter and thicker, and he’d keep cutting, all night, every night, until there was nothing left of the light or the shield or him. He felt it.

One thread, out of the fifteen. Coming. Straight. Not wandering, not bending, not slowing at all. The way it had pointed at him since the clock room on floor 7, steady, the way a compass points. It wasn’t pointing now. It was coming. And the fourteen others came behind it. And then the other bundle, the twenty, turned in the dark and came too, at an angle, toward hers, and ran into it. A grey morning, a long time ago. The foot of the seven. A fire on the moss, and a heap of wood, and someone sitting beside it putting a branch on. And Ice coming in out of the dark, with his shield in two pieces. And Gabe looking up.

He knew now. He could feel it on his own face.

It was the face of somebody who’d been sitting by a light all night, and couldn’t do anything. Couldn’t go. Couldn’t help. When he woke up he was in one of the hanging houses, in a heap of blankets, like a puppy, and he couldn’t lift his left arm. He tried. It went up a hand’s width off the blanket and came down again, and lay there, and the whole of him felt the way it had felt on the morning of the first real sleep, on floor 8. Heavy. Loose. Like a rope somebody had finally let go of.

Except that this time it hurt.

“Floor 9,” said Rachael. She was sitting on a stool by the round door with her arms folded. She’d been there a while, he thought. “You know what floor 9 does?”

“Healing closes it,” said Ice. “It doesn’t make it not have happened.”

Rachael looked at him.

“That’s what Alea said,” said Ice.

“Alea’s right.” Rachael unfolded her arms. “Your thigh’s closed. It’ll scar. It’s going to take a week to stop hurting, whatever I do. Your arm the same. And you can’t lift anything for two days because you held your hand over your head for eleven hours, you absolute—“ She stopped. She breathed out through her nose. “I got the echo.”

He looked at her.

“You said you’d tell me,” said Rachael. “And I’d do it. And I’d get the echo. Once.” She was very calm. “I put my hands on your leg on that heap and I got the whole night. All of it. At once.” She looked at the round window, where the green light came in. “I sat down. I didn’t mean to. Rachael doesn’t sit down.”

“Rach—“

“Once,” said Rachael. “That was it. That was my once.” She stood up. “You don’t get another one this floor. So don’t.” She went to the door. Then she stopped in it, with her hand on the frame, and didn’t turn round. “You stood still,” she said. “Eleven hours. With that leg.”

“Yeah.”

“Good,” said Rachael. And went out.

They looked after him for four days.

He hated it. He’d known he would. He lay in the heap of blankets in the hanging house with his arm on the blanket, and people came in. All day. They brought things. He couldn’t stop them.

Hako brought him grubs, roasted, in a leaf, and sat by the door watching him to make sure he ate one. Tiger came in on the second morning with a sack of onions and sat cross-legged on the floor and peeled them, all of them, every one, without saying anything, the way Ice did, layer by layer, and put the skins in a neat pile, and went out again. Aleaka told him off for an hour for something he hadn’t done yet. Dennis came and negotiated with the bough-folk, through the window, at length, for a better kind of blanket. Kei left a jar of something green that Dose said was disgusting. Bee drew him asleep, without asking, and showed him.

He let them.

That was the strange part. He lay there and let them. He didn’t get up. He didn’t go anywhere. When they put things in his hands, he held them. It was the hardest thing he’d done on the whole floor, harder than the night, and nobody could see it but him and Alea.

“Your foot’s not tapping,” said Alea, on the third day.

“I can’t move my foot.”

“I know. I’m saying it anyway.”

Higu came on the fourth evening with her board.

She sat down on the floor beside the blankets and turned it round so he could see. It wasn’t the map of the ceiling. It was a new one. She’d done it in the two days he’d been asleep, from the platforms and from what everybody told her and from her own memory, with Chris’s notes beside it.

The Down, the day and the night of the split. In the middle, a small cross, where the log had been. Out of it, eight lines.

Seven of them were short. They wandered. They went out and away from the cross and from each other, every one the wrong way, the way the Down had walked them for the two hours in the afternoon, and each one stopped at a small mark, where a party had stood still when he asked. And the eighth went out west on its own, long and wandering, further than any of them, to a mark by itself.

And then seven more lines.

From the seven marks, at dusk. All seven. All at once. Turning toward the one mark on its own. And going straight at it. And running into each other on the way, two and two and three, until there were only two lines. And then one. One thick line, going the last of the way, straight into the mark in the west.

It looked like a star.

He lay there and looked at it for a long time.

“That’s you,” said Higu. She put her finger on the mark in the west, where all the lines met. “Everything else on this floor moves. Every path. Every fern. Every morning.” She took her finger away. “You didn’t.”

He didn’t say anything. He couldn’t.

“I drew it because I wanted to see it,” said Higu. “I’m going to keep it.” She looked at him. “I’m not going to draw that one again, though. So don’t make me.”

“Okay.”

“Okay.” She got up. At the door, she turned round. “Chris says the tower read the party lines. Off the windows. The same ones we read.” She was quiet. “It knows what a party is. It knows you’re one, on your own.”

“I know.”

“Xav says you need a party.”

He looked at the round green window.

“They’re all full,” said Ice.

Higu looked at him for a moment longer, and didn’t say anything to that, and went out with her board.

On the fourth night Drew came in with the rock.

He put it down on the floor by the blankets, face up, so Ice could read it in the foxfire. Under UP BEFORE DARK, and ALL OF US, there was a new line. Fresh chalk.

IF THE DOWN SPLITS US: ICE STANDS STILL AND MAKES A LIGHT. EVERYONE COMES.

Ice read it twice.

“I’ll hate that,” he said.

“I know,” said Drew.

“Every time.”

“I know.” Drew picked the rock up again, and tucked it under his arm, and looked at him. “Somebody thank me.”

“Thank you, Drew.”

“Okay,” said Ice. To the rock, as much as anybody.

Gabe came last. Late. After everyone else had gone up to their own hanging houses and the town had gone quiet except for the whistles, very soft, from tree to tree, the bough-folk saying goodnight. He came in with his arm still bound and sat down on the floor with his back against the woven wall beside the blankets, and his shield across his knees, the way he’d sat at the open side of a stone shelter on a wet hillside on floor 8.

They didn’t say anything for a while.

Down below, in the dark, very far off, the ticking. And further, so far you could only just hear it, north, somebody sawing wood.

“Your arm,” said Ice.

“Malcolm says it’ll scar.” Gabe looked at it. “Thorns. Right through. In one side and out the other. Like a nail.” He turned it over. “It’ll look cool.”

“It was going through your arm, Gabe.”

“I know where it was going.” Gabe didn’t look at him. “You let go of me.”

“Yeah.”

“Don’t do that again.”

Ice looked at the ceiling. At the woven withies going round and round to a point at the top, like the inside of a basket.

“Which bit?” he said.

“The letting go.” Gabe was quiet. “The other bit you can keep.”

“Which other bit?”

“The bit where you asked,” said Gabe.

Ice shut his eyes.

He thought about a fire. On the moss, at the foot of the seven, a long time ago. A heap of wood beside it. Three nights. Somebody sitting up beside it in the dark with the ticking all round him, putting a branch on, and another branch, for somebody who didn’t come, and didn’t come. And he’d never asked. And he couldn’t now. It had never happened. Not to this Gabe. This Gabe had never sat by that fire, and never would, and there was no way in the world to ask him what it had been like.

“Gabe,” he said.

“Mm.”

“If I’m ever late back. Anywhere. Any floor.” He stopped. “Make a fire.”

Gabe turned his head and looked at him.

He didn’t ask why. Ice watched him not ask. He watched him think about it, about whatever was in Ice’s voice, all of it, and decide not to.

“Dec,” said Gabe. “I’d make one every night.”

Ice turned his face to the woven wall.

“I know,” he said.

He didn’t say anything else. He couldn’t. Gabe didn’t make him. He sat there on the floor against the wall with his shield on his knees and his bad arm in his lap, and after a while he started to hum, very low, the way he’d hummed on floor 8 in the rain the night Ice first slept. And after a longer while, Ice slept.

When he woke in the grey of the morning, Gabe was still there. Sitting against the wall by the round door, with his shield on his knees and his eyes open. He’d watched it all night.

So Ice wouldn’t have to.
