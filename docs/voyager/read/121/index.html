Chapter 121

Slack Water

10,279 words · 45 min read

Nobody slept much.

The light turned all night. He knew, because he didn’t sleep either. He lay on his back on the floor of the long loft between Gabe and the wall, under the big grey nets hanging from the beams like the sails of a ship nobody had rigged yet, and watched it come and go on the ceiling. Through the gap where the shutter didn’t meet the frame. A pale stripe across the old wood, very faint, from a long way off.

Three short. One long.

He’d lain like this once before. In a bunk in the keeper’s house at the end of the Long Water, under a small square window, for three nights. Watching it go round on the ceiling. Counting it to sleep. He’d been so tired then. Two months on a ship. He’d fallen asleep on the second long beat, every night, and never once got to the end of a count.

He got to the end of every one of them now.

Some time after the second tide-bell, Thea came and lay down on the floor on his other side, between him and the wall, with her blanket. She didn’t say anything. She’d been lying across the loft with her own party, by the door, and she just got up and came across in the dark, stepping over Calvin, and lay down beside him and put her hand on his arm, in the place, and left it there.

He felt Gabe, on his right, half wake up, and look, and go back to sleep.

The light went round on the ceiling. Three short, one long.

After a while he stopped counting.

In the morning it was still there.

You could see it in the daylight, just. Higu stood at the end of the porch with the sun coming up out of the sea behind it and her hand over her eyes and looked for a long time, and then said there, and pointed, and after a moment everyone could see it: a tiny pale point on the edge of the world, far out to the east, past the mouth of the bay, past the mud where the tide had gone out again. Not a lamp, in the day. Just a speck. Something white, standing on something dark.

“That’s a tower,” said Hyvred. Who could see further than any of them. She was standing on the rail with one hand on a post, very still, with her eyes doing the thing they did. “A white tower. On black rocks. There’s a house at the bottom of it. There’s a light at the top, still going round. I can see the lamp move.” She was quiet. “There’s nothing in it.”

“Nothing?” said Higu.

“No people. No animals. No—“ Hyvred frowned. “It’s not like the Rimed. It’s not hollow. It’s just—“ She stopped. “It’s just empty. Like a picture of a house.”

Ice didn’t say anything.

They were all looking at him. Not asking. Just looking. The way they’d looked at him across the fire in the morning on the far side of the Gate. He felt thirty-five threads all leaning his way, gently, waiting, and Thea’s hand on his arm, and Alea on his shoulder, small and still.

Higu took the rock out.

“Ice,” she said. “What do you remember?”

He’d thought about this half the night. What to say. What it already had. What it didn’t. It’s building it out of what you remember, Alea had said, on the step, with her fur up. And if it was building it out of what he remembered, then there was nothing he could say on this porch that it didn’t already have. Nothing he could keep from it by not saying.

The only thing he could keep from it was what he did.

“It looks like the Last Light,” he said. “I told you. White tower. Last rock.” He kept his voice very level. “It’s in the wrong place. It’s two months in the wrong place. And it’s a day old.”

Higu looked at him for a long moment. Then wrote.

On the left, at the bottom of REMEMBERED, she wrote: LAST LIGHT: WEST. 2 MONTHS.

On the right, at the bottom of the other column, which still had no name: A LIGHT: EAST. 1 DAY. ON THE TEETH. NEW.

And then she drew a small square round it, which she’d never done to anything.

“We don’t go today,” she said.

“Higu—“

“We don’t go today,” said Higu again. Calm. “It came on last night. It wants us to look at it. So today we look at it, from here, and we let it turn, and we do our jobs.” She put the chalk away. “Tomorrow we decide.”

“I’ll go,” said Calvin immediately.

“Tomorrow,” said Higu, “we decide.”

The rota was on the back of the rock. Ice saw it at breakfast, when Higu propped the stone against the wall by the porch door, the way Drew always had, so that everybody could read the day off it as they went past.

He read it twice.

SHOALS (S. REEF): ICE. THEA, XOLA WATCHING.

He looked at it.

He looked at Higu, who was eating porridge on the top step with her eyes half closed and absolutely no expression on her face.

“It’s meant to be a different party every day,” he said.

“It was,” said Higu, without opening her eyes.

“It’s meant to be—“

“It’s on the rock, Dec,” said Deston, from inside the loft, not looking up from his bowl.

Somebody laughed. Then several people. Then Calvin said something that Rachael said Calvin to, and Xola, at the far end of the porch, with Squiddles on her head and her porridge forgotten in her lap, was making a long, high, almost inaudible noise like a kettle, with both hands pressed flat on her cheeks.

Thea was at the rail with her back to all of them, looking at the light on the Teeth. The back of her neck had gone bright red.

“Drew’s taking the party north,” she said. To the sea. “To the long rocks. He’s head for the day. It’s fine. He was head the first—“ She stopped. Very slightly. “He’s good at it.”

Drew, sitting cross-legged on the porch boards with his bowl in his lap and his feet bare, looked up at the back of her head. Then at Ice.

“I’m very good at it,” said Drew. “Somebody thank me.”

“Thank you, Drew,” said about half the porch.

Ice looked at the back of Thea’s neck. At the stop in the middle of the sentence. He was head the first—

Nobody else had heard it. Or everybody had, and let it go by, the way you let a gull go by.

He went and got his shield.

The Shoals were south of the harbour, round the point.

Marrit told him how to get there, from the top of the slip, with a mallet in her hand and pitch to her elbows. Out along the south wall, down the steps at the end, and then straight out across the mud and the rocks with the sun on his left, for about an hour. Until the mud stopped and it was all rock. Black rock, flat and broken, in shelves and ridges and pools, going on and on out toward the sea where the tide had gone.

“Wrackers,” said Marrit. “That’s what you’ll want, if you want a fight. They come up out of the weed at the bottom of the tide and strip the rocks. Anything on them. Mussels, weed, wreck, people.” She looked at him. “They took a boy last year. Off the edge. He went out too far for the big mussels and didn’t watch the water.”

“I’ll watch the water.”

“Everybody says that.” She went back to the hull. “The tide turns about the second hour after noon. When it turns, you get out. You don’t finish what you’re doing. You don’t go back for your hat. You get out.” She hit a peg with the mallet, hard. “It comes across the Shoals faster than you can run. And in that armour you’ll sink like an anchor.”

“Noted,” said Alea, in his ear.

He went out along the south wall, with Thea and Xola behind him.

Not with him. That was the rule. Behind him. Far enough back that it was his fight and not theirs. He could feel them there anyway, the whole way out along the wall and down the steps onto the mud, two threads at his back, one warm and pale like water in the sun, and one so close and bright it felt like holding a lamp.

The mud was cold and sucked at his boots. After a while it gave way to rock. Wet black rock, ridged like the skin of something enormous, with weed hanging off it in long brown ropes and pools in every hollow, clear as glass, full of small moving things. The sea was a long way off. He could hear it. The breathing. Out past the end of the rocks, past a line of white where the swell broke.

He walked out toward it for an hour, the way Marrit had said, with the sun on his left.

And behind him, Xola found the pools.

He heard it before he saw it. A splash, and then a shriek, and then a whole run of words he couldn’t make out in a voice that went up and up and up like a gull. He turned round on the rock. Thea was standing at the edge of a big pool fifty metres back, with the lantern in her hand, laughing. And in the pool, up to her neck, with her hair spread out round her on the water like a lilac-and-pink cloak and her gills fanned wide and Squiddles bobbing beside her like a cork, was Xola.

“It’s WARM,” shrieked Xola. “Babe! Babe! It’s WARM in the little ones! The sun’s been on it! There’s fish! There’s a little crab, he’s waving at me, hello, hello—“

“You’re meant to be watching,” Ice called.

“I am watching!” Xola ducked under. Came up again, shining. “I’m watching from a pool! I can see you perfectly! Freshwater animal, she says. Freshwater. Look at me!” She rolled over on her back in the water and floated, with her arms out, and looked at the sky. “I’m never getting out. I live here now. Tell everybody I’m dead and I’m happy.”

Thea, at the edge of the pool, looked across fifty metres of black rock at him. Still laughing. With her free hand over her mouth.

He’d never seen her laugh like that in daylight. In the first life. Not once that he could remember. Not where he could see it.

He stood on the rock and looked at it.

“Your foot’s not tapping again,” said Alea.

“Shut up,” he said, and turned round, and went to find a fight.

He found it where the rocks ended.

The last shelf of the Shoals went out into the sea like a pier, flat and black and slick, and stopped. Past it there was a channel, deep, green, with weed waving in it, and then the open water, and the line of white where the swell broke. The tide was nearly at the bottom. The sea was very quiet. Almost still. As if it had gone as far as it was going to go, and was thinking.

The weed at the edge of the shelf moved.

Not with the water. Against it.

And they came up out of it.

He counted while they came. Alea counted too, out loud, in his ear. Six. Eight. Eleven. Crabs. Except that crabs was the wrong word, the way rats had been the wrong word on floor 4. They were the size of a cartwheel, each of them, low and wide and blue-black, with shells like upturned barrels gone green with weed and barnacles, and legs like a row of bent iron rods, and two claws apiece that opened and shut with a sound like heavy shears. Eyes on stalks, looking at him. All of them. Coming up over the lip of the shelf out of the weed with water running off their backs, and spreading out across the rock in a long, low, clicking line.

“Then he’s an idiot as well,” she said. “You’re both idiots. All of you. Every single—“ And her voice went on single, and she turned her face away to the sea, quickly, and wiped it with the back of her wrist, which did nothing, because everything was wet. “I watched it on nine. All day. I thought I’d got used to it.” She sniffed, hard. “I have not got used to it.”

Thea hadn’t said anything.

She was still kneeling in front of him. She’d let go of his face while Xola healed him, and she was sitting back on her heels now with her hands on her knees and the lantern on the wet rock beside her, and she’d listened to every word with nothing on her face at all. He knew that face. He’d seen it on nine, at midnight, after the brood. I’m still angry.

“Xola’s right,” she said.

Quietly. The mean voice. The one she kept for when she needed it.

“I called the tide,” said Thea. “I called it, and you heard me. I saw you hear me. You looked at the water. And then you looked at her coming up out of the channel, and you went.” She didn’t look away from him. “You didn’t even decide. I watched you. You just went. As if the sea was somebody else’s problem.”

“Thea—“

“If Xola had been anybody else,” said Thea. “If she’d been on the land instead of in a pool. If she’d been me. You’d be at the bottom of the bay with your greaves half off. And I’d be standing on a rock with a lantern in my hand. Watching.” Her voice didn’t shake. Her hands, on her knees, had closed. “Following the rule. Is that what the rule’s for? So I can watch?”

He didn’t have anything to say to that. There wasn’t anything.

“Under the lights,” said Thea. “On ten. I said heal sooner. A bit. Try. And you said you’d try.”

“I tried,” he said. “Sixty-four.”

“You tried at sixty-four. And then you stayed out in the sea for a crab.” She took a breath. “Try isn’t enough. I thought it would be. It isn’t. I’m not having try.”

She reached out and took his sword hand off the hilt across his knees, and held it in both of hers. Hard. Wet and cold and hard.

“Promise me,” she said.

“Promise what?”

“That you’ll look after yourself. Properly. Not when it’s nearly too late. When it hurts. That when Alea says a number you don’t like, you heal, and you don’t wait for a worse one. That you get out when the tide turns. That you come back.” Her grip tightened. “I know you won’t do it for you. You never have. I don’t think you know how. So don’t do it for you.” Her eyes were very bright, and very steady. “Do it for me.”

He looked at her.

At her hands round his. At the lantern on the black rock beside her, burning in the full sun where nobody needed it. At Xola, very still now, with the water running out of her hair, watching him the way she’d watched him from the pool.

Nobody had ever asked him for that. He’d never stood still long enough for anybody to.

“Ice,” said Thea.

“I promise.”

“Say it properly.”

He held her hands.

“I promise,” he said. “I’ll look after myself. I’ll heal when it hurts. I’ll get out when it turns.” He swallowed salt. “For you.”

Thea let her breath out. All of it, slowly, as if she’d been holding it since the tide turned. She didn’t smile. She nodded once, the way you’d nod at something that had just gone on the rock. Then she leaned forward and put her forehead against his and stayed there.

“I heard that,” said Xola. Thickly. “I’m a witness. That’s a witnessed promise. I’m putting it on the rock.”

“It’s not going on the rock,” said Thea, into his hair.

“It’s going on my rock.” Xola sniffed again. Then she leaned in and put her own wet forehead against the side of his head, briefly, hard, the way she did to Thea sometimes. “You idiot,” she said. And then, after a moment, very quietly, as if it was something she’d been holding back as well: “Icey.”

The tide came in round them while they sat there.

There was nothing else to do. The rock was the highest point for half a mile. They watched the whole of the Shoals go under, ridge by ridge, until there was nothing left between them and the land but a long bay of green-grey water, choppy, glittering, with the harbour wall and the stilts of Hythe very small on the far side of it. The rock was an island now. Ten metres across. Flat on top. Warm.

“How long till it goes out again?” said Thea.

“Six hours,” said Ice. “About.”

“Six hours.”

“It doesn’t wait.”

Thea looked at the water. Then at him. Then she started to laugh again, helplessly, with her face in her hands. “Higu’s going to kill us. We’re meant to be back for the slip. Marrit’s going to—“

“Marrit said get out when it turns,” said Ice. “We didn’t get out.”

“You didn’t get out.”

“I didn’t get out,” he agreed.

Xola had been lying on her back on the warm rock with her eyes shut, steaming gently in the sun. She sat up.

“Right,” she said. “Right. I’ll go.”

“Go where?”

“Back. Swim back. Tell them you’re on a rock. Tell Higu. Tell Marrit.” She was already standing up, wringing out her hair. Squiddles climbed back up onto her head, slowly, like someone getting into a cold bath. “Somebody has to. Or they’ll send Calvin. And then we’ll have Calvin on a rock.”

“Xola—“

“It’s a mile,” said Xola. “I just did a hundred metres with you on my back. A mile is nothing. A mile is a lovely little swim.” She looked down at Thea, sitting on the rock. Then at Ice. And something in her face went very soft and very wicked at the same time. “And I’ll go very slowly,” she said. “I’ll take my time. I’m a freshwater animal. It takes us ages. Bye, Icey. Bye, babe.”

And she went off the edge of the rock in one long, perfect dive, and was gone, and a moment later came up twenty metres away, and waved, and went under again, a long lilac shape sliding away across the bay toward Hythe, not slowly at all.

Thea watched her go with her mouth open.

“She’s—“ she said.

“Yeah.”

“She planned that.”

“She planned it the second she saw the rota,” said Ice.

Thea turned round on the rock and looked at him. And then she stopped laughing, slowly, and just looked. At him. Sitting on a rock in the sea with his armour half off and his hair full of salt and his sword across his knees, in the sun, in the middle of the day. With nobody else for a mile in any direction.

“Hi,” she said.

“Hi,” said Ice.

They sat there for six hours.

It was the longest he’d ever sat anywhere with her in daylight. In either life. He worked it out, about the third hour, lying on his back on the warm stone with her head on his chest and the sky going over: the longest, by a long way. The first time there’d been the dinner on 43, which had been two hours, and the night on 70 when she’d put her hand on his arm, which had been ten minutes, and every other hour he’d ever spent with her had been in a camp, or a fight, or a room full of other people. Never this. Never a rock in the sea with nobody on it.

They didn’t talk much, at first. She took the rest of his armour off and laid it out on the rock to dry, piece by piece, very carefully, the way she’d laid out his things on the rock by the tarn. She put the lamp shield flat in the sun with the rose side up. She looked at the rose for a long time.

“Jared did this?”

“On five. At the Linkworks. Nobody was meant to see it.”

“It’s lovely.”

“Don’t tell him. He’ll go red for a week.”

She laughed. She sat beside him with her knees up, looking at the sea. Then she said, without looking at him, “It was so hard.”

He knew what she meant.

“I know.”

“You don’t. You were fighting.” She was very quiet. “I stood on that rock with the lantern and I watched it pull you toward the channel and I couldn’t. I couldn’t. It’s the rule. You don’t lift a hand unless he goes down. And you hadn’t gone down. And I had a spell ready. I had it written. I could have put it straight through her eye from fifty metres, Ice. I had it in my hand.” She opened her hand on her knee and looked at it. “And I held it. The whole time. Until it went out.”

“You held it,” he said.

“I held it.” She closed her hand. “And I knew. At the end. When she put you on your back.” She turned her head and looked at him. “You tipped your head slower. Like on nine, the third time. I told you I’d learn it.” She looked at her closed hand. “I knew before your hand went gold. That’s the first time. The first time I’ve ever known before.”

She was quiet.

“You promised,” she said. Not asking. Checking. The way you’d put your hand on a pocket to feel the thing was still in it.

“I promised.”

“Good.” She opened her hand again and looked at it.

“Is that what it’s like?” said Thea. “For you? Every time? Watching one of us?”

He thought about it. About eleven hours on floor 9 with his hand up. About standing on the edge of the Steward’s ring and not getting up. About ten years.

“Yes,” he said.

“I hate it.”

“I know.”

“I’m going to do it every day,” said Thea. “Every day you go out. For the whole floor. All the floors.” She looked at him. “Because Higu wrote it on the rock and Deston said it’s on the rock, Dec, and nobody argued, and I’m not going to make Xola do it on her own.”

“You could swap. With Drew. With—“

“No,” said Thea. Flatly. The mean voice. The one she used when she needed it. “No. I watch you. That’s mine as well.” And then, more quietly: “I’d rather watch it and hate it than not watch it and not know.”

He didn’t say anything for a while. Then he reached out and took her closed hand, on her knee, and opened it, very gently, finger by finger, and held it.

In the fourth hour she asked him about the light.

He’d known she would. She’d been looking at it for a long time, off to the east, a speck on the edge of the sea, white on black, still turning, even in the sun.

“You knew it,” she said. “Last night. On the porch. You stood up before it finished. You didn’t even count it. You knew.” She turned her head on his chest to look up at him. “What is it?”

He looked up at the sky.

Alea was sitting on his stomach. She’d come down off his shoulder in the second hour, when it was clear there was nobody for a mile, and she wasn’t small. She was the size of a cat, pale teal and white, with her tail round her feet, curled on the warm wet cloth of his shirt in the sun with her eyes half shut like any cat on any warm thing. Thea had looked at her for a long time when she first did it. Then put out her hand. And Alea had put her forehead in it again, without being asked.

Tell her, said Alea now. Not opening her eyes. It’s got it already. It’s not your mouth it’s reading. Tell her.

“It’s the rhythm,” he said.

Thea waited.

“Three short and a long,” said Ice. “That’s what the Last Light does. That’s what it did. The first time. I lay under it for three nights, in the keeper’s house, at the end of the Long Water. Counting it. I counted it on other floors for years after. In tents. Without meaning to.” He was quiet. “I never said it. On the ledge. I said white, and a tower, and the last rock. I never said three and a long. Not to anybody. Not out loud. Not once in this life.”

He felt her go still on his chest.

“And it knew,” she said.

“It knew. Alea checked. She keeps the list.”

“I always keep the list,” said Alea, to the sky, with her eyes shut. Thea couldn’t hear her. Thea looked at her anyway.

“So it’s not what you say,” said Thea slowly. Working it out. He could feel her doing it. The way she’d done it for nine floors, reading under windows. “On floor 7, and the high side. The Squall, the Wanting. It made those out of what you said. It listened. And it made a thing to block it.” She sat up. “But this isn’t blocking. It’s not in the way of anything. It’s a day east, on a rock nobody goes to. It’s not a wall. It’s—“ She stopped.

“An offer,” said Ice.

“A question.” Thea was looking at the light. “It’s the same question. Where did you come from. It asked you at the Gate. Twice. And you said them, and it said that’s not a place. So it’s asking again. But not with words.” She turned to look at him. “It’s built you a place. Out of the other one. Out of the record it found. And it’s put it where you’ll see it. And it’s waiting to see if you go.”

He didn’t say anything.

“If you go,” said Thea, “you’re telling it you know the way. You’re telling it which one of you is real.”

“And if I don’t go?”

“Then it builds another one.” She was very pale. “And another. Closer. Until you do.”

The light turned on the edge of the sea. Three short. One long. Even in the sun.

“Record found: one of two,” said Thea, very quietly. “That’s what it said. You told me. On the rock. One of two.” She looked down at him. “It’s got one. It’s reading it. That light’s out of it.” She swallowed. “Ice. How much of it has it got?”

“I don’t know.”

“Has it got the boss room?”

He didn’t answer.

“Has it got the ward?” said Thea. “Has it got me?” Her voice had gone very small. “The first time. Has it got what I—“

“I don’t know,” said Ice. He sat up. He took her face in his hands the way she’d taken his. “I don’t know, Thea. I don’t know what it’s got. I know it’s got the rhythm of a light I counted to sleep. That’s all I know. That’s all it’s shown me.”

She looked at him. Her eyes were very bright.

“Then that’s what we find out,” she said. “That’s what Higu said. You remember. We find what’s true.” She took a breath. “So we go.”

“We?”

“You’re not going on your own,” said Thea. “You’re not going in a party of one. You’re going in a boat, with people, and you’re going to walk in there and look at it, and you’re going to do nothing it wants.” Her hands had come up over his, on her face. “You’re going to be the man who’s never been there. That’s all. You just have to be the man who’s never been there.”

“I’ve been there.”

“I know,” said Thea. “That’s why it’s hard.”

He laughed. It came out of him like a cough, and turned into a real one, and she was laughing too, with her hands over his on her face, on a rock in the sea, with a fox the size of a cat sitting on his legs watching them both.

“And there’s another thing,” said Thea, when they’d stopped.

“What?”

“It doesn’t know I know.” She said it very carefully. “Does it? It wasn’t watching. On the far side. You said. Alea said. Nothing was watching that night.” She looked at Alea, who had opened her eyes. “So it doesn’t know you told me.”

No, said Alea. Very quietly. In his ear. And then, to Thea, though Thea couldn’t hear her, with her white pinprick eyes fixed on Thea’s face: And it mustn’t.

“She says no,” said Ice. “She says it mustn’t.”

Thea nodded. Slowly. As if she’d already known.

“Then I keep it from it as well,” she said. “Not just from them. From it.” She looked at the light. “I don’t look at that light like I know what it is. I don’t count it. I don’t know it’s three and a long. I’m just Thea. Who’s with Ice. Who reads windows.” She almost smiled. “I’m good at that. I’ve been the one who reads windows for ten floors.”

“Thea—“

“That’s the deal,” said Thea. “Same deal. It’s mine. I’m keeping it.”

He looked at her for a long time.

He thought about the first time. About a wall of rewritten light, and somebody stepping round him. About I can’t promise that. About thirty-five people sitting down in the snow in front of a gate.

He thought: it’s going to find out. One day. Because she’s her. Because she won’t be able to help it.

He didn’t say it.

He leaned forward and put his forehead against hers, the way Xola had, and stayed there.

The tide went out at the end of the afternoon, the way it always would.

They walked back across the Shoals in the low gold light, with his armour on again, still damp, and the rocks coming up out of the water round them shining. Pools. Weed. Small things moving. Nothing that wanted them. At the edge of the mud, near the steps up to the south wall, there was a little crowd waiting: Higu, with the rock. Drew, barefoot, with his arms folded. Chi with Waddles under one arm. Gabe. And Xola, wrapped in somebody’s cloak, sitting on the bottom step with her hair in a towel, looking extremely innocent.

“Six hours,” said Higu.

“The tide turned,” said Ice.

“I know it turned. Marrit told me it turned. Marrit told me a lot of things.” Higu looked at him. Then at Thea, beside him. Then at their hands. Then back at him, with absolutely no expression at all. “You got out when it turned?”

“No.”

“You held onto the shield,” said Gabe.

Ice looked at him. Gabe was standing at the bottom of the steps with his own shield on his back and his arms folded and his face doing something very complicated.

“Xola told you.”

“Xola told everybody,” said Gabe. “Xola told the harbour. She told Corran. She told a baby.” He unfolded his arms. “You held onto it. In the sea. With an old crab pulling you into the deep. Because it was mine.”

“It’s mine,” said Ice. “You said. It was never yours.”

Gabe looked at him for a long moment.

Then he came up the mud and put both his arms round him, armour and salt and all, and held on. Hard. And said, into his wet hair, very quietly, where nobody else could hear: “Idiot. I’d have made you another one.”

“I know,” said Ice. “I didn’t want another one.”

Gabe didn’t say anything to that. He just held on a bit longer. And then let go, and turned round, and walked back up the steps to the wall, and didn’t look back.

Higu wrote something on the rock.

Ice didn’t see what it was. He found out later. At the bottom of the right-hand column, under the light on the Teeth, small, in her neat capitals:

TIDE: DOESN’T WAIT. (TRUE.)

Corran was on the porch when they got back. In the same place, against the wall, in the last of the sun, with his mug. He’d been at the slip all day, Maigo said. Not working. He couldn’t work any more. Sitting on his bucket under the bow, telling Salty and Jared which pegs were wrong, and being right.

He watched Ice come up the ladder with salt in his hair.

“You went out on the Shoals,” said the old man.

“Yes.”

“Marrit says you took Old Shears.”

“Yes.”

Corran looked at him for a while with his pale milky eyes. Then he laughed, a dry thin laugh like a rope running through a block.

“She took my brother,” he said. “Seventy years ago. Off the far end. Same as the boy last year. I hope you got her good.”

“I got her good,” said Ice.

“Good.” The old man drank from his mug. “Sit down, stair-boy. You’ve a face like you’ve seen me before.”

Ice stopped with one foot on the porch.

He made himself keep moving. He sat down on the boards, near the old man, with his back against the wall, and stretched his healed leg out, and looked at the harbour. The tide was out. The mud was shining. The light on the Teeth was turning, far off, three and a long.

“I’ve heard of you,” he said.

“Have you.” It wasn’t a question. Corran was looking at the light too. “From who?”

“Somebody who loved you.”

The old man was quiet for a long time.

“Marrit,” he said. “That’ll be Marrit. She’s got a mouth on her.” He didn’t ask how. He didn’t ask when, or why a stair-boy who’d come down out of the cliff two days ago would have heard anything from anybody. He just sat with it, the way the tide-folk sat with everything, the way they’d looked at thirty-six climbers on the quay and gone back to scraping their boats. “She’ll sail the Patience. When I’m gone. She doesn’t know it yet. She thinks she’s going to make me do it.” He smiled at the light. “She’ll be good. She’s better than me. She just hasn’t had to be yet.”

Ice didn’t say anything. He couldn’t.

She sat up with you on the thirtieth night, when the rudder went, and told you about her grandfather.

“Nobody goes to the Teeth,” said Corran, after a while. “Did they tell you that? Nobody. It’s rock and wreck. There’s ships out there from before my grandmother. The sea’s had them.” He drank. “And now there’s a light on it. Where there’s never been a light. And you lot are sat on my porch looking at it the way my father used to look at weather.”

“We’re going to go and see,” said Ice.

“I know you are.” The old man closed his eyes. “Marrit’ll take you. She’s been wanting to see what’s on the Teeth since she was four. I told her no for thirty years.” He sighed, comfortably, like a man settling into a chair. “I’ll not be able to tell her no for much longer. I may as well stop now.”

They decided after supper.

Higu stood on the top step with the rock and everybody listened, and it was short, because Higu was always short. The light had turned all day. It hadn’t moved. It hadn’t changed. Nothing had come off it, and nothing had gone to it, and the Teeth were a day’s sail east for a tide-boat, and Marrit Patience had said, when asked, flatly, at the slip, with pitch on her hands, I’ll take you, I’ve been wanting to since I was four.

“Thea’s party,” said Higu. “And Ice.”

Nobody argued.

He looked round. At thirty-four people on a porch in the dark with a lamp, nodding. At Drew, who’d been the head of a party the first time and was not the head of one now and didn’t seem to mind. At Chi, very calm, with Waddles asleep in her lap. At Xola, who’d done nothing all evening but look at Thea and squeak. At Higu herself, putting her own name on the list with her own chalk, without blinking.

Thea’s party. The club. Five of the thirteen. The ones who’d had the theory for half a dozen floors and never asked him once.

“Why them?” he said. He knew why. He wanted to hear Higu say it.

“Because it’s for you,” said Higu. Calm. “Whatever it is. And you don’t go in a party of one.” She put the rock down. “And because we don’t ask.”

He looked at her.

“Whatever’s in there,” said Higu, “we’ll find what’s true. We’ll write down what’s true. That’s all.” She looked at him, half-lidded, steady. “Anything that’s yours stays yours. It doesn’t go on the rock.”

There was a short silence on the porch.

Then Deston, at the back, said, “Good,” and went back to his tea. And that was all anybody said about it.

He had the late watch with Thea again. Ten till twelve. It was on the rock.

They sat on the top step with the lantern between them and the harbour all mud and stars. Far off to the east, the light turned. Three short. One long.

She didn’t look at it.

He watched her not look at it. All through the watch. She sat beside him with her knees up and her shoulder against his and looked at the harbour, and at the stars, and at Ebba’s window across the water, where the lamp was still on and somebody was still singing the one line over and over to a baby. She looked at the stilts. She looked at the sky. She looked at him.

She never once looked east.

“You’re very good at that,” he said, near midnight. Very quietly.

“At what?”

“Not looking.”

“I’ve been the one who reads windows for ten floors,” said Thea, and smiled at the harbour, and didn’t look east. “I told you. I’m very good.”

Alea, between them, in the light of the lantern, small again, the way she had to be on a porch where anybody could come out, put her chin down on her paws.

She is, said Alea. That’s what frightens me.
