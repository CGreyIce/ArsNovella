Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 1: Thirty-Six Circles

Chapter 35

The Last to Know

3,092 words · 13 min read

Winter had been the last to know things before, and she’d decided a long time ago that it was never going to happen again.

She didn’t talk about it. She didn’t talk about most things. People thought she was cold, because she looked cold: very long silver-white hair, pale skin, two short black horns curving back through it, one eye pale blue and the other pink, and a face that didn’t do very much until it had decided about you. She knew what she looked like. She’d chosen it, years ago, for her model, and she’d liked it. It was a face that kept people at a distance until they’d earned their way in.

But she’d been the last to know before. She’d come back from somewhere, once, and found that the world had moved on without her. Things had happened. Everybody else had already had time to get used to them, and she’d been standing in the middle of it all, out of date, like someone walking into a room just after the joke.

So now she watched. All the time. Not people, exactly. The spaces between them. Who looked at who. Who stopped talking when somebody came into a room. Who knew something.

And for three days on floor 2, she’d been watching Hyvred.

It wasn’t hard. Hyvred was the least secretive person Winter had ever met. She cried at sunsets and shouted across rooms and told everybody everything, all the time, at full volume, with her horns catching the light. She was so open it was almost unbearable.

And she was keeping something from Gabe.

Winter saw it on the first evening, in the trapper’s hall. Gabe came in from the cold, and Hyvred, by the board, looked up and saw him. And her face did a thing. A guilty thing, quick, gone. Then she turned round to the board and started writing very busily on it, about nothing.

She did it again the next morning. And again that afternoon. Every time Gabe came near her, Hyvred’s face did the guilty thing and she found something urgent to do on the other side of the room.

And there was Deston.

Deston’s party had come up the stair on the seventeenth day, the last up, after Chris’s. Everybody had been relieved. Deston had walked into the trapper’s hall with his tall careful calm and his hand on his shirt pocket, and gone straight to Hyvred. They’d talked by the fire, low, for ten minutes. Hyvred had nodded a lot. Deston had said something and put his hand on her shoulder. And then they’d both, at the same moment, looked across the hall at Gabe.

Winter had been sitting in the corner by the window with her bow across her knees, and she’d seen all of it.

They know something, she thought. And they’re not telling him.

She filed it. And waited to see what it was.

Gabe came back down from the Mere on the eighteenth day, in a coat.

The whole trapper’s hall went quiet when he walked in. Xav’s party had cleared the Old Stag the day before, the first of all of them to do it. Everybody knew that from World. But what nobody had known until the door opened was that Gabe would come back wearing that.

It was enormous. Grey wolf fur, inside and out, down to his knees, stiff with age. It fitted him as if it had been cut for him. And everybody in that hall had heard it told at the board, nearly a week ago, by Newish, with her hands. A wolf-fur coat that stank to high heaven. Grey. Down to his knees.

“Oh my God,” said Newish, from the big table.

Gabe stood in the doorway in Voyager’s coat and didn’t say anything. His face was doing something complicated. Behind him, Xav had his arms folded and an expression of deep, weary resignation.

“It was on the bench,” said Gabe finally. “In the ascension room. By the door. Folded.”

Nobody said anything.

“He left it,” said Gabe. “For whoever came next.”

“For you,” said somebody. Calvin, probably.

Gabe didn’t answer that. He just stood there in the coat.

And Winter, in the corner by the window, wasn’t looking at Gabe. She was looking at Hyvred.

Hyvred was standing by the board with a piece of chalk in her hand, looking at the coat. Her face had gone very white. Not guilty this time. Something worse. Her left eye, the one with the little red mark in it, was locked on the coat. On the fur, just at the collar. As if she could see something there that nobody else could.

Then she put the chalk down very carefully and went out of the back door of the hall into the snow.

Winter got up and went after her.

She found her round the side of the trapper’s hall, by the woodpile, with her arms wrapped round herself and her tail curled tight round her legs.

Winter didn’t say anything at first. She stood a little way off, by the stacked logs, and waited. She was good at waiting. She’d found that most people, if you stood near them and didn’t say anything, eventually told you what was wrong. It worked best on people who’d been carrying something too long.

It took Hyvred about thirty seconds.

“It’s on the coat,” she said, without turning round.

“What is?”

“His light.” Hyvred’s voice was thin. “I can see where people have been. Where their light’s been. Everyone’s is a different colour. And his is—it’s like a lamp. Warm. Nobody else’s is like it.” She turned round, and her mismatched golden eyes were wet. “It’s all over that coat, Winter. At the collar. Where his hands were when he folded it. And Gabe’s wearing it, and he doesn’t know, he can’t see it, and I can, and I can’t tell him.”

Winter looked at her for a long moment.

“Why can’t you tell him?”

Hyvred told her.

All of it, standing by the woodpile in the snow. The bench at the Copper Spoon. The Fold. The ascension room, and the warm person-shaped glow where he’d slept, and the small curled one beside it. The stairs, a warm mark on every step: he was counting. The footprints in the snow on floor 2, all the way along the treeline to the pine, and up, and over the pass, and down to the Mere. The trail fading, a little every day. And Deston, asking her not to tell Gabe. Please. Not till we’re all on floor 3. He’s being careful and I need him to stay careful.

“Deston thinks he’ll go after him,” said Hyvred. “If he knows I can see the way. Up the stair to three, and then four, on his own. Following a trail he can’t see.” She wiped her eyes. “And he’s right. Gabe would. You saw him. He’d go tonight.”

Winter didn’t say anything for a while.

She thought about the coat. About Gabe standing in the doorway in it, with his face doing something complicated. About Hyvred, who couldn’t keep a secret to save her life, keeping this one for five days because someone she trusted had asked her to, and how much it was costing her.

And she thought about what it was like to be the last to know.

“You should tell him,” said Winter.

Hyvred stared at her.

“Not because he’d go after him,” said Winter. “Deston’s right about that. He would.” She said it bluntly, the way she said everything. “But he’s the one who’d want to know most. More than anybody here. He’s been looking for him since the white room.” She paused. “And if he finds out later, from somebody else, that half the people in this hall knew where Ice had been walking, and nobody told him—“

She stopped.

“That’s worse,” she said. “Being the last to know. That’s always worse. Whatever the reason.”

Hyvred looked at her for a long time.

“I promised Deston,” she said.

“Then make Deston tell him.” Winter folded her arms. “On floor 3. When everyone’s together, the way he wants. Make him promise you he’ll do it. Not maybe. Not when the time’s right. Floor 3, all together, first night. He tells Gabe.” She looked at Hyvred. “That’s fair. That’s careful and fair.”

Hyvred looked at her for a moment longer. Then something in her face eased, like a fist opening.

“Floor 3,” she said. “First night.”

“First night.”

“I’ll make him promise.” She wiped her face on her sleeve. “Thank you, Winter.”

Winter didn’t say you’re welcome. She didn’t really know how. She just nodded once, and then, because Hyvred was still shaking slightly, she did something she almost never did. She reached out and put one hand on Hyvred’s arm, briefly, and took it away again.

That evening, the rankings changed.

Winter saw it because she was watching Gabe. She was always watching somebody, and that evening it was Gabe. He was sitting at the big table in the wolf coat, opening and closing his window the way he did every evening, checking. And she saw his face go very still.

She opened her own.

Floor 4.

Chris, at the other end of the table, had his notebook out. She heard his pencil stop.

Nobody said anything. The floor 3 clear had come two nights ago, everybody had seen it. They’d all known he’d go up. Maigo had said it at this table, in his quiet voice: he doesn’t wait. But knowing he would and seeing it on the list were different things.

Floor 4. Alone. While the rest of them sat in a wooden town on floor 2, at the wall at level ten, waiting to go up to floor 3 together.

Winter looked at Deston.

Deston was sitting near the fire with his party, and he’d opened his window too. He was looking at the rankings, at Floor 4, and his face had gone a colour Winter had never seen on him. Grey. His hand had gone flat on his shirt pocket and stayed there, pressing, as if he were holding something in.

Careful, Winter thought, watching his hand on the pocket. Deston said it all the time. He’d put it in the guide in World, please, twice. Whatever’s in that pocket, it’s about being careful.

She filed it.

Chris came and sat down next to her in the corner by the window, later, when the hall had gone quieter. He had his notebook open on his knee at the back page, where the little graph was. Days along the bottom, levels up the side. A flat line of dots at ten, one going straight up to sixteen, and now a new dot, seventeen, with a small 4 written beside it.

He didn’t say anything for a while. Neither did she. They’d played six hundred games together, at least, and she’d learned early on that Chris was one of the very few men she could sit next to without feeling she had to be ready for something. He talked too much and said strange things and always, always meant well.

“You’ve been watching Deston,” said Chris finally.

Winter looked at him.

“I notice things,” said Chris. “It’s the logging. You can’t log things without noticing them.” He turned a page back in the notebook and held it out. She read it. Day 11. I think Deston is babysitting us. Day 12. Deston is definitely babysitting us. And, on a later page: Something in his pocket. Not asking yet.

“You’ve been watching his pocket,” said Winter.

“For two weeks.”

“Hyvred knows something too. About Ice. Deston asked her not to tell Gabe.” She didn’t say what. It wasn’t hers to say. “I told her to make him promise. Floor 3, the first night. All of it.”

Chris looked at her for a long moment over the top of his notebook.

“Good,” he said. “That’s good, Winter.” He closed the notebook. “I hate it when people find things out last. It’s the worst bug there is. Everything’s been running wrong for ages and nobody told you.”

Winter didn’t say anything. But something in her chest, something that had been clenched since she’d followed Hyvred out to the woodpile, let go a little.

He gets it, she thought.

She didn’t tell him that. She just sat next to him in the corner by the window while the fire burned down, two people who noticed things, watching the room.

Ina put the mask on the next day, on the north slope, and Winter was the reason.

It was a big pack, bigger than any they’d seen, led by an Alpha with a scar down its face that had come down off the high pines in the night. They went round the way they always went round. And Winter, on a rock at the back with her bow, frost on her arrowheads the way there’d been frost on them since floor 1, was watching the front. She should have been watching the back.

Two of them came over the rock behind her.

She heard them too late. She turned. The first one was in the air, white and huge, ice-blue eyes, teeth. She had time to think, very clearly, oh, that’s annoying.

The fire came past her so close it singed her hair.

It wasn’t orange. Ina’s fire had always been orange: a good, warm, ordinary fire, the kind you’d cook on, coming off his palm in balls and streams. This was white. White with blue at the edges, so hot the snow on the rock turned to steam in a ring round her boots. It hit the wolf in mid-air and the wolf was simply gone, and the second one turned and ran.

Winter turned round.

Ina was standing below the rock with his hand still out. On his face, for the first time since the white room, was the mask.

It was a grey oni mask, carved, grinning, with fangs and furious eyes, pushed up from where it usually hung at his hip. With it on, under his single purple horn, he looked exactly like what everybody had always been afraid of. A demon. A monster out of a story. The thing that comes in the night.

He took it off again immediately. As if it burned.

“Sorry,” said Ina, in his smooth British voice, which had gone very slightly unsteady. “Sorry. I know it’s—I know what it looks like.”

Winter looked at him for a long moment.

She knew what it looked like. She had horns too. Two short black ones, curving back through her silver hair, the kind that made people look twice in the street and then look away. She’d seen the way the woman at Larchwick’s gate had looked at both of them the first morning, her hand going to the axe on her belt. She’d seen it on floor 1, in Hearthstead, once or twice. People who’d heard old stories about things with horns.

We look like the bad guys, she thought. Both of us.

“It looks like you saved my life,” said Winter.

Ina blinked.

“That’s what it looks like,” said Winter, bluntly. “From up here.” She climbed down off the rock, and stood in front of him, and looked at the mask in his hand. “Put it on more.”

“I don’t—“

“I know you don’t like it. I know what it looks like. I’ve got horns, Ina. I know.” She looked him in the eye, both of hers, the blue one and the pink one. “Put it on anyway. When it matters. Your fire’s twice as hot. I’d rather look like the bad guy and be alive.”

Ina looked at her for a long time. Then, slowly, something in his face eased.

“That,” he said, “is the most anyone’s ever said to me at once, Winter.”

“Don’t get used to it.”

“I won’t.”

She turned round. There was one Rimehound left, the Alpha, the big one with the scar, coming back round the edge of the rock with its head low. She put an arrow on the string without thinking, and pulled. And she thought, the way she’d been thinking every time she shot since floor 1: stay where you are.

The arrow hit the Alpha in the shoulder. The frost on its tip didn’t melt. It spread. Out from the arrow across the white fur in a fast white bloom, over its shoulder and down its leg and into the snow under its feet. And the Alpha stopped. Not dead. Just stopped, frozen to the ground, its leg locked in ice, snarling.

“Huh,” said Winter.

Ina, beside her, put the mask back on and finished it.

On the twentieth morning, there was a new line on Drew’s board.

It was right at the top, above the map, in big careful capitals. Two people had written it; Winter could see that. Two hands, one after the other, taking turns.

ALL PARTIES — FLOOR 3 — DAY 22. TOGETHER. NOBODY GOES UP EARLY. NOBODY GOES ON PAST 3. — DREW & DESTON

Winter stood in front of it for a long time.

Nobody goes on past 3.

She looked across the hall. Deston was by the fire with his hand on his pocket. Drew was beside him with a stub of chalk, looking at the board as if he’d built a bridge. Hyvred was at the big table, and when Winter caught her eye, Hyvred nodded once, very small. First night. He promised.

And Gabe was sitting by the window in the wolf coat, looking up the valley toward the pass. He didn’t know any of it. Not the trail, not the light on the collar of his own coat, not whatever was in Deston’s pocket. Not yet.

He’d know on floor 3. The first night. Winter would make sure.

She wasn’t going to let anybody be the last to know. Not this time.
