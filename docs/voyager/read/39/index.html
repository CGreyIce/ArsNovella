Chapter 39

Thirty-Five Voices

3,206 words · 14 min read

On the morning of the twenty-second day, thirty-five people stood on the shore of a frozen lake at the top of floor 2. For the first time since the white room, Deston could see all of them at once.

He counted. He couldn’t help it. He’d counted in the white room, going round the ring of circles, and he’d counted every night since in the rankings. He counted now on the shore of the Mere in the cold: seven parties, standing in a long ragged line along the edge of the ice in their furs, breath smoking.

Thirty-five.

Gabe in the wolf coat. Hana with an iron rose in her crown. Yan with a white snake round his neck and his hands tucked into Salty’s iron box. Aleaka with a wolf pup at her heels. Hyvred, horns and crimson streaks, her left eye locking and unlocking on the lake. Thea, very quiet, at the end of her party, reading the ice. Chris with his notebook. Drew with a slate under his arm, a small one he’d bought off the trapper, because the big one wouldn’t come off the wall.

All of them. Every single one.

Out in the middle of the Mere, the Old Stag stood with its head up and its icicles chiming, waiting.

“Right,” said Drew. He didn’t raise his voice. He never did. He just said it, and thirty-four people turned to look at him. “Same as on the slate. John, lights. Everyone else, you know where you’re standing.” He looked down the long line of them. “Nobody steps on a window.”

“Nobody steps on a window,” thirty-four people said back, ragged, half laughing.

John crouched on the shore and sent his lights down into the ice, and the Mere lit up in patches, blue-white, like a dark house full of windows.

It took eleven minutes.

Deston had been in the Fold with five people and it had taken them most of a morning. Xav’s party had taken the Stag with five and it had nearly drowned Gabe. Thirty-five against one was something else.

Afterwards he couldn’t remember most of it as a fight. He remembered it as a kind of noise. The Stag came across the ice at Salty, and Salty took it on the flat of his greatsword. Hana caught its eye and said something soft, and its head turned for two seconds. Winter’s arrows came off the string with frost on their tips and froze its forelegs to the ice where it stood. Ina had the mask on, and his white fire was steaming the lake. Tiger was everywhere at once. When it stamped and the line of ice-spikes came up, Drew stepped into the line with his buckler and turned it, the way a door turns, and the spikes went off sideways into nothing. And when it bellowed and the whiteout came down, Chi’s voice came out of the white, slow and calm: it’s okay. It’s just snow. Look at the windows. Thirty-five people looked at the windows.

The Stag went through the biggest one in the middle. Gabe and Salty both got hold of its antlers this time. Gabe held its head out of the water while Rui Xiang did the rest. Deston saw Gabe’s face while he did it. He didn’t say anything about it afterwards.

There was a silence on the Mere. Then everybody started shouting at once.

They climbed the stair together. All thirty-five, in single file, a hundred and sixty steps up into the dark.

Deston didn’t know who started counting. He thought it might have been Azac, ahead of him, in his storytelling voice: one. Then somebody else joined in. Then everybody. Thirty-five voices in a narrow stone stairwell, chanting numbers in the dark, like a crowd at a match, the sound bouncing off the walls and coming back.

Fifty-one. Fifty-two.

He counted with them, and listened.

He knew all of these voices. Not all of them well. Some of them he’d only learned in the last three weeks. But he knew them now. Calvin’s, too loud. Rachael’s, dry and deadpan, counting as if it were beneath her and doing it anyway. Gabe’s, ahead of him, the biggest in the stairwell. Newish’s, complaining between numbers. Yan’s, deep and slow, a beat behind everyone else.

Thirty-five voices.

And somewhere in the middle of the count, round about ninety, Deston felt it. The gap. The place in the sound where one more voice should have been, and wasn’t. He didn’t know how he knew it. You can’t hear a voice that isn’t there. But the chant had a shape, the way a choir does. Back in the second row of a school choir, with Gabe beside him and somebody else on his other side, he’d learned that you could hear when the shape had a hole in it.

Thirty-six. It should have been thirty-six.

He kept counting. He didn’t say anything.

A hundred and fifty-nine. A hundred and sixty.

“A hundred and sixty,” said Gabe, at the top, with his hand on the door. He was quiet for a moment. “Huh,” he said. And opened it.

Floor 3 was hot.

That was the first thing, after the snow: a wall of wet heat that came through the door like a hand pressed over your face, smelling of green rot and sweet flowers and still water. Thirty-five people came out onto the top of a low hill above a flat brown-green world of reeds and black channels. They stood there sweating in their furs, and somebody laughed, and somebody swore, and Calvin said oh my God it’s a swamp, it’s an actual swamp.

And then the windows came.

All of them, at once, in the corner of thirty-five eyes. Not small and plain this time. Bigger. Gold at the edges, the way the first-clear windows were. In no hurry.

The hill went quiet.

Deston read it twice. Then he looked at his own window, at the line under his name. Lv 10, held. Everyone on this hill was ten. Every single one of them. They’d been at the wall for days, banking experience, waiting for trials they hadn’t done yet.

Eighteen.

He felt his hand go to his shirt pocket. He let it.

“Eighteen,” said Calvin. He was looking at the window with his mouth open. “That’s eight levels. That’s—we’re ten. That’s a jump of eight levels in one floor. One, two, three, they were all like two or three levels each, and then four is—“

“The tutorial ends,” said Chris, very quietly, from behind his notebook. “That’s what it’s saying. Floors one to three were the tutorial. The Threshold ends here. Four’s where it stops being kind.”

Nobody said anything.

Deston looked down the hill at the Fen. Then he looked along the line of thirty-four people standing on the hilltop in the heat, reading the same window. He found Gabe. Gabe was looking at him already.

Be careful, said a card in Deston’s pocket. Train longer on the 3rd floor.

Not train. Train longer. Somebody had known, before any of them had woken up, that there’d be a number on this hill, and what it would be, and what it would mean.

“Reedmoor,” said Drew, beside him, quietly. He was looking at Deston’s hand on his pocket. “There’s a town. Let’s get everybody under a roof and get them fed. Then we talk.”

“Yeah,” said Deston. “Then we talk.”

Reedmoor stood on stilts in the middle of the Fen. A village of reed houses joined by walkways of planks and rope, all of it raised up over the black water, with eel-traps hanging off every railing and smoke going up from a hundred little cookfires. The people who lived there were small and brown and quiet, and they watched thirty-five Climbers come in across the long plank causeway from the shore with an expression Deston was starting to recognise from every floor. Interested. Not surprised.

The woman who met them at the end of the causeway was the eel-woman. That was how she introduced herself. She ran the smoking hall, a long low building at the heart of the village where everybody ate, and she’d find room for them if they didn’t mind the smell.

“Thirty-five,” she said, counting them as they came off the planks, the way Mother Crane had counted them on the first night. “That’s a lot of you.”

“There’s a lot of us,” said Deston.

“Hm.” She looked them over. Horns and ears and a tree and a snake and a wolf pup. “You’ll be here a while, then. They always stay a while, at the end of the Threshold.” She turned and started back along the walkway. “Mind the gaps in the planks. There’s mirelings.”

It was Hyvred who asked about the tower.

She’d been looking out across the Fen since they came down off the hill, her left eye locked on something in the far distance. Deston had been watching her do it. Out in the middle of the reeds and the black water, so far off it was almost nothing, there was a light. Small. Steady. Amber. Not like the low yellow cookfires of Reedmoor. Warmer. Like a lamp in a window on a winter night.

“That light,” said Hyvred, to the eel-woman. “In the middle. What is it?”

The eel-woman stopped on the walkway and looked where Hyvred was pointing. And her quiet brown face did something Deston didn’t expect. It softened.

“That’s the old tower,” she said. “The drowned one. In the middle of the Fen, where the water’s deepest. Nobody goes there. You can’t get there without walking through the wisps.” She paused. “Nine nights back, somebody did.”

Nobody said anything.

“We were all out on the platforms,” said the eel-woman. “It was dusk. The wisps were out, the way they always are, all over the Fen. Hundreds of them. And then, all at once, they went out.” She snapped her fingers, softly. “Like that. Every one. From the middle outwards, in a wave, like somebody blowing out a room full of candles. And there was that light on the tower instead. Where there’s never been a light in my life, nor my mother’s.” She looked back at it. “It’s been burning ever since. Never goes out. And the wisps came back, after a few days, but they’re quieter now. They don’t come so close.” She shook her head. “We call it the night the lights went out. The children won’t stop talking about it.”

“Who was it?” said Thea. Very quietly.

The eel-woman looked at her.

“Nobody saw,” she said. “Nobody here. Only the light.” She turned back to the smoking hall. “Mind the gaps.”

Deston looked at Hyvred. Hyvred was standing very still on the walkway with her left eye locked on the drowned tower. And Deston knew, the way he’d known in the stairwell about the gap in the count, that she could see something across that black water nobody else could.

A trail. Warm. Amber. Going out into the Fen, through the reeds, all the way to the tower. And back.

Hyvred turned her head and looked at him.

Then, past him, at Gabe.

Then, very deliberately, at Winter. Winter was standing at the end of her party with her bow and her mismatched eyes, and she looked back at Hyvred and nodded once.

First night, said that nod. You promised.

Deston let out a long breath.

“Okay,” he said, to nobody. “Okay.”

Supper in the smoking hall was smoked eel and a kind of flat bread made from reed-root, and it was the strangest meal Deston had ever eaten. He ate all of it.

Thirty-five people at long tables under a roof of reeds, with smoke drifting up through a hole in the middle and the windows open to the Fen. It was loud. It was the loudest it had been since the first night at the Copper Spoon, when everybody had been frightened and pretending not to be. This was different. This was people who’d been apart for three weeks on two floors, finally in one room, all talking at once.

Deston watched it, the way he always watched rooms.

Tiger and Newish racing each other along the walkway outside, shrieking, until the eel-woman came out and shouted at them about the gaps in the planks. Aleaka at the end of a bench with Sleet in her lap, refusing to go anywhere near the open windows, because Sleet had looked at the black water and decided it was the worst thing she’d ever seen. Chris and Drew with their heads together over the little slate, already drawing a map of the Fen from what the eel-woman had told them.

And PenguChi’s penguin.

Waddles had taken one look at the Fen through the smoking-hall window and gone very still. Then, before anybody could stop him, he’d waddled to the edge of the platform, looked down at the black channel below, and dived in. A small yellow splash. Chi was on her feet with a sound Deston had never heard her make, halfway to the window. And then Waddles came back up out of the black water, scrambled up a stilt onto the walkway, and stood there dripping and enormously pleased with himself. In his beak was an eel half as long as he was.

The eel-woman, who’d come in to see what the noise was, stood in the doorway and looked at the penguin for a long time.

“That bird,” she said finally, with deep respect, “can work.”

The smoking hall fell about laughing. Chi sat back down with her hand over her heart, and Waddles brought her the eel and laid it at her feet like a gift. Deston sat at the long table in the smoke and the heat and laughed until his ribs hurt, for the first time in he couldn’t remember how long.

Thirty-five. All in one room. Whatever came tomorrow, they’d had tonight.

He found Gabe after supper, at the end of one of the walkways, sitting on the edge with his boots hanging over the black water and the wolf coat folded beside him because it was too hot to wear. He was looking out at the amber light in the middle of the Fen.

Deston sat down beside him. Hyvred came and stood behind them, with her arms wrapped round herself. Winter stood further back along the planks, where she could see, and didn’t come closer.

Gabe looked round at the three of them, and his face changed.

“Okay,” he said. “What.”

Deston didn’t make him wait. He’d made him wait long enough.

“Hyvred can see where he’s been,” he said.

Gabe went very still.

“His light,” said Deston. “It leaves a trail. Like footprints. Nobody else can see it, only her. Since floor 1. She found where he sat at the Spoon, where he slept in the ascension room. And on floor 2, the whole way. From the stair, east along the trees to the pine. Up the mountain to the tower. Over the pass, down to the lake.” He looked out at the Fen. “And now. Out there. Across the water, to that.” He nodded at the amber light. “And back.”

Gabe didn’t say anything. He was looking at Hyvred.

“It’s on your coat,” said Hyvred. Her voice was very small for Hyvred. “At the collar. Where his hands were, when he folded it.”

Gabe looked down at the coat beside him. He didn’t touch it.

“She told me on the twelfth day,” said Deston. “Ten days ago. By whisper. And I asked her not to tell you.” He made himself keep looking at Gabe. “I asked her not to tell you, Gabe, because I thought you’d go after him. Up the trail. On your own. That night. And I didn’t think you’d come back.”

Gabe was quiet for a long time.

The amber light burned out in the middle of the Fen, steady, small. The wisps were coming out over the water now, pale and quiet, keeping their distance.

“You were right,” said Gabe finally.

Deston looked at him.

“I’d have gone,” said Gabe. “That night. On the twelfth. I’d have been up the stair and along the trees and up that mountain before you’d finished telling me.” He let out a long breath. “I’d have gone after him, and I’d have got myself killed on the pass in the dark, and he’d have had to come back down and find me. And he’d never have forgiven himself.” He looked at the coat. “You were right.”

“I’m sorry,” said Hyvred. “I’m so sorry, Gabe. I hated it. Every day.”

Gabe looked up at her. And something in his face, something that had been tight since the white room, eased.

“You kept it,” he said. “For ten days. You, Hyv. You can’t keep a secret for ten minutes.” He almost smiled. “That must have been horrible.”

Hyvred burst into tears. Gabe got up and put his arms round her, which made it worse, and she cried into the shoulder of his shirt on the end of a walkway over black water. And Deston sat there with his boots over the edge, and looked out at the amber light, and felt something heavy in his chest come down off a shelf it had been sitting on for ten days.

Further back along the planks, Winter turned round and went quietly back to the smoking hall. Promise kept.

Later, when Hyvred had gone, Gabe sat back down on the edge of the walkway beside him.

They sat there together, the way they’d sat on the edge of things together since they were kids. Neither of them said anything for a while.

“And the pocket,” said Gabe.

Deston didn’t pretend not to know.

“What else is in it, Dest? Because it’s not just the note. I’ve watched you touch it every day for three weeks. It’s something you think’s going to happen. Something bad.” He looked at Deston. “Up there.”

Deston looked at the window still hanging faintly at the edge of his vision. Floor 4 · recommended level: 18.

“Tomorrow,” he said. “Everyone. All of us, in the smoking hall. I’ll show them all of it.” He put his hand flat on his pocket. “I’m not keeping anything else, Gabe. Not from you. Not from anyone. After tomorrow, that’s it.”

Gabe looked at him for a long moment.

“Okay,” he said.

They sat on the end of the walkway until the cookfires of Reedmoor went down to red, looking at the light in the middle of the Fen.
