Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 1: Thirty-Six Circles

Chapter 1

Thirty-Six

3,566 words · 16 min read

He woke up whole.

That was the first thing, before the light or the floor or the silence. Ice lay on his back with his eyes shut and waited for the hole in his side to start hurting again, and it didn’t. He waited for his left arm, the bag of gravel that had been his shield arm, and it lay beside him, ordinary and warm and attached. He closed his left hand. All five fingers answered.

He opened his eyes.

White.

Not the white of the throne room’s dead light, or the white of a window. Just white, plain and soft, with no source he could find. A ceiling somewhere so far above him that it might as well not have been there. A floor under his back, cool and smooth like the inside of a shell.

He knew this room. He’d woken up in it once before, ten years ago, with a headache and thirty-five friends and absolutely no idea what was happening.

“Hi,” said a voice inside his head.

He didn’t move. He didn’t trust himself to.

“It’s me,” the voice said. “I’m still me. You can breathe. Your lungs work now. I checked.”

Ice breathed. It went all the way down and came back up without catching on anything. His throat did something complicated.

“Alea,” he said. It came out as a croak.

Something settled on his chest, light as a folded towel. When he lifted his head, the fox was sitting on his sternum, made of the same teal and white light as before, her tail curled neatly round her feet. She looked exactly the same. He hadn’t been sure she would.

“We’re back,” she said. “I didn’t know if I’d make it through. I did. You did too. Your hair’s doing the thing.”

He reached up. His fringe was in his eyes, the way it always was, black with the teal streak on the right that had been on his avatar since the day he’d drawn it. He’d never changed it in ten years. Hair in the Spire didn’t grow unless you asked it to, and he’d never asked.

“Did we get younger,” he said, “or did I get shorter?”

“Younger. You’re twenty-six. It’s the first minute.”

[ Ice · Lv 1 · Tier 1 · Class: — ]

The window opened in the air over his face, polite and centred and in no hurry, as if it hadn’t been ten years. A second one opened beside it. That one was new. Or rather it was old, and it had never before been allowed to open on the first day.

[ ALEA · Autonomous Leveling and Evolutionary Assistant
Status: carried over
Logs: 3,652 days
]

He read the last line three times. Three thousand, six hundred and fifty-two days. Every one of them in a file somewhere behind those white pinprick eyes: every map, every boss, every road, every name. Every where.

“You kept it,” he said.

“I keep everything,” Alea said. “You know that. You used to complain about it.” Her ears tipped forward. “Ice. We don’t have long. Sit up and look.”

He sat up.

The room was round. He’d forgotten that, or he’d remembered it wrong: he’d always pictured it as a hall, because that was how it had felt the first time, full of people shouting. But it was round, and not that big, and there were thirty-six circles of faint light on the floor in a ring around the middle, each a little bigger than a person.

There was a person in every circle but his.

They were all asleep. Lying on their backs or curled on their sides, the way people fall asleep when they don’t mean to. Some of them were in bodies they’d never had on Earth, horns and tails and ears and a tree’s bark skin, and some of them were just themselves, looking very small and very ordinary on that white floor. Faint lines ran from each circle toward the centre of the room, where nothing at all stood.

On the far side of the ring, an arch was cut into the white wall. It was plain and round-topped and not very large. Through it he could see grass, green and bright, and a slope of hillside going down.

The arch was open.

“Sixty-one seconds,” Alea said. “That’s how long it’s open. In the first run it was open for sixty-one seconds while everybody was asleep, and then it was a wall for two hours, and nobody ever knew. I didn’t know either. I was a ball of light that said Welcome, Player. But I logged it.” Her tail flicked. “Their systems are booting. When the boot finishes, they wake up, the arch closes, and the tutorial starts. Yours didn’t need to boot, because I didn’t stop. That’s why you’re up. That’s your minute.”

“How much is left?”

“Fifty-two.”

He’d planned this. He’d planned it the whole long white fall between the throne room and here, in whatever amount of time that had been, with Alea’s voice going through it with him step by step like the night before a raid. Out of the arch inside the first minute. Hearthstead before anyone else. The class hall, the sword, the long way round the east side of the valley to the old chapel before nightfall. Floor 1’s boss, alone, under level five, before the second day began.

Everything they’d found too late the first time, he’d take first. Every first clear the Spire handed out for being fast and alone and outmatched, he’d take, and he’d use it to stay ahead of them. Not to carry them. You couldn’t carry thirty-five people up a hundred floors, he’d learned that. But you could go first. You could see what was waiting on each floor before they walked into it.

And if he stayed, the light would come down in two hours and pick nine heads, and one of them would be him. And then he’d be standing in the middle of all of them with ten years in his head, and the first time Calvin said I’ll scout ahead, he’d open his mouth, and it would all come out, and they’d look at him like—

He didn’t know how they’d look at him. That was the problem.

“Forty-five,” said Alea.

He stood up. His legs were new and strong and twenty-six years old, and he nearly fell over anyway.

Gabe was on his left.

That was where he’d been last time too, he remembered now: Gabe on the left and Deston on the right, as if even the white room knew which two people he’d be lost without. Gabe slept on his side with his arm under his head and his mouth slightly open, a big man in a crumpled shirt, looking about fifteen. There was no shield. There was no forge-burn up the inside of his forearm, no scar through his eyebrow from the sixty-first floor. There was no shouting at nothing behind twenty metres of iron.

Ice opened his mouth to say his name.

He shut it again.

He doesn’t know, he told himself. He’s never stood at a gate. He’s never made you a shield. He’s twenty-four and he went to sleep in Singapore with his phone on his chest, and that’s all.

He’d have to do that every time, he realised. Every time he looked at one of them, he’d have to stop and put the ten years back in their box before he said a word. Otherwise he’d talk to the Gabe in his head, and the one on the floor would have no idea what he was talking about.

“Forty,” said Alea, very gently.

Deston was on his right, flat on his back, long legs crossed at the ankle, as tidy asleep as he was awake. He had a white card in his right hand. They all did; Ice could see the little pale rectangles in thirty-five sleeping hands around the ring. Blank on both sides. In a minute each one would have a name on the front.

Ice looked down at his own hand. There was a card in it too. He’d been holding it since he woke and hadn’t noticed.

His was still blank. It was going to stay blank, he suspected. Whatever wrote the names on the cards was the same thing that ran the boot, and he was outside the boot now.

He put it in his vest pocket, behind the zip.

“The cards take a fingertip,” Alea said. “For the first minute. It’s how they’re meant to take your name, if you’re awake to sign it. Nobody ever is.”

He crouched by Deston and slid the card out of his hand. Deston didn’t stir. He’d slept like that at every sleepover since they were thirteen: like a man who’d been unplugged.

Ice turned the card over. He’d thought about this too, on the way down. He’d written and rewritten it in his head a hundred times. Rachael and Dennis die on floor 4. Calvin loses his arm. Don’t let him scout. Don’t let anyone go up before Lv 15. Trust me, it’s Declan, I’ve done this before—

And every version ended with Deston reading it, and then looking up, and walking straight out of the arch to come and find him. Deston would. He’d organise all thirty-five of them into search parties inside ten minutes. And nobody would train, and nobody would wait, and they’d go up to the fourth floor at level eight looking for him.

He put his fingertip on the back of the card. A thin line of light followed it, and where it passed, the light sank into the card and dried to ink.

be careful, train longer on the 3rd floor

He stopped. He looked at it.

- D.

The D was the most he could give him. Deston started with a D too. So did Dennis, and Drew. Let him wonder. Let him wonder hard enough to listen.

He put the card back under Deston’s hand, words down, and folded the fingers over it the way they’d been.

“Thirty,” said Alea.

He stood. He looked across the ring.

Xav was two circles down, curled up small with his fists under his chin, the way he slept on buses. Then Rui Xiang, then Azac, big and calm and snoring very slightly, exactly as he snored in every voice call he’d ever fallen asleep in. Then Rachael, Dennis and Calvin in a row, because of course they were. Rachael had a frown on even in her sleep. Dennis had somehow ended up lying artfully. Calvin was sprawled flat out with both arms flung wide over his head.

Both arms.

Ice looked at that for longer than he had time for.

“Twenty-five.”

He made himself go round the ring the long way, toward the arch, and he made himself not stop. Yan, all silver-white hair and black-and-crimson robes, with a white snake coiled on his chest. Salty with an arm across Wendy’s shoulders. Maigo curled up like a seed in an orange hoodie, sunglasses on. Hyvred with her golden horns and her glasses pushed up. Bee. Tiger. Kei, with a small green snake asleep on his wrist. Winter and Ina, horns catching the light. Chris, who was older than all of them and somehow slept like it.

Thea.

He did stop, then. He didn’t decide to. His feet just did it.

She was on her side with one hand under her cheek. Her ponytail was still tied. Black hair, streaks of pink and blue, a little star clip in her bangs. The jersey she wore like a dress had ridden up over one knee. Her face was completely smooth. She looked like someone who’d never stepped in front of anything in her life.

She hadn’t. Not yet. Not in this world. That was the whole point.

“Ice,” Alea said. “Twenty.”

“Yeah,” he said. He didn’t move.

He’d liked her once, on Earth, years before any of this. She’d known it, and it had passed, the way those things pass, and they’d stayed friends and talked most nights. And then ten years in here, and it had come back so slowly he hadn’t noticed until it was the biggest thing in the room. He’d never said anything. She’d never said anything. There was going to be time.

She’d said something, at the end. He still didn’t know what.

“Fifteen.”

He went.

The light came up behind him at the ten-second mark.

It started in the circles: a thin brightness under each sleeping body, then a column of soft white light rising out of every ring, up and up toward a ceiling that wasn’t there. Thirty-five pillars of it. The systems were coming online, the boot reaching its last stage. In a moment the pillars would fold down into thirty-five little balls of light, and each of them would say Welcome, Player, and the white room would start being loud.

Ice was nearly at the arch when he heard someone sit up.

He turned before he could stop himself.

Gabe was awake.

Of course he was. Gabe had always been a light sleeper; Gabe had woken up for every stupid dawn raid they’d ever planned and then complained about it for three hours. He’d pushed himself up on one elbow in his circle on the far side of the ring, next to the empty one, half in and half out of his own rising pillar of light, and he was blinking, and he was looking straight across the ring.

At Ice.

Ice watched him see it. The confusion, first. The white room, the light, the faint what. Then his eyes landed on Ice and stopped, and Ice watched them go wide.

“Dec—“ Gabe said.

Ice paused.

It was the habit he’d made thirty seconds ago, and it was already there, already in his throat: the stop, the putting-away. He had something he wanted to say back. He didn’t know what it was. Hi or sorry or stay away from the gate.

The light rose. Gabe’s pillar went up all the way and filled, and Gabe vanished behind a wall of white.

“Go,” said Alea.

Ice turned. Further round the ring, Yan had opened his eyes too. Yan always woke slowly, like a man surfacing from deep water, and his pale cyan eyes were still half-lidded. He’d turned his head. He was looking past the snake on his chest, through the gap between two rising pillars, at the figure by the arch.

Then Suzu lifted her head. The white snake rose off Yan’s chest in one smooth curve, uncoiling to full length, and laid herself across his face, deliberately, like a curtain drawn across a window. Ice heard Yan make a muffled, outraged noise.

He almost laughed. He almost said Yan.

Five metres from the arch, someone else sat bolt upright.

Alice.

She came out of sleep the way she did everything: all at once and already composed, her lavender hair falling straight, the little crown sitting crooked on her head. Her one visible eye, violet, found him immediately. He was three metres away. Close enough to see her small fang as her mouth opened.

He knew what she was seeing. A black tactical vest with teal tabs over a white shirt with the sleeves rolled up. Black hair, a teal streak. The boy from the little drawings he’d put on every emote and profile picture he’d ever made, and who had once spent an entire stream of hers drawing increasingly stupid cats in her chat to make her laugh. He watched her know him.

Then Milli, asleep beside her, jerked awake and grabbed her arm. “Honey?” he said, frightened, and Alice turned her head toward him without thinking, the way you do for the person you married. And the moment broke, and when she turned back, the light from Milli’s pillar was between them.

“Five,” said Alea.

Ice stepped through the arch.

The air on the other side was warm and smelled of cut grass.

He took three steps down the slope before he let himself look back. The arch stood in a low, grassy bank like the mouth of an old cellar. As he watched, the opening clouded white from the edges inward, like breath on glass, and then it wasn’t an arch any more. It was just a curved stone set into the hillside, the kind of thing a farmer would build his wall around.

He could still hear them, faintly, through it. Thirty-five voices waking up. Then one, louder than the others, a big voice he’d know through any wall in the world, shouting something that might have been his name.

He stood and listened until it was gone.

Then he turned round.

Floor 1 of the Spire looked exactly as he remembered, which was the strange part. A long green valley running down between low hills. Stands of birch, a stream, the silver line of a road. Fields in patches of yellow and green, and far down at the bottom of the valley a town with red roofs and a white tower, with smoke going up straight from a dozen chimneys: Hearthstead, where everybody started. A flock of something that might have been sheep, and might, on this floor, have been considerably worse than sheep.

And over all of it, a sky that wasn’t a sky. It was blue and deep, with clouds moving across it slowly, and a warm sun hanging in it, a little high, a little to the east. It took most people a week to notice that the sun went the same way every day on exactly the same track, like something on a rail. It took most people a month to notice that the clouds never touched the hills at the edges. And it had taken all thirty-six of them, the first time, nearly a year to stop pretending they hadn’t noticed either.

It was a ceiling. A ceiling so high and so well made that it had weather.

Ice sat down on the grass.

He hadn’t meant to. His legs had done it, the way they had in the throne room. He sat on a hillside on floor 1 with his arms around his knees and looked at the valley, at Hearthstead, at the smoke. Everything in it was ten years younger than it should have been. And he started to shake, very quietly, all the way down.

Alea sat down beside his boot. She didn’t say anything. She’d learned that too.

After a while, when the shaking had mostly stopped, he said, “Gabe saw me.”

“Yes.”

“And Yan.”

“Yes. Suzu got in the way. I think she did it on purpose.” A pause. “I don’t know why. I can’t read her. I never could.”

“And Alice.”

“Alice saw you properly. She knew you.”

“Yeah.” He rubbed his face with both hands. “I know. I saw her know me.”

“Does it matter?”

He thought about it honestly, because she’d asked honestly. “Three of thirty-five,” he said. “They’ll tell the others. The others will say they were dreaming. Their systems will say the room only had thirty-five people in it. That’s what they said the first time, when we counted. There are thirty-five Players. They’ll believe the systems over each other by tonight.” He swallowed. “Gabe won’t. Gabe’ll never believe it. He’ll be looking for me on every floor.”

“He was always going to look for you,” Alea said. “Even if you’d stayed. He’d have looked for you inside the same party.”

That was true. He laughed, a little wetly. “Yeah.”

[ Quest available: Climb. ]

The window opened in front of him, very small and very plain, the way it had the first time.

He looked at it. Ten years ago he’d looked at it with thirty-five other people crowded round and Calvin asking it directly, out loud, climb what, though?, and everybody laughing a bit too hard, because they were frightened.

Now he knew exactly what. He knew every one of the hundred floors above that painted sky, and roughly what was waiting on each, and roughly where all thirty-five of them had died.

“Ten years,” Alea said quietly beside him. “Again.”

“Again,” Ice said.

He accepted the quest. He stood up. He brushed the grass off the back of his trousers and checked the straps on his thighs out of habit, though there was nothing in them yet. Then he looked down the valley at the red roofs of Hearthstead, and at the white tower where the class hall was, and the sun, which said it was a little after the start of the morning.

“Fastest route to the hall?”

“The stream path, then the north road. Forty minutes if you run. There’s a goat on the stream path that will fight you.”

“I remember the goat.”

“It’s level two.”

“I remember the goat, Alea.”

“I’m just saying. You’re level one, and you don’t have a weapon, and you’re crying.”

“I’m not crying,” said Ice, who was. He wiped his face on his sleeve. “It’s hay fever. It’s a grass floor. Trust.”

He started to run.
