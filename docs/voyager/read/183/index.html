Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 5: Everyone Comes Back Down

Chapter 183

Tomorrow Night

4,452 words · 19 min read

John had spent his whole life off to one side, and he’d never minded.

That was the thing people got wrong about him. They saw him in the corner of a room, or at the end of a table, or at the edge of a photo with half his face cut off, and they thought he was waiting to be let in. He wasn’t. He’d been at the edge of things since he was small and he’d found out early that the edge was where you could see everything. Everybody in the middle of a room was looking at one person. From the corner, you could see all of them.

Best seat in the house, he’d said once. In a small room in Singapore, a long time ago, in the corner of a screen. Dec’s first-year streams. Dec in the middle, talking, with the camera on his face, and John off to one side on a beanbag, a shoulder and half a face and a voice saying the wrong thing at the wrong moment. Chat had called him the cameraman. He’d never once touched the camera.

Then Dec had moved to Malacca and the Singapore lot had drifted, the way people do, and nobody said anything, and John had gone on being off to one side of other things.

And then a white room. And on the first day a window had said Skill learned, and a small white light had come out of his palm and floated up, and he’d found out that he could send a little bit of himself anywhere he liked, and see what it saw. The corner of the room, in the air. Anywhere.

It was still the best thing that had ever happened to him.

They worked out the rope problem on the second day.

Not quickly. Dennis had said that’s thirty-seven on the church steps and Merle had laughed and said then somebody’ll have to work it out, and for a day and a half everybody had tried.

Malcolm said he’d stand out. “Backup,” said Malcolm. “Somebody should be backup. In case somebody lets go.” Nobody let him.

Dec said he’d stand out. He said it quietly, at breakfast, the way he said things now, and he didn’t even finish the sentence before nine people said no and Gabe said shut up, Dec and Thea, without looking up from her porridge, said “Absolutely not,” in a voice that made Calvin put his spoon down.

Dec didn’t say it again.

Merle said the town would stand out. “It’s yours,” she said. “You rang it first.” And then the smith came in, and the Dawley boy, and Mrs Penn with her knee, and old Hobbes who couldn’t hear, and the caretaker with an apple, and stood in the door of the inn, and said they’d been practising for a month and they weren’t standing out of anything, thank you very much, and Mrs Penn told everybody about her knee.

So there were seventy-odd people who wanted to be on thirty-six ropes.

It was Rui Xiang who worked it out. Quietly. In the afternoon, in the ringing chamber, standing in the middle of the circle of ropes with their thirty-six new grips, looking at them. He’d been looking for an hour. Rui Xiang could look at a thing for an hour and see something nobody else would.

“Two hands,” said Rui Xiang.

Everybody looked at him.

“On a rope,” said Rui Xiang. “Not one person. Two.” He took hold of the nearest one, the red grip, and held it, and stood a little to one side. “One in front. One behind. On the same rope. Both pulling.” He looked at Merle. “Nobody pulls alone.”

Merle looked at him for a long moment.

“That’s the stupidest thing I’ve ever heard,” said Merle. “It’ll never work. You’ll pull against each other.”

“Not if somebody counts,” said Rui Xiang.

They tried it.

It didn’t work. Not the first time. Not the fifth. The smith pulled before Hako. Mrs Penn let go to tell Chi about her knee. Old Hobbes couldn’t hear Newish and pulled on two. It was the worst noise John had ever heard, thirty-six bells going off one after another all round the belfry like somebody falling down a staircase full of saucepans.

But on the eleventh try, at dusk, it nearly did.

Thirty-four bells at once. And two late. And the whole tower hummed, for a second, the way it had hummed on the night, a deep round sound under everything. The town people on the ropes stopped and looked at each other.

“Oh,” said Mrs Penn.

“Again,” said Newish.

On Thursday night the whole of Evermorrow came to the square.

John watched them come. From the top of the clocktower, from the open belfry, from a mote. He’d put three up there at dusk: one on the great hour bell in the middle, one on the beam with the old carved words on it, When every bell is rung at once, the Hour may be struck, and one out on the ledge of the belfry looking down at the square.

He was sitting in the ringing chamber. On the floor. In the middle.

That was where they’d put him. He hadn’t asked. When they worked out the two hands, and counted up, and there were thirty-six climbers and thirty-six from the town and Merle, it came out at seventy-three, and seventy-two hands went on thirty-six ropes, two to each, and there was one over.

John had said “Me.”

Nobody had argued. They’d known him long enough. He’d said “me” and then “I’ll do the light”, and Xav had looked at him and nodded, and that was that.

“Somebody has to see it,” John had said. “From the outside. Otherwise nobody will.”

So he sat in the middle of the circle, on the boards, cross-legged, with his back to nothing and the ropes all round him in a ring. Where a bedroll had been once. A bedroll with flour on it, and a cloak rolled up for a pillow. Dec’s. For twenty-six nights, trying to ring thirty-six bells on his own.

John had found it, the first time. Up here with Calvin and Hyvred and Chris and Thea and Drew. Hyvred had said he’s been up here every night for twenty-six days. And Thea had knelt down and touched the rolled cloak where his head would go.

John sat where it had been.

He let a mote go up off his palm and hang in the air over the circle. Not bright. Just enough. A little white light in the dark of the ringing chamber, the way he’d lit the stair on the night, like the bulbs over a night market.

And he looked at all of them.

That was the thing about the middle, it turned out. He’d never sat in it before. He’d been off to one side all his life. And from the middle of a circle, you could see everybody too. All the way round. Every face. Except that from the middle they could all see you.

He found he didn’t mind that either.

Seventy-two people. Thirty-six ropes. Two to each. A climber in front and somebody from the town behind, the town’s hands on the grip above the climber’s, the way you’d hold a child’s hands on an oar.

Higu on the first rope, with the caretaker behind her, apple in his pocket.

Calvin, with the Dawley boy, who was about twelve and had named Tomorrow Night and was so excited he couldn’t stand still.

Hako, with the smith, a huge woman with burn-scarred forearms who looked at Hako’s spear-hand on the rope and said you’ll do.

Rachael, in her tanner’s gloves, with Mrs Penn. Mrs Penn was telling Rachael about her knee. Rachael was listening. Properly. John could see her listening.

Dennis with the cheesemonger, who’d come up the tower at the last minute in her white cap and said I’m not having him on a rope with anybody else, he’ll talk them down.

Salty with old Hobbes, who couldn’t hear, and who Salty had spent the whole afternoon teaching to watch Newish’s hand instead of listening for her voice.

Newish herself, on the rope by the stair, alone. The only one alone. Because she had to count, and she had to see everybody, and she’d said I’m not having somebody breathing on my neck while I count, thank you. Her hand was up. Ready.

Gabe. On the rope where he’d been the first time. With a big old farmer behind him John didn’t know, who’d come in from the edge of town that morning because he’d heard.

And next to Gabe, the rope that had been Dec’s.

The first time, the night of the Hour, there’d been a gap. Gabe had left it. One rope’s width, between his rope and the next, so that when Dec came to the last rope, he’d be between Gabe and nothing. John had seen it. He’d lit it. He thought he knew why Gabe had done it, and he thought it was the kindest thing he’d ever seen anybody do without saying anything.

There was no gap now.

Dec was on his rope. And Merle was in front of him.

She’d insisted. Merle in front, Dec behind, both their hands on the same red-and-white grip, his above hers. You had it last time, she’d said. I took it. Off you. You stood in the middle and got hit. She’d looked at him. This time you hold on. And I pull.

And on Dec’s other side, on the next rope, where the gap had been, Thea.

John hadn’t seen her take it. Nobody had. They’d all come up the stair and spread out round the ring to their ropes, and when they’d settled, Thea was there. On the rope next to Dec’s. The one that had been the gap’s. With the lantern on the floor by her feet, lit, and a woman from the bakery’s street behind her, and her hands on the grip.

She hadn’t said anything about it.

She didn’t look at Dec. She looked at Newish, the way everybody was meant to. But she was there. Between Dec and the next rope. Where there’d been nothing.

John watched Dec see her.

He’d lit Dec’s face before. On the night of the thousand words, in a corner of a square. You’re on camera. All of it, the purple jaw and the flour and the shadows. He didn’t light it now. He didn’t need to. The mote over the circle was enough. Dec turned his head, and saw who was on the rope where the gap had been, and his face didn’t do anything at all.

That was how John knew.

He’d sat in the corner of Dec’s streams for a year. He knew what Dec’s face did when it was being looked at. It did things. It talked. It laughed. It was always doing something, because somebody was always watching.

It went still when it wasn’t sure it could hold whatever it had.

At half past eleven the square was full.

John watched through the mote on the belfry ledge. The whole of Evermorrow. Everybody who wasn’t on a rope. Standing round the fountain, on the church steps, in the mouths of the streets, up on the walls. Children on shoulders. The butcher with the dog. Lamps in every window round the square. And nobody talking. All of them looking up at the clocktower.

The clock was ticking. John could hear it through the floor. The slow wooden tock. The great wheel turning, forward. He’d heard it on the night, a year ago, stop mid-tock on the third stroke while the Hour hung halfway out of the dial. It wasn’t going to stop tonight. There was nothing in the dial. Just a crack, and a brass strip across it.

Still.

He saw it on everybody’s face, in the ring. At half past eleven, and twenty to twelve, and quarter to. The same thing, on seventy-two faces. Not fear. Something a bit like it. The thing you feel at the top of a stair in the dark when you’ve counted the steps and you’re not sure about the last one.

Merle’s knuckles were white on the grip.

“It won’t,” said Dec. Behind her. Very quietly. John only heard it because the mote over the circle was close. “It won’t jump. It can’t. It’s gone.”

“I know,” said Merle.

“I’ll be right here.”

“I know,” said Merle. “You’re behind me.” And then, after a moment, quieter: “That’s the bit I’m holding on to.”

At five to twelve Newish lifted her hand higher.

The square below went absolutely silent. John felt it through the mote. A whole town holding its breath.

The clock’s first stroke came up through the floor.

BONG.

“ONE,” said Newish.

Seventy-two hands pulled.

And thirty-six bells rang at once.

John was in the middle of it. He’d been in the middle of it once before, on the night, with his hand on a rope like everybody else, and he’d felt it then: the tower sing. But he’d been pulling. He’d been looking at Newish and feeling the rope pull back as the bell swung and thinking about nothing but the next stroke.

He wasn’t pulling now. He was sitting in the middle of the floor with nothing in his hands.

And he heard it.

All thirty-six. At once. Not one after another. Not falling down a staircase. At once. Bronze and iron and the one that looked like silver and the great hour bell in the middle, all of them swinging up together and striking together and the sound coming down through the ceiling and the ropes and the walls like something enormous breathing out. It wasn’t loud. It was too big to be loud. It was a sound you were inside.

He sent his mind to the motes.

The one on the great bell. Up in the belfry, in the dark, with all thirty-six of them swinging round it, mouths up, mouths down, catching the moon on the downswing, every one of them at the same angle at the same moment, like a field of something all bending in the same wind.

The one on the beam. When every bell is rung at once.

The one on the ledge. And below it, the square. The whole of Evermorrow looking up. And on the first stroke, he saw it go through them. Like a wave. Every face in the square, at once, lifting.

BONG.

“TWO.”

Seventy-two hands.

Thirty-six bells.

He looked round the ring. From the middle. He’d never seen anything like it. Seventy-two faces, every one turned to Newish, every one with the same look. Hako and the smith pulling together like they’d done it all their lives. Rachael and Mrs Penn. Dennis and the cheesemonger, who was pulling so hard her cap had fallen off. Salty and old Hobbes, Hobbes with his eyes on Newish’s hand and his lips moving, counting with her, though he couldn’t hear the count.

Gabe.

And next to Gabe, Merle, with Dec’s hands above hers on the rope, both of them pulling, her face wet and his very still.

And next to Dec, Thea.

BONG.

“THREE.”

On the third stroke, the first time, the clock had stopped.

John felt the whole ring hold its breath. All seventy-two of them, at once. Pulling, and holding their breath, and listening through the bells for the tock.

Tock.

It didn’t stop.

Somebody laughed. In the ring. John didn’t see who. Then somebody else. Then the cheesemonger said oh, for heaven’s sake out loud and somebody else laughed louder, and they pulled on four and the bells rang, all of them, at once, and the clock went tock, and nobody was holding their breath any more.

They pulled all twelve.

Newish counted every one. John watched her. Standing alone by the stair with her hand up and her face lit by his mote, counting the strokes of the clock and calling them, FIVE, SIX, so that seventy-two people could pull on the same moment. She’d counted the mowers on floor 14. She’d counted every stair since the white room. She counted now, and her voice cracked on TEN, and she didn’t stop.

On TWELVE, they pulled the hardest.

Thirty-six bells. All at once. The biggest sound John had ever been inside.

And then the clock.

Tock.

And the minute hand went past the twelve.

John saw it. Through the mote on the beam, looking down through the floor of the belfry and the trapdoor to the clock room, at the back of the dial. The great wheel turning. Forward. One click. And on the other side of the dial, on the four faces of the tower, looking out at the square and the town and the river and the hills, the long hands moving. One minute past midnight.

And the moon didn’t move.

Down in the square, the whole of Evermorrow made a sound.

It wasn’t a cheer. John had thought it would be a cheer. It was bigger and stranger than that. It was a whole town letting out a breath it had been holding for a year. A long, loud, ragged sound, all at once, from every throat in the square, that went up the side of the tower and into the belfry and in among the bells, still swinging, still ringing, and mixed with them.

And then it was a cheer.

In the ringing chamber, nobody let go of their ropes.

They should have. The bells were still swinging. The ropes were pulling up and down on their own now, the weight of the bells taking them, and you were supposed to let them go or steady them, the smith had said, or you’d get your arms pulled off. Nobody let go. Seventy-two people stood in a ring holding thirty-six ropes going up and down, laughing, crying, holding on.

Merle had turned round.

Still holding the rope. Inside Dec’s arms, which were still on it above hers. She’d turned round to face him and she was laughing so hard she couldn’t stand up properly, and the rope was lifting both of them a little on every upswing, and she didn’t seem to care.

“It didn’t,” said Merle.

“I said.”

“I know you said. It didn’t.” She was crying too. “A year. A whole year. And I still thought—at twelve—I still thought—“

“I know,” said Dec. “Me too. Every time.”

Merle looked at him.

“Every time?” she said. “You’ve only been here three nights.”

Dec didn’t say anything.

John watched him not say anything. Through the mote. From the middle. Merle’s face, wet, laughing, looking up at him. And Dec’s, still, looking down at her. And John, who’d watched Dec’s face for a year from the corner of a screen, saw the thing go across it that Xav had told them about. It took me a lot longer than that. Saw him nearly say it again. And not.

“Every time it gets to twelve,” said Dec. “Anywhere.”

Merle looked at him a moment longer. Then she laughed again, and turned back round to the rope, and leaned back against him, and let the bell pull them both up on the next swing.

She didn’t ask.

On the next rope, Thea had let go.

John saw her do it. She’d taken her hands off the grip, and stepped back, and the woman from Lantern Street behind her had the rope now, and was laughing, letting it go up and down. Thea stood in the space between her rope and Dec’s. Where the gap had been.

She was looking at Dec.

Not angry. John had seen her angry. Everybody had. For sixty days. At the doorpost, and on the stairs, and on the Salt, and in Tell, and on the deck of the Patience. Dennis had a graph. John had seen the graph. It had gone down, and down, in bumps. Two, Dennis said this morning. Maybe one and a half.

This wasn’t angry.

John didn’t know what it was. He didn’t have a word for it. He’d never seen it on her in his life, or on anybody. He thought it was what had been under the angry all along. The thing Azac said he’d seen on the quay at Hythe. The thing the angry had been standing in front of, the way a shield stands in front of a man.

It wasn’t standing in front of anything now.

Dec turned his head and saw her.

And John, from the middle, with the best seat in the house, did something he’d never done in his life. In a year of streams, and five hundred days of motes, and every corner of every room.

He put the mote out.

The one over the circle. He closed his hand, and it went. Just for a moment. So that for a moment, in the ringing chamber of the clocktower of Evermorrow, with the bells still going overhead and seventy-two people laughing and the whole town cheering in the square below, there was no light on the two of them at all.

Some things weren’t for the camera.

He sat in the dark in the middle of the circle and counted to ten. Then he opened his hand, and the light came back.

Thea was holding Dec’s hand. Over Merle’s head. On the rope.

Neither of them said anything.

The clock struck one at one.

Merle laughed. The way she’d laughed a year ago, on the first real night, walking the dark square touching everything. It’s never been one o’clock. I’ve never seen it. Somebody in the square had shouted some of us are trying to sleep. Nobody shouted tonight. Nobody in Evermorrow was trying to sleep. They were in the square, all of them, round the fountain, where Merle had brought out the bread.

Tomorrow’s bread. At one in the morning. Rosemary loaves, dozens of them, out of her ovens and onto the fountain’s edge, and the whole town eating them in the lamplight, and the thirty-six eating them on the church steps. The dog got a whole one. The butcher didn’t shout.

John sat at the end of the church steps. Off to one side.

Dec came and sat next to him.

He didn’t say anything for a while. He had a rosemary loaf in his hands, and he was eating it the way he’d eaten them the first time, Merle said, whole, on the fountain’s edge, while she laughed at him. Except he wasn’t on the fountain’s edge. He was on the end of the church steps, next to John.

“You were in the middle,” said Dec.

“Best seat in the house.”

“You put the light out.”

John didn’t say anything.

“In the chamber. At the end.” Dec looked at his loaf. “For about ten seconds. I saw it go.”

“Some things aren’t for the camera,” said John.

Dec looked at him.

He looked at him for a long time. The way he’d looked at him on the night of the thousand words, with the mote by his face. You’re on camera. Off to one side? Best seat in the house. We drifted. Nobody said anything. Me neither. I’m sorry. Me too.

“There was a lamp in your corner,” said Dec. “After. On Earth. After we drifted. I always meant to tell you. I used to look at the corner and there was a lamp there where you used to sit. I never moved it.”

“You said. On seven. The first time.”

“I know. I’m saying it again.” Dec looked at the square. At the town, eating bread in the lamplight. “I’m saying a lot of things again. I’m trying to say them all. While there’s time.”

John didn’t ask what he meant by while there’s time. He didn’t need to. He was off to one side. He could see everybody.

He let a mote go up off his palm. Small. White. And sent it up, over the square, over the fountain and the town and the tower, and let it hang there among the real stars, the way he’d done on the night of the thousand words. A little white point, a long way up.

Dec looked up at it.

“That one’s for Thursday,” said John. “Every year. I’ll leave one.”

“You won’t be here.”

“No,” said John. “But I’ll know it’s there.” He looked at it. “Off to one side.”

Dec laughed. Quietly. With his mouth full of rosemary loaf. And didn’t say anything else, and didn’t need to.

Across the square, by the fountain, Thea was laughing at something Merle had said. Out loud. With her head back. Dennis, on the step below John, said one under his breath, and Rachael didn’t say Dennis. She just put her gloved hand on his back.
