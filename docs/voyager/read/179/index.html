Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 5: Everyone Comes Back Down

Chapter 179

The Hammer

5,274 words · 23 min read

Salty listened for the hammer before he heard anything else.

He couldn’t help it. Forty nights, on floor 8, on a stool in a forge under the second arch, with Ressa’s small old hammer in his lap and the fall coming down outside the door like a wall of white noise, listening. Sit. Listen to the hammer. He’d heard it every four seconds for forty nights. THUD. Through the stool. Through the floor. Through the whole of Wendmere, the stone arches and the stilt houses and the jetties and the walkways, through the water itself. A waterwheel in the edge of a fall, a cam, a tree-thick beam, and a hammer the size of a barrel walking down a blade a finger’s width at a time.

He’d heard it in his sleep, after. On floor 9. On floor 10, under the Breath. On the Patience. He’d heard it on the stair at Tell when he couldn’t sleep, a thud every four seconds in the dark behind his eyes, and folded to it, in his head, and gone to sleep.

So when Tiger opened the door at the bottom of the stair, behind the Great Fall, and the roar of the water came in, Salty stood in the dark and listened under it.

For the thud.

It wasn’t there.

They came out along the shelf.

The lowest one, at the foot of the Great Fall, with your back to the cliff and the water coming down a hand’s width from your face, so loud you couldn’t hear yourself think. They’d come along it the other way on the two hundredth day, going up, single file, roped, with Hako in front and Salty at the back because if anybody went off the shelf it was going to be Salty, who sank. He’d sunk on floor 8 twice. Once in the lake, once in the net. Neither time on purpose.

He didn’t go off the shelf. He came out into the light at the end of it with spray in his hair and stood on the wet rock at the foot of the falls and looked at Wendmere.

It was autumn.

He’d known it would be. It had been autumn the first time, when they came down the falls from floor 7 on the hundred and fifty-fifth day and Calvin had shouted that the leaves were going yellow. The floors have their own years. A year and a bit later, it was autumn again. The trees on the cliffs round the lake going yellow. The light low and gold. Mist on the water in the morning.

The city hung across the last fall like a net. Grey stone arches, stilt houses in the green lake below, walkways and stairs and water going through all of it. Green slate roofs. Gold lamps behind the falling water, hundreds of them.

And no thud.

He listened. He stood on the rock with thirty-five people round him, and the falls roaring, and Calvin saying oh, it’s the same, it’s exactly the same, and listened so hard his ears hurt.

Nothing. Water. Wind. Voices. The low hum the river-folk made sometimes, when they were working, that came across the lake like the sound of a hive.

No hammer.

“Salty,” said Wendy. Beside him. She’d seen his face. “What?”

“The hammer,” said Salty. “It’s stopped.”

He went to the forge first.

He didn’t ask. He didn’t tell anybody. Higu was taking out the rock and saying eight, what do you remember, and Ice was saying nothing, and Higu was drawing the line, and Salty was already walking. Along the lake edge. Up the stair to the second arch. Through the spray. He knew the way. He’d walked it forty nights in the dark.

The forge was under the arch, in the cliff, with the fall coming down across the door. Through the door, the cave. Low, smoky, with the river-iron smell he’d never got out of his nose. The clay ovens. The racks of tongs. The anvil, the size of a bed. And the hammer.

It was still.

He’d never seen it still. The great beam up in the dark under the roof, the barrel-sized head hanging off the end of it, with the cam that lifted it and the wheel outside in the edge of the fall that turned the cam. The wheel was turning. He could hear it, outside, in the water. But the cam was off. The pin pulled. The beam lay on its rest, and the hammer hung over the anvil, and didn’t fall.

Ressa was sitting by it.

On her stool. The one he used to sit on. In her ankle-length leather apron, with her green hair knotted up, and her skin dark like river stone, and the backs of both webbed hands burned white like lace. She was sitting very still, the way the river-folk sat, with her hands in her lap, looking at the wall.

There was a blade on the wall.

Salty looked at it.

Long. Thin. Curved like a new moon. Hung on two pegs, at the height of her eyes. Not on a pole. Just the blade, by itself, the length of his arm and half again, with a tang at one end for a socket. And down the whole length of it, in the steel, a ripple. Like water moving. Like the light on the lake in the morning. He’d seen one like it once before. On the end of a long pale pole, in Aud’s hands, on the causeway, at the lip of the Edge.

This one was lighter.

He could see it from the door. Thinner in the back. Narrower. Every line of it a little less. As if somebody had taken the edge-blade Aud carried and made it again for somebody who couldn’t carry that.

“You came back, little light,” said Ressa.

She didn’t look round.

He sat down.

On the floor, by the stool. There wasn’t another stool. He sat on the stone floor of the forge with his back against the anvil and his knees up, the way he’d sat the first week, before he understood what he was listening for. The fall roared outside the door. The wheel turned in it.

“It’s stopped,” said Salty.

“Since the spring,” said Ressa.

“You finished it.”

“I finished it.” She was looking at the blade. “The week she went.”

He didn’t need to ask who. He’d known since the shelf. Since the silence. Since he’d stood on the rock at the foot of the falls and listened for the thud and it wasn’t there, and some part of him had already known what he was going to find.

“Aud,” said Salty.

Ressa nodded. Slowly.

“Eleven years,” said Ressa. “I told you. On your first night. You asked what I was making and I said a blade, and I’d finish it in the spring. You didn’t ask what for.” She almost smiled. “You were too busy listening. Like I’d told you.”

“What was it for?”

“Her hands.” Ressa looked at her own. The white lace on the backs of them. “Hers were done. You saw. On the causeway. Every boat, her arms shook more. The pole I made her a hundred and ninety-one years ago was a good pole. For a young woman. It was too heavy for an old one.” She looked at the blade on the wall. “So I made her a lighter one. Eleven years. Two thousand folds. A finger’s width a day. So that when her arms were done, she’d have something her arms could still carry.”

The fall roared.

“I was eleven years too slow,” said Ressa.

Salty didn’t say anything.

“She went in the spring,” said Ressa. “Before the floods. She went out to the causeway at dusk for Old Brann, from the fourth arch. She got to the first stone. And she stood on it. And she couldn’t lift the pole.” She was very still. “She stood there on the first stone with the boat at her feet and the whole city humming and she couldn’t lift it. And she knew. You always know. And she sat down. On the first stone. And she went.”

“On the stone?”

“On the stone.” Ressa looked at her hands. “I’d finished the blade that morning. It was on the anvil. I was going to take it to her after.”

Salty sat with his back against the anvil and looked at the floor.

He’d made things in a hurry his whole life. A hundred nails on floor 1. Swords that did the job. That is a sword made in a hurry, she’d said to him, on the first day, and he’d said yeah, it’s what I do. He’d learned, here, on this floor, with her hammer, that some things you couldn’t make in a hurry. That the blow had to go in. That the iron had to be ready and not only hot. That the edge was the last day, not before.

And here was a woman who’d done it all right. Every fold. A finger’s width a day. Eleven years. And been too slow anyway.

“That’s not fair,” said Salty.

“No,” said Ressa. “It isn’t.” She looked at him for the first time. Down, at him sitting on her floor. “You’ve got very sad in the face, little light. That’s new.”

“I’ve been some places.”

“I can see.” She looked at him a moment longer. “You made it. Didn’t you. Your blade. I can see that too. You’ve got a different face. Like somebody who’s finished something.”

“I made it.”

“Fold?”

“Two hundred and something. Under the Breath. On ten.” He swallowed. “Hardened in the sea. Hafted in ash. The first ash of a ship.” He looked at his hands. “The edge was the last day.”

Ressa looked at him for a long time.

“Show me,” said Ressa.

He didn’t have it. Yan had it. That was the whole point.

He went out of the forge into the spray and stood at the top of the stair under the second arch and looked down at the lake, at the house for lights on its stilts at the edge, where the thirty-six were going in, and the river-folk coming out of their houses across the water to look. He didn’t have to look long. Yan was on the last jetty.

Of course he was.

The last jetty. Past the last stilts. Where the causeway started. Where Aud had walked out every dusk for a hundred and ninety-one years beside a boat with a lamp at the bow. Yan was standing at the end of it with Working On It over his shoulder, the long pale haft, the blade folded back along it the way he carried it in towns. And Wendy a pace behind him. Looking out at the Edge.

Salty went down.

He went along the walkways and the jetties, past the stilts, across the slow green water of the lake, with the river-folk turning their heads to watch him go. Long, thin, slow, grey-green, with their big wet eyes. Some of them knew him. Little light, one said, an old one on a balcony. The hammer boy. He nodded and kept going.

He got to the end of the last jetty.

Yan didn’t look round. Wendy did.

“He’s been looking for twenty minutes,” said Wendy. Quietly. “Since we came out on the rock. He didn’t even go to the house.”

“At what?”

Wendy didn’t answer. She looked at Yan.

Yan was looking at the Edge.

Past the end of the jetty, the causeway went out. Flat stones, under ankle-deep fast water, from the end of the last jetty to the lip, where the lake just ended, in a long straight line like the edge of a table, and poured over into white. Mist. Nobody had ever seen the bottom. The river-folk’s dead went over it, in reed boats, with a lamp at the bow, and Aud walking beside them with her pole, until the light lay down, and then she cut the line.

It was dusk. The light was going. There was nothing out there. Water, and stones under it, and the lip, and the white.

“Yan,” said Salty.

“Fourteen,” said Yan.

Pleasantly. The way he said everything. Not looking round.

“Fourteen what?”

“At the lip.” Yan lifted one hand off the haft and pointed. Out, along the causeway, to the end of it, to the line where the water went over. “Sitting on the water. In a row. Like lamps on a windowsill.” He tilted his head. “Low. River-folk always burn low. But they’re not going out. They’re just sitting there.” He was quiet for a moment. “Waiting.”

Salty looked. He couldn’t see anything. He never could. Water, and the lip, and the white.

“And one on the first stone,” said Yan.

He pointed again. Closer. At the first flat stone of the causeway, a pace off the end of the jetty, under ankle-deep water.

“Right there,” said Yan. “Sitting. With its hands in its lap.” He looked at it. “It’s very, very low. Lower than the others. Like a lamp somebody turned down to nothing and then forgot to blow out.” He smiled a little. “But steady.”

He’d said that before. Salty remembered. On the first floor-8 night, when Aud came to the house for lights and asked him what he saw. Very low. Like a lamp at night. But steady.

“Aud,” said Wendy.

“Aud,” said Yan.

Oarin told them in the house for lights, after dark.

The old river-man with the deep slow voice. He’d met them on the stair the first time, a year ago, and said lights at your shoulders, it has been a long, long time. He came now across the water with a lamp and stood in the door of the long low hall on stilts at the lake’s edge, which they’d swept for three hundred and twelve years before the thirty-six came, and had kept swept since, and looked at them, and his big wet eyes went very bright.

“You came down,” said Oarin.

He didn’t say back. Salty noticed. He said down.

“Everyone who goes up, goes up,” said Oarin. “Elder Sefa said. Nobody comes down to tell.” He looked at the hearth, where Ina had lit the fire on the first night with his ordinary orange flame, the fire the last ones had laid three hundred and twelve years ago and nobody would light. It was lit now. Somebody had kept it lit. “You came down.”

And then he told them.

Not much. River-folk didn’t say much. He told it the way Ressa had: the spring, before the floods, Old Brann’s boat, Aud on the first stone with the pole she couldn’t lift. Sitting down. Going.

“I cut her boat,” said Oarin.

The hall was very quiet.

“Someone had to. There was no one. No apprentice. A hundred years, no apprentice. The young will not walk the stones.” He looked at his own long webbed hands. “I am old. I walked her out. Badly. The water leaned on me and I could not stand in it as she stood. I got to the lip on my knees. And I cut.” He was quiet. “I did not wait. I did not know how long. I could not feel it the way she could. I cut, and the boat went over, and I thought she had gone with it.”

“She hadn’t,” said Yan.

Oarin looked at him.

“No,” said Oarin. “We know. We can feel them. Warm. At the lip. Like a hand on your back. All of them.” He looked at the floor. “Fourteen, since her. The old go before the ice. We sent every one. We cut every line. Too soon, every one. And every one stayed.” His voice went lower. “The young will not go near the water at dusk now. They say it is full of hands.”

“It is,” said Yan. “Sort of.”

Wendy’s elbow went into his ribs.

“Ow,” said Yan.

“The lamp at the end of the causeway has not been lit since the spring,” said Oarin. “Aud lit it, every dusk. For a hundred and ninety-one years. For whoever was walking.” He looked at the window, at the dark lake, at the place out there where there was no lamp. “There is no one walking.”

Salty found Yan on the jetty again, later.

Everybody else was asleep. The house for lights was full of thirty-six people breathing, and Ice’s soup in the old kitchen, and Ina’s fire going low in the hearth. And out at the end of the last jetty, in the dark, a figure with a long haft over its shoulder, standing very still.

Salty sat down on the boards beside him.

He didn’t say anything for a while. It was cold. The lake was black. Out past the end of the jetty, there was nothing to see at all. Just the sound of the water going over the lip, a long way off, soft, the way the sea sounds from inside a house.

“You’re going to,” said Salty.

“Yes,” said Yan.

“All of them.”

“All of them.” Yan didn’t look round. “One night. That’s all I’ve got. Then we go up the falls, and on.”

Salty thought about it.

“You talked to her about four times,” said Salty. “Aud. On the way up. Four times. I counted.”

“Four’s enough.”

“Is it?”

“She gave me the pole,” said Yan. “She stood on the jetty with her arms shaking and handed me a thing she’d carried for a hundred and ninety-one years and told me how. Keep it short. Don’t look at the Edge till you’re there. Wait till it lies down.” He was quiet. “And then she said the young always cut too soon. And I said somebody taught me a knot. And she looked at me like—“ He stopped. “She was one of mine. After that. Four times is enough. Once was enough.”

“And the others?” said Salty. “The fourteen?”

Yan shrugged. A small shrug, with the haft on his shoulder.

“They’re in the way,” said Yan.

Salty looked at him.

He said it so pleasantly. That was the thing about Yan. He’d always said things so pleasantly. I’ll know his light now. Anywhere. Not much. Not unless it’s one of you. They’re in the way. As if he were telling you the time. And you knew, because you’d known him for years, that he wasn’t joking, and that he wasn’t being cruel, and that it was just true. That there were people who were his, and Yan would walk to the end of the world for them and over it if he had to, and then there was everybody else. And everybody else he’d walk past.

Fourteen strangers sitting on the water at the lip in a row. Between him and Aud.

He wasn’t doing it for them.

He was going to do it anyway. Every one. Properly. Because they were in front of her, and she’d want them done first, and done right.

“You’re a bit scary, mate,” said Salty.

“Wendy says only a bit.”

“Wendy’s being nice.”

“Wendy’s never nice,” said Yan. “Wendy’s right. It’s different.” He looked down at Salty, at last. In the dark. And smiled. “You said that on the first night here. You’re a bit scary. And then you asked if your light looked all right.”

“Does it?”

“It’s enormous,” said Yan. “Like a forge. It never keeps still.” He looked back out at the Edge. “Same as it was.”

“Sounds right,” said Salty.

They sat there.

“Ressa wants to see it,” said Salty, after a while. “Working On It. She asked. She made the other one. Aud’s.”

Yan was quiet.

“Tomorrow,” said Yan. “Before.”

Yan brought it in the morning.

Salty had gone up early and sat on the floor by the anvil and waited. Ressa was on her stool. The hammer was still. The light blade hung on its pegs on the wall. Outside, the fall came down across the door, white.

Yan came in through the spray with Working On It over his shoulder and Wendy behind him. He stood in the door for a moment, looking at the cave, the ovens, the anvil, the still hammer, the way he looked at places. Then at Ressa. Lower. At the place under her breastbone.

“You’re the one Aud gave the pole to,” said Ressa.

“Yes.”

“Have you still got it?”

“Yes.” He’d carried it since floor 8. On his back, under his pack, wrapped in oilcloth. Salty had seen him unwrap it on the deck of the Patience and look at it and wrap it up again. All the way up and all the way down. He’d never once used it after Salty gave him Working On It. He’d just carried it.

“Show me the other one,” said Ressa. “The boy’s.”

Yan took Working On It off his shoulder. He unfolded the blade from along the haft, the way you unfold a knife, and the long curve of it came round and locked, and the light from the door went down it.

He held it out to her.

Ressa took it. In her burned white hands. She held it the way Salty had seen her hold her own work, at the end of a day, by the window of the forge: up, and turned, and sideways to the light, to see the lines in the steel. He’d folded it two hundred and something times, under the Breath, on floor 10, a fold a night, some nights none, with Geir standing behind him saying no. You could see the folds. Fine lines, in the steel, all along the blade. Not a ripple like hers. Not water. Something else.

Ressa looked at it for a long time.

She ran her thumb down the back of it. Then, very lightly, the flat. Then she turned it over and looked at the edge, all the way along, from the haft to the point, with her eyes almost shut.

“Hardened in the sea,” said Ressa.

“At Hythe,” said Salty. His voice came out wrong. “Off the slip. On the last night before the launch.”

“Hafted in ash.”

“The first ash he cut for the Patience. Corran. Eleven years seasoned. He gave it me.”

Ressa nodded slowly. She held the blade to the light again.

“It does not cut too soon,” said Ressa.

Salty didn’t say anything. He couldn’t. The window had said that. On the Patience, in the dark, when he gave it to Yan. It does not cut too soon. He’d never told anybody what the window said. Yan hadn’t either. And she’d looked at it, and said it, as if it were written on the steel.

Maybe it was. Maybe you could read a thing like that, if you’d made enough of them.

Ressa handed it back to Yan. She looked at Salty, sitting on her floor with his back against her anvil.

“That,” said Ressa, “is not a sword made in a hurry.”

He didn’t cry. He wasn’t going to cry in Ressa’s forge. He sat on the floor and looked at his hands very hard and the fall roared and he didn’t cry.

Wendy put her hand on his head. Just for a second. He didn’t look up.

“It’s a scythe,” said Yan. Pleasantly. To Ressa. “Not a sword.”

“I know what it is,” said Ressa. “I made the other one.”

She got up.

Slowly. The river-folk did everything slowly, and Ressa was older than Oarin, and her knees made a sound. She got up off her stool and went to the wall, and took the light blade down off its pegs, in both hands, and stood holding it.

“You’re going to walk them,” said Ressa. To Yan. Not a question.

“Tonight.”

“All of them.”

“All of them. Her last.”

Ressa looked at the blade in her hands. Eleven years. Two thousand folds. Made for an old woman’s arms, a finger’s width a day, finished the morning she sat down on the first stone and went.

“Not with this,” said Ressa.

Yan looked at her.

“This is for after,” said Ressa. “For whoever’s next. When you’ve gone up.” She looked at him. “You’ll walk them with hers. Aud’s. The pole she gave you. She’d want that. Her own pole, walking her out.” She turned the light blade over in her hands. “And then you’ll give it to somebody. Because you’re not staying. I can see you’re not staying. You’ve got a face like somebody who’s only passing through.” She almost smiled. “And when you give it, I’ll put this on it. A lighter blade. For somebody whose arms aren’t long enough yet.”

“There isn’t anybody,” said Wendy. Quietly. “Oarin said. A hundred years. The young won’t walk the stones.”

“Then he’ll find somebody,” said Ressa. Looking at Yan. “Won’t you.”

Yan didn’t say anything.

He looked at her. At the place under her breastbone. For a long time. And then he looked out of the door, through the falling water, at the lake, at the city, at the hundreds of low gold lights of the river-folk going about their morning on the walkways and the balconies and in the water among the stilts.

Salty watched him look.

He didn’t know what Yan saw. He never would. But he saw Yan’s eyes go over the city slowly, the way Burr’s went over them on a platform, counting. And stop. Somewhere. And stay there a moment. And move on.

“Maybe,” said Yan.

Ressa put the pin back in the cam.

Salty watched her do it. At the end, as they were going. She went to the great beam in the dark under the roof and reached up with her burned hands and slid the iron pin back into the cam, where it went, and stepped back.

Outside, the wheel was turning in the edge of the fall. The cam came round. Caught the beam. Lifted it. Up, slowly, into the dark, the barrel-sized head going up and up until it hung at the top of its swing.

And fell.

THUD.

On the empty anvil. Nothing on it. Just the hammer, coming down on the bed-sized block of iron, for the first time since the spring.

The sound went out of the forge. Through the stone. Through the floor. Salty felt it go through his back where it was against the anvil, and into the cliff, and out along the arches and the walkways and the stilts and the water. Through the whole of Wendmere.

Four seconds.

THUD.

Out in the city, on the walkways, Salty heard the river-folk stop. All of them. The low hum of the city going quiet the way it went quiet for a boat. And then, after a moment, starting again. Different. Higher.

“What are you making?” said Salty.

“Nothing,” said Ressa. She sat back down on her stool. “Not yet.” She looked at the empty anvil, with the hammer coming down on it every four seconds. “Somebody’s going to walk the stones tonight. I can feel it. The whole city can.” She folded her burned hands in her lap. “I wanted the hammer going. So they’d know somebody was.”

THUD.

Salty sat on the floor of the forge with his back against the anvil and felt it come through him every four seconds, the way he’d felt it for forty nights, the way he’d heard it in his sleep on floor 9 and on the Patience and on the stair at Tell. He didn’t get up. He sat and listened.

Sit. Listen to the hammer.

He’d thought, the first week, that it was a test.

It wasn’t. It had never been a test. It was just the sound a place made when somebody in it was still working. Still folding. A finger’s width at a time. So that everybody else in the city could hear it through the floor and know.

THUD.

Yan had gone. Out through the spray with Working On It over his shoulder and Wendy behind him. Down the stair. Toward the lake.

Salty stayed.

He’d go down at dusk. They all would. Ice had said so, at breakfast, quietly, to nobody, the way he said things now: we’ll all be on the jetty. Nobody had asked why. Higu had written it on the rock. DUSK. THE LAST JETTY. ALL OF US. And under it, small, the way she wrote the things that belonged to somebody: YAN’S.

Until then, he’d sit.

THUD.

Ressa didn’t say anything. She sat on her stool with her hands in her lap. After a while, without looking at him, she held out her hand. Palm up. Burned white on the back, like lace.

He looked at it. Then he understood. He reached into his pack, and took out a small old hammer, worn smooth on the handle by somebody else’s hand for longer than he’d been alive, that she’d given him on his first night and that he’d carried up six floors and back down six.

He put it in her palm.

She closed her fingers on it.

Then she opened them again, and held it out to him. Back.

“Keep it,” said Ressa. “You’ve a long way down. And a long way up after.” She put it in his hand and closed his fingers over it with her own, the way you’d close somebody’s hand over a coin. “And you’ll want something to listen to, little light. Where there isn’t a hammer.”

THUD.

He kept it.
