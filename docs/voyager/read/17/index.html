Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 1: Thirty-Six Circles

Chapter 17

Frozen Ink

3,112 words · 14 min read

Wendy had worn headphones for most of her life, and now they were silent.

She’d come through the white room with them on, the lavender over-ear ones from her model, round her neck where she always wore them when she wasn’t using them. They’d come through because she’d been wearing them. That was the rule, apparently: things you wore came, things you held didn’t. Her phone hadn’t come. Her music hadn’t come. The headphones had come, and they were just headphones now, two soft cups on a band, plugged into nothing.

She put them on anyway, sometimes. At night, mostly. Not for music. Just for the weight of them on her head, and the way they muffled the world down to something she could manage.

She hadn’t told anyone that. Salty knew. Salty always knew things without being told. Which was the most annoying thing about him and also, she suspected, the reason she was with him.

Larchwick was a wooden town inside a wooden fence, at the bottom of a white valley, by a frozen river, and it was the coldest place Wendy had ever been in her life.

The cold here wasn’t Hearthstead’s soft grey morning chill. It was a dry, bright, hard cold that got into your sleeves and down your collar and up through the soles of your boots, and made your eyes water and your nose run and your fingers stop working. The people of Larchwick wore furs and fat leather boots stuffed with straw, and they looked at the five Climbers coming in through their gate in cardigans and coats and a waistcoat, and laughed out loud.

“First day?” said the woman at the gate, who had a beard of frost on her scarf. “Up from the green floor?”

“Yes,” said Yan.

“Thought so. You’ve got that look. Like you were expecting summer.” She jerked her head at the street behind her. “Trapper’s hall, left side, past the well. Get furs before you get dead. Then come and find me if you want work. Rimehounds have been at the woodpiles.”

“Rimehounds,” said Wendy.

“Wolves,” said the woman. “Big white ones. They come down from the high pines in packs, when it’s cold.” She considered. “It’s always cold.”

“Wonderful,” said Wendy.

They got furs. It took nearly every coin they had between them, and Tiger’s dead sheep wool, and Cursiffe talking to the trapper for twenty minutes in his calm, slow, perfectly reasonable voice until the trapper agreed to a price mainly, Wendy suspected, to make him stop. They came out of the trapper’s hall looking like five bears, and Wendy could feel her fingers again, and she was so grateful she could have cried.

That was the first afternoon.

The second morning, they went out after the Rimehounds, and Wendy found out her arrows didn’t work.

They didn’t fly straight. That was the problem.

On floor 1, on the soft green slopes with no wind, she’d got good. One in three by the second day, one in two by the third. In the eye, every time, at forty metres. She’d been almost proud of it, which she’d never say out loud, because being proud of things made the universe take them off you.

Floor 2 had wind. Not a steady wind you could learn. Cold gusts that came down off the high pines without warning, side-on, swirling in the trees. Her arrows from the Hearthstead fletcher were light and cheap, and in the gusts they drifted. A metre off at forty metres. Two metres at sixty. Sometimes they just lifted, as if a hand had taken them, and went off into the trees and were gone.

The first Rimehound she shot at, she missed by a body-length.

It was a wolf. Not a sheep pretending to be a wolf. A real wolf, or what a real wolf would look like if somebody made it bigger and whiter and meaner. It stood about waist-height at the shoulder, with a thick white coat that was almost invisible against the snow, and pale blue eyes like chips of ice. They came in fours and fives. And they didn’t charge, the way the Woolbacks did, stupidly, in straight lines. They came round. They circled. One of them would come at you from the front, making a lot of noise, while the other three came from the sides and behind, where you weren’t looking.

The first fight was very bad.

Salty held the front, as he always did. But the front was everywhere. The hounds kept going round him. Tiger was fast enough to keep up with them, just, and spent the whole fight yelling left, left, behind you, LEFT. Cursiffe’s little darts of force hit and the hounds flinched and kept coming. Yan’s hands went white twice. And Wendy, on a fallen log at the back, shot eleven arrows and hit one, in the shoulder, and it didn’t even slow down.

They won. Barely. They stood in the bloody snow afterwards with their breath steaming, all of them hurt, and Wendy looked down at her bow, and at the four arrows she had left, and said:

“I’m useless.”

“You’re not useless,” said Salty.

“I hit one. Out of eleven. In the shoulder.”

“The wind—“

“I know it’s the wind. Everybody else is dealing with the wind.” She was aware she was being unfair. She didn’t care. She was cold and frightened and her hands were shaking. “Tiger’s dealing with the wind. Cursiffe’s dealing with the wind. I’m the one standing at the back shooting at trees. If I’d hit even one more of them, Yan wouldn’t have had to—“

She stopped. She didn’t want to say it. Yan was standing a little way off with his hands tucked into his furs, very carefully not looking at anyone.

Salty didn’t say anything for a moment.

Then he reached into the big pocket of his new fur coat and took out a small leather bag, tied with string, and held it out to her.

“I was going to give you these tonight,” he said.

She opened it. Arrowheads. A dozen of them, maybe more. Black iron, rough, a bit uneven, each one as long as her thumb. When she picked one up it was heavy, much heavier than the ones from the fletcher, a real weight in her palm.

“Made them at Ulla’s,” said Salty. “The day after the nails. She showed me. They’re not pretty.” He looked at the bloody snow. “But they’re heavy. They won’t drift.”

Wendy looked at the arrowheads for a long time.

“When did you—“

“Last night. And the night before. While you were asleep.” He shrugged. “Didn’t sleep much. Doesn’t matter.”

She wanted to say something. She didn’t trust her voice. So she put the bag in her pocket, and put her forehead against his shoulder, through all the fur, for about three seconds, which was all either of them could manage in front of other people. Then she went and sat on the log and started taking the cheap heads off her last four arrows with her teeth.

They worked for the rest of the day. Not on hounds. On the arrows.

Salty knew how to make arrowheads now but not arrows, and Wendy knew how to shoot arrows but not make them. So they worked it out together, by the fire in the corner of the trapper’s hall, while Tiger watched and asked questions and Cursiffe took notes. The heavy heads needed longer shafts, which needed better wood, which the trapper sold them for nearly the last of their money. They needed bigger feathers to steer them, which Tiger went out and got from the woodpiles. He came back with an armful of grey goose feathers and wouldn’t say how.

By evening she had a dozen ugly, heavy, black-headed arrows, and she took them out to the edge of the town in the dusk, to the stockade wall, and shot at a knot in a log at sixty metres.

The wind came down off the pines, side-on, swirling.

The arrow went through it like it wasn’t there.

Thunk. In the knot. Dead centre.

She stood there for a while. Then she shot another one. Then another. Three in the knot, at sixty metres, in a wind that would have thrown the old ones into the trees.

“Lucky,” she said, out loud, to nobody.

“That’s not luck,” said Salty, behind her. He’d been standing there the whole time. “That’s iron.”

She didn’t argue. For once.

The next morning they went back out after the hounds, and she hit seven out of nine, and four of them were eyes.

But it was Cursiffe who surprised everybody.

He’d been quiet since the fight in the snow. Not unusually quiet; Cursiffe was always quiet. He spoke slowly and seldom and usually only when he’d thought something through to the end. But Wendy had noticed him on the walk out, holding the little glass vial of dark blue ink at his neck in his gloved fingers, turning it, frowning at it.

“It’s frozen,” he said, when she asked.

She looked. It was. The ink inside the vial, which had always moved when he moved, like something alive, had gone solid. A little dark stick of ice in a glass tube.

“Does it matter?” she said. “It’s ink. You’ve got a staff.”

Cursiffe was quiet for a moment.

“On the green floor,” he said slowly, “on the last day, I tried something. When Yan’s hands went white the first time. I put my finger in the vial, and I wrote a word on a stone in front of a Woolback. It was the only thing I could think of.” He paused. “I wrote STOP. In capitals.”

“And?”

“It stopped.” He turned the vial in his fingers. “For about a second and a half. It stood there with its foot in the air, in front of the word, as if it had walked into a wall. Then the ink dried, and it went on.” He looked at her over the rim of his glasses. “I’ve been practising since. Every evening. On stones, on the inn table. Mother Crane caught me writing on her bread board and nearly threw me in the river.” A small, dry smile. “I was going to try it on the hounds. Properly. But the ink won’t come out.”

Wendy looked at the frozen vial.

Then she looked over her shoulder, at Yan, walking at the back of their little line with his hands buried in his furs. Inside them, she knew, he was holding Salty’s iron box, because Yan had been holding that box every spare minute since Salty gave it to him.

“Yan,” she said. “Can Cursiffe borrow your hand-warmer?”

It took a while. The coal in the iron box had to be fresh, and the vial had to sit against it, wrapped in a scarf, for nearly a quarter of an hour, while they stamped their feet in the snow. But slowly the little dark stick of ice went soft at the edges. Then it moved. Then the ink inside the glass was liquid again, shifting when Cursiffe moved, like something alive.

He kept it there, against the warmer, under his fur, against his chest.

The hounds came at midday. Five of them, from the tree line, white on white, circling.

And Wendy, on a rock at the back with her ugly black arrows, watched Cursiffe walk out in front of Salty, which nobody had ever seen him do. He crouched in the snow and took the warm vial out from inside his coat, uncorked it, and dipped his finger in. Then he drew a line across the snow in front of the party, from one side to the other, in letters as tall as his hand.

STAY

The lead hound came at the line at full speed.

It stopped.

Wendy saw it happen and didn’t believe it. The wolf hit the word the way a man hits a glass door: dead stop, nose first, all four legs going out from under it in a scrabble of snow. It stood there with its lips back and its ice-blue eyes wide, snarling at the word on the ground. It couldn’t come over it.

The other four hounds, coming round the sides, hit the end of the line and couldn’t get round it either.

They stood there. Five white wolves, all in a row behind a word written in ink in the snow, snarling. And for one long, ridiculous second, nobody moved, on either side.

“Ah,” said Cursiffe, quite calmly, still crouching. “It works. Somebody may want to shoot them now. It won’t hold long. The ink’s freezing.”

Wendy shot them.

She had seven arrows left in her quiver, and she used five, and four were eyes. Salty and Tiger did the rest. By the time the line of ink froze and cracked and went pale in the snow, there was only one hound left on the far side of it. It looked at the cracked word, and at the five Climbers, and decided it had business somewhere else.

Cursiffe stood up. He looked down at the word in the snow, frozen now, cracked, harmless. He looked at the window. Then he took out his little book and wrote something in it, slowly, carefully, as if nothing in particular had happened.

“Keep the ink warm,” he said, writing it. “Noted.”

The window came that evening, while they were sitting round the fire in the trapper’s hall with their furs steaming and the trapper’s wife bringing them bowls of something hot that was mostly barley.

Everyone saw it at once. Wendy knew that by now.

The trapper’s hall went quiet. Not because anyone in it could see the window; none of the Larchwick people could. Because the five Climbers by the fire had all gone still at the same moment, staring at nothing, and the people of Larchwick had learned, it seemed, what that meant.

“One of yours got the Old Stag,” said the trapper’s wife, setting down the last bowl. “Did he?”

“I think so,” said Yan.

“Huh,” said the trapper’s wife. “Quick.” She went away again.

Wendy opened the rankings. She did it with a feeling she didn’t like, a kind of cold drop in her stomach, because she knew what she was going to see.

Floor 3. On his own. On the eighth day.

She looked over at Yan. He’d opened the rankings too. He was sitting very still, with Salty’s iron box in his hands and Suzu round his neck, looking at the name at the top. His candle-thing, the thing she knew he saw that he hadn’t told the rest of them about, was in his face. She didn’t know how she knew that. She just did.

She’d only ever really known Ice through Yan.

That was the truth of it. She’d been in the same calls for years, late nights, the six of them, with Ice making jokes and Yan laughing his slow deep laugh. She’d liked Ice. Everybody liked Ice. But she’d never been close to him the way Yan was, or Salty. When she thought about him, what she mostly thought about was Yan’s face when Ice was in the call. How it relaxed. How he laughed more.

So she didn’t care about the name at the top of the list the way Gabe did, or Yan. Not for its own sake.

She cared because Yan was sitting there with his hands round a little iron box, looking at it.

“He’s on floor three,” said Tiger quietly. “On his own.”

“Yeah,” said Wendy.

“Do you think he’s—“

“I think he’s fine,” said Wendy. She heard herself say it, and was surprised, because it wasn’t a thing she’d usually say. Wendy didn’t say things were fine. Wendy said things were going to go wrong, so that when they did, she’d been right, and when they didn’t, it was a nice surprise. “I think he’s fine,” she said again, more slowly. “I think he’s an idiot, and he’s fine, and he’s doing exactly what he wants. And I think when we find him, Yan’s going to say something very calm and very wise, and then Salty’s going to hit him.”

“I’m going to hit him,” Salty agreed.

Yan looked up. And after a moment, the corner of his mouth went up.

“Gabe’s already called it,” he said.

“Then Gabe can hit him first,” said Salty, “and I’ll go second.”

Later, in the dark, in the bunk room at the back of the trapper’s hall, with the fire down to red and four people asleep around her, Wendy put her headphones on.

Silence. Muffled, close, familiar silence. The weight on her head. The soft cups over her ears. She lay on her back with her hands folded on top of the furs and looked up at a wooden ceiling she couldn’t see, and listened to nothing.

Then something, very faintly. Through the cups.

Salty, in the bunk next to hers, was humming.

He wasn’t a hummer. She’d never once heard him do it. But he was humming anyway, very softly, a little off, a tune she knew. It was the one from her playlist. The one she always put on last, at night, before she slept. She’d played it in calls a hundred times. He must have learned it from there, without either of them noticing.

He wasn’t humming it for anybody. He thought she was asleep. He was just humming it, in the dark, in the cold, to himself.

Wendy lay there with her silent headphones on and listened to him get it slightly wrong. She didn’t take them off. She didn’t say anything.

She’d tell him tomorrow it was terrible.

She wouldn’t tell him it was the best thing she’d heard in eight days.
