Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 1: Thirty-Six Circles

Chapter 31

Mote

3,162 words · 14 min read

John had been on Declan’s stream for almost every hour of it, and he’d never once been in front of the camera.

That was how it worked. Declan had streamed with a facecam, back then, in the poly years and a bit after. He’d sit in his room in Singapore with a cheap camera clipped to the top of his monitor, playing whatever he was playing and talking to whoever turned up. And John turned up. Every time. In the chat, mostly, or in the call, a voice off to one side. He’d been the one who said behind you and left, left and you’ve got something in your teeth. He’d been the one who kept the chat going when Declan went to get a drink. He’d sat through three-hour streams of Declan losing at things, just because it was Declan, and because Declan’s stream was the one place John ever sat still for three hours.

He’d liked being off to one side. He was good at it. You could see everything from off to one side.

Then Declan moved to Malacca, and the streams got fewer, and then stopped, and John had found, slightly to his own surprise, that he didn’t know what to do with his evenings.

He hadn’t said anything about it. None of them had. That was the thing about the Singapore lot. They’d all drifted at the same time, in the same way, and none of them had ever said a word about it to each other. As if saying it would make it true.

Larchwick was full.

There were five parties on floor 2 now, twenty-five people in one small wooden town, and the trapper’s hall was the only place big enough to hold more than ten of them at once. John stood in the doorway on their first evening with snow melting in his hair and looked at it. Fires, furs, noise. A big black slate on the wall by the fire covered in chalk. And everybody, all at once, turning round to look at Gabe.

They’d all heard. Of course they had. Gabe was the one who’d shouted Declan in the white room until his voice cracked, and typed it into World every hour on the first night. Everyone knew. And Gabe stood in the doorway in his thin Hearthstead coat and looked back at them all. He didn’t say anything. He walked across the hall to the slate on the wall and started reading it, line by line, as if it were a map of somewhere he’d been told about in a dream.

John went with him, because he always did.

The board was Drew’s, somebody told him. Drew had taken it off the trapper and organised all four parties on it in an afternoon. It was the most useful thing John had seen since the white room. A map of the valley. Slopes, packs, which way the wolves went round. A terrible drawing of a wolf going round a stick. A tiny onion, in the corner, for no reason anybody would explain.

PASS: MOTHER TALLOW. BE RUDE.

And at the very bottom, small and neat: VOYAGER — FL 3 — LV 10 — (locked, waiting for trial). Under it, something even smaller, in a script John couldn’t read at all.

Gabe stood in front of that bottom line for a long time.

It was Newish who told them about the coat.

She came over to the board while Gabe was still standing there, with her furs open and her chin up, and said, without any preamble at all: “So you haven’t heard about the tower.”

They hadn’t heard about the tower.

She told them. The whole thing, fast, with her hands, the way she told everything. The light on the mountain that had gone up in the Long Storm. The tiny old woman in the candle hut, who’d shouted at Newish for ten minutes about lamps and then given her a candle. The young man who’d come up the day before the storm. Who’d split the old woman’s wood with his sword, like a barbarian. Who’d picked her up like a sack of flour and carried her up the tower and lit a fire nobody had lit in a hundred years. Who’d sat in the tower door all night with the wolves.

“In a wolf coat,” said Newish. “That’s what she said. A wolf-fur coat that stank to high heaven. Grey. Down to his knees.” She looked at Gabe. “And a shield. And he told her his name was Voyager. And she told him that’s not a name, that’s a job.” She paused. “He said yes, it is.”

Gabe didn’t say anything.

“Hana sat in his door,” said Newish, more quietly. “That night. There’s marks in the stone. Where his boots were. She put hers in them.”

Gabe turned round and walked out of the trapper’s hall into the snow without a word.

John went to go after him. Xav caught his arm.

“Give him a minute,” said Xav.

“He’s going to—“

“He’s going to stand outside and breathe. Give him a minute. Then go.”

John gave him a minute. When he went out, Gabe was standing at the stockade, looking up at the gold spark on the very top of the mountain, with the thin Hearthstead coat pulled round him, shivering. John stood next to him and didn’t say anything, which was the hardest thing he’d done since the white room. John always had something to say.

After a long time, Gabe said, “In a coat.”

“Yeah.”

“He’s up there in a wolf coat. Carrying old women up towers.” Gabe’s voice cracked. “And I’m down here in this.” He plucked at the thin Hearthstead wool. “Freezing. Doing nails.”

“You’re being careful,” said John.

“I’m being careful,” said Gabe. He said it the way you’d say the name of a medicine you had to take and hated.

John found out what his light could do on the second morning, in the high woods, by accident, the way everybody seemed to find out everything.

He’d been making the little lights since floor 1. Small floating points of white light, the size of a marble, that came off the end of his staff when he wanted them to and hung in the air wherever he put them. They lit up dark places: the space under a bridge, the inside of a burrow. He’d put one in the floor-1 well once, to see what was down there. Mostly they were just useful. Light where you needed light.

He hadn’t known they could see.

They were going into the high woods after a pack, the five of them, through the deep blue shade under the pines where it was hard to see more than twenty metres. And John did what he always did, which was send a light ahead to see what was there. He sent it the way you’d throw a ball, out ahead of them through the trees, bobbing along at head height. Then, because he was always looking at what his lights lit up, he looked at it.

And for a moment, he wasn’t standing where he was standing.

He was there. Fifty metres ahead, in the trees, at head height, looking out of the little light as if it were an eye. He could see the pine trunks going past. He could see the snow on the ground, and the shadows between the trees.

And he could see the wolves.

Five of them. Lying down in the snow behind a fallen trunk, white on white, perfectly still, ice-blue eyes on the place where John’s party would come round the corner in about ten seconds. Waiting.

John came back into his own head so fast he staggered.

“Stop.”

Everyone stopped.

“Five,” said John. He was breathing hard. “Behind the fallen tree. Up ahead, on the left. Lying down. Waiting for us.” He swallowed. “They’re not going round. They’re waiting. It’s an ambush.”

Xav looked at him.

“How do you know?”

“I saw them.” John held up his staff. “Through the light. I sent it ahead and I—I was in it, Xav. I could see what it saw.”

[ Unique skill: Mote
Send a little light ahead. See what it sees.
]

They went round the fallen tree the other way, from above, with John’s light hanging over the wolves like a lamp so everybody could see exactly where they were. They didn’t lose a drop of blood.

Afterwards, Rui Xiang, who learned everything fastest and was always slightly annoyed when somebody else learned something first, said, “That’s a very good skill.”

“I know,” said John, who had never in his life been modest about anything.

“That’s the best scouting skill on the floor.”

“I know.”

“I hate it,” said Rui Xiang, and meant it, and John grinned so hard his face hurt.

He’d been off to one side his whole life. Now he could send a bit of himself out ahead, wherever he wanted, and see everything. It was the most John thing that had ever happened to him.

He thought Declan would have laughed. Then made a joke about it. Then, later, quietly, told him it was cool.

That night he tried to see the tower.

He stood at the stockade after dark with his staff and sent a light up the mountain, as far as it would go. It went up over the trees and the white slopes, a tiny point climbing into the dark. He went with it, the way he’d gone with the first one, until the town was a smudge of firelight far below and the cold was in the light itself. He could feel it, faintly, like cold on his own face.

It got about two-thirds of the way. Then it began to thin, the way a voice thins at the edge of hearing. And just before it went out, at the very edge of what it could see, he saw the top of the pass. The tower, black against the painted sky, with its column of steady gold standing straight up out of it, unmoving, into the dark. And beside the tower, a tiny hut, with one small yellow candle burning in its window.

Then the light went out, and he was back at the stockade, blinking, with snow in his eyelashes.

Somebody keeps a light, Newish had said the old woman told her.

He stood there for a while, looking up at the gold spark on the mountain that everybody in Larchwick could see. He was off to one side again, he thought. The way he’d always been. Watching somebody else do something brave, a long way off, and not being able to help.

He went back inside.

Gabe went out on the evening of the second day, and didn’t say where.

John noticed because he noticed everything. He was at the board with Malcolm, reading the slopes for tomorrow. Out of the corner of his eye he saw Gabe get up from the fire, put on his thin coat, and go out of the trapper’s hall. Not toward the stockade gate into town. Toward the other gate. The one that led out onto the treeline, going east.

John looked at Xav. Xav was at the other end of the hall, talking to Yan, and hadn’t seen.

John didn’t go after him. Not straight away. He picked up his staff, went to the small window by the fire, and sent a light out through it. Out across the snow, after Gabe, high and quiet, a little white point in the dusk that nobody would notice. He sent it along the treeline, east, four hundred metres, to where a great dark pine stood at the edge of the wood like a building.

He saw Gabe through it.

Gabe was sitting under the pine. In the snow, by a ring of stones and an old cold fire, with his back to the trunk. Just sitting there. Not doing anything. He had his window open in front of him, and even from fifty metres away, through the little light, John could see what was on it. A whisper box. The word sent. And under the box, on the snow by the stones, a few pale things that might have been onion skins.

Gabe was looking at them. Just looking.

John pulled his light back and stood at the window for a moment. Then he picked up the spare fur Malcolm had bought in case, because Malcolm always bought two of everything in case. He went out after him.

It took him ten minutes to get there in the snow. Gabe didn’t look up when he came under the branches. He’d heard him.

John sat down on the other side of the cold fire. He didn’t say anything. He held out the spare fur across the stones, and after a moment, Gabe took it and put it round his shoulders without a word.

“He sat here,” said Gabe.

“Yeah.”

“Tiger said. With his back to the tree. Peeling onions. On his own.” He looked at the pale things in the snow. “His first night up here. In the snow. On his own.”

“Yeah.”

“I sent him a whisper,” said Gabe. “On the first night. At four in the morning. I asked him to tell me he was okay. One word.” He was looking at the open window, at the box, at the word under it. “It still says sent. Every morning I open it and it says sent.”

John didn’t say anything.

“He read it,” said Gabe. “I know he read it. He reads everything. He always has.” His voice cracked on the last word. “He read it, John, and he didn’t answer. He sat here, under this tree, the next night, and peeled onions, and he didn’t answer.”

John looked at the onion skins in the snow.

He didn’t know what to say. He’d always known what to say. On the stream, in the chat, off to one side, he’d always had the next line ready. He didn’t have one now.

So he said the only true thing he had.

“He peeled them properly,” he said.

Gabe looked at him.

“That’s what Tiger said. That’s what Mother Crane said. Properly. Top and tail.” John nodded at the skins. “He sat here on his own on the worst night of his life, and he still did it properly. That’s not a guy falling apart, Gabe. That’s a guy holding something together very carefully. With both hands.” He paused. “You don’t answer whispers when you’re doing that. You can’t. You’d drop it.”

Gabe was quiet for a long time.

“When did you get wise?” he said finally.

“I’ve always been wise. Nobody listens to me because I talk too much.”

Gabe made a sound that wasn’t quite a laugh.

It started to snow. Gently at first, big soft flakes coming down through the branches of the great pine, settling on the cold fire and the onion skins and the spare fur round Gabe’s shoulders.

Then there were boots in the snow behind them.

“It’s going to snow,” said Xav.

He was standing at the edge of the branches, with his hands in his pockets and snow already in his hair, looking at the two of them. He didn’t look surprised. He looked like someone who’d known exactly where they’d be.

“It’s already snowing,” said John.

“It’s going to snow more.” Xav looked at Gabe. “Get up.”

Gabe looked at him for a long moment. Then at the onion skins, half covered now in fresh white. Then at the word sent.

He closed the window.

He got up.

On the third morning, Wendy put a line in World.

[ World · Wendy: we tried the old stag yesterday. at the lake north of the pass. the ice is thin in places and you CANT SEE WHERE. salty went through to the waist. dont. ]

And under it, a moment later:

[ World · Salty: im fine ]

[ World · Wendy: he is NOT fine he is FROZEN ]

John read it at breakfast, and then he read it again, and then he put his spoon down.

“You can’t see where,” he said.

“Hm?” said Malcolm.

“The ice. Wendy says you can’t see where it’s thin.” John was already getting up. “Xav. The river. The frozen one, by the town. Can we go and look at it?”

They went and looked at it. The river ran past the bottom of Larchwick under a skin of white ice, smooth and solid-looking from bank to bank. John walked out onto the bank, crouched down, took his staff, and sent a light down.

Not across the ice. Into it.

He didn’t know if it would work. He just thought: light goes through ice. Everybody knows that. Ice is clear underneath the white. So he pushed a little mote of light down into the river ice, the way you’d push a coin into snow, and watched it go.

And the ice lit up.

Not all of it. In patches. Where the ice was thick, the light went into it and spread out and was swallowed, a faint dull glow. But where it was thin, over the deep cold channels where the river still moved underneath, the light went straight through. It lit the water below. Those patches glowed. Bright, blue-white, unmistakable, like windows in a dark house.

“Oh,” said Malcolm, behind him.

“There,” said John. He pointed. “And there. And that one. That’s thin. You’d go straight through that.” He stood up, grinning. “You can see where. You just need a light under it.”

Xav was looking at the glowing patches in the river with the expression he got when a plan was assembling itself in his head.

“How many can you make?” he said.

“Lights?” John thought about it. “As many as I’ve got mana for. Twenty, maybe. Thirty, if Malcolm gives me some.”

“I can give you some,” said Malcolm. “I’ll give you two lots. In case.”

Xav looked up at the mountain, toward the pass, toward the lake beyond it that none of them had seen. Then at Gabe, standing on the bank in Malcolm’s spare fur, looking at the lit-up ice with something in his face John hadn’t seen there since the white room.

Something that wasn’t sitting still.

“Then we go in two days,” said Xav. “When it’s on the board. When we’re ready.” He paused. “Carefully.”

Gabe looked at him.

“Carefully,” Gabe agreed.
