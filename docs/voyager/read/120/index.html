Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 4: The Second Row

Chapter 120

The Long Water

7,638 words · 33 min read

The door let them out onto a ledge in the sky.

That was what it felt like. A shelf of grey stone the width of a road, high on the face of a cliff, with the door behind them in the rock and nothing in front of them at all. No rail. No wall. Just the edge, and past the edge, a long way down, the sea.

All of it.

It went out from the foot of the cliff in every direction there was. Blue near the rocks, where the swell broke white and slow and came back. Darker further out. Then a long silver haze where it met the sky, so far away that his eyes kept trying to find the line and couldn’t. And the sky over it was enormous. Not a ceiling. Not a painted moon. Not the low grey lid of floor 8, or the cloud that floor 10 had stood on like a floor. A sky. Clouds in it, high and white, moving. Actually moving. Going somewhere on a wind he could feel on his face, from left to right, slowly, with their shadows sliding across the water underneath them like dark boats.

To the west there were islands. Small ones. Dozens. Scattered out across the blue like stepping stones somebody had thrown and not bothered to pick up, each with its own ring of white round it, getting smaller and greyer and further apart until the haze took them.

And it was loud. The sea. That was what he hadn’t been ready for. He’d known it would be there. He’d remembered it. He’d remembered the colour and the islands and the ledge. He hadn’t remembered the sound. A huge slow breathing that came up the cliff from everywhere at once, in and out, in and out, and didn’t stop, and wasn’t going to stop, and had been going on long before anybody came out of a door to listen to it.

[ Floor 11 · The Long Water ]

Behind him, thirty-five people came out of the door onto the ledge one at a time, and stopped.

He heard each of them stop. He could feel it, too, through the threads: thirty-five lights coming out of the dark of the stair and going still, all at once, the way a room goes still when somebody opens a window on a storm.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then Calvin said, very quietly, “Oh my days.”

“Don’t go near the edge,” said Rachael.

“I’m not going near the edge.”

“You’re going near the edge.”

“I’m looking at the edge,” said Calvin. “That’s different. That’s completely—“ He’d gone a metre closer to it. Azac reached out without looking and took hold of the back of his collar.

Newish was standing with both hands over her mouth. Hana had taken off her crown, which she never did, and was holding it against her chest. Salty was looking down at the swell breaking on the rocks below with the face of somebody working out how long it would take to fall. Maigo had sat down on the stone, right where he’d come out of the door, cross-legged, and closed his eyes, and was just listening.

And Xola walked past all of them to the very edge of the ledge, with Squiddles on her head, and stood there with her toes over nothing and her gills spread out behind her ears like a fan in the wind, and looked at the sea, and made a sound Ice had never heard her make. Not a squeal. Lower than that. A long, soft, shaking breath out.

“Babe,” she said. To nobody. To the sea. “Babe. Look at it. Look at all of it.”

“You’re a freshwater animal,” said Chi. Calm. From behind her. “Axolotls are freshwater. It’s a lake thing. You’d hate it.”

Xola turned round very slowly.

“I’m a demihuman,” she said, “babe,” with enormous dignity. And turned back to the sea. And then, after a moment, more quietly: “I’m going to go in it anyway.”

“Not off the ledge,” said Rachael.

“Not off the ledge,” Xola agreed. Squiddles had gone pale lilac and was clinging to the top of her head with every arm available.

Ice stood a little back from all of them, by the door, where the rock was, and looked at the sea, and let himself have it.

He’d come out of this door once. In the first year. Late in the spring, two months later than this, give or take. Thirty-four of them. He’d stood about here. He’d looked at this, and it had hit him the same way, in the chest, and somebody behind him had said oh my days, because Calvin had been alive then too. With one arm. And he’d said it anyway.

He’d been standing next to Gabe. Thea had been at the far end of the ledge with her own party, a long way away from him, the way she always was. He remembered looking down the ledge at her, once. Just once. And looking away again.

He didn’t have to look down the ledge this time.

She was standing next to him. Close enough that her sleeve touched his. The lantern was in her other hand, unlit, because it was morning, and she was looking at the sea with her mouth slightly open and her eyes very bright, and every few seconds, without seeming to know she was doing it, she looked at him instead. To check. That he was there. That he was seeing it too.

He was.

“You’ve seen this,” she said. Very quietly. Only to him.

“Once.”

“Was it like this?”

He thought about it.

“No,” he said. “I was looking at the wrong thing.”

Thea looked at him. And then she went faintly pink, and looked back at the sea, and bumped her shoulder against his arm, hard, the way you’d bump somebody for saying something stupid.

Behind them, thirty-four people very carefully didn’t see it.

Higu sat down on the ledge with her back to the cliff and took the rock out of her pack.

It was her day. She’d had it since the tarn. She set it on her knees, flat side up, and found the chalk, and looked round at all of them until one by one they stopped looking at the sea and looked at her instead. And then at him.

“Okay,” said Higu. “Ice. What do you remember?”

Everybody sat down.

It was a strange thing to watch. Thirty-five people on a ledge in the sky, in the wind, sitting down on cold stone with their packs off, in a rough half-ring with their backs to the cliff and their faces to him. The way they’d sat round the long table in the house for lights. The way they’d sat round him in the white room, on floor 8, in a dream that wasn’t a dream. Gabe sat down on his right, on the stone, with Hearthwall propped beside him. Thea sat down on his left with the lantern in her lap.

He didn’t sit. He stood with his back to the sea and looked at them.

“It’s listening,” he said.

“We know,” said Higu.

“I mean it. Everything I’ve said out loud since seven, it’s used. The Squall. The Wanting. The white Quiet on the high side. If I tell you where the stair is, it’ll move the stair. If I tell you what’s in the water, it’ll put something else in the water.”

“We know,” said Higu again. Patient. “That’s the point.” She held up the chalk. “You say it. It changes it. And then we go and look, and we write down what it changed.” She drew a line down the middle of the rock with the chalk, top to bottom. On the left side she wrote, in small neat capitals, REMEMBERED. On the right, she wrote nothing. “Then we know two things instead of one. What was there. And what it doesn’t want us to find.”

Chris, at the end of the ring, made a small pleased noise, like a man watching somebody else’s code compile first time.

Ice looked at the empty column.

“Okay,” he said.

And he told them.

He told them the way he’d have told them anything, a long time ago, on Earth, over a call at two in the morning: badly, out of order, with his hands. He told them that this island was called the Stair, by the people who lived on it, and that there was a path down the back of the cliff behind the door that went round the hill to the other side. That on the other side there was a town. Hythe. Built round a harbour shaped like a horseshoe, the houses all up on poles, on stilts, because of the water. That the people were called the tide-folk, or called themselves that, and that they lived by the tide the way the people of the Down had lived by the light at the top. That the tide here went out a long way, a very long way, and came back fast. Twice a day. That there was a bell on the harbour wall that rang when it turned.

“Nobody waits for anybody here,” he said. “That’s the thing I remember most. You’re on the boat when it goes, or you’re not. The tide goes, the boat goes. It doesn’t matter who you are.”

Higu wrote. TIDE. BELL. BOATS DON’T WAIT.

“There’s a ship,” he said. “The biggest one in Hythe. Three masts. She’s called the Patience. Her captain’s called Marrit. About thirty. She’s got a burn across the back of her left hand, from a lamp, she said, when she was a girl.” He stopped. “Her grandfather built the ship. He died in the spring, before we came. She said he’d have liked us.”

Higu wrote. PATIENCE. MARRIT. BURN, LEFT HAND. GRANDFATHER: DIED.

“We went west on her,” said Ice. “Across that.” He pointed back, over his shoulder, at the islands going away into the haze. “The Long Water. Two months. Island to island. There’s things on the islands. There’s things in the water. There’s something under it.” He paused. “The tide-folk call it the Undertow. It took the rudder off the Patience on the thirtieth day, and Marrit steered with a sweep oar for a week, standing up, in the rain, and wouldn’t let anybody else take it.” He paused again, longer. “Nobody’s going to die here. On this floor. I want you to know that. Nobody.”

Nobody said anything. He felt it go round the ring anyway. Thirty-five threads, going very slightly warmer. He didn’t look at Thea. He felt her hand move in her lap and settle on the lantern.

“And at the end,” he said, “at the end of the Long Water, there’s a light.”

Higu waited, chalk up.

“The Last Light,” said Ice. “That’s what they call it. On the last rock. Where the sea stops.” He thought about how to say it. “It just stops. It goes over the edge. Into white. There’s a lighthouse on the last rock, a white tower, and the door to the stair’s underneath it.” He spread his hands. “That’s it. That’s floor 11. A town, a ship, two months, a light.”

Higu wrote. LAST LIGHT. WHITE. LAST ROCK. STAIR UNDER.

Then she looked at the rock for a while. At the left column, full, all the way down, in her small neat capitals. And the right one, empty.

“Good,” she said. And put the chalk away.

“That’s it?” said Calvin.

“That’s it,” said Higu. “Now it’s heard it.” She stood up, and put the rock in her pack. “Now we go and look.”

Calvin lasted until the path.

Ice had known he would. He’d been watching him do it for half an hour. Not asking. Very visibly not asking. Sitting on the ledge with his arms round his knees while Ice talked about the Long Water, nodding, and then looking at Thea, and then at Ice, and then at Thea, with his whole face working on it like a dog with a door it isn’t allowed through.

They were halfway down the back of the cliff, on a path cut into the rock like a stair with no steps, single file, with the sea on their right a long way below and gulls going past at eye level shouting at them, when Calvin finally broke.

“So who—“

“Calvin,” said Rachael, from behind him, without looking up from her feet.

“I’m not asking anything. I’m not. I’m asking a general question. About a general—“ He waved a hand, nearly fell off the path, and was grabbed by Azac again. “I’m asking who kissed who. That’s not a secret. That’s history.”

There was a short, profound silence on the cliff path.

“I kissed him,” said Thea.

She said it perfectly calmly. She was in front of Ice on the path, with the lantern on her back, and she didn’t turn round.

“She didn’t,” said Ice.

Thea stopped walking and turned round.

“I did,” she said.

“You didn’t. I did.”

“You were crying.”

“So were you.”

“I was crying less,” said Thea. “I was crying a normal amount. And I put my hand on your face, and I—“

“You put your hand on my face,” said Ice, “and then I—“

“There it is,” said Gabe, two people behind, with enormous satisfaction. “There it is. Two grown adults. On a cliff.”

“Grown,” said Thea, outraged, about something, and turned round and kept walking, and the whole line behind her was laughing so hard that Hako had to sit down on the path. Xola had both hands over her face again. Calvin looked absolutely delighted with himself, which was the worst part.

“That’s not an answer,” said Calvin.

“That’s the answer,” said Drew, from the front, without turning round. “That’s the whole answer. They’re going to have that argument for a hundred floors.” He considered. “Somebody should keep a tally.”

“I’ll keep a tally,” said Chris instantly.

“Nobody’s keeping a tally,” said Thea.

“It’s on the rock,” said Higu, from the middle of the line, and Ice heard the chalk.

Hythe was there.

That was the first thing that went in the right-hand column. He saw it before anybody else did, because he knew where to look: the path came round the shoulder of the hill and there it was below them, all at once, the way he’d last seen it. A harbour shaped like a horseshoe, opening east. Houses all round it on long grey poles, stilts, dozens of them, with ladders down and rope walkways strung between them, and nets hung out on every rail to dry. Smoke going up. Brown sails. A stone wall across the mouth of the horseshoe with a gap in the middle and a squat tower at the end of it, and on the tower, small and black against the bright water, a bell.

Except there wasn’t any water.

He stopped on the path.

The harbour was empty. Completely. The sea had gone out of it. Where the blue should have been, there was mud, grey-brown and shining and enormous, running out through the gap in the wall and away across the bay toward the line where the sea had gone, a long way off, a mile, maybe more. Boats lay on their sides on it like dropped shoes. The poles of the houses went down and down into it, bare and dark and slick with weed all the way up to a line, far above Ice’s head, where the weed stopped and the wood went grey.

That was where the water came to.

He’d remembered the tide. He hadn’t remembered that. He’d come into Hythe the first time at high water, in the evening, with the harbour full and the lamps lit and boats coming in, and it had been the prettiest place he’d seen in a year. He’d never seen the bottom of it.

“Oh,” said Newish, beside him. “Oh, it’s all gone.”

“It comes back,” said Ice.

“All of it?”

“Up to the line.” He pointed. The weed on the poles. Higher than a house. Newish looked at the line, and then at the mud, and then at the line again, and he watched her work out how much water that was, all at once, twice a day, and go a bit pale.

There were people on the mud.

Lots of them. Small and brown and bent, a long way out, spread in a line across the bay where the water had gone, digging. Pulling things out of the mud and putting them in baskets on their backs. Barefoot. Some of them had children with them. One of the children saw the climbers on the hill and pointed, and the whole line looked up, one after another, and stopped digging.

And then, one after another, they went back to it.

That went in the column too. Ice watched Higu write it, walking, with the rock against her hip. THEY DIDN’T STOP.

The tide-folk didn’t stop for them in the town, either.

That was the thing he’d remember. Thirty-six strangers came down off the hill above Hythe in a long line, in armour, with weapons and companions and a lantern and a fox nobody could see, out of a door that the tide-folk knew was there and must have had stories about. And the town looked at them. Every face. On every ladder, on every rope walk, on every stilted porch with a net across its knees. Looked, and took them in, and went back to what it was doing.

There was a boat to be got ready. The tide was turning. Ice could feel it, somehow, in the air: something changing out on the mud, out past the wall, a long way off. The boats lying on their sides in the harbour had people round them, scraping, loading, checking ropes, and those people looked up at thirty-six climbers walking down the harbour steps onto the hard stone of the quay, and nodded, some of them, and kept scraping.

“They’re not scared of us,” said Calvin. Wondering.

“They’re busy,” said Chris.

“We came out of a door in a cliff.”

“So does the tide,” said a voice above them. “Twice a day. And it don’t stop for you either. Move your feet, you’re on my rope.”

Ice looked up.

There was a woman on the harbour wall. Sitting on it, with her legs hanging over the inside, a net across her knees and a wooden needle going in and out of it so fast he could barely see it. Brown and weathered. Dark hair tied back in a cord. A tide-cord at her wrist, knotted, the way they all had. About thirty.

He knew her face.

He stood on the quay with Calvin’s foot on her rope and Thea at his shoulder and looked at her, and he felt the thing he always felt. The pause. The breath before he spoke to anybody, every time, for two hundred and eighty-six days. She doesn’t know you. She’s never seen you. You crossed the Long Water with her and she sat up with you on the thirtieth night when the rudder went and told you about her grandfather, and she has never seen you in her life.

“Sorry,” said Calvin, and moved his foot.

“Ta,” said the woman, not looking up.

Ice looked at her hands.

At the left one, holding the net. Brown. Strong. Rope-scarred along the fingers, the way all their hands were.

And the back of it smooth.

No burn.

He stood there. He felt Thea, beside him, look at the hand too, and then at him. She’d heard it. On the ledge. A burn across the back of her left hand, from a lamp, when she was a girl.

“Marrit?” he said.

The needle stopped. The woman looked down at him. Properly, for the first time. Dark eyes, wary, sharp.

“Who’s asking?”

“Nobody,” said Ice. He made himself smile. “Somebody told me your name. On the way down. I’m—“ He stopped. Ice. Declan. Voyager. “We’re looking for a ship.”

Marrit looked at him for a long moment. Then at the thirty-five behind him on the quay. Then she laughed, short, not unkindly, and went back to the net.

“Then you’ve come nine days too soon,” she said. “Ask my grandfather. He’s on the slip.” She jerked her head along the wall. “He’ll tell you. He tells everybody.”

Behind him, he heard the chalk.

MARRIT: YES. NO BURN.

The slip was at the inside curve of the horseshoe, where the hill came down to the harbour. A long ramp of stone and timber running down into the mud, greased, with wooden cradles along it. And in the cradles, so big that the climbers walking round the end of the quay all stopped at once, like a line of people walking into a wall, a ship.

He’d remembered her. He’d sailed in her for two months. He’d slept in her hold and been sick over her rail and stood on her deck in the rain while Marrit fought the sea with an oar. He knew every line of her.

He’d never seen her out of the water.

She was enormous. Out of the water you could see all of her, the whole deep curve of the hull going down and down into the cradles, dark tarred wood, three bare masts going up and up into the moving sky. No sails yet. No rigging. Her name across her stern in white letters, new paint, so new he could smell it. PATIENCE. And under her, on the slip, propped on blocks, there were adzes and mallets and buckets of pitch, and wood shavings everywhere, curled and pale, blowing about on the stone in the wind.

She’d never been in the sea.

Somebody was sitting on an upturned bucket at the foot of the slip, under her bow, with a mug in his hands.

An old man. Very old. Older than Mother Crane. Older, even, than the one on the chair of rock in the cave behind the falls in Wendmere, who he’d thought was the oldest person he’d ever see. He was small and brown and folded, the way very old people fold, as if he’d been put away in a drawer for a long time and only just taken out. White hair. Bare feet on the shavings. Hands round the mug like rope round a post. He was looking up at the ship over his head the way you’d look at somebody asleep.

He looked down, slowly, when thirty-six people came round the end of the quay and stopped.

“Stair-folk,” he said.

He didn’t sound surprised. He sounded the way you’d sound if a bird you’d heard about all your life landed on your windowsill.

“Well, I never,” said the old man. “There’s a song about you.” He looked at them all for a long time, one at a time, the way the town had, with eyes that had gone pale and milky at the edges. “My grandmother used to sing it. I never thought I’d see you.” He thought about it. “Well. I did think it. I hoped it. You get to my age, you hope all kinds of daft things.” He lifted the mug at them. “Corran. Of the Patience. That’s her.” He tipped his head back at the ship. “Eleven years. Every board. She swims on the big tide. Nine days.”

Ice stood on the stone with the shavings blowing round his boots.

Her grandfather built the ship. He died in the spring, before we came. She said he’d have liked us.

He’d never met him. He’d never known his name. He’d known him as a sentence in the dark on the thirtieth night, with the rudder gone. He’d have liked you. That was all. That was all of him there’d been, the first time.

And here he was. On a bucket. With a mug.

He heard the chalk stop.

He didn’t have to look round to know that every one of them had heard him on the ledge. That they were all standing there, behind him, thirty-five of them, looking at an old man who should be dead and wasn’t. Doing the sum. Two months later than this, give or take. He died in the spring.

Nine days.

“She’s beautiful,” said Thea. Into the silence. Gently. Stepping forward, past Ice, with the lantern on her back. “She’s really beautiful.”

Corran looked at her. And his whole folded face lit up.

“She is, isn’t she,” he said. “She is. Come and look at her bow. Nobody ever looks at the bow. Come here, lass. Mind the pitch.”

And Thea went and looked at the bow.

Rachael found Ice afterwards, at the edge of the slip, while Corran was showing Gabe and Salty and Jared the iron in the Patience’s keel and the three of them were making the noises blacksmiths make at good iron.

She stood beside him. She didn’t say anything for a bit. Then she put her hand on his arm, flat, the way she put her hands on everybody, every morning, checking. Just for a second.

“There’s nothing wrong with him, Dec,” she said.

He looked at her.

“I had a look,” said Rachael. “When he shook my hand. I couldn’t help it. I always do.” She was looking at the old man, not at him. “His heart’s tired. His lungs are tired. All of him’s tired. There’s nothing in him. Nothing I can find. Nothing I can mend.” She was quiet. “He’s not ill. He’s just old.”

“I know,” said Ice.

“I can’t do anything about old.”

“I know.”

Rachael didn’t say anything else. She didn’t ask how he knew the old man would die in the spring, or what spring, or whose. She just stood beside him with her arms folded and watched Corran laugh at something Salty said, with his hand on the keel of the ship he’d built.

“Nine days,” said Rachael eventually.

“Nine days,” said Ice.

“Well,” said Rachael. “Then we’d better help him get her in the water.” And went to tell the others.

This one isn’t a fight. Alea, on his shoulder. Not a window. Just her voice, very quiet, so close to his ear that it was almost inside it. There’s nothing to stand in front of. You know that.

I know.

I’m just saying it so you’ve heard it.

He’d heard it.

Corran gave them the long loft.

It was the oldest building in Hythe, he said, which meant it was the only one that had never fallen down: a net-loft on stilts at the back of the harbour, one long room under a high roof, with nets hung from every beam like grey curtains and a smell of tar and salt and old rope so strong that Calvin walked in, breathed in, and walked straight back out again. There was a ladder up from the mud and a wide porch along the front, over the harbour, with steps going down from it into nothing.

“It used to be for the big nets,” said Corran. “Nobody uses the big nets now. Nobody’s got the arms.” He looked at Salty, and Gabe, and Azac, and Hako, with frank interest. “You’ve got the arms.”

“We’ll help with the ship,” said Deston.

“I know you will,” said Corran. “Marrit’ll make you. She’s worse than me.” He sat down on the porch with his back against the wall and closed his eyes in the sun. “Pay me in that. It’s what I want.”

Marrit, who’d come along the wall behind them with her net over her shoulder, looked at the thirty-six climbers moving into her grandfather’s loft with an expression Ice had seen on her face once before, on the first morning, a long time ago, on the deck of a ship that was still a ship. The face of somebody revising a cargo list.

“You’ll pull,” she said. “On the big tide. All of you. She’s heavy, and the slip’s old, and if she sticks halfway she’ll break her back on it, and then that’s eleven years in the mud.”

“We’ll pull,” said Deston.

“Good,” said Marrit. And then, to her grandfather, not quietly: “Song about you. You never sang me any song.”

“I sang you plenty of songs,” said Corran, with his eyes closed.

“Not that one.”

“That one’s not for you,” said the old man, comfortably. “That one’s for them.”

The tide came in at the end of the afternoon.

He’d been waiting for it. He hadn’t known he was. He was on the porch of the long loft with a pan on a little iron stove Corran had lent him, cooking the fish the mud-diggers had sold him for three of the spare knives Salty had made on floor 10, and he heard it before he saw it. A change in the breathing. The sea, a long way off across the bay, getting louder.

Then the mud started to shine.

It came in across the bay in a long thin sheet, silver, so flat and fast that it didn’t look like water, it looked like light sliding over the ground. It came through the gap in the harbour wall, filling the channels first, running into every dip in the mud, joining up. The boats lying on their sides began, one by one, to sit up. Slowly. Like people waking. The water came up the stilts of the houses an inch at a time and then not an inch, faster, much faster, a hand’s width while he turned a fish, a forearm’s length while he found the salt.

Everybody came out onto the porch to watch.

Nobody said anything. They stood along the rail and on the steps going down and watched the harbour fill up under them. The weed on the poles going under. The ladders going under. The bottom rungs of the loft’s own ladder, and then the next ones, and then the water was lapping at the foot of the steps, black and green and moving, with the light on it, and there were boats floating where there’d been mud, and the people of Hythe were in them, poling out toward the gap, because the tide was here and you went when the tide went.

“It doesn’t wait,” said Newish. Very softly. Like a discovery.

“No,” said Ice.

On the harbour wall, on the squat tower at the end, somebody rang the bell.

Once. Deep. It went out across the water and came back off the cliff.

The tide had turned. It would go out again now, all of it, back across the bay to wherever it went, and come back again in the night, and go again in the morning, and keep doing it after every one of them had climbed out of this floor and gone. It had been doing it before Corran was born. It would be doing it after.

The fishing boats were going out through the gap in a line, brown sails filling.

And then, from somewhere across the harbour, a different bell.

Small. High. Rung fast. Three times, then three times again. Not from the tower. From one of the houses on stilts, on the far side of the horseshoe, where a lamp had come on in a window.

Every boat in the harbour stopped.

He saw it happen. All the way across the water. Every pole, every oar, every face, turning toward the lamp in the window. And then, from somewhere, one voice started singing. A woman’s, old and cracked and carrying. One line. He couldn’t make out the words. And the next boat took it up, and the next, until the whole harbour was singing one long line together across the water, rising and falling like the swell.

Then they stopped. All together. As if they’d been told.

And picked up their oars, and went out on the tide.

“What was that?” said Hana. She’d come to the rail beside him. Her crown was on. Her voice wasn’t quite steady.

“I don’t know,” said Ice.

He didn’t. He’d never heard it. Not in two months, the first time.

“A baby,” said Corran, from behind them. He was still sitting against the wall of the loft, where he’d been sitting all afternoon, with his eyes closed. “That’s the little bell. That’s Ebba’s girl. Due today.” He smiled without opening his eyes. “She’ll be a good one. On the turn of the tide. They always are.”

Rachael, at the end of the porch, had stood up straight. Ice felt her thread jump. She was looking across the harbour at the lamp with her hands already half up, the way they went.

“She’s got people,” said Corran, as if he’d seen it. “Ebba’s got her mother and her aunties and old Sab, and Sab’s brought more babies than you’ve had hot dinners.” He opened one milky eye and looked at Rachael. “They’ve done this a long time, love. Long before stair-folk. Sit down.”

Rachael looked at him.

Then, slowly, she put her hands down.

She didn’t sit down. Rachael didn’t sit down. But she leaned on the rail beside Ice and watched the lamp in the window across the water, and he felt her thread go quiet. Not cold. Just quiet. The way a light goes when nobody needs it.

“Huh,” said Rachael.

He knew what she meant.

On floor 1 the people had stood at the same corners every day and said the same words. On floor 4 the dead had stayed where they fell, and it had been the first floor where anything stayed. On floor 9 a whole city had sat up all night watching a light. And on floor 11, a girl had been born on the turn of the tide in a house on stilts, and nobody had needed thirty-six climbers for any part of it. Not to save her. Not to watch. Not to know.

The world didn’t need them. It just went on.

He turned back to the stove. The fish was nearly burned.

They ate on the porch with the harbour going dark under them.

The tide went out. Of course it did. He watched it go while he ate: the water slipping back down the stilts the way it had come up, quietly, leaving the weed hanging and dripping in the last of the light, and the mud coming up out of it again, shining. The boats that hadn’t gone out lay down on their sides again, one by one, like people going back to sleep.

Higu sat on the top step with the rock on her knees and a lamp beside her, and wrote, and nobody watched her do it. The right-hand column was nearly as full as the left now. Ice had seen it on the way past. HYTHE: YES. STILTS: YES. BELL: YES. TIDE: MORE. And then: PATIENCE: NOT LAUNCHED. MARRIT: YES. NO BURN. CORRAN: ALIVE.

Nobody had said anything about the last one.

He’d watched them not say it all evening. Watched it go round the porch. Kei, sitting very still, looking at the old man asleep against the wall with his mug in his lap. Maigo, who’d sat down near Corran on purpose and had been quiet beside him for an hour. Rachael. Deston, who’d looked at Ice once, across the porch, with the face he’d had in the white room, when he looked about thirteen, and then away. They’d all done the sum. He died in the spring before we came. And then they’d looked at the ship on the slip, and the nine days, and none of them had said it.

“He’s going to see her swim,” said Gabe, beside him. Not quietly enough.

Ice didn’t say anything.

“He is, Dec,” said Gabe. “Nine days. We’ll get her in the water. You’ll see.”

He died in the spring, before we came. It was the only fact he had about Corran. One sentence. And he’d said it on a ledge, out loud, to something that was listening.

He thought: I told it he dies.

He didn’t say that either.

Thea had the watch with him. It was on the rock.

Nobody had commented on that, either. Higu had written it at supper, in the corner where the rota went, in her small neat capitals, and gone back to her fish. PORCH: ICE, THEA. 10–12. And Xola had looked at it and opened her mouth and then shut it again so hard that her teeth clicked, and Squiddles had gone pink.

So they sat on the top step of the long loft’s porch with the lantern between them, lit, low, and the harbour below them all mud and stars. The sea was a long way out. He could hear it. The breathing. Far off, and patient.

“You said he died,” said Thea.

She said it very quietly. Her shoulder was against his.

“I know.”

“On the ledge. With it listening.”

“I know.” He looked at the old man’s shape, against the wall, behind them through the open door of the loft, with a blanket over him that Maigo had put there. “It’s the only thing I knew about him. He was a sentence. He’d have liked you. That was all he was.” He was quiet. “And now he’s on a bucket, with a mug. And he’s got nine days, and a ship, and a song his grandmother sang.”

“Nine days isn’t a long time,” said Thea.

“No.”

“But it’s not nothing.”

He looked at her.

“He’s going to be in it,” said Thea. “Whatever happens. Whatever it does. He’s going to be in the nine days. Not in a sentence.” She put her hand on his arm, above the wrist, in the place. “That’s not you, Ice. That’s not something you did. That’s just what’s true now.”

He didn’t say anything for a while.

“There’s no burn on her hand,” he said eventually.

“I saw.”

“It hasn’t happened yet. In two months. Whatever it was. A lamp. She said a lamp, when she was a girl, but she’s not a girl, she’s—“ He stopped. “So either she lied to me, the first time. Or it hasn’t happened yet. Or it never happens now.”

“Or she didn’t want to tell you how she got it,” said Thea. “And a lamp was easier.”

He looked at her.

“People do that,” said Thea. “You did that. For two hundred and eighty-six days.” She almost smiled. “I’m just a good cook.”

He laughed, very quietly, so as not to wake anybody.

“Higu’s going to write no burn on the rock,” said Thea, “and then in two months she’s going to write something else. That’s what it’s for. That’s what true is. It’s just what you find out when you go and look.” She bumped his shoulder. “You’re the remembered. We’re the true. Chris said.”

“Chris said documentation.”

“Chris says a lot of things.”

Alea, on his shoulder, made a small amused noise that Thea couldn’t hear, and then, after a moment, he felt her decide something, and she stepped down off his shoulder onto the step between them, beside the lantern. Small. Hand-sized. The way she was in front of other people. Just for a moment. Just enough. A pale teal shimmer in the lantern light, with her tail round her feet.

Thea looked down at her. Her whole face changed.

“Hello,” she said. Very softly.

Alea looked up at her, and blinked her pinprick eyes, slowly, once. And then sat there between them on the step, with her back against the warm glass of the lantern, and watched the harbour.

“She says hello,” said Ice. “She says she likes the stilts.”

“Tell her I like the stilts too.”

“She heard you.”

“I know,” said Thea. “I’m being polite.”

They sat there, the three of them, with the harbour empty under them and the stars very big. On the far side of the horseshoe, the lamp was still on in Ebba’s window. Somebody in there was singing. Very low. The same line, over and over, the one the whole harbour had sung on the turn.

He was thinking about Corran. About nine days. About the tide, going out and coming back, without asking anybody. About the first time, two months later, at high water, with the lamps lit and the harbour full, and the prettiest place he’d seen in a year, and none of this. Not the mud. Not the old man. Not the bell. He’d come in at the top of the tide and never seen the bottom of it.

He was thinking that when the light came on.

It was to the east.

Out past the mouth of the harbour, past the wall, past the bell tower, far out across the bay, where the mud ended and the sea began, and then further, much further. Low on the horizon. A point of light. White. It hadn’t been there a second ago.

He was on his feet before he knew it.

It went out.

He stood on the step with his heart going.

It came on again. Short. Off. Short. Off. Short. Off.

And then on, and held, a long slow beat, the length of a breath.

And off.

And then again. Three short. One long. Three short. One long. Steady. Patient. Like the sea. Turning out there on the edge of the dark like it had been turning for a hundred years.

He knew it.

He knew it the way he knew Gabe’s voice, and the weight of a sword, and the shape of the white room. He’d seen it come up out of the haze at the end of two months on the Long Water, at dawn, on the sixty-first day, with Marrit at the wheel saying there, and the whole deck going quiet. He’d lain in his bunk for three nights under the window of the keeper’s house and watched it go round on the ceiling. Three short and a long. He’d counted it to sleep. He’d counted it on other floors, years later, without meaning to, in the dark, in tents. Three short and a long. Three short and a long.

The Last Light.

A day’s sail to the east. Where there was nothing.

“Ice,” said Thea. She’d stood up beside him. She’d seen his face. “Ice. What is it.”

Behind them, in the loft, somebody was moving. Corran. The old man had woken. He’d come to the door with the blanket round his shoulders, and he was standing on the porch in his bare feet, looking out across the harbour at the light. And beside him, from the ladder, Marrit, who must have been coming to check on him, with a lamp in her hand. And both of them had gone completely still.

“That’s the Teeth,” said Marrit. Her voice was very flat. “That’s out on the Teeth.”

“There’s no light on the Teeth,” said Corran.

He said it slowly. Like a man reading out a fact. Like a line on a slate.

“There’s no light on the Teeth,” he said again. “There’s never been a light on the Teeth. There’s nothing on the Teeth. It’s rock and wreck and nobody goes.” He was holding the doorframe. “Eighty-nine years. I’ve looked at that horizon every night of my life. There’s never been anything there.”

Three short. One long.

All along the porch, people were waking up. Coming to the door. Coming to the rail. Standing in their blankets, looking east. He heard Higu say his name. He heard Drew say everyone in his calm voice, the way he said it before anything. He felt thirty-five threads come awake one after another and turn, all of them, toward him.

He didn’t look away from the light.

I said white, he thought. On the ledge. I said a white tower, on the last rock, at the end of the Long Water. I said the Last Light.

You didn’t say that.

Alea. On the step, beside the lantern. Not small any more. He felt her. Every hair of her standing up.

Say what.

The rhythm. Her voice was very quiet. You said white. You said a tower. You said the Last Light and the last rock and the stair underneath. You never said three and a long. You never said it on the ledge. You’ve never said it out loud on any floor, in this life. Not once. I’d know. I keep the list.

He stood very still.

It’s not building it out of what you said, said Alea. Not this time.

Three short. One long.

It’s building it out of what you remember.
