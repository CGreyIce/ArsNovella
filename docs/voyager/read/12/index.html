Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 1: Thirty-Six Circles

Chapter 12

What's Underneath

3,193 words · 14 min read

Rachael woke up on the third morning and lay very still, listening to herself breathe.

It was a habit. She’d had it as long as she could remember. Before she opened her eyes, before she moved, she lay there and took an inventory. Throat: scratchy, or not? Chest: tight, or not? Head, joints, the place behind her eyes where a fever started. Twenty-six years of mornings, and every one of them had started with that list, because the day you skipped it was the day you found out too late.

Throat: fine.

Chest: fine.

Head: fine. Joints: fine. Behind her eyes: nothing.

She opened her window anyway, the way she’d been doing every morning since the white room, to check.

Condition: none
]

None. Three mornings in a row.

She lay there and looked at it. She didn’t trust it, exactly. She didn’t trust anything that floated. But it had said none three mornings running, and her throat agreed with it, and so did her chest. Yesterday she’d run the whole length of a valley after Calvin and not once thought about her lungs until she was at the top of it, and then only to be surprised.

She was going to have to get used to this. She didn’t know how. Nobody gave you a leaflet for getting better.

She shut the window and got up.

The bonesetter’s house was at the top of the high street, behind a blue door with a hand painted on it. Rachael found it by asking Mother Crane, who’d looked at her over the porridge pot with those sharp little eyes and said, “Old Sedge? What d’you want with Sedge?”

“I need to learn how bones work.”

Mother Crane had looked at her a moment longer. Then she’d nodded, as if that were the most reasonable thing a Climber had ever said to her. “Blue door. Knock loud. She’s deaf in the left.”

Old Sedge was tiny and ancient and very brown, with forearms like a blacksmith’s and no patience whatsoever. She listened to Rachael explain, at length, with her good ear turned forward and her arms folded. Rachael told her what had happened at the top of the valley, how the heal had splashed off when she aimed it at the wound and gone in when she aimed it underneath. And then Sedge said:

“You’re a healer.”

“I’m a Cleric. Apparently.”

“I don’t know what that is. But you’re a healer. You’ve got the look.” She sniffed. “You’ve been on the wrong end of it, haven’t you? A long time.”

Rachael said nothing.

“Thought so. It’s always the ones who’ve been on the wrong end.” Sedge turned round and went into the back of the house. “Come on, then.”

There was a skeleton in the back room.

A real one, old, yellow-brown, hung up on a wire from a hook in the ceiling, every bone held to the next with little loops of copper. Rachael stopped in the doorway.

“That was my teacher,” said Sedge comfortably. “Left herself to the craft. Very generous of her. Come here.”

And for two hours, Old Sedge taught her bones.

Not the names. Sedge didn’t know the names Rachael knew from hospital charts and the backs of her own scans. She had her own: the long-bones, the knuckle-bones, the cradle for the ribs. But she knew how they went together. How they broke. How they knit, slowly, from the outside in, the body laying down new bone across the gap like frost across a pane. How a break that wasn’t held straight would knit crooked, and stay crooked forever, and you’d walk with it.

“You held the cup,” Sedge said, when Rachael told her about the cold water and the two halves fitting. “That’s right. That’s all a bonesetter does, girl. The body does the mending. You hold it straight while it works.” She tapped Rachael’s breastbone with one hard finger. “You don’t mend anything. You agree with it.”

Rachael stood in the back room of a bonesetter’s house in a magic tower, with a dead woman’s skeleton turning very slowly on its wire beside her, and felt something rearrange itself behind her ribs.

Every doctor she’d ever had had tried to fix her. Twenty-six years of it: pills, tests, careful voices, a long, expensive, exhausting war fought on the territory of her own body, with her body as the enemy. Nobody had ever agreed with it. Nobody had ever held it straight and let it work.

“Can I come back?” she said.

Old Sedge looked at her for a long moment with her good ear forward.

“Bring me eggs,” she said. “And don’t tell that Crane woman I’m being nice to you. She’ll never let me hear the end of it.”

Somebody banged on the blue door before Rachael could answer.

It was a woman with a boy of about eight. The boy was grey-faced and silent, holding his right arm against his chest with his left, the way you hold something that’s stopped belonging to you. He’d fallen off the mill wall, the woman said, very fast. He’d been told and told.

“Wrist,” said Old Sedge, without even looking properly. She looked at Rachael. “Well? You wanted bones.”

Rachael knelt in front of the boy. She knew that face. She’d worn it in a hundred waiting rooms: the face of someone who’s been brave for so long they’ve forgotten how to be anything else.

“Hi,” she said. “I’m Rachael. I’m going to hold your arm very still. Is that okay? It’ll hurt for a second, and then it’ll stop. I promise.”

The boy looked at her. Then he nodded, once.

She held it while Sedge felt the break with her hard brown fingers and set it with one quick pull. The boy screamed, short and high, and Rachael didn’t let go. Then she reached underneath, past the swelling and the hurt, to the place where the two ends of the little bone had come back together. She held them straight. She agreed with them. The cold came up her arm, and with it a bright, small echo of pain in her own right wrist, and she let it come.

The boy stopped crying. He looked down at his arm. Then he looked up at her, with wet lashes and enormous eyes, as if she’d done something nobody had ever done before in the history of the world.

“Thank you,” said his mother. She was crying a bit herself. “Thank you, Climber.”

Rachael didn’t know what to say. Nobody had ever thanked her for being the one who held still. She’d been on the other side of that sentence her whole life.

“It was mostly her,” she said, nodding at Sedge.

“It was mostly him,” said Old Sedge. “Bones mend. We just hold them.” She looked at Rachael over the boy’s head. “Eggs. Tomorrow.”

The cost was the thing she hadn’t expected.

She worked it out over the third day, out on the north fields with the others, healing every scrape and knock and bruise anybody would let her near, which was mostly Azac. Azac took the hits. That was what he did; he’d always done it, in games, back home, ever since Declan had moved away and stopped logging on. In this world he did it with a sword and a lot of narration, and he kept getting knocked into bushes. And every time she healed him, it cost her.

Not just the mana. The window showed the mana, a blue bar going down. It was something else. Every time she reached into somebody and held something straight, a little of the cold came back up the line into her. And, for half a heartbeat, she felt it: an echo of the hurt she was holding. A sting in her own ribs where Azac’s had cracked. A dull ache in her left forearm, exactly where Calvin’s had snapped.

She didn’t tell anyone. She was used to not telling anyone things about her body. It was the oldest skill she had.

But at lunch, sitting on a wall eating Mother Crane’s bread, she caught Deston watching her. She knew that look. She’d known him a long time.

“What,” she said.

“You keep rubbing your arm.”

“I’m cold.”

“It’s warm.”

“I run cold.”

“You’ve never run cold in your life. You used to make me turn the air-con down.” He looked at her steadily. “Which arm did Calvin break?”

She didn’t answer. She didn’t have to.

“Does it hurt you?” said Deston, very quietly. “When you heal it?”

“For a second,” said Rachael. “Then it’s fine. It’s an echo. It’s not mine.” She looked at her bread. “Old Sedge says you don’t fix anything. You agree with it. I think when you agree with something, you have to feel what it’s agreeing about. Just for a second. Like shaking hands.” She shrugged. “It’s fine, Dest.”

“You’d tell me if it wasn’t.”

“No,” said Rachael, honestly. “I wouldn’t. But it is.”

Deston looked at her for a while. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed, short and tired, and shook his head.

“Okay,” he said.

She’d always liked that about Deston. He never made you justify yourself twice.

But she was watching him too.

She’d been watching him since the white room, and she knew she wasn’t the only one, because Gabe watched him the same way. Deston’s right hand kept going to his shirt pocket. Not all the time. Just when something happened. When the window said Floor 1 has been cleared. When Mother Crane said the onion boy. When Calvin’s arm went the wrong way at the top of the valley. Every time, the hand drifted up and pressed flat against the pocket, the way you’d press your hand on your chest to feel your heart.

That evening, after the Spoon, she found him on the step outside under the switched-on stars, and stood next to him with her hands in her coat pockets, a polite distance away, and said:

“What’s in the pocket?”

He didn’t pretend not to understand. He never did. He looked at her for a moment, and something in his face gave way, as if he’d been waiting for someone to ask.

He unbuttoned it and took out a white card and handed it to her.

On the front: DESTON. On the back, in handwriting that had gone faintly shiny where the ink had dried:

be careful, train longer on the 3rd floor - D.

She read it three times. She didn’t know anybody’s handwriting well enough to say whose it was. She’d met Declan through Deston, at seventeen, years ago, back when everyone wrote everything on their phones. But she didn’t need to know the handwriting. She read be careful, and train longer, and the little dash before the D, and she knew.

“He was scared,” she said.

Deston didn’t answer.

“Not of us. For us.” She looked at the card again. “You don’t write be careful to people you’re running away from. You write it to people you think are going to get hurt. And you don’t write train longer on the third floor unless you know something’s waiting on the fourth one.” She held the card out to him. “He knows something’s waiting on the fourth one, Dest.”

“Yeah.”

“How does he know that?”

“I don’t know.”

“Who else has seen this?”

“Gabe. Now you.”

She nodded slowly. She didn’t ask why he hadn’t shown the others. She knew why. She’d watched thirty-five frightened people pass bowls down a long table, and she’d watched them panic when one small window opened in the corner of their eye. She knew what be careful would do to them from a man who’d vanished. It would do the opposite of what it said.

“When are you going to tell them?” she said.

“When it matters,” said Deston. “When we get to the third floor. Before anyone even thinks about the fourth.”

“And if he’s wrong?”

“Then we wasted some time training.” Deston put the card back in his pocket and buttoned it. “And if he’s right—“

He didn’t finish. He didn’t need to. Rachael thought about the top of the valley: Calvin lying on the grass under a ram, and the sound his arm had made. She’d been the one who got there. If she’d been a minute later. If she hadn’t run.

She thought about a fourth floor where something was waiting that a man who’d cleared the first floor alone on day one thought was worth a warning.

“Then we do what it says,” she said.

“Yeah.”

She was quiet for a bit, looking up at the stars that had all come on at once.

“I asked him something once,” she said. “Declan. Years ago. Something really embarrassing. Something I couldn’t ask anyone else, because they’d have laughed, or made it weird.” She didn’t say what. She never would. “And he didn’t laugh. He just answered. Properly. Like it was a normal question. Like I wasn’t stupid for asking.” She paused. “He was always the one you could ask anything. Not because he knew. Because he’d never make you feel like you shouldn’t have asked.”

“I know,” said Deston.

“So if he wrote you be careful and didn’t say why,” said Rachael, “it’s because he can’t say why. Not because he won’t.” She looked at him. “That’s all. I said that in the white room, and I’ll keep saying it. Whatever he’s doing, he’s got a reason.”

Deston looked at her for a long time.

“Thanks, Rae,” he said.

“Don’t make it a thing.”

“I’m not.”

“You’re making a face.”

“This is just my face,” said Deston, and she laughed, and that was the end of it. They went in.

Dennis, meanwhile, had discovered fraud.

“It’s not fraud,” he said the next morning, offended. “It’s a glamour. It’s an illusion spell. I’m a Mage. This is literally my job.”

He was sitting at the long table in the Spoon with a single copper coin on the wood in front of him and his hand held flat over it, frowning with concentration. As Rachael watched, the coin doubled. There it was, and there, beside it, a second one: identical, gleaming slightly too much, the way a thing in a photograph gleams.

“See?” said Dennis. “Two coppers.”

“It’s one copper,” said Rachael.

“It’s one copper and an idea of a copper.”

“That’s fraud, Dennis.”

“It’s an idea.”

Mother Crane came down the table with the porridge. She glanced at the two coins on the table without breaking stride, picked up the real one between her finger and thumb, and put it in her apron pocket.

“Forty years I’ve been counting coppers, boy,” she said, and went back to the kitchen. The second coin flickered and went out like a candle.

Dennis stared after her.

“I’m going to adopt that woman,” he said.

“She’s eighty,” said Calvin, through porridge.

“Then she’ll need someone to look after her, Calvin. It’s called planning.”

But he kept at it, all morning, out on the fields, with a doggedness Rachael had only ever seen him bring to a sale. He doubled coins. He doubled stones. He doubled a stick, and then Azac, which made Azac shout, and then, just before lunch, with his face screwed up so tightly that his nose went white, he doubled Calvin.

A second Calvin stood on the grass three metres to the left of the first one. Same clothes, same grin, same twin daggers, gleaming slightly too much. It held for maybe four seconds.

And the Woolback that had been charging the real Calvin swerved, mid-charge, toward the fake one. It went straight through him like running through a curtain, and hit a dry-stone wall at full speed, head first, with a sound like a dropped sack of potatoes.

It lay there with its legs in the air.

Everyone looked at it.

“Did you mean to do that?” said Azac.

“Obviously,” said Dennis, who obviously hadn’t.

He read it out loud, twice. Then he looked up at the rest of them with an expression of total and slightly frightening delight.

“Unique,” he said. “It says unique. That means it’s mine. Nobody else has it. I have a unique skill.” He looked at Calvin. “Do you have a unique skill, Calvin?”

“No.”

“Rae?”

“No.”

“Dest? Azac?” Dennis spread his hands. “Treasurer. And unique. I’m carrying this party.”

“You doubled a guy into a wall,” said Rachael.

“I doubled a sheep into a wall. The guy’s fine.” He looked at the Woolback, still lying upside down against the wall. “That’s the boss, though, isn’t it? That’s how you do it. The ram. The thing at the top. It runs at things. If it runs at the wrong thing—“

Deston had gone quiet. He was looking at the Woolback, and at the wall, and at the place where the fake Calvin had been, with his thinking face on: the one where he was putting something together and not saying anything until it was done.

“It runs at things,” he said slowly. “And at the top of the valley there’s a ring of big stones.” He looked at Calvin. “When you saw it. Was there one with a crack in it?”

Calvin blinked. “Yeah. A big one. Right up the side.”

“Something hit that stone,” said Deston. “Hard enough to crack it. Recently.” He was quiet for a second. “I think we’ve been shown how to do it.”

“By who?” said Calvin.

Nobody answered. Nobody needed to.

That night the rankings said Voyager · Lv 9 · Floor 2. Rachael looked at it for a long time sitting on the bed that was hers on alternate nights, while Dennis, whose night it wasn’t, snored on the floor.

Level nine. Alone. On a floor none of them had seen.

Only he hasn’t got anyone running up the valley after him, she’d said to Calvin at the top of the Fold. She’d meant it as a joke. Mostly. It had come out true, the way things did sometimes when Declan was involved.

She thought about the cold water, and the two halves of a bone fitting in the dark of somebody’s arm, and the echo of it in her own. She thought about Sedge’s hard finger on her breastbone. You don’t mend anything. You agree with it.

She thought: I’d agree with you, idiot. If you’d let me get close enough.

She closed the window.

Tomorrow they were going to kill a ram.
