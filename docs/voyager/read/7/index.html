Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 1: Thirty-Six Circles

Chapter 7

The Unkept Vigil

3,625 words · 16 min read

The trick with Woolbacks was that you didn’t cut them.

Everybody cut them the first time. They looked like sheep, big grey shaggy ones with red eyes and a bad attitude, so your instinct was to swing at them like you’d swing at anything, side to side, a big flat arc. The sword went into the wool and stopped there, as if you’d swung it into a mattress, and the Woolback turned round and put its head into your stomach at about forty kilometres an hour.

Ice had learned that ten years ago from the floor, and from Salty, who’d learned it first and loudest.

You stabbed them. Point first, low, under the jaw, where the wool was thin. And you never fought one on its own, because there was never one on its own. They came in pairs. You looked for the second before you dealt with the first.

He found the second one behind a dry-stone wall, exactly where it always was. He let the first one charge him, stepped left so it went past close enough that he felt the wind of it, and put the point of the plain Knight’s sword under the second one’s jaw as it came over the wall. Then he turned round with his back to the wall and waited for the first one to come back.

It came back.

[ Ice · Lv 2 ]

“Solo bonus,” said Alea quietly. “You got about half again on those. And a little more because they were above you.”

“I know.”

“I’m just logging it. You always used to say log everything.”

“I did say that.” He wiped the sword on the grass. “Remind me to stop saying things.”

He did the next pair the same way, and the pair after that. By the time the sun was halfway up its rail, he’d worked his way across two rises of the east fields. He’d stopped thinking about it, the way you stop thinking about a song you’ve played a thousand times. Step left. Look for the second. Point, low, under the jaw.

His hands remembered. That was the strange part. His body had never done any of this; it was twenty-six years old and had spent most of its life in a chair. But somewhere under it, in whatever had come back with him, there were ten years of floors, and when a Woolback charged, his feet moved before he asked them to. They weren’t fast enough yet, or strong enough. He got knocked flat twice and had to scramble. But they knew where to go.

He looked at it sitting on a wall at noon, with a heel of Mother Crane’s bread in one hand.

He’d put his free points into strength and vitality, the way a Knight always did, and one point into spirit, which a Knight never did.

“SPI,” Alea had said, when he did it. “For later.”

“For later.”

“Taunt,” she said now, reading the new line. “Who are you going to taunt?”

“The sheep.”

“The sheep already hate you.”

“The sheep could hate me more,” said Ice, and ate his bread.

He ate half of one loaf. He looked at the other half for a while, and at the second loaf still wrapped in its cloth. Then he wrapped the half back up with the whole one and put them away, and didn’t think too hard about why.

“Stop,” said Alea, about an hour later.

He’d just put down the eighth pair. He stopped.

“You’re at eighty-one percent of the way to four,” she said. “I need you to stop killing sheep.”

“Four’s fine. It’s under five.”

“Four is fine. But if you keep going at this rate you’ll be five before the sun’s anywhere near the window. And then the chapel won’t open. And then we come back in four years and it says You are too late, and you cry in a field again.”

“I didn’t cry in a field.”

“You did a bit. In the first run. Year four. I logged it.”

“You log too much,” said Ice, but he put the sword away.

He got to four anyway, off one last Woolback that came over a wall at him unasked. After that he walked very carefully. There was a whole new problem: getting across two more rises of the east fields without killing anything, when everything in the east fields wanted to kill him. He went the long way, round the backs of walls, through the gorse, keeping his head down. Twice a pair of Woolbacks saw him and came after him, and he ran, actually ran, sword on his back and loaves under his arm, until they lost interest. The second time he went head first over a wall into a patch of nettles.

“Very knightly,” said Alea.

“Shut up.”

“Three percent,” said Alea. “Don’t touch anything.”

The chapel stood on the third rise on its own, the way it had stood for a hundred years.

It wasn’t big. Four walls of grey stone, no roof, a doorway with no door. Long grass grew up the middle of the aisle where the floor had been, and a young birch had come up through a crack in one corner and was taller than the walls now. Lichen everywhere. The kind of ruin that farmers’ children dare each other to go into after dark and then don’t.

Ice stood at the bottom of the rise and looked at it for a while.

The first time, they’d come here in year four. Year four: he’d been on the forty-first floor by then, and somebody, he thought it had been Hako, had found a book on the thirty-fourth floor in a dead library: The Testament of the Lantern-Knights. It told the whole story. A hidden path to a class that could stand in front like a Knight and mend like a Cleric. It started on the first floor, in a roofless chapel in the east fields of Hearthstead, which they’d all walked past a hundred times on the way to somewhere else.

So he’d come all the way back down through forty floors to stand in this doorway. A window had opened in front of his face, very small and very plain:

You are too late.

That had been all. No explanation. He’d read the Testament again on the way back up and found the line he’d missed: The vigil is kept by one Knight, alone, untried, before the second dawn of his climbing. Untried meant under level five. Second dawn meant the first day. Nobody in the history of the Spire had ever woken up and known to come here before the second dawn.

Nobody but him.

He went up the rise.

The doorway was low. He had to duck. Inside, the light was green and dim through the birch leaves. The air was very still, and smelled of moss and old stone and, faintly, of candle wax, though there hadn’t been a candle lit here in a hundred years.

At the far end, where an altar should have been, there was a statue.

It was a knight, life-sized, carved from the same grey stone as the walls. He stood with his feet planted and a round shield on his left arm. In his right hand, held up and slightly out, he held a lantern. The lantern was carved too, but its little door was open, and inside it, where the flame should have been, there was nothing at all. The knight’s face had been worn smooth by a century of rain. He had no expression. He just held the empty lantern out over the aisle, toward the door, as if he were lighting the way in for somebody who hadn’t come yet.

Over the doorway, on the inside of the lintel, carved deep enough that a hundred years hadn’t taken it:

THE LIGHT IS KEPT BY THOSE WHO STAND

Ice stood in the aisle and looked at the statue. The long grass came up to his knees.

The window opened.

He read it twice, the way he always did. Not because he didn’t understand it. Because the first time, it had said You are too late, and he wanted to make sure.

“Conditions met,” said Alea. Very quietly, the way she said things that mattered.

“Yeah.”

“Ice. Nobody’s ever—“

“I know.” He swallowed. “Accept.”

The window in question was high in the west wall: round, glassless, about the size of a cartwheel. For most of the afternoon it was only a hole in the stone with sky through it.

He waited for it. He sat in the doorway with his back against the stone and the sword across his knees and waited, the way he’d waited for things a thousand times, and watched the sun go slowly along its rail.

It was so regular. That was what got to you after a while in the Spire. The sun never hurried and never lingered. It went along the same smooth curve every day, from the eastern hills to the western ones, as if it were being drawn across the ceiling on a wire. Which, he’d decided around year six, it probably was.

Late in the afternoon, the sun reached the round window.

He saw it happen. The light came through the hole in the west wall in a single solid beam, gold and full of dust, and fell straight down the length of the chapel onto the statue’s lantern. And the lantern, which was stone, which was empty, which had held nothing for a hundred years, began to glow.

Not with fire. With light. Soft and gold and steady, filling the little carved cage from the inside like water filling a cup.

Something moved in the fields.

Ice stood up.

He stepped into the doorway, exactly into it, his heels on the worn stone of the threshold, and settled the sword in his right hand. He didn’t have a shield. Knights started with a sword. That was one of the cruelties of the vigil: it was built for a Knight on his first day, and a Knight on his first day had nothing to hold up.

Out on the slope, the grass was moving. Not in the wind. In lines. Coming up the rise toward the chapel from three directions, slow, the long grass bending and parting.

They came out of the grass at the bottom of the rise. Grey shapes, lamb-sized, sheep-shaped, made of something like wool and something like mist. They had no faces. Where the faces should have been, there was only a smooth grey blank, like the knight’s.

And more. Lots more.

“They don’t give experience,” said Alea. “They aren’t alive. They’re the dark the lantern keeps out. So you won’t level and you won’t break the condition. But Ice, they hit like they’re alive.”

“I know.”

“There are a lot of them.”

“I know.” He shifted his feet. Heels on the threshold. Not one step out. Not one step back. “How long’s the light in the window?”

“Fifty-two minutes.”

“Okay,” said Ice. “Fifty-two minutes.”

The first Mournwool came up the rise at a run and threw itself at the door.

The doorway was narrow. That was the only mercy. It was a low, thick-walled, hundred-year-old door in a chapel built by people who’d expected to have to hold it, and only two things at a time could reach him through it.

He put the point of the sword into the first one. It went in like going into a cloud, and the Mournwool tore apart in rags of grey that blew away down the hill. The second one hit him in the knees. He went down on one of them and came up again with the sword’s pommel in its blank face, and it came apart too.

Then there were three, and two of them could reach him.

He used Guard. It wasn’t much, at Lv 4, with no shield: a turn of the body and a set of the blade across his chest that made the next hit glance instead of land. It took the edge off. Two hits glanced. The third one didn’t. He felt it in his ribs.

He used Strike, which was just a better-than-usual thrust, on the one in front of him. He used Taunt, which was the first time in this life he’d used it. He felt the faint pull go out of him like a hook on a line, and the Mournwool trying to get round the side of the doorway, toward the gap in the wall where the birch had come through, turned round and came back to him instead.

“Good,” said Alea. “They want the lantern. Keep them on you.”

“I know what they want.”

He kept them on him.

It was ugly. That was the honest word. In ten years he’d been elegant in fights; by the end, people had stopped to watch. This wasn’t that. This was a body that was four hours old in this world, fighting with a plain sword and no shield in a narrow door. He took hits he’d have slipped in his sleep at the top of the Spire. Every time he swung, he was a beat behind the shape his hands were trying to make. A Mournwool caught his sword arm and he nearly dropped the blade. Another came in low and took his left leg out from under him, and for one terrible second he was going backward into the chapel, off the threshold—

He caught the doorframe with his left hand. His heels skidded on the stone and stopped, right on the edge.

He hauled himself back upright. Put the point into the next one.

“Forty-one minutes,” said Alea.

At twenty minutes his health was at thirty percent.

At twelve it was at nineteen.

He’d stopped talking. He was breathing in short, hard pulls through his teeth, and his sword arm had gone from burning to a dull, distant ache. He knew that was bad. He’d stopped feeling it the way he’d stopped feeling the hole in his side at the top of the Spire. The Mournwool kept coming up the rise, two at a time, faceless and silent, and the light kept falling through the high window behind him, and the long gold beam had shifted, very slowly, along the floor, as the sun went along its rail.

At eight minutes, two of them hit him at once and his bar dropped to nine percent, and his vision greyed at the edges.

“Ice,” said Alea. Her voice was very calm, the way it went when things were bad. “You can step back. If you step back, the quest fails. You won’t die. They won’t follow you in past the lantern.”

“No.”

“You’ll be alive. We’ll find another—“

“No.”

He put his back against the doorframe. Not out, not back. Into the stone. And he felt it: warmth.

Behind him. Between his shoulder blades. The beam of light from the high window was falling on the statue’s lantern, and the lantern was full of light, and some of it was reaching him. Spilling past the stone knight, all down the aisle, through the long grass, to the door. To the man standing in it.

It was very faint. It was the kind of thing you’d never notice unless you were at nine percent health, leaning on a cold stone doorframe, with nothing else left to notice.

The Testament of the Lantern-Knights. He’d read it a dozen times in the first run, in the dark, on the forty-first floor and all the floors after, learning everything he’d never be able to use. There was a line. The Knight does not carry the light. He stands in it. He lets it reach him.

He’d thought it was poetry.

He stood in it.

He didn’t know how to do the next part. Nobody had ever told him. He did the only thing he could think of, which was to take his left hand off the doorframe and press it flat against his own chest, over his heart. The light was behind him, and he leaned back into it the way you’d lean into a fire on a cold night, and he thought, as clearly as he could, at the warmth:

Please.

Nothing happened.

A Mournwool hit him. His bar dropped to seven.

He’d got the shape wrong. He could feel it: like holding a key in a lock the wrong way up. Stand in it. Let it reach you. Not please. Asking was a Cleric’s thing. A Knight didn’t ask. A Knight stood.

He stopped asking. He stood. He set his heels on the threshold and his back against the stone, and let the light come down the aisle and into him, the way he’d let every hit for ten years come into the shield, and didn’t push it anywhere. He just held still and let it arrive.

Something closed in his chest. Like a hand.

The pain in his ribs eased. His sword arm came back to him, aching but there. The grey at the edge of his eyes drew back, a little, like a tide.

He laughed, out loud, a cracked, breathless sound. The Mournwool in front of him didn’t care. He put the point of the sword into it anyway.

“Four minutes,” said Alea, and her voice wasn’t calm at all.

The light left the window at the fifty-second minute, exactly, the way the Spire did everything.

He felt it go. The warmth at his back faded, and the gold beam slid off the statue and up the wall and out through the round hole into the evening. Out on the slope, the last few Mournwool stopped where they were, halfway up the rise. They didn’t run or retreat. They just stood there in the dusk, faceless, and then came apart in the wind like smoke and blew away down the hill. They were gone.

Ice stood in the doorway for a long time after that.

Then he sat down on the threshold, because his legs had decided it for him. He sat with his back against one side of the door and his boots against the other, and the sword across his lap, and breathed.

Behind him the lantern was still glowing. Not with the sun now. On its own.

He turned round, slowly, on the threshold.

The stone knight was standing exactly as before, feet planted, lantern held out. But his left arm was empty. The carved round shield was gone. On the altar stone at his feet, where nothing had been before, lay a real one: wooden, iron-rimmed, battered, with a faint pale circle painted on its face, like a lantern seen from above.

And in the stone lantern, small as a seed, a single ember of gold light, waiting.

He went down the aisle through the long grass on legs that didn’t quite work. He picked up the shield. It was heavier than it looked and fitted his arm as if it had been waiting for it. He reached up into the stone lantern, and the ember came into his palm like a drop of warm honey and sank into it and was gone, leaving only a faint warmth there that didn’t fade.

[ Lantern-ember · carried ]

“One of three,” said Alea.

“One of three.”

“The first time—“

“I know.”

“No, let me say it.” The fox appeared on the altar stone at the statue’s feet, made of teal and white light, very small. She looked up at him. “The first time, you stood here in year four, and it said you were too late. You read that book every night for six years after, Ice. Every night. You knew every word of it. You never got to use a single one.” She paused. “I logged every time you read it. Two thousand one hundred and four times.”

Ice didn’t say anything for a while.

“Was it worth it?” he said finally. “Reading it.”

“You just used it,” said Alea. “So yes.”

He looked up at the statue’s smooth stone face. It didn’t have an expression. It never would. But the lantern it held out was full of light, for the first time in a hundred years, and it was pointing at the door.

“Thanks,” he said to it. He didn’t know why. It seemed rude not to.

Then he put the shield on his arm and the sword on his back, and went out of the chapel into the dusk, toward the head of the valley, where the Bellwether was waiting.
