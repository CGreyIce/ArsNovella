Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 2: One Floor Ahead

Chapter 47

The First Sun

5,108 words · 22 min read

He didn’t cross anything that night. Nobody crossed a chain bridge in the dark on floor 5. Not the first time, not ever.

He sat down on the little island with his back against the stone hut round the door, and pulled the Hartshorn Cloak round himself. It kept the cold off everything but his face. He let it have his face. The wind came and went, and came again from a different side, and every time it changed he felt it change on his cheeks and in his hair. He’d forgotten that. That wind could come from somewhere, and go somewhere else, without anybody telling it to.

“It’s cold,” said Alea. She was curled up inside the cloak against his chest, which she didn’t need to do, being made of light. She did it anyway. She’d done it on every cold floor for ten years.

“It’s real cold,” said Ice.

“That’s what I said.”

“No. Listen.” He held his face up to the wind. “On floor 2 it was cold, but it was cold the same way all the time. Same wind, same direction, same snow. Like a fridge. This is—“ The wind dropped, and came back from the left, carrying a smell of rain from somewhere far off. “This is weather.”

The fox was quiet for a moment.

“It gets realer,” she said. “Every floor. I logged it.”

“I know. I remember.”

He did. He hadn’t thought about it in years, but he remembered. The first three floors with their painted skies and their sun going along its rail like a lamp on a wire, and everything you killed going to light. Then floor 4, where nothing went anywhere. Then this, the wind and the stars. And on up, floor after floor, so slowly you didn’t notice it happening. The skies got deeper. The food got better. The people you met had grandparents, and grudges, and bad knees. From six up, a cut could leave a scar. By the sixties it rained for a week and everybody caught colds. On the nineties, the last time, he’d stood on a hill in the evening with the grass wet round his boots and a smell of somebody’s cooking coming up from a village below. And he’d thought, for a whole minute, before he remembered where he was, I’m home.

Nobody had ever known why. They’d argued about it round fires for years. Kei had thought the tower was getting better at being a world, the way a painter gets better. Chris had thought it was a curve, and drawn it, and it hadn’t helped. Thea had read the windows on the eightieth floor, the words under the words, for three whole nights. Then she’d shut her notebook and said she didn’t want to talk about it, and never had.

“You’ve got a theory,” said Ice. “I can tell. You go quiet in a particular way.”

“I’ve got a feeling,” said Alea. “It’s not a theory until I can say it without it frightening you.”

“Does it frighten you?”

The fox didn’t answer for a long time. The wind moved a rag of cloud across the stars, and they went out behind it, and came back.

“Go to sleep, Ice,” she said. “The sun’s coming up in four hours. A real one. You’ll want to see it.”

He didn’t think he’d sleep. He did.

He woke because his face was warm.

He didn’t understand it at first. He lay with his head against the stone hut and his eyes shut and felt warmth on his eyelids, soft, red-gold through the skin. It was the kind of warmth you feel on a Sunday morning when you’ve forgotten to close the curtains. He hadn’t felt it in ten years and fifty-eight days. Something in the back of his head that was older than the Spire opened its eyes and said: morning.

He opened his eyes.

The sky had gone pale. Not grey. Pale the way water is pale, a clear washed blue going up over his head into the last of the stars. Down at the edge of everything, where the floor of cloud met the sky, a line of gold had come up. Thin. Then thicker. The cloud was catching it. The whole floor of cloud below him, going out for miles in every direction, was turning from silver to pink to gold. And the islands stood up out of it.

He hadn’t seen them in the dark. He saw them now. Hundreds of them. Big ones and small ones, flat ones and tall ones, hanging in the air above the cloud at every height. Grass on their tops, trees on some of them, white water spilling off the edges of a few and falling away into the cloud and never landing. Between them, everywhere, threads. Long thin dark lines, sagging in curves from island to island, catching the light. Chain bridges, hundreds of them, so many that from a distance the whole Reaches looked as if somebody had tied the sky together.

And in the middle, a long way off, taller than all the rest, a single needle of grey rock went up out of the cloud. Up and up, past every other island, until its top was a black point against the morning. Something was circling it. Something with wings. Very small, from here.

Then the sun came up.

It didn’t come along a rail. It didn’t come out of a seam in a painted ceiling. It came up out of the cloud at the edge of the world, the top of it first, a curve of light so bright he couldn’t look at it. It came up slowly, the way the real one does, so slowly you can’t see it move, and then you look away and back and it’s higher. The light came across the cloud and up the sides of the islands and onto his little island, and onto him.

Warm.

On his face. On his hands. Through the white shirt. Not the warmth of a fire or the ember or Mend Self, or of anything that came from the tower. Just the warmth of a sun, coming ninety-three million miles, or however far it was here, and landing on the side of his face.

He sat on the little island with his back against the hut and the sun on his face and cried like a child.

The first time, they’d come up at dusk too.

All thirty-four of them who were left, after six weeks in the Red City. Six weeks of silence. Six weeks of nobody laughing, nobody talking above a whisper, Calvin with one arm, and two graves they’d dug in the red stone below the first square because floor 4 didn’t take things away. They’d come up the two hundred and forty-four steps in the dark, and out of the door, and found themselves on a little island in the middle of the sky. Nobody had wanted to cross a chain bridge in the dark. So they’d sat down, all thirty-four, crammed onto an island the size of a house, and waited.

And in the morning the sun had come up.

He remembered the noise. That was the thing. After six weeks of silence, the noise. Somebody had shouted. Then everybody had. Hyvred, in capitals. Tiger, running round the edge of the island so fast that Wendy had grabbed him by the tail. Newish swearing at the sun because it was beautiful and she didn’t know what else to do. And Calvin, sitting against the hut with his left sleeve pinned up, had said out loud, for the first time in six weeks, at a normal volume: oh.

That was all. Just oh. Rachael and Dennis were in the red stone under the first square, and Calvin had said oh, at a normal volume, and half the island had turned round and looked at him and started crying.

And Thea.

Thea had stood at the very edge of the island in the wind with her face turned up to the sun and her eyes shut. She’d laughed, and cried, both at once, for a long time, with the light on her face. Her ponytail blew out sideways, black and pink and blue. She hadn’t been wiping her face. She’d just let it happen, standing there, with the sun on her.

He hadn’t looked at the sun at all. He’d looked at her.

He’d been looking at her for the whole of the first sunrise in the Spire, and he’d never told her, and then it was ten years later, and he couldn’t.

This time he looked at the sun.

He looked at it the whole way up, until it was clear of the cloud and too bright to look at, and the whole of the Windward Reaches was lit gold round him. He made himself see all of it. The islands, and the threads of the bridges, and the white water falling, and the needle of rock in the middle with the thing circling it. Because in thirty days, or forty, she was going to come out of the door behind him. She’d stand on this island and turn her face up to it. And he wanted to know what she was seeing. He wanted to have seen it too.

“Ice,” said Alea, very gently, from inside his cloak.

“I know. Give me a minute.”

“Take ten,” said the fox.

The first bridge nearly killed him.

He’d known it would try. They all tried, the first day, on floor 5. The bridges were iron chains as thick as his arm, two of them, slung from post to post across the gap between islands, with planks laid across them and a third chain at waist height on either side for a rail. They sagged in the middle. They swayed. Under the planks there was nothing at all, two hundred metres of nothing, and then the cloud, and nobody had ever found out what was under the cloud.

He was halfway across the first one, walking steadily, one hand on the rail chain, when the wind changed.

There was no window.

That was the thing. On floor 2 there’d been a window for every whiteout, two minutes before it came: Whiteout incoming. On floor 3 there’d been a window for the wisps. On every floor up to now, the tower had told you, one way or another, before it did something to you.

Nothing told him. The wind dropped, and he felt it drop, and had a quarter of a second to think oh no. Then it came back from the right, all at once, hard, like a door slamming. The whole bridge went sideways under him. The planks tipped. His boots went out from under him. He went down onto his knees, then onto his side, sliding across the tilted planks toward the left-hand chain and the drop beyond it, with the shield on his arm dragging him.

He got his right hand round the rail chain. The iron bit into his palm. He hung there with his legs out over two hundred metres of air, and the bridge swinging, and the wind screaming through the chains like a kettle, until the gust went past.

Then he lay on the planks in the middle of the bridge, flat on his back, holding the chain with both hands, breathing.

“There wasn’t a window,” said Alea. She sounded shaken.

“No.”

“There’s always a window. For weather. There’s always—“

“Not from here.” He lay there and looked up at the real sky, and a real gull went over, very high, crying. “Not from five. I remember now. Weather doesn’t get a window any more. It just happens.”

“Like home,” said Alea, slowly.

“Like home.”

He got up, very carefully, and walked the rest of the way across holding the rail chain with both hands. He didn’t let go of it on any bridge for the rest of that day.

The town was called Tether.

It sat on the biggest island of the lower Reaches, where seven chain bridges came in from seven directions and met. Every house in it was tied down. That was the first thing you saw. Ropes ran from every roof to iron rings sunk in the rock, and from every door to the next door, and from the chimneys to each other. The whole town was laced together like a boot, so that when a big wind came, nothing went anywhere. Kites flew from half the roofs: long, thin, many-coloured kites on long lines, rattling and humming in the wind. The streets smelled of tar and salt fish and woodsmoke. There were gulls everywhere, on every roof ridge and every rope, screaming at each other.

He’d liked Tether, the first time. He’d forgotten how much.

At the edge of town, on the side facing the high islands, there was a chain-ferry. A big wicker basket on an iron cable that ran out across a gap far too wide for any bridge, to an island so far off it was blue with distance. A woman sat in a little hut beside the winch. She was older than anybody he’d seen since Mother Tallow, brown and creased as a walnut, with a pipe and four shawls and a gull sitting on her hat as if it lived there. She was singing, to herself, under her breath, while she mended a rope.

He’d walked past her the first time. They all had. They’d been grieving, and frightened, and in a hurry, and none of them had stopped.

He stopped.

She was singing something slow, a sort of work song, in time with her hands on the rope. He stood by the winch and listened to it.

They came up the stair with their lights in their hands,

and they asked for the way to the top.

They went on to the sky, and they never came by,

and we never knew where they’d stop.

She finished the verse and looked up at him with very sharp eyes under the shawls. The gull on her hat looked at him too.

“You’re one of them,” she said.

He stood very still. “One of who?”

“Them.” She pointed the stem of her pipe at his chest. Not at him. At a place just beside his head, about the size of a small fox. “With a light. My nan’s nan sang about you lot. Came up the stair with lights, asked everybody the way, went up the Spindle and never came down.” She sniffed. “Never paid the ferry, either. That’s in the second verse.”

“I’ll pay,” said Ice.

“Course you will. Double. For the ones before.” She went back to her rope. “Where’s the rest of you? Song says there’s always lots.”

He looked at the blue island across the gap for a moment.

“They’re coming,” he said. “In a month. About thirty-five of them.” He paused. “Be nice to them. One of them’s called Dennis. He’ll try to haggle.”

The old woman looked up again, and something in her face changed a little, a crease going sideways.

“Your lot,” she said. “In the song. They never said that.”

“Said what?”

“They’re coming.” She put the pipe back in her mouth. “Song never says anybody came after. Just the ones that went up.” She looked at him with her sharp old eyes. “Nan Gully. That’s me. You going to buy fish, or stand there?”

He bought fish. He paid double. And that night, on the rock behind her hut, out of the wind, he cooked it for both of them on a little fire, with salt and the last of his floor 3 herbs and a wild onion he’d found growing on the bridge posts. Nan Gully ate hers in silence, and then said it was too much salt, and then ate his as well while he wasn’t looking.

“She remembered,” said Alea, later, while he lay in his bedroll behind the hut with the kites humming overhead. “Her nan’s nan. That’s—Ice, that’s five generations. Floor 1, nobody remembered anything past last week. Mother Crane couldn’t tell you what she’d had for breakfast.”

“I know.”

“Players.” The fox was quiet. “There were others. Before us. They came up the stair with lights, and went up, and never came down.”

“Only the thirty-six are players,” said Ice. “Now. This time.” He looked up at the kites, black against the real stars. “Doesn’t mean there weren’t others once.”

“Where did they go?”

He didn’t answer. He didn’t know. Nobody had ever known that either.

He spent four days learning floor 5 again.

He’d forgotten most of it, and Alea had the rest. Which bridges creaked before they swung. Which islands had gale-kites under them: flat grey things like rays, as wide as a door, that rode the gusts up from under a bridge and hit you from below. Which cliffs the rock-gulls nested on, in tens of thousands, and how you crossed past them without making a sound. He killed what he had to. He levelled. Twenty-seven, then twenty-eight. He didn’t hurry. Thirty-five threads had gone quiet in his chest, and he checked for them every morning anyway, out of habit, the way you check for a phone in your pocket.

None on this floor.

On the fifth day, the seventy-first, he went to look at the Spindle.

It went up out of the cloud in the middle of the Reaches like a nail. Grey rock, sheer on every side, five hundred metres from the cloud to its top. Every other island was below it. Its top was a small flat crown of rock, with a stone arch on it, and a door in the arch. The stair to floor 6.

And round it, all day, circling, the Squall.

He lay on his belly in the grass on the nearest high island, three hundred metres away across the gap, and watched it go round. It was a bird. That was the simplest way to say it, and the most wrong. It was shaped like a bird the way a storm is shaped like a cloud. Grey and white and huge, as big as a barn, its wings so wide that when it banked, the tip of one dipped into the cloud below and the other was against the sky. Its feathers were the colour of rain. Where it flew, the wind went with it. You could see it: the grass on the islands below flattening as it passed, the chain bridges swinging after it, one by one, like a wave going round.

“Squall,” said Alea quietly. “Floor guardian. Level thirty, the first time. It’s thirty here.”

“Pattern?”

“It circles. It watches. When something’s out in the open on the Spindle’s crown, it stoops.” She brought up a window. Small, blue, crowded with notes in her neat hand. “Forty-one stoops logged, the first time. Same every time. It folds and drops, straight down, faster than anything. It hits whatever it’s aiming at, hard. Then it pulls out at the last second and climbs. Never lands. Not once in forty-one.”

“And the way through—“

“You were the way through.” The fox’s voice went careful. “You stood on the crown with your shield up and took the stoops. All of them. Braced, still, like a post. And while it was pulling out, at the bottom of the stoop, its legs were in reach for about a second. Drew and Xav and Rui Xiang hit the legs. Again and again. It took forty-one stoops. You were a mess by the end of it. But it worked.”

He remembered. He remembered his arm going numb on the twentieth stoop and staying numb for two days. He remembered the sound, like a building falling on the shield.

“Then that’s what I’ll do,” he said.

“Alone.”

“I’ll take a stoop and see if I can reach its legs on the way out. I’m not trying to kill it today. I just want to see how fast it is now. Up close.” He got up. “One stoop. Then back.”

The fox looked at him for a long time.

“One,” she said.

He crossed to the Spindle by the last bridge, a long thin one that went up at a steep angle from the high island to a ledge halfway up the needle. Then he climbed. There was a path, of a sort, carved into the rock in a spiral, with iron rings to hold. It took him an hour. Above him, all the way, the Squall went round and round, and the wind went with it, and every time it passed he pressed himself flat against the rock and waited.

At the top, the crown was flat and bare and about thirty metres across, with the stone arch at the far side and a crack running into the rock near the edge, like a cave mouth. He noted the crack. Then he walked out into the middle of the crown, in the open, in the real sun, and stopped.

He braced. Feet apart. Knees soft. Shield up, angled, the way he’d taken a thousand blows. And he stood still.

He’d learned that from the Kiln. You didn’t dodge some things. Some things you stood in front of, and stood in, and let them come. He’d stood in the Kiln’s fire on the forty-fourth day and again on the sixty-sixth, and come out the other side.

The Squall’s shadow went over him. Then again. Then it didn’t come round. It had stopped circling. He felt the wind die on the crown. And far above, against the blue, a grey shape folded its wings.

It came down like a building.

He’d forgotten how fast. Faster than anything. A grey blur, then a roar of wind, then the whole sky was wings and feathers and a hooked yellow beak the size of a door, and it hit the shield.

The impact went up his arm and through his shoulder and into his teeth. He’d braced for it. He’d braced for forty-one of these, once. His boots slid half a metre across the rock and stopped. He held. He held, and waited for the second it would pull out and climb, and its legs would be there, in reach, for a second—

It didn’t pull out.

It landed.

It came down on top of him with its whole weight, the way a hawk comes down on a mouse. Talons the length of his forearm closed on the rim of the Vigil Shield and squeezed, and he heard the steel of the rim make a sound he’d never heard it make. Wings went up on both sides of him, huge, beating, and every beat was a wall of wind that hit him from a different direction. The beak came down. He got the shield over his head just in time, and the beak hit it, and hit it again. Each blow drove him down, onto one knee, onto both, into the rock.

“It’s landed—“ Alea was shouting. “It doesn’t land, it never lands, Ice, it’s—”

It lifted him.

Its talons were still locked on the shield’s rim, with his arm in the straps. It beat its wings once, twice, and he felt his knees come off the rock. Then his boots. He was in the air, a metre, two, three, hanging from his own shield with his arm screaming in the straps, the crown of the Spindle swinging away under his feet. The edge came closer. Beyond the edge there was nothing for five hundred metres.

He let go of the shield.

He didn’t decide to. Ten years decided for him. He twisted his arm out of the straps, and dropped three metres onto the rock. He hit badly, on his shoulder, and rolled. His sword was in his hand. He didn’t know how.

The Squall screamed. It flung the shield away. Ice watched it go, spinning, over the edge of the crown, and then the Squall was coming at him again, low across the rock, not stooping this time. Running. Wings half out, talons reaching, faster on the ground than anything that big should be.

He went sideways. One talon went past his face close enough to cut his cheek. He struck at the leg as it passed. Wait, then move once. The sword went in above the scaly foot, into the feathers, and something warm came down his wrist. The Squall shrieked and turned on him, and its wing caught him across the chest like a falling tree.

He went across the crown. He didn’t know how far. He hit the rock and kept going, rolling, and the world went sky-rock-sky-rock. Then there was no rock, there was the edge, and his legs went over it, and he grabbed.

His hand caught the lip of the crack. The cave mouth he’d noted. He hung by one hand from the edge of the crown, half in the crack, half out over five hundred metres of air, with his health at a third, and the Squall’s shadow coming over him.

Mend Self. The ember went through him, warm. He pulled. He got an elbow in, then a knee, then he was in the crack, wedged into a gap in the rock barely wider than his shoulders, as far back as he could get. The Squall’s head came down outside it. One huge yellow eye looked in at him. The beak struck the rock at the mouth of the crack, again and again, and stone came off it in chips. It couldn’t get in.

It screamed at him for a long time. Then the light outside the crack changed as the wings went up, and the wind went past, and it was gone. Climbing. Circling again.

He lay in the crack in the dark, with his cheek bleeding and his shoulder on fire and his shield gone. He breathed.

“Ice.” Alea was right against his face, small and teal and white and bright with fear. “Ice. Ice, talk to me.”

“I’m fine.”

“You’re at a third.”

“I’m fine. I’m mending.” He was. Slowly. The ember was pulling him back up, the way it did. “Shield’s gone.”

“I know the shield’s gone. I watched it go.” She was shaking. He’d never seen her shake. “It landed. Ice, it never landed. Forty-one stoops. I logged every one. It came down and hit and pulled out and climbed, forty-one times, every time, it never—“

“I know.”

“It picked you up.”

“I know, Alea.”

He lay there and let the ember work. He thought about it. About what it had done, and what it hadn’t. It hadn’t stooped and pulled out. It had stooped and landed. It had come down on the one thing standing still on the crown, and stayed, and kept hitting it, and then tried to carry it off the edge.

As if it knew he’d stand there. As if it knew that whatever stood still, braced, in the open, was going to be the one it had to kill.

“It goes for whoever holds still,” he said slowly.

The fox went quiet.

“The first time,” said Ice, “it went for whatever was in the open. It didn’t care. And I stood still because that was how you took it. But it didn’t—it didn’t know I’d stand still. It just stooped, and I was there.” He swallowed. “This one knew. It was waiting for me to stop moving.”

“Ice.”

“I stood still in the Kiln’s fire,” he said. “On the forty-fourth day. And again two days ago. In front of it. Still, while it burned. That’s how I beat it. That’s the only way I beat it.”

He could hear the Squall going round outside, the wind going with it, round and round the needle in the sun.

“It learned,” said Alea. Very, very quietly.

“Something did.”

“Irregular,” said the fox.

Neither of them said anything for a long time.

The first time, the Squall had been the same on the forty-first stoop as on the first. Forty-one times the same. Because nobody had ever done anything to it that the tower hadn’t expected. They’d all just been thirty-four frightened people going up a tower the way you were supposed to.

But he’d said I’ve done this before, in a stone room on floor 4. And then he’d gone and stood in a fire for the second time, the same way, alone, on the day thirty-five people came up a stair who should have been thirty-six.

And somewhere, something had watched him do it, and made a note, and gone and changed a bird.

“Your map’s wrong,” he said. “Isn’t it.”

Alea didn’t answer for a moment.

“For the first time,” she said, “yes.”

He lay in the crack in the rock with the sun outside and the wind going round, and his cheek bleeding, and the ember mending him. He thought about thirty-five people who’d come up a stair two days ago talking, because everything on floor 4 had already met somebody who knew how to stand in front of it.

Floor 5 wasn’t going to be like that. Not if things changed after he met them.

“Okay,” he said.

“Okay?”

“Okay.” He wiped the blood off his cheek with the back of his hand and looked at it. Real blood. On a real floor, under a real sun. “Then I’ll have to learn it again. All of it. Like I’ve never seen it.” He breathed out. “Like the first time.”

“You’ve never done anything like the first time,” said Alea. “Not once. Not since the white room.”

“I know.” He almost smiled. “Should be interesting.”

He waited till the Squall was on the far side of the needle. Then he climbed out of the crack, and went down the spiral path without his shield, holding on to the iron rings with both hands. Floor 5 had stopped telling him things, and he’d have to listen to everything himself now.
