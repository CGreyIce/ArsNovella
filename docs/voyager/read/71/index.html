Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 2: One Floor Ahead

Chapter 71

The Shots You Can't

3,508 words · 15 min read

Wendy noticed on the hundred-and-thirty-ninth day that Yan had let a shardback go. She didn’t say anything for two more.

She was good at that. Noticing and not saying. People thought she was a pessimist, and she was. She’d never pretended otherwise. Bring a coat, she’d said on floor 2. Bring two coats. Bring the person you love most and lie on them. Everybody had laughed, and then everybody had been cold, and she’d been right. She was usually right, about things going wrong. It wasn’t a gift. It was just paying attention.

And she’d been paying attention to Yan since floor 1.

He looked at people. That was the first thing she’d noticed, on the first morning in Hearthstead, walking out to the south fields. Not at their faces. Lower. Under the breastbone, about where your heart was. He did it to everybody, a little sideways, as if he were checking something. Like a man checking a clock. She’d caught him at it on the first day, and he’d laughed and waved her on, and she’d let it go.

She hadn’t let it go. She’d just stopped asking.

On floor 6, it had got worse.

The shardbacks were the things to hunt out on the Glass. Low, flat, lizard-shaped, the size of a big dog, with scales of real glass down their backs that caught the sun and flashed it in your eyes. They came out at dusk and dawn, when the heat was bearable. They ran in little family groups, three or four, a big one and smaller ones. They had teeth like broken bottles. They were worth killing because the scales sold in Deepwell for good money, and because if you didn’t kill them, they killed you.

On the hundred-and-thirty-ninth day, at dawn, a few hundred metres out from the pit, Wendy had put an arrow into the big one of a group of four. It had gone down on the glass, kicking. And one of the small ones had turned round and run back to it. Not away. Back. It had pushed its flat glassy nose against the big one’s side, and made a sound.

Yan had been right there. Two metres from it. With his staff up.

He’d lowered it.

He’d stood there on the glass with his staff down, looking at the little shardback pushing at the big one, and let it go. It had run off into the dunes with the others. Then it had come back round the side of a dune thirty seconds later and gone for Tiger’s ankle, and Tiger had had to kill it himself, swearing, with a bite through his boot.

Yan hadn’t said anything. He’d healed Tiger’s ankle. He’d looked at the dead shardbacks on the glass for a long time. Then he’d walked back to the pit without a word.

Wendy had watched him do it.

She’d watched him the next day, too. And the day after. He didn’t let any more go. He killed what he had to. He was a Cleric; he didn’t do much killing anyway. But every time something died near him out on the Glass, he looked at it. Not at the body. At the place under its breastbone. And his face did something, every time. As if he’d watched a light go out.

On the hundred-and-forty-first evening, on the lip of the pit, she sat down next to him.

He was alone, for once. Salty was asleep. Tiger was down on the third ring arguing with Calvin about who was faster. Cursiffe was in the round room making copies of his beam pages in honey-ink for Drew, very slowly, with his tongue between his teeth. Suzu was round Yan’s neck, white and small, with her head on his collarbone. She looked at Wendy when she sat down. She didn’t say anything. Suzu never said anything to anybody but Yan.

Wendy didn’t say anything either, for a while. She was good at that, too.

“You let one go,” she said at last.

Yan didn’t look at her.

“The day before yesterday,” said Wendy. “The little one. It went back to the big one and you put your staff down.” She looked out at the Glass, going purple in the dusk. “I’m not having a go. I just saw.”

Yan was quiet for a long time.

“It went back to her,” he said.

“I saw.”

“It went back to her, Wen. It ran back. Into us. To push at her. Because she was—“ He stopped.

“Because she was its mum,” said Wendy. “Yeah. I saw.”

He didn’t say anything.

“You look at things,” said Wendy. “You’ve looked at things since the first day. People. Under here.” She put two fingers on her own breastbone. “I’ve never asked. I’m not asking now, if you don’t want me to.” She looked at him sideways. “But you’ve been looking at the shardbacks like that for a week. And your face does a thing. Every time one dies. And I’d like to know what it is, so I know if I should be worried.”

Yan was quiet for a long time. Suzu shifted round his neck.

Tell her, said Suzu.

Wendy didn’t hear it. Nobody but Yan heard Suzu. But she saw Yan’s face change, as if somebody had said something, and she saw him take a breath.

“Everything’s got a light,” he said.

Wendy waited.

“Here.” He put his own fingers on his breastbone, where hers had been. “Everybody. You. Me. Tiger. Salty’s is huge, like a forge. Yours is small and very bright and it never moves, not even when you’re scared.” He wasn’t looking at her. He was looking out at the Glass. “I’ve seen them since the first morning. I call them candles. In my head. I didn’t know what else to call them.”

“Okay,” said Wendy.

She didn’t say anything else. She’d been expecting something like that. Not that, exactly. Something.

“Animals too,” said Yan. “Everything alive. The sheep had them, on floor 1. Small. Dull. Like coals. When you killed one, it went out like a coal goes out. Slow. Red to grey.” He swallowed. “I got used to that. It was sad. But they were coals. They weren’t—“ He stopped.

“Weren’t what?”

“Weren’t like ours.”

He turned and looked at her. His pale cyan eyes were wet.

“They’re like ours here, Wen,” he said. “On this floor. The shardbacks. The little glass ones. They’re not coals any more. They’re candles. Real ones. Like yours. Like Tiger’s. They flare up when they’re scared. They lean toward each other when they’re close. The little one’s leaned toward the big one’s the whole time I was watching them, before you shot.” He was very quiet. “And when the big one went out, the little one’s flared up so bright. Like Tiger’s does when he’s frightened. Exactly like that.”

Wendy didn’t say anything.

“I can’t tell the difference any more,” said Yan. “Between them and us. I never could, with people. Mother Crane, the egg girl, Ostrel, they’ve got candles exactly like ours. I made a rule on floor 1 that I’d never let what I see tell me who’s real. Because I can’t. Nobody can.” He looked out at the Glass. “But the animals were different. They were coals. And now they’re not. And I don’t know what I’m killing any more.”

The dusk had gone dark. The moon was coming up over the far rim of the pit, huge and white. Down below them, somebody on the third ring had started the singing again, the family that sang every night.

Wendy thought about it for a long time. She thought about it the way she thought about everything: honestly, assuming the worst, and then looking for the bit that was still all right.

“Okay,” she said.

Yan looked at her.

“Okay,” said Wendy again. “So that’s what your face does.” She pulled her knees up and put her arms round them. “That’s horrible, Yan. I’m sorry. That’s really horrible. I’d hate that.”

“I do hate it.”

“Yeah.” She looked out at the moon. “So here’s what we do. You don’t take the shots.”

He stared at her.

“You’re a healer,” said Wendy. “You don’t take many anyway. So you don’t take any. Not on this floor. Not on any floor where they’ve got candles like ours.” She shrugged. “I’ll take them. I take shots anyway. That’s my whole thing. I’ve got a bow.”

“Wen—“

“I’ll take the shots you can’t.” She said it flatly, the way she said things that weren’t up for discussion. “I don’t see what you see. It’s just a shardback to me. It’s got teeth and it wants to bite Tiger. I can shoot it and go to sleep.” She looked at him. “So let me. That’s what a party’s for.”

Yan looked at her for a long time.

“You’re not going to tell anyone,” he said. “About the—“

“About your candles?” Wendy snorted. “Who’d I tell? Hako’d want to write a book about it. Calvin would put it in World in capitals.” She looked at him sideways. “No. It’s yours. You tell people when you want to.”

Yan let out a breath. Suzu’s head came up off his collarbone and looked at Wendy, for a long moment, with her small dark eyes.

Then the white snake did something she’d never done in a hundred and forty-one days. She stretched her head out from Yan’s neck, slowly, across the gap between them, and touched the tip of her nose to the back of Wendy’s hand. Cool and dry. For one second. Then she drew back and put her head down on Yan’s collarbone again and shut her eyes.

Wendy looked at her hand.

“What was that?”

“I think,” said Yan, very quietly, “she said thank you.”

They did the Prism on the hundred-and-forty-sixth day, at dusk.

Not noon. Drew and Higu had argued about it for two days, and Higu had won. He did it at noon because nobody would choose noon, Higu had said, in her calm voice. We’re not him. We don’t need to hide how we do things. We need to do it the way that brings everybody back.

Not night, either. At night the Prism slept, and you could walk out and hit it, and Calvin had said that was boring. Rachael had said boring was the point. And Drew had looked at Cursiffe’s pages and said: dusk. The sun low and red in the west. Few beams, and slow, and only on one side. Enough light to see. Enough time to get it done before dark. And everybody with one of Cursiffe’s honey-ink pages in their pocket, so they’d know where the gaps were.

Wendy had read hers twenty times. Higu was right. It wasn’t just writing. When she looked at it, she could see the beams. Not on the page. In her head. Where they’d go. When. Like knowing the steps of a dance you’d never learned.

They came down into the bowl at the end of the afternoon, thirty-five of them, and spread out round the ring of black rocks. Gabe was at the front. Leaning. With his own round shield on his arm and the other one, the one with the lamp, on his back, the way he always carried it now. Tanks spread out either side of him: Salty, Jared, Azac, Hako with a new shield. Healers behind. Archers on the rocks.

Wendy was on the north rock. Yan was below her, with the healers. She’d put him there. She’d asked Drew to.

The Prism sat in the water in the low red light and watched them come.

It woke as they came close. The red sun hit its faces from the side and it caught the light and bent it. A beam came out, slow and red-gold, and swept across the bowl toward the tanks.

“Left,” called Cursiffe. He was on the rock beside Wendy with his pages in his lap, not looking at them. He didn’t need to. “Gabe, left two steps. Now. Hold. Three. Two. Go.”

Gabe went. The beam went past him where he’d been.

That was how it went, the first ten minutes. Cursiffe calling, slow and calm, the way he said everything. Left. Hold. Three, two, now. The tanks moving through the gaps, leaning. Everybody else reading their pages in their heads and moving when the beams came. The Prism turned in the water, catching the low sun on one face after another, and the beams came out red and swept the bowl, and nobody was where they went.

Nobody got burned. Not once.

And then Ina went in.

That was the plan. Winter’s plan, actually, which she’d come up with on the hundred-and-forty-third day sitting at the bottom of the pit eating eggs. It’s glass. What breaks glass? Hot, then cold. Fast. A glass dish, straight out of the oven onto a cold sink. Bang. Everybody had looked at her. Then everybody had looked at Ina.

Ina went in through the gaps with his mask down. White fire came off his hands, the fire that wind couldn’t take. He put both hands flat on the nearest face of the Prism and burned it. White, blue at the edges. The glass of the Prism went red, then orange, then yellow under his palms, glowing, the heat coming off it so strong that the water round it hissed.

“Now,” said Drew.

Winter shot.

One arrow, from the south rock, straight across the water, into the white-hot face Ina had just let go of. Hoarfrost. What you hit stays where it is. The frost went out from the arrowhead across the burning glass like ice across a pond. White over yellow.

The face cracked.

It cracked with a sound like a gunshot, a long jagged line from top to bottom. The Prism shuddered in the water. Every beam coming out of it flickered, and bent wrong, and went wild for a second, sweeping the sky instead of the bowl.

“Again,” said Drew. “Next face. Ina.”

They did it four times. Burn, freeze, crack. Ina came back out through the gaps between each one, staggering, with Malcolm’s hands on him. Winter’s hands were shaking on her bow by the fourth arrow. Four faces, cracked through, the beams coming out of the Prism broken now, going wrong, scattering. The thing in the water turned and turned, as if it was looking for something.

“The middle,” called Higu, from the west rock. “There’s something in the middle. Under the cracks. Look.”

Wendy looked.

She couldn’t see anything. Just glass, clear and cracked, the red sun going through it. She drew an arrow anyway and held it, looking. The middle of the thing. Somewhere under four faces of broken glass.

“Wen.”

Yan. Below her, on the rocks, with the healers. He’d stood up. He was looking at the Prism. Not at it. Into it. At the place under its breastbone, if a thing made of glass had a breastbone. And his face was doing the thing.

“There,” said Yan.

He pointed. Not at the middle. A little to the left of the middle, and low. At a place where four cracks met, deep in the glass.

“There,” he said again. His voice had gone strange. “Under the cracks. Where they meet. Wen. It’s there.”

She didn’t ask what. She didn’t need to. She’d sat on the lip of the pit with him in the dusk.

It had a candle. Of course it had a candle. Everything did, on this floor. And he could see it, deep in the glass where four cracks met, and she couldn’t.

She drew the arrow back to her cheek. She didn’t aim at the glass. She aimed at the place where Yan’s finger was pointing. Through four cracks of broken glass, at something she couldn’t see.

“Don’t watch,” she said to him. Without looking down.

“I have to,” said Yan. “Somebody should.”

She let go.

The arrow went across the water in the red light, straight, through the place where the four cracks met. And the Prism stopped.

Everything stopped. The beams went out, all at once, every one. The glass went from clear and bright to dull, cloudy, grey, in one long slow wave from the middle outward, the way a window fogs. And the Prism leaned over, slowly, in the water. It lay down on its side in the shallows beside the other dead one, with a sound like a very large bell being set down very gently.

Nobody cheered. Not straight away.

Wendy lowered her bow.

Below her, on the rocks, Yan was still looking at the Prism. At the place where the four cracks met. Watching. She saw his face. She saw it do the thing, the slow terrible thing it did, as he watched something go out that nobody else could see. Red to orange to grey. And then a thread of something, too faint to call light, going up off it and gone.

He didn’t look away until it was over.

Then he sat down on the rock very suddenly, as if his legs had stopped, and put his face in his hands. Suzu wound herself up round his neck, close, her white head pressed against his jaw.

Wendy climbed down off the north rock and sat next to him. She didn’t say anything. She didn’t touch him. She just sat there, shoulder to shoulder, with her bow across her knees. Round them, thirty-three other people started to laugh and shout and hug each other in the red dusk. Calvin went round the ring of rocks yelling NOBODY GOT BURNED. And Cursiffe sat on his rock with his pages in his lap and a very small, slow smile.

“I took it,” said Wendy, after a long time. Quietly. “Not you. Me.”

“I know.”

“So it’s mine. Not yours.”

Yan took his hands off his face. He looked at her. His eyes were red.

“It’s both of ours,” he said. “I showed you where.”

Wendy thought about that.

“Okay,” she said. “Both of ours, then.” She looked out at the two dead Prisms lying side by side in the shallows of the Still Water, dull and grey in the dusk. “That’s fair. That’s what a party’s for.”

That night, at the bottom of the pit, Wendy found Gabe.

He was in the round room, sitting on his bedroll with the lamp shield across his knees. He wasn’t on the lip. Drew had sent him to bed. He was looking at the shield in the lamplight, turning it a little, running his thumb along the dark red rim.

She sat down beside him. She wasn’t sure why.

“You didn’t use it,” she said. “Today.”

“No.”

“You carried it all the way out there. On your back. And you didn’t use it.”

“It’s not mine,” said Gabe.

“I know.” Wendy looked at the shield. Dark grey, with the lamp in the middle and the fine lines going out like the lines round a sun. “Everybody knows.”

Gabe ran his thumb along the rim. There was something on the inside of it, Wendy saw. Right where a hand would grip. A tiny shape, worked into the dark red iron. Five petals and a dot.

“Jared did that,” said Gabe, when he saw her looking. “On five. So he’d know which bit was his.”

Wendy looked at the little iron rose for a long time.

“I took a shot today,” she said, “that Yan couldn’t. Because he could see something I couldn’t. And he showed me where.” She was quiet for a moment. “I think that’s what everybody’s going to have to do. For him. When we find him.” She looked at the shield. “He’s been taking all the shots himself. For a hundred and forty-six days. He doesn’t have to.”

Gabe didn’t say anything. But after a while he nodded, slowly, and turned the shield over in the lamplight. He put his own hand on the inside of the rim, over the little iron rose, where somebody’s hand would go.

“Soon,” said Gabe quietly. To the shield. “Not yet. But soon.”

Wendy didn’t know where he’d got that from. It sounded like something somebody else had said first.
