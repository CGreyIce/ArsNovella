Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 6: Unwritten

Chapter 228

Unfinished

5,251 words · 23 min read

Rachael was the best watcher of the thirty-six, and she’d never once told anybody why.

It wasn’t hard. It was the easiest thing she did. You sat on a step and you rested your eye on a street, sideways, the way Ottilie did, and you didn’t look away. You didn’t get bored. You didn’t need to talk. You didn’t need anybody to look back at you.

She’d been doing it since floor 1. Long before Turnstead.

You watched. That was the job. In the house for the wounded on ten, at four in the morning, at the end of a long room, you sat where you could see every bed, and you watched, and nobody knew you were doing it, and that was how you caught the ones going wrong before they knew they were going wrong themselves. You were the one at the end of the room. You were the one nobody looked at. That was the whole point of you.

She’d watched Calvin since the first day on floor 1.

His arm. That was where it had started. Floor 1, the first days, and his left arm broken, and her hands on it, and the first heal she’d ever done, not knowing she could, just knowing that something in her went there and closed it. And then the echo, after, twenty minutes of it, in her own left forearm, the same place, while Calvin talked and talked and showed everybody his arm and didn’t know.

She’d put her fist in his collar that afternoon. For no reason. Because he’d started to walk toward the edge of something and she’d thought, very clearly, no.

It had been there ever since. On every floor. Every stair. Every wall and field and river. Two years. Her fist in Calvin’s collar, and her eye on Calvin’s back.

She didn’t know why. Not then. She’d thought it was just Calvin. Calvin, who went first, who talked, who never looked where he was going.

She knew now. Not the whole of it. The shape. She’d sat a watch with Dec on a lake ledge and told him, you tell me, and I do it, and I get the echo; that’s not a request, and he’d said okay, and his face had done something. And on ten she’d watched him read every name on the Long Wall, making sure none of them are ours. And on eight, in the snow at Wendmere, Dennis had run to her shouting, and Dec had come round the corner faster than she’d ever seen anybody move and stopped dead and stared at Dennis’s arm. You ran to her. Shouting. And then sat down in the snow with his face in his hands.

She knew the shape.

She’d known it for a long time. She’d chosen not yet. On thirteen, on the Tellhouse steps, with the whole of Tell writing his name on slates. Not yet. She’d ask him when it was safe. When there was nothing left on the floor that could go wrong. When she could stand to hear the answer.

It had never been safe yet.

And she’d been at seventy since the Hollow, since the night the Moth opened, with a window in the corner of her eye that she’d looked at once and closed. Because she’d been frightened, the way you’re frightened of a letter you know is going to have bad news in it, that it was going to say ask.

She opened it on the morning they went into the middle.

On her step. On watch. Facing north. With the grey coming up over the roofs of the Crook and the dozen strays drifting over the street, turned down, keeping it, and Salty’s hammer going two houses along, THUD, every four seconds, so the street wouldn’t so much as lean.

She opened it.

She read it three times.

Then she laughed.

Once. Out loud. On watch. She never laughed on watch. She never laughed much at all; Calvin kept count, he said it was four in two years and Dennis wrote them down. On the step of the sixth house, Dennis said Rachael without turning round, in exactly the voice she’d used on him three nights before.

“Sorry,” said Rachael. “Sorry. It’s just—“

She looked at the last line.

Do not ask how they remembered you.

She’d been afraid for thirty falls that it would say ask. And it said don’t.

“It’s a don’t,” said Rachael. To her street. “Mine’s a don’t.”

“What’s a don’t?”

“Nothing.” She shut the window. “I can do a don’t. I’ve been doing it for a year.”

Higu put them in a ring.

Round the edge of the still square, at the end of the Loose, where every house had windows and no doors. All thirty-six. Not far apart; close enough that you could reach out and touch the one beside you, if you had to. Facing in. At the square, and the flags, and the shape that wasn’t, standing in the middle at the edge of every eye.

And every one of them watching the one on their left.

Sideways. That was the trick. You rested one eye on the square, the way you’d rest it on a street. And the other, the edge of it, on the person on your left. You didn’t look at them. You didn’t need to. You just didn’t stop knowing they were there.

Cover the one on your left. Let the one on your right cover you.

Azac had said it. Out loud. On the edge of the square, before they spread out. Not his Saga voice. His ordinary one. And then he’d been sick, quietly, against the wall of a house with no door, and wiped his mouth, and taken his place.

Rachael was between Calvin and Dennis.

Calvin on her right. Watching her. Dennis on her left. She was watching him. That was Higu’s ring: every one of them watching left and being watched from the right, all the way round, thirty-six people, a chain with no ends.

Her fist was in Calvin’s collar.

It was the wrong way round for the ring. Her fist in his collar meant she was holding on to her right, and she was supposed to be watching her left. Higu had looked at it, at the edge of the square, for a long moment. And then said nothing. And Rachael had kept it there.

“You’re holding on to the wrong one,” said Calvin. Out of the side of his mouth. Very quietly, for Calvin.

“I know.”

“You’re meant to watch Dennis.”

“I’m watching Dennis.”

“You’re holding me.”

“I can do both,” said Rachael. “I’ve been doing both for two years.”

Calvin didn’t say anything. She felt him not say anything. He’d said nothing, completely nothing, maybe five times in two years, and she’d counted every one. This was the sixth.

Across the square, a long way across, on the far side of the ring, she could see Dec. Small, at that distance. On Thea’s right. Thea with the lantern, held low, amber leaking out of the cloak. Dec with Tuli’s pole. And on his belt, the little lantern, humming. She couldn’t hear it from here. She could see it. A pale point at his hip.

He wasn’t looking at her. He was looking at the square. Sideways. And the edge of his eye on Thea.

Good.

Out in the middle of the flags, at the edge of every eye, the shape that wasn’t there stood very tall and very still.

And then, for the first time since anybody had seen it, it moved.

It didn’t walk. It didn’t do anything she’d have called moving if she’d seen it anywhere else.

It got more.

That was the only way she could say it, later, on the step, to Dennis, who wrote it down. It got more. The shape at the edge of her eye, the tall thin person-shaped gap in the middle of the square, didn’t step or turn or reach. It was just, suddenly, bigger. Not taller. Fuller. As if somebody had been standing behind it the whole time and stepped out.

And then there were more of them.

Shapes. On the flags. Standing up.

Grey. Thin. The shape of people. Not tall like the one in the middle; ordinary. People-sized. Standing up off the grey flags all over the square, the way mist stands up off a field in the morning. Ten. Twenty. A hundred. More. All at the edge of her eye. When she looked straight, the square was empty. When she looked sideways, it was full.

People-shaped places. Where somebody should be and wasn’t.

She knew what they were before anybody said.

“Forty-two,” said Dennis. On her left. Very quietly. His hand was flat on his coat, over the book. “It’s them. That’s them. That’s who it’s got.”

They didn’t come at the ring.

That was the strange thing. They didn’t attack. They didn’t run. They just walked, slowly, across the grey flags, toward the ring. Toward the thirty-six. The way people walk toward a lit window in a street at night when they’ve been outside in the dark a long time.

To be looked at.

She saw one come toward Dennis. A woman’s shape. Grey. Thin. With a limp. On the left. Walking toward him across the flags, slowly, the limp very clear, and stopping, an arm’s length in front of him. And standing there.

Dennis made a sound.

“The usual,” he said. Under his breath. Rachael heard it. “Brown loaf and a twist. The usual.”

The grey woman stood in front of him. Waiting.

Rachael kept her eye on Dennis. Sideways. On the side of his face.

Another came toward Deston. A man’s shape. Walking over on the outsides of his heels. Another toward Hako, small, a boy’s shape, with something in its hand that might have been a whistle. Another, an old woman’s, toward Aleaka.

All round the ring. Grey shapes coming across the square to stand in front of people. Close. Waiting.

They want to be seen, she thought. That’s all they want. Somebody to look at them.

And to see them, you’d have to look straight at them.

And if you looked straight at them, you’d stop watching the one on your left.

“Don’t,” said Higu. From somewhere along the ring. Her voice shaking. “Don’t look at them. Don’t. Sideways. Everybody. Sideways—”

It was Calvin.

She’d know that afterwards and it wouldn’t help. It was Calvin, on her right, whose job was her.

A shape came across the flags toward him. She saw it out of the corner of her eye, past his shoulder: grey, thin, a girl’s shape. Small. Ten, maybe. With her arms out. The way a child holds her arms out when she wants to be picked up.

And Calvin, who’d talked his way up seventeen floors, who’d shouted ALYS across a square in Tell five times until a woman who’d been written over came back, who’d never once in his life seen somebody waiting to be looked at and not looked—

Calvin turned his head.

Not far. A fraction. To see her face. To see the face of a grey girl with her arms out, who nobody had looked at in however many years.

He looked straight at her.

She wasn’t there.

And for one breath, the length of a turned head, the thing on Calvin’s left wasn’t being watched by anybody.

It was cold.

That was the first thing. Not like the Rimed on ten. Not the cold she’d pulled out of a hundred frostbitten fingers with her bare hands and carried until her arms went white. This cold didn’t come in. It went out. Out of her. From the middle, from somewhere behind her breastbone, like warmth going out of a room when somebody leaves the door open in winter.

She looked down at her own hand. The one in Calvin’s collar.

It was grey.

Not white. Not frost-white, the way it went when she carried. Grey. The colour of the flags. Of the shapes. From the fingertips back. And as she watched, it went thinner. Not smaller. Thinner. As if there were less of it.

Be forgotten.

She looked up.

Calvin had turned back. Fast. She saw it in his face, the moment he realised he’d looked away, the horror of it, his head snapping back round to her—

And stopping.

He looked at her.

He looked at her with his mouth open and his eyes going over her face, and she watched it happen. She’d watched a lot of things happen to faces. In the house for the wounded at four in the morning. On a field on fourteen. She’d never watched this.

She watched him not know who she was.

“Who—“ said Calvin.

Very quietly. Looking at her. At the grey woman standing on his left with her fist in his collar.

“Who—“ He looked down. At the hand in his collar. Grey. Thin. Holding on. He looked at it the way you’d look at a stranger’s hand on your arm in a crowd. “Whose—“

He didn’t finish.

She didn’t say anything. She couldn’t. Her mouth had gone grey too, she thought. Or her voice had. She opened her mouth to say Calvin, it’s me, you idiot, and nothing came out at all.

On her left, Dennis had turned.

She saw him do it. His hand on the book in his coat. Pulling it out, the way he’d pull it out at a stall. Opening it, fast, the pages going. Looking for her. She saw him look. Calvin, 11 (eggs). Hako, 3. Dec, two loaves and a pot. Every one of them was in there. Everybody who’d ever owed anybody anything.

She’d never once been on Dennis’s tab.

Not in two years. She’d made sure. She’d never let anybody owe her and she’d never owed anybody, not a loaf, not a favour, not a heal; she’d done them and walked away and never once let anybody write it down. It was the way she’d always been. You did the job. You didn’t keep a tally. You didn’t let anybody keep a tally of you.

She watched Dennis go through the whole book and not find her.

He looked up from it. At her. His face doing the thing Calvin’s had done. A man reaching into a pocket for keys that weren’t there.

“I don’t—“ said Dennis. “You’re— I know you. I know you, I know I— You’re not on the—“

He stopped.

Along the ring, she felt it go. Not see. Feel. The way you feel a draught go along a corridor. Deston, three people down, with his hand on his chest, turning his head with his face going blank. Azac. Hako. Xav. One after another. Every one of them, all the way round the ring, as the cold went out of her and along the chain, losing something. Not knowing what. Their faces going still, the way faces go still when you’ve walked into a room and can’t remember why.

She was being let out.

Of everybody. All at once. Like a line taken out of a page, so carefully the page closed over the gap and you’d never know there’d been anything written there.

She stood on the edge of the square with her grey fist in Calvin’s collar, and nobody knew who she was.

It was very quiet.

She’d thought it would hurt. It didn’t. It didn’t feel like anything. It felt like the end of a long night in the house for the wounded on ten, when you go out into the yard at dawn and it’s empty and nobody knows you’ve been there all night, and you stand in the snow for a minute before you go to bed, and you think: nobody saw any of that.

That was all it was. That feeling. Except it didn’t end. You didn’t go to bed.

The grey went up her arm.

She could feel the square pulling. Behind her eyes. Toward the middle. Toward the tall shape at the edge of everybody’s eye. Not a pull, really. A sort of lean. The way the streets of Turnstead leaned when nobody was looking. She was leaning. Toward the middle of the flags. Toward all the other grey shapes, the woman with the limp and the man on the outsides of his heels and the girl with her arms out.

They’re not frightening, she thought. They’re just us. Everybody nobody looked at.

She thought: I’ve been one of them my whole life. I just didn’t know it had a name.

She thought: Calvin’s going to be all right without me. He’s got a whole ring. He’s got Dennis. He’s got thirty-four people.

She thought, and it was the last clear thing: I never asked him. I never asked Dec. I was going to, when it was safe. And it was never safe. And now there isn’t anybody left who knows I wanted to.

Her fist was going thin in Calvin’s collar.

She held on.

She didn’t know why. She didn’t know who she was holding on to any more. Somebody. A collar. A back she’d watched for two years. She held on to it with a hand that was nearly not a hand and didn’t let go.

“Rachael.”

It came across the square.

Not loud. It didn’t need to be loud. It was the only sound on the whole floor. One word. Said plainly. The way you’d say somebody’s name across a room when you want them to know you’ve seen them come in.

She turned her head.

She didn’t know how she did it. She didn’t have much head left to turn. She turned it, and looked across the square, past the grey shapes and the flags and the tall thing in the middle, all the way across, to the far side of the ring.

Dec was looking at her.

Straight at her. Not sideways. He’d turned his whole head. He was standing on Thea’s right with Tuli’s pole in his hand and Gabe’s terrible lantern on his belt, a long way off, small, and he was looking straight across the square at her the way nobody in Turnstead ever looked at anything.

And Thea, beside him, had turned her head too. Not to the square. To him. Watching him. Full on. Not sideways. Every bit of her watching him, so that he could look away from everything else.

He was looking at Rachael.

“Rachael,” said Dec again.

He knew her name.

Everybody on the floor had lost it. Calvin, with her fist in his collar. Dennis, with his book. Deston, who’d been on the first floor with her, the first party, the four of them and Azac. Every one. And Dec, all the way across the square, at the other end of the chain from her, with no tab and no book and no reason, said it like it had never once been anywhere but in his mouth.

Like he’d carried it through worse.

She knew. That was the thing. She knew, standing on the edge of the square with the grey going up her arm. She knew the shape. She’d known it for a year. A man who’d stared at Dennis’s arm on four and sat down in the snow. A man who’d read every name on the Long Wall making sure none of them are ours. A man who’d been somewhere, once, where her name had been a thing you had to remember on purpose, because there wasn’t anybody left to say it.

He’d remembered her through that. Whatever it was.

He could remember her through this.

Bearing, she’d think afterwards. On the step. Turning it over. Fix a bearing on someone whose name you have set down. He’d set them all down, she supposed. Somewhere. Every name. On every floor. While you share a floor, you will always know which way they are. That was the reason. That was a perfectly good reason. She’d write it on a slate if anybody asked.

It wasn’t the reason. She knew that too.

Do not ask how they remembered you.

She didn’t ask.

The cold stopped.

Not slowly. All at once. The way pain stops when you finally get the splint on. The going-out, the open-door feeling, the draught along the corridor: it stopped, and turned round, and came back. Into her. From the middle. Warm.

She looked down at her hand in Calvin’s collar.

Pink. Her own. Her knuckles white, because she was holding on so hard.

“RACHAEL.”

Calvin.

Right in her ear. So loud it hurt. So loud the windows of the doorless houses round the square seemed to shake with it. Calvin, who’d turned his head, who’d looked at a grey girl, who’d stood beside her for the length of a breath and not known her. Calvin, shouting her name the way he’d shouted ALYS across a square in Tell. Like it was the first thing anybody had ever said that got in.

“Rachael. Rachael. RACHAEL—”

“Shut up,” said Rachael. Her voice came back. Rough. “Shut up, you absolute— Watch Dennis—“

“I forgot you—“

“I know.”

“I looked at her and I forgot you, I didn’t know who you— your hand was in my collar and I didn’t know whose—”

“I know, Calvin.”

“Two years. You’ve had your hand in my collar for two years and I looked at it and I didn’t—“ His voice broke. Right down the middle. “I didn’t know whose hand it was—“

He turned round.

Not to the square. Not sideways. All the way round, out of the ring, his back to the flags and the grey shapes and the thing in the middle. And put both his arms round her.

She wasn’t a hugger. Everybody knew. She’d never once in two years let anybody, not after a field, not after a fever, not after a wall. She did the job and stood back.

She let him.

She stood on the edge of the still square with Calvin’s arms round her and her fist still in the back of his collar, because she couldn’t make her hand let go, and didn’t cry, because she didn’t, and shook. All over. For a long time.

“Two,” said Dennis.

On her left. Very quietly. With the book open in his hand. He’d turned too. He was looking at her. Straight. His face was wet.

“Rachael,” said Dennis. Writing. His pen going. “Rachael. Never owed anybody anything. Never let anybody owe her.” He looked at the page. “I’m putting you on the tab.”

“Don’t you dare.”

“You’re on it,” said Dennis. “Rachael. Owed: everything.” He shut the book. “It’s not going to be paid. It’s never going to be paid. It’s going to sit there forever. That’s the point.”

She looked at him over Calvin’s shoulder.

She didn’t say anything. She couldn’t.

All round the ring, the faces were coming back. She could see it. Deston, with his hand on his chest, his face going from blank to white to something she’d never seen on Deston. Azac. Hako. Xav. One after another, all the way round. Every one of them turning, just for a moment, out of the ring, to look at her. Straight. Full on.

Every one of them seeing her.

Across the square, on the far side, Dec had turned back. To the square. Sideways. Thea had turned back too. To him. Sideways. The ring had closed up again behind her, the way water closes behind a boat.

He didn’t look at her again.

He didn’t need to.

She read it with her chin on Calvin’s shoulder.

Not Yet.

She laughed. The second time in one day. Calvin would put it in his count and Dennis would write it down.

Not yet. On floor 10, on the Long Wall, a woman called Brenna had carved her name, RACHAEL, after the Gate, and not put a line under it. And Rachael had stood in front of it with her glove on it and said still not finished, and Brenna had said no. And she’d looked at it every time they went past the Wall since. Still not finished.

She was still not finished.

She took her chin off Calvin’s shoulder.

“Back in the ring,” said Rachael. “Now. Both of you. Watch the one on your left.”

Calvin let go. Slowly. He stood back. He looked at her for a moment longer, full on, the way you’d look at a door you’d nearly walked through and found a drop behind.

Then he turned back to the square. Sideways. And the edge of his eye on her. And she felt it there. Like a hand on her back.

She turned to Dennis. Sideways. And the edge of her eye on him.

And out in the square, at the edge of every eye, all the grey shapes were standing very still.

She saw them differently now.

She didn’t know how. It was the window. What is ending, you may hold open. She’d been a healer for two years and she’d seen a lot of things ending. Patients. Fevers. The light going up out of an old woman on floor 1 and Yan saying it’s lifting. You got a feel for it. A thing at the edge, going.

The grey shapes were ending.

All of them. All over the square. She could see it now, the way she’d see it in a face in the house for the wounded. The woman with the limp, in front of Dennis. The man on the outsides of his heels. The girl with her arms out, still standing where Calvin had looked at her. Every grey shape on the flags was thinning. Slowly. The way she’d thinned. Going out of the edges in. Going back into the middle. To the tall shape at the edge of every eye.

It had held them up. That was what she understood, standing there. The Unseen had stood them up off the flags and walked them across the square to the ring, every forgotten person it had, all at once, so that somebody would look. So that somebody would turn their head. So that the ring would break.

And now the ring was closed, and nobody was looking, and it was taking them back.

Into the middle. Into the gap. Forever.

Get it back, Pask had said. To his lane. If you’re going to put it on a tab.

She didn’t think.

She put out her hand. The one that had been grey. Bare. Palm out, toward the square. Toward all of them. The woman with the limp, the man with his heels, the girl, the forty-two in Dennis’s book and the hundreds that weren’t, standing on the flags, thinning.

“Not yet,” said Rachael.

Out loud. To all of them. The way she’d say it at a bedside, at four in the morning, to somebody who was going and didn’t have to. Quietly. Firmly. Not a request.

Not yet.

They stopped thinning.

All over the square. She felt it. Every grey shape on the flags, held. Not coming back. Not going. Held. Open. At the edge. The way you’d hold a door open with your foot while somebody behind you got their bags.

It cost. She felt it cost. Not cold, not grey; weight. Every one of them a weight on her outstretched hand. Hundreds. Her arm shook.

She didn’t put it down.

“Dennis,” said Rachael. Without looking at him. “Your tab.”

“What—“

“You’d have to know who’s got it.” Her arm was shaking. “You know who’s got it now. It’s standing in the middle of the square. Call it in.”

Dennis looked at her. Then at the book in his hand. Forty-two lines. Forty-two dashes.

Then he looked across the square. Sideways. At the tall shape at the edge of every eye, standing in the middle of the flags, with all of them, hundreds of them, held open on the square in front of it by one woman’s bare hand.

His face changed.

She’d seen that face on Dennis exactly once before. In Evermorrow. At a cheesemonger’s. Six and a half.

“Okay,” said Dennis. Very quietly. Opening the book. “Okay. Collecting.”

And at the far end of the ring, on Deston’s left, Azac lifted his head. And in a voice she’d heard on the fifth wall on ten, in the dark, to a line of strangers, the voice that made people fight to make a thing true, he began:

“And so,” said Azac, “our heroes came to the middle of the town that forgot—”

The tall shape in the middle of the square turned toward the ring.

Everybody saw it. Not sideways.

Straight.
