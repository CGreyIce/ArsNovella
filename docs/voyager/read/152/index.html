Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 4: The Second Row

Chapter 152

Is It Bad

4,193 words · 18 min read

Drew counted them out.

He’d counted them in. Six. Himself, and John’s light ahead, and Thea, and Xola close enough to grab, and Chi, and Higu last with her slate. He’d counted them up four plain steps and through a door that opened on its own and down ten paces of grey stone into a hall full of people with nothing in their faces, and he’d counted them all the way down the aisle with a hundred and twelve heads turning, and at the foot of the lectern he’d stopped counting because there was nothing to count but Thea, on her own, on the step, reading.

And then out. Six. Down the aisle, through the passage, out of the door into the warm dark of the Wellgate. He’d stood at the top of the four steps and counted them past him one at a time, the way he’d counted thirty-five through the Gate at the dark of the moon on floor 10, and the way he’d count them through every door there was until there weren’t any more doors.

Six. Nobody left in.

Then he’d looked down at the step, and seen Thea sit down on it beside Ice, and Ice put his face in his hands. And he’d stopped counting, and taken Xola by the shoulder, and gone across the road with Gabe.

He hadn’t asked her. Not on the step. Not at the gate. Not in the copy-room after, when she came in at midnight with her eyes swollen and lay down on her blanket with her hand on the lantern and Ice on the floor beside her, and everybody very carefully went to sleep.

He’d asked her in the hall. At the foot of the lectern. Is it bad? And she’d said I’ll tell you in a minute.

He’d been asking that question since floor 9. Nobody had ever once answered it.

He could wait one more night.

She answered it in the morning.

He was in the yard, at the trough, with his knuckles bare, the way they were all the time now. He’d taken the iron knuckles off on floor 10, for the Empty Hand, and found he didn’t want to put them back on. He was washing his face. She came down the stable stairs with the lantern and stood beside him, the way she’d stood beside Xola at the pump the morning before, and waited till he’d finished.

“Drew.”

“Thea.”

“You asked me something.” She was looking at the Countinghouse across the road. “In there.”

He dried his face on his sleeve. He didn’t say anything. He’d learned that, from her, in three hundred and ninety-five days. When Thea was about to tell you something, you let her.

“Yes,” said Thea.

He looked at her.

“It’s bad,” she said. “It’s the worst thing I’ve ever read. It’s the worst thing I’ve ever had to read in my life.” She was very steady. “And it’s true. And it’s his. And I didn’t let them cross it out.”

Drew stood there with his wet sleeve and his bare hands and looked at her for a long time.

He’d asked it on floor 9. On a root, with a sounder of rooters coming out of the ferns. And on floor 10, at the slate, under IRREGULAR. And on this floor, at the slate again, when Cursiffe’s ones before came up to ninety-nine and Ice’s head tipped slowly and he said I don’t know. And every time, nobody had answered. Not because they didn’t want to. Because they didn’t know.

She knew.

“Okay,” said Drew.

That was all. Okay. He didn’t ask what. He didn’t ask why it was bad, or what was true, or what his meant. He’d been general of thirty-five people for three hundred and fifty-five days. He’d learned which questions were his. That one had stopped being his a long time ago. On a bridge on floor 7, maybe. Or a rock at the edge of a tarn.

“Okay,” he said again. “Then we stand next to him.”

Thea looked at him.

“Malcolm’s plan C,” said Drew. “Whatever they read out. We stand next to him while they read it. And we don’t move.” He picked up his buckler off the edge of the trough, looked at it, and put it down again. He didn’t need it. “That’s not a plan, Xav says. Malcolm says it’s the only one that always works.”

“It always works,” said Thea.

“I know. I was there.” He looked at her. “Is there anything else? That I need to know? Not the thing. For the plan.”

Thea thought about it. He watched her think. She did it the way she read: under the surface, quickly, looking for the line that mattered.

“I wrote told,” she said. “In the book. Beside both of his hands. In my own. The tower wanted me to strike one, and I wouldn’t, and I wrote told beside both. And the clerk wrote witnessed.” She looked at him. “When they read it out at the Reckoning, every line, they’ll read that too. My hand. My name.”

Drew went very still.

“So it’s not just him they’re reading,” he said slowly.

“No.”

“It’s you. Standing up for both of him. In the tower’s own book. In front of the whole of Tell.”

“Yes.”

Drew looked at her. At the lantern in her hand. At the plain grey building across the road, with a clerk on its top step.

“Right,” he said. “Then I need to put you on the plan too.”

He wrote it on the slate that afternoon. For everyone. Because it was a fact, and facts went on the slate, and Drew had been writing facts on slates since floor 3.

He didn’t write what was on the pages. Thea had asked him not to. It’s his. He wrote what had happened.

THE COUNTINGHOUSE. DAY 394.

SIX IN. SIX OUT.

THEA READ THE BOOK. ONLY SHE COULD.

THE TOWER WANTED HER TO STRIKE ONE OF HIS TWO HANDS.

SHE WOULDN’T.

SHE WROTE “TOLD” BESIDE BOTH. IN HER OWN HAND.

THE CLERK WROTE “WITNESSED.”

AT THE RECKONING THEY’LL READ IT ALL. HERS TOO.

He stepped back from it. The copy-room read it.

He watched them read it. All of them. He’d been watching them read his slates for a long time, and he knew how each of them did it. Hyvred out loud under her breath. Calvin with his finger. Rachael all the way through once, fast, and then again, slow. Deston not at all, at first, just standing in front of it with his hand flat on his chest, feeling the room read it.

He watched them get to STRIKE ONE OF HIS TWO HANDS, and go quiet.

He watched them get to SHE WOULDN’T.

And then he watched something happen that he hadn’t expected. Something he’d never seen happen at one of his slates in three hundred and ninety-five days. Thirty-four people reading a line of chalk, and every one of them, at the end of it, turning round. Not to Ice. To Thea. Sitting on her blanket by the end wall with the lantern in her lap.

Nobody said anything.

Then Calvin, at the front, with his fist shut against his chest, said, quite quietly for Calvin:

“Good.”

And then, after a moment, everybody.

Not out loud. Not all of them. But Drew watched it go round the room. Hyvred nodding with her hand over her mouth. Winter, arms folded, jaw set, nodding once, hard. Gigi’s hand finding Bee’s. Yan, with Suzu round his neck, saying something very low that might have been good, or might have been a name. Rachael looking at Ice, across the room, for a long time, and then at Thea, and then back at the slate, and not saying anything at all. Cursiffe, in the corner, with his VOYAGER book on his knee, open, and his pen in his hand. Not writing.

Gabe, by the door, looking at nothing. Like a wall.

And Ice. At the end wall. Next to Thea. With his head down and the cord on his wrist. Not looking at anybody.

Drew looked at him for a long time.

Then he picked up the chalk again, and wrote one more line, at the bottom, under the rest. Smaller.

SOMEBODY THANK HER.

The copy-room laughed. Not much. It came out of thirty-four people all at once, startled, wet, and stopped.

“Thank you, Thea,” said Calvin.

“Thank you, Thea,” said everybody.

Thea put her face in her hands.

The clerks changed the next day.

Drew noticed it first, because Drew noticed everything that stood on a corner. He’d been counting them since the first week. One at every turn of the Wellgate. And since the host’s hand, every one of them had turned its plain face to follow Thea whenever she went up or down the road with the lantern. Like flowers following the sun, Rui Xiang said. You couldn’t walk ten paces beside her without feeling it.

On the morning of the three hundred and ninety-sixth day, Thea went down the Wellgate to the Lantern Market with Xola for bread. And Drew walked behind them, ten paces back, the way he did now. And watched the corners.

The clerks didn’t turn.

Not one. Every corner, every turn of the road, all the way down. Each clerk stood under its dead lamp with its grey book on its arm, as it always had. And as Thea went past, with the lantern, each one of them lowered its head. Not much. A little. And kept it lowered until she was past. Like somebody standing aside on a narrow stair to let somebody go by.

Drew stood still in the middle of the Wellgate and watched it happen, corner after corner, all the way down to the Lantern Market.

“Huh,” he said.

He didn’t know what it meant. He didn’t like not knowing. But he didn’t think it was bad.

And there was something else. At the corner by the turn into Cooper’s Lane, on the second ring, where there’d always been a clerk. Every morning, every evening, for forty days. Under the dead lamp, with its book.

There wasn’t one.

The corner was empty.

Calvin went to Cooper’s Lane at noon on the fifth day, for the fifth time, and nobody came round the corner.

Drew went with him. He’d decided to. He’d watched Calvin come back up the Wellgate four times with his face doing four different things, and he’d decided that the fifth time, whatever it was, Calvin shouldn’t have to come back up on his own.

Hawise was on her step. She’d been there since morning, Calvin said. She hadn’t been sitting. She’d been standing, by the door, with her arms folded, looking at the corner at the end of the lane.

Noon came. The bell on the Tellhouse struck it, a long way up.

Nobody came round the corner.

They waited. An hour. Calvin stood in the middle of the lane with his fist shut. Hawise stood on her step. Drew leaned on the wall of the cooper’s yard and watched the corner, and the bit of the Wellgate you could see past it, and the clerks on the Wellgate, one at every turn, with their heads very slightly down.

Nobody came.

“She dropped her pen,” said Drew, at last.

Hawise turned her head and looked at him.

“In there. When Thea went past with the lantern.” He hadn’t said it before. He hadn’t known if he should. “Thea heard it. Fourth row. I saw it on the floor on the way out. She didn’t pick it up. She just sat there with her hands on her book.” He looked at the corner. “She watched the lantern all the way out. Thea said.”

Hawise didn’t say anything for a long time.

“What happens,” said Calvin. Hoarse. “When a clerk drops its pen. What does that—“

“I don’t know,” said Hawise. “Nobody knows. Nobody’s ever seen one drop it.”

She stood on her step and looked at the empty corner.

“She was good at this,” said Hawise, after a while. Not to them. To the lane. “Alys. When we were girls. Before. She was the best hand in our year. Better than me. She could write a whole door-roll and never blot. She wanted to be a teller more than anything in the world.” She was quiet. “And then she wanted to know what it was all for. And she went in.”

Nobody said anything.

“I’ll be here next week,” said Hawise. She unfolded her arms. “Noon. Same as always.” She looked at Calvin. “Will you?”

“Yes,” said Calvin.

“Good.” Hawise went in, and shut her door.

Calvin stood in the lane with his fist shut and looked at the empty corner.

“Is it bad?” he said. To Drew. Without looking round.

Drew looked at the corner too.

“I don’t know,” he said honestly. “It’s not nothing.”

Higu wiped the line on the fourth evening.

Drew saw her do it. He was in the tack room with the rock, because Higu had asked him to bring it down from the roof, and she was sitting on the straw bale with it on her knees, front side up. The two columns. The left one full, all the way down, small capitals, floor after floor since floor 11. ALDO RAN. 2 MONTHS. OLD ROAD. NORTH. THE MAST HAD ONE MAST. COUNTINGHOUSE: NEVER WENT IN. The right one full too. And the chalk line down the middle, where it had been since the first day on the sea cliff, when she’d drawn it with Ice watching.

She licked her thumb, and put it at the top of the line, and drew it down. Slowly. All the way to the bottom.

The line went. The two columns stayed. Side by side, with nothing between them.

Drew didn’t say anything. He watched her look at it.

“Not wrong,” said Higu, to the rock. “Just somewhere else.”

She didn’t explain. Drew didn’t ask her to. He’d been in a party with Higu for three hundred and ninety-eight days, and he’d learned that when Higu did a thing like that, she’d done it for a reason she’d already thought about for a long time, and she’d tell you when she was ready, or never, and either way it was right.

But he looked at the rock. Two columns, with no line between them. One for what somebody remembered, and one for what they’d found. Next to each other. Both in Higu’s hand.

Two hands, he thought. Agreeing.

When Ice came down to the tack room later, to find Thea, he saw it. He stopped in the doorway and looked at the rock on Higu’s knees for a long time.

He didn’t say anything. But Drew saw his face. And Drew had seen that face exactly once before: at the top of a tarn on floor 10, at dawn, when Ice had come back round the water with Thea and seen Gabe’s second fire on the near shore, and John’s light hanging over the far one, and Deston’s gold going up out of the dark. The face of somebody who’d come back the long way round, expecting nothing, and found somebody had left a light on.

He made the plan for the Reckoning in the tack room. With Xav, and Malcolm, and Deston, and Higu, and the rock and the slate and everything else they had.

It wasn’t much of a plan. That was the point. It was Malcolm’s three, and everything else hung off them.

The great hall of the Assize. The Magistrate behind its screen at the far end. The long table in front of the screen with the stack of Tellhouse slates on it, chalk provided, and Malcolm’s spares in his pack in case. The disputed one, in front of the table, alone, to be read.

“Not alone,” said Drew. He put a stone on the rock where the disputed one would stand. “Gabe on his right.”

“Looking at the screen,” said Xav. “Like a wall. Thea said.”

“Like a wall.” Another stone. “Thea on his left.”

“Is that allowed?” said Deston. “Hawise said the disputed one stands alone before—“

“It’s allowed,” said Drew, “because nobody’s going to stop her.” Another stone. “And then the rest of us. Behind. All thirty-four, in a line, the way we stood on the wall on floor 10. Shields up. No shields. Doesn’t matter.” A row of pebbles. “Calvin at the end with his fist. John off to one side with a light. Hawise’s list behind us. Seventy-something people from the Low Town in their market clothes.”

“Seventy-three,” said Malcolm. “Orlo’s on the fourth sheet.”

Drew looked at the rock. At a stone for Ice, and one on each side of him, and a line of pebbles behind, and a lot of nothing behind that, which was Tell.

“And then they read it,” he said. “Every line. And whatever it is, we stand there. And then the Magistrate says who writes him. And we write him.”

“And Tull?” said Xav.

Drew didn’t answer straight away.

Hamo Tull had been seen at the Countinghouse twice more since Rui Xiang. At night. Hooded. The door opening for him. And coming out again, John said, a lot faster than he went in.

“Tull’s going to stand up and say we’re liars,” said Drew. “Whatever they read. He’s going to say climbers make things not be told, and we’ve broken the Rolls, and Cursiffe makes pages, and Thea writes in the Count’s own book. He’s going to say we’re dangerous.” He looked at the rock. “And he’s not wrong. We are. We’ve been dangerous to the tower since floor 1.”

“So what do we do about him?” said Deston.

Drew picked up the last stone. A small one. He turned it over in his fingers.

“Nothing,” he said. “We stand there. Joan’s will goes first, Hawise says. Tull against Tull. Then Ice.” He put the stone down, at the front, by itself, facing the screen. “Let Tull talk. Let the clerk read. Let Tell decide.” He looked at the rock. “That’s what this floor’s for. People choose. We’ve been on it forty days and they’ve chosen every single thing. Shutters. Buns. Bread on the step. Stones through the window. The host’s hand.” He looked up. “They’ll choose this too.”

Nobody said anything for a moment.

“That’s a terrible plan,” said Xav.

“It’s Malcolm’s plan,” said Drew. “I’m just the general. Somebody thank him.”

“Thank you, Malcolm,” said everybody.

Malcolm went red.

He found Ice on the roof on the sixth night, on his own, for once. Thea had gone down with Xola. Gabe was on the step, the copy-room one, the way he’d taken to sitting there at night since the Countinghouse, with Hearthwall on his knees. Ice was on the parapet with his back to the town, the way he always sat now, looking up the hill at the pale hall at the top of it. The Assize. With its square bell tower and its flag.

Drew sat down on the parapet next to him. Not too close.

They sat there for a while.

“You’ve been going out twice a day,” said Drew.

“Yes.”

“Thea says you’re sixty-five.”

“Sixty-five.”

“Seventy’s your padlock. Tier five.” Drew looked at him sideways. “You want to be there by the Reckoning.”

Ice didn’t say anything. Which meant yes.

“Xola says you heal at sixty now,” said Drew. “Every time. With her right there. She says she nearly fainted the first time.” He looked at his own hands, bare, on his knees. “She says she’s never been so annoyed about anything in her life.”

Ice’s mouth did something at the corner.

They sat a while longer. Drew looked at his hands.

He’d been looking at them a lot, since floor 10. Since the Empty Hand. Put down what you carry. He had. He’d stood on a slope in the snow in front of the Steward’s book with nothing in his hands, and said my name’s Drew, and its hand had stopped. Strike once, with nothing. He had. At the Gate.

And then the third window. The one that had been sitting in his log since the dark of the moon, a hundred and fourteen days, and that he still didn’t understand.

Make it stand.

Make what stand? Stand where? He’d asked Thea once, if she could read anything under it, and she’d looked at the window for a long time and then looked at him in a way he’d never seen her look at anybody, and said no, and changed the subject. He’d asked Chi. Chi had said you’ll know when it’s in front of you, which was very Chi and no help at all.

He turned his hands over on his knees. Palms up. Then down again.

And when he looked up, Ice was looking at them.

Not at Drew. At his hands. On his knees, on the parapet, in the moonlight. With a face Drew had seen on him exactly three times before. On a jetty on floor 8, looking at Calvin’s arms. In the snow on floor 10, looking at Dennis’s. And on floor 9, on the root, at the bottom of the rope, when Drew had held up his buckler and said that’s mine, the turning.

The face of somebody looking at a thing he’d seen before. A long time ago. And it hadn’t been in front of him since.

“What?” said Drew.

Ice looked away. Up the hill. At the Assize.

“Nothing,” he said.

Drew looked at him for a long time.

He thought about asking. He’d been asking is it bad for three floors. He thought about asking what did I do, or what did I make stand, or was I there. He was in the club. He knew what the club thought. He knew what Thea had read in a grey book on a lectern and wouldn’t say. He knew what always meant, and ours, and you ran to her, shouting.

He thought about it very carefully, the way he did. Very fast. Under the jokes.

And decided not to.

“Okay,” said Drew.

They sat there.

“You were good at it,” said Ice, after a long time. Quietly. Still looking up the hill. “This. Making people stand where they needed to stand. You always were.”

Drew sat very still on the parapet.

Always.

He put it away. Carefully. The way Cursiffe put things in his book. The way Higu put things on the rock. He didn’t say anything about it, and he didn’t ask, and he didn’t look at Ice, and Ice didn’t look at him.

But he looked at his own hands for a long time after that. Palms up, on his knees. Empty.

“Calvin says the old man wants you to go up,” said Drew eventually. “Aldo. Himself. Before the Reckoning.”

“I know.”

“Are you going?”

Ice looked at the narrow row of houses at the top of the Wellgate, under the Assize wall. At the last one, with the green door. He couldn’t see it from here. Drew could tell he knew exactly where it was.

“Before the Reckoning,” said Ice.

“Want company?”

Ice was quiet for a while.

“No,” he said. Then: “Yes. To the bottom of the steps.” He almost smiled. “I’m getting good at steps.”

“So I hear,” said Drew. “Somebody thank Thea.”

“Thank you, Thea,” said Ice, to the hill. Quietly.

And for the first time since floor 9, since a root in the Down with a buckler on his arm, Drew heard him laugh at one of Drew’s jokes.
