Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 2: One Floor Ahead

Chapter 45

Shriven

5,073 words · 22 min read

The padlock sat beside his level for two weeks before he did anything about it.

It was a small thing. A little grey lock, drawn in the same thin lines as the rest of the window, next to the number twenty-five. If he looked straight at it, a line of text came up under it. Tier trial required to advance · Experience held. He’d seen it before, at ten, on floor 2. He’d seen it the first time, at every tier, for ten years. It didn’t hurt. It didn’t do anything. It just sat there, every morning, under Gabe’s whisper, being patient.

“You’re looking at it again,” said Alea.

“I’m not.”

“You are. You do this thing with your mouth.”

He was sitting on the high terrace in the red dusk, on the fifty-sixth day, with his back against the wall by the door and his knees up. The door at the top of the two hundred and four steps hadn’t opened. Down the canyon, nothing moved. Floor 4 was clean. He’d spent ten days making sure of it, district by district, roof by roof, and there was nothing left in the Red City that hadn’t already met him.

“Day fifty-six,” said Alea. She had the rankings open in front of her, small and blue, the way she always did in the evenings. “Floor 3, all of them. Average eighteen. Rui Xiang’s twenty. Calvin’s nineteen and has asked Rui Xiang how, in World, four times.”

“They’ll come up at twenty.”

“All of them at twenty,” Alea agreed. “Ten days. Maybe less. Rui Xiang’s going to have to wait for everybody else and he’s going to hate it.”

Ten days. He looked at the padlock again.

He’d been leaving it. He knew why, too, though he hadn’t said it out loud. The trial at twenty-five was the first one he’d never done as a Paladin. On floor 3 he’d known what was coming: the chain and the trial had folded together, and he’d walked through the Fen with a candle in his hand, and it had been terrible, but he’d known its shape before he started. This one he didn’t know. Nobody did. The Paladin trials weren’t in Alea’s logs, because the first time he’d never been a Paladin. All he had was a book.

But the door was going to open in ten days. And when it did, he didn’t want a padlock beside his name. He wanted to be whatever came after this. He wanted to be ready for floor 5 before any of them saw it.

“Open it,” he said.

Three windows came up in the dusk in front of him, side by side.

[ Tier 2 trial · Paladin
Easy: Confess a small wrong.
Normal: Confess a great one.
]

And on the right:

[ Hard: Shriven
Confess everything you carry, to the last name.
The House of Ash · the third terrace
]

He read them all three times. The fox read them over his shoulder. Neither of them said anything for a while.

“Shriven,” said Alea at last.

“It’s the tier name. Tier 3. Shriven.”

“I know it’s the tier name, Ice. I’m saying it because I don’t like it.” She sat down on the stone beside him and curled her tail round her feet. “What did the book say?”

He’d been trying to remember since the padlock came up. The book was in a dead library on the thirty-fourth floor, the first time. A big, cracked, leather-bound thing with a stone knight on the cover. He’d read it two thousand times in the dark, in the years when he’d known he’d never be a Paladin. He knew the first part almost by heart. The chapel, and the vigil, and the light. The Knight who keeps it does not carry the light, nor stand in it. He is it. That part had been clear, once he’d stood in the Fen.

The part after that had been shorter. Older. Half the page had been water-stained.

“Before he may keep the light,” said Ice slowly, “he must set down what he carries. That’s all I’ve got. There was more, but it was gone. It just said that. And something about ash.”

“Set down what he carries,” Alea repeated. “Ice. You carry thirty-five people.”

“I know.”

“You carry thirty-five people and ten years and a room on the hundredth floor. That’s what it means. Everything you carry, to the last name. It means them.” She was looking at him with her two white pinprick eyes. “You’ve never told anybody. Not the whole of it. Not even me, out loud. I only know because I was there.”

“I know, Alea.”

“You don’t have to take the hard one.”

He looked at the three windows. Easy. Normal. Hard.

The first time, he’d taken the normal one at every tier until the sixth. Because everybody had. Because the hard ones looked like suicide, and the normal ones got you through, and getting through was all any of them had been trying to do. On the sixth he’d taken the hard one and nearly died. He’d come out of it with a skill that had saved his life a hundred times afterwards, and saved other people’s, and he’d thought, lying in the mud at the end of it with blood in his mouth: I could have had this five tiers ago.

He’d taken the hard one every time after that. It had been too late for most of them by then.

“I’m taking the hard one,” he said. “Every tier. Every time. I decided on floor 3.”

“I know you did. I’m saying you don’t have to.”

“I know.” He reached out and touched the window on the right. “That’s why I’m doing it.”

[ Trial accepted: Shriven
Go to the House of Ash. Kneel. Speak.
]

He knew the House of Ash. He’d walked past it eleven times, clearing the third terrace.

It was a long, low hall cut straight back into the canyon wall, with a doorway like a mouth and no door in it. Inside, it went back into the rock further than his light could reach, a single long corridor with small stone cells down both sides. Fifty or sixty of them, each one just big enough to kneel in. Each one had a stone grille set into its back wall, carved into a pattern of leaves, and behind each grille there was nothing but dark.

The first time, they’d thought it was a prison. Then Thea had read the carving over the door, slowly, the way she read everything, and said: No. It’s where they came to say things.

He’d never gone in. Nobody had. They’d been too frightened of everything on floor 4 to go into a dark hall in the rock just to look.

He went in now, with his shield on his arm and the cloth still round his boots out of habit, and walked down the long corridor between the cells. His own light went ahead of him, faint and amber, the way it had since the Fen. It lit the dust on the floor. Nobody had walked here in a very long time. There were no footprints at all, except his.

At the very end of the corridor there was a cell that was different. Bigger. The grille in its back wall was carved not with leaves but with a lantern, a small stone lantern with the leaves growing round it. And on the floor in front of it, worn into the stone, there were two shallow hollows side by side. Knees. Hundreds of years of knees.

“This one,” said Alea quietly.

“Yeah.”

He took off the shield and laid it against the wall. He unbuckled the sword and put it beside the shield. He didn’t know why. It just felt like the kind of place where you didn’t kneel with your sword on.

He knelt. His knees went into the hollows as if they’d been cut for him.

Behind the grille, the dark looked back at him.

And then something spoke.

It wasn’t Alea. It wasn’t any voice he knew. It came from behind the grille, from the dark, low and slow and dry, like dust moving across a floor. It didn’t sound like a person. It didn’t sound like a window, either. It sounded like the Red City. Like the wind coming up the canyon, if the wind had learned one question and been waiting a very long time to ask it.

“What do you carry?”

He didn’t answer.

He’d thought about this on the walk down. He’d thought: I’m a Knight. I’ve been a Knight for ten years. Before he was a Paladin, before the light, he’d been the one who stood in front. He’d held doors. He’d held bridges. He’d held a gate on floor 61 for nine minutes against something with three heads while thirty people got through behind him. That was what he did. He held things.

So he’d hold this.

He knelt in the stone hollows with his hands on his knees, and said nothing, and held it.

“What do you carry?” said the dark, after a while.

He held it.

And the weight came.

It came slowly. He didn’t notice it at first. Just a heaviness across his shoulders, the way armour feels heavier at the end of a long day than at the start. Then it was in his arms, as if he were holding his shield up against something leaning on it. Then in his back. Then in his chest, pressing, so that every breath took a little more than the one before.

[ Burden · rising ]

He held it.

An hour went past. Two. He didn’t know. There was no time in the House of Ash, only the dust and the dark behind the grille and the weight, coming down on him like water filling a room. His knees ached in the hollows. His arms shook. His breath came short. And every so often, out of the dark, the voice asked again. Not louder. Not impatient. Just the same dry, slow question, as if it had all the time there was.

“What do you carry?”

“Ice,” said Alea, somewhere very close to his ear. “Your HP.”

He looked. It was going down. Not fast. A sliver at a time, the way a Fen eel bleeds you, so slowly that you don’t see it until it’s a third gone.

He held it. He’d held worse. He’d held the gate on 61. He’d held Thea’s body on 100 until he couldn’t hold anything any more. He could hold this.

[ Burden · rising ]

[ Trial · failing · You are holding it. ]

He read that line twice, through the sweat running into his eyes.

You are holding it.

“I know,” he said, out loud, through his teeth. His voice came out cracked and strange in the stone cell. “I know I’m holding it. That’s what I do.”

The dark behind the grille said nothing at all.

The weight came down. His arms gave. He caught himself on his hands on the stone floor, and stayed there, on his hands and knees in front of the grille, and the weight kept coming, pressing his shoulders down toward the floor, his chest down toward the stone. And at a quarter of his health, with his elbows starting to bend, he understood that he could hold it until it killed him, and it would never stop.

“Ice.” Alea was in front of him now, small and teal and frantic, between him and the grille. “Ice, get out. Get up and get out. It’s a trial, you can leave, you can come back, just get—”

He got out.

He didn’t remember doing it. He remembered the corridor, the long dark row of empty cells going past on both sides, and the dust, and his own breath roaring. Then the doorway like a mouth. Then the third terrace, and the red dusk, and the dry wind on his face. He went down on his back on the red stone outside the House of Ash and lay there, breathing, while the weight came off him by inches and the ember behind his breastbone pulled his health back up.

[ Trial · paused ]

Alea sat on his chest. She was very light. She didn’t say anything for a long time.

He went back in the next morning. He didn’t do anything clever. He just tried a smaller thing.

He knelt in the hollows, and the voice asked, and before the weight had properly come he said: “The first time, on floor 2, I told everyone the stew was rabbit. It wasn’t rabbit.”

The dark was quiet for a moment.

“That is a small wrong,” said the dry voice. “You chose hard.”

The weight came anyway. He’d known it would.

So he tried a great one. It took him an hour of kneeling to get it out, and it came out in a voice he didn’t recognise.

“I let Rachael run past me,” he said. “On the stair. On this floor. The first square. I was in front. I was the tank. It was my job to be in front of everybody, and she went past me, and I didn’t—I didn’t stop her. I didn’t even see her go. She went past me and down the stair to Calvin, and the Quiet—“

His voice stopped. He knelt there.

The weight eased. A little. For a few seconds his breathing came easier, and his shoulders came up off his knees.

“And?” said the voice.

The weight came back.

Everything you carry, to the last name. Not one great wrong. Not the worst one, even. All of it.

He knelt there for the rest of the day with his health going down by slivers, saying nothing more, and when it got to a third he got up and went out again, under his own power this time. He sat on the step outside the House of Ash with his elbows on his knees and his face in his hands.

“I can’t,” he said.

Alea said nothing.

“Not can’t.” He pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes. “I can say it. I can say all of it. That’s not—“ He stopped. “Alea. Whose is it?”

“Whose is what?”

“The trial. The voice. What do you carry.” He took his hands off his face and looked at her. “The windows are yours. The other thirty-five have their lights. The lights are on their side. They’ve been on their side since the white room. Every one of them looked straight at me on the first morning and said there are thirty-five players, and never told anybody different. But the trials—“ He looked back at the doorway like a mouth. “The trials aren’t the lights. The trials are the tower’s. They always were.”

The fox was very still.

“If I go in there,” said Ice, “and say all of it. The whole thing. Every name, and where, and when. I’m saying it to the tower.”

“Yes,” said Alea. Very quietly. “You are.”

They sat on the step for a long time.

Down the canyon, the dry wind came up from the bottom of the Red City, carrying dust. Somewhere far below, a pack of dust-hounds was barking along the dry river. On a roof across the terrace, a row of carrion birds turned their long necks to watch a man and a small fox made of light sitting on a step.

“You could take the normal one,” said Alea. “Just the once. Nobody would know.”

“I’d know.”

“Ice.”

“I’m not taking the normal one.” He said it quietly, without heat, the way you say a thing you decided a long time ago. “I took the normal one for five tiers, the first time. Everybody did. It got us through. And then on the sixth I found out what I could have had, and by then—“ He didn’t finish. He didn’t have to. She’d been there. “I’m not doing that again. Every tier, I’m taking the hard one. If the hard one means the tower hears me—“ He looked at the doorway. “Then it hears me.”

The fox looked at him for a long time.

“Okay,” she said. “Then I’ll be in there with you.”

He didn’t go back in that night. He sat on the step and thought about why it hadn’t worked.

You are holding it.

He’d thought the trial was asking him to carry the weight. That was what a Knight’s trial would ask: hold this. Hold it longer than you think you can. He’d been training for that one his whole life. But it hadn’t been testing whether he could hold it. It had been telling him he was. Holding it. The way you hold your breath. The way you hold a shield up against something that isn’t there any more, for ten years, because you’ve forgotten how to put your arm down.

Before he may keep the light, he must set down what he carries.

Set down. Not hold. Not even carry. Set down.

He thought about the Fen. About the candle going out in his hand on the reed path, and the moment he’d understood it wasn’t his light, it was Mother Tallow’s, and you couldn’t carry someone else’s light through that. He’d put the ember into his own chest, and stopped carrying the light, and become it. The Knight who keeps it does not carry the light.

And he thought: I’ve been carrying them the same way.

All thirty-five of them. Every one, everywhere, at every hour, for ten years. Like a shield on his arm he’d never once put down. That was why it got heavier every time he didn’t speak. Because he wasn’t letting it be heavy. He was holding it up off the ground with everything he had, so that nobody else would ever have to see how heavy it was. Not even Alea. Not even himself.

The trial wasn’t asking him to hold them. It was asking him to put them down. Somewhere he could see them. Somewhere it could see them. Out loud, in front of something that would hear.

You couldn’t set something down without letting go of it.

“Alea,” he said, around midnight, with the red dusk exactly the same as it had been at noon.

“I’m here.”

“I’m going to say it. All of it. You don’t have to listen.”

“I’ve heard it,” said the fox. “I was there for all of it. I’m going to listen anyway.”

He knelt in the hollows on the morning of the fifty-eighth day, without his shield, without his sword, and the weight began to come, and the dark behind the grille asked its question.

“What do you carry?”

He opened his hands on his knees. Palms up. He didn’t know why. It just seemed like the thing to do with them.

“My name’s Declan,” he said. “I’m thirty-six years old.”

Behind the grille, the dark went still.

He felt it. He couldn’t have said how. The voice didn’t say anything, the weight didn’t change, nothing moved in the cell. But something in the dark that had been patient for a very long time had stopped being patient, for one breath, and listened.

He knew what his window said. Voyager · Lv 25. Somewhere in the tower’s own books, the ones the lights didn’t keep, there’d be a line with an age on it, and the age would say twenty-six. He’d been twenty-six for fifty-eight days. Inside, he’d been thirty-six the whole time.

“I’ve done this before,” said Ice. “All of it. The white room, and the arch, and every floor. All hundred. I did it for ten years. And this is what I carry.”

And he set it down.

He started with Rachael, because she was on this floor. The thirtieth day, at dusk. The stair to the first square. She went past him. He told the dark how she’d gone past him and he hadn’t seen her go, and how she’d held Calvin’s arm while she died. Then Dennis, shouting her name, by the wall. Then Calvin, who’d lived, with one arm, and never talked above a whisper on this floor again.

He didn’t hold any of it up. He just said it, and let it lie on the stone in front of the grille, where he could see it.

The weight eased.

Then he said the next one.

He said them all. He’d never said them all, not once, not to anyone, not even to himself in the dark. He’d told them round fires in pieces, leaving gaps. He didn’t leave any gaps now. He said every name. Where, and when, and what he’d been doing at the time, and what he hadn’t done. Some of them took a minute. Some of them took an hour. Some of them he had to stop in the middle of, and kneel there with his palms up and his face wet, until he could go on. The dark behind the grille waited every time. It wasn’t impatient. It had never been impatient. It just listened.

And every time he finished one, and let it lie there, the weight came off him a little more.

Somewhere in the middle of it, he stopped being able to see the grille, and stopped trying. Somewhere after that, Alea came and sat on his open left palm, small and warm and teal, and stayed there. She didn’t say anything. Every so often, when he couldn’t remember a date, she said it for him, very quietly. That was all.

He said the gate. Gabe, and Xav, and Azac. Gigichi, and Cursiffe, and Ina. The last thing he’d seen of them was the doors closing on three of them still fighting. He said the boss room. Deston nearest the door, with his hands out. Yan against the left wall with Suzu on his chest. Alice’s crown on the floor, a little way from her hand. Drew by the throne steps, with his knuckles still on.

And Thea.

He said Thea last. She was always last.

“She stepped round me,” he said. His voice had gone somewhere very small and far away. “I was holding. I’d been holding for—I don’t know. A long time. My shield had broken. It had been Gabe’s shield, he made it on sixty-one, and it broke, and I was holding with my arm. And she stepped round me. In front of me. She put a ward up, and she said something. Three words. Maybe four. And I didn’t hear it. I was too far away, and it was too loud, and I didn’t hear it.” He stopped. “I never heard it. And then the thing she was stopping went through the ward, and through her. And I got up, and I killed it. And everybody was dead.”

He knelt there with his palms open.

“That’s everything,” he said. “That’s all of it. Everybody. That’s what I carry.”

The dark was quiet for a long time.

Then the dry voice spoke once more. Not a question, this time.

“And you.”

He sat very still.

To the last name.

“And me,” said Ice. “I died too. A bit. On the hundredth floor. Sitting in that room with all of them. And the tower asked me if I wanted to go home.” He breathed. “And I said no. I came back. I’m here.” He looked at the grille, at the stone lantern carved in the middle of it, with the leaves growing round. “My name’s Declan. That’s the last one.”

The weight came off him.

All at once. Not a little at a time any more. As if somebody had lifted a shield off his arm that had been there so long he’d stopped knowing it was there. He breathed in, and his chest went all the way out, further than it had gone in ten years. And he knelt in the stone hollows in the House of Ash, empty-handed, with the fox on his palm, and didn’t hold anything.

And the light came out of him.

Not the way it had in the Fen, slowly, like sunrise up a valley. This time it came all at once. Amber, warm, the colour of a lamp in a window on a winter night, pouring out of his chest and his open hands into the cell, and up the walls, and through the stone grille into the dark behind it. And the dark behind the grille wasn’t dark any more. There was nothing there. There had never been anything there. Just a small, empty, dusty room, with a stone bench in it, where somebody had once sat and listened to people.

The light went out through the cell door into the long corridor, and down it, all the way to the doorway like a mouth. In all fifty or sixty of the empty cells, the dust on the floor lit up gold.

There was a window in front of him.

It was there for one frame. Less. He almost didn’t see it. A flicker in the corner of his eye, the way you catch the flash of something you’ll spend the next hour telling yourself you didn’t see.

[ Irregular ]

Then it was gone, and the real windows came.

[ Shriven · complete ]

[ Tier trial passed · Hard ]

[ Voyager · Lv 27 · Tier 3: Shriven · Paladin (Unique: Lodestar)
Experience released: 2 levels
]

[ Unique skill: Bearing
Fix a bearing on someone whose name you have set down. While you share a floor, you will always know which way they are, and when they are hurt.
]

[ Bearings held: 35
None on this floor.
]

He read them slowly, the way he read every window. Bearing. He read it again. Someone whose name you have set down. He’d set down thirty-five. Every one of them, on the stone, in front of the grille.

“Bearings held: thirty-five,” said Alea. She was still on his palm. Her voice had gone strange. “Ice. The book. The bit that wasn’t water-stained, at the bottom of that page. Do you remember?”

He did, now. One line, near the end of the Paladin’s second trial, half washed away. Most hold one. A king, a wife, a child, a friend.

“Most hold one,” he said.

“You’ve got thirty-five.” The fox’s two white eyes were very bright. “None on this floor. Because they’re not here yet. They’re on three.” She swallowed. “When they come through that door. When they’re on the same floor as you—“

“I’ll know,” said Ice. “Which way they are. All of them.”

And when they are hurt.

He sat back on his heels in the hollows. He didn’t say anything for a while. He was thinking about a stair in the first square, and twenty metres, and Rachael going past him in the dark without him ever seeing her go.

Never again. Not on any floor where he was standing. Not without him knowing.

He picked up his sword and his shield and walked back up the long corridor between the cells, through the dust that was still faintly gold under his boots, and out through the doorway into the red dusk. He stood on the third terrace and looked at the windows one more time.

Then he looked at Alea.

“You saw it,” he said.

She didn’t pretend she didn’t know what he meant. “I saw it.”

“Irregular.”

“One frame. Before the trial window. It wasn’t one of mine.” She sat down on the red stone and wrapped her tail round her feet, very tight. “I’ve logged every window you’ve ever had. Every one. Ten years. I’ve never seen that one.”

“What does it mean?”

“I don’t know.” She looked back at the dark doorway. “Ice. That wasn’t a trial. Not really. It was a door. And there was something on the other side of it, listening.” Her voice went small. “And you told it everything.”

He looked at the doorway too.

“I’m thirty-six. I’ve done this before,” said Alea. “You said that to the tower. In the tower’s own room. Out loud.”

“I know.”

“The lights have been keeping it for you since the white room. All thirty-five of them. Not one of them has said a word. And you walked in there and—“ She stopped. “I’m not angry. I’m frightened. There’s a difference.”

“I know,” said Ice. “Me too.”

He sat down beside her on the edge of the terrace with his legs over the drop, the way he’d sat on the parapet of the drowned tower. Below him, the Red City went down and down to the dry river in the endless dusk. Somewhere at the very bottom, in the furnace hall, the Kiln had come back the day after he killed it, and stood in the dark, and waited. Somewhere a long way above, ninety-six floors away, the thing he’d killed on the hundredth floor didn’t exist yet. Or it did, and it was watching. Or it had just heard someone say I’ve done this before, in a stone room on floor 4, and stopped, for one frame, and looked down.

He didn’t know. Nobody had ever known what it saw.

“Would you do it again?” said Alea. “If you’d known? The hard one?”

He thought about it. He thought about it honestly, because she deserved that, sitting on the terrace with her tail wrapped round her feet, frightened for him.

Bearings held: thirty-five.

“Yeah,” he said. “Every time.”

The fox looked at him for a long moment. Then she leaned against his arm, very lightly, the way she did when she’d decided to stop arguing. They sat on the edge of the third terrace, and the dry wind came up the canyon, and it didn’t feel heavy at all.
