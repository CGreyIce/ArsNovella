Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 4: The Second Row

Chapter 123

The Big Tide

7,778 words · 34 min read

The port way was eleven centimetres low at the bottom.

Milli had measured it three times, because the first time he hadn’t believed it and the second time he’d thought he’d done it wrong. He’d done it with a length of knotted cord from Marrit’s net-pile and a flat stone and a bucket of harbour water poured carefully into a groove, and he’d lain on his stomach on the greased timber of the slip in the dark under the Patience’s hull for an hour, at low tide, with the smell of tar and old fish and the mud ticking round him, watching the water run.

It ran to port. Every time. Down the last stretch of the slip, where the two long greased timbers the ship would slide on went down into the mud, the left one sagged. Not much. Eleven centimetres over twenty metres. You’d never see it.

But a ship that size, sliding on two rails, with one rail lower than the other, would lean as she went. And slow. And at the bottom, where the slip went into the water and she was half afloat and half not, with her stern held up by the sea and her bow still on the timber, she’d stop.

And then her own weight would break her back.

He crawled out from under the hull into the grey before dawn with grease to his elbows and his tail stiff with cold, and found Marrit already on the slip with a mallet, because Marrit was always already on the slip with a mallet.

“Your port way’s low,” said Milli.

Marrit stopped hammering. She looked at him. At the grease. At the knotted cord in his hand.

“How low?”

He told her.

She didn’t say anything for a long moment. Then she put the mallet down very carefully on the timber, the way you’d put down something you were afraid you might throw, and turned round, and looked up the slip to where her grandfather was sitting on his bucket under the bow, with his mug, in the cold, watching them both.

“Grandad,” said Marrit.

“I know,” said Corran.

“You know?”

“Three years,” said the old man. He drank from his mug. “Sank in the big storm, the winter before last. The mud went out from under the bottom end of it. I felt it go.” He looked at the ship over his head. “I couldn’t lift it. Nobody’s got the arms.” He looked at Milli. Then past him, at the long loft on its stilts at the back of the harbour, where thirty-five people were still asleep under the nets. “I was going to tell you when she was ready.”

“She’s ready,” said Marrit. “She swims in six days.”

“Then I suppose I’m telling you now,” said Corran, comfortably, and drank his tea.

Hana found him at the water butt behind the loft, trying to get the grease off.

She didn’t say anything. She just came round the corner in the half-light with her crown on and her tail up, and saw him, and stopped. And looked at his arms. And his knees. And the back of his shirt, which was black. And sighed. The long one. The one that meant you went under a ship in the dark on your own and didn’t tell anybody.

“I measured it,” said Milli.

“I know you measured it.”

“It was going to break her back.”

“I know.” Hana came over and took the rag out of his hand and started on his shoulders, where he couldn’t reach, without asking. Her ears were flat. Not angry. The other thing. “You could have woken me up.”

“You’d have come.”

“Obviously I’d have come.”

“You’d have come under the ship,” said Milli. “In the dark. With your crown on. And then we’d both be greased.” He looked at her over his shoulder. “And somebody has to be clean. To talk to Deston.”

She looked at him. Then she laughed, short, through her nose, and hit him on the back of the head with the rag, gently, and went back to scrubbing.

“Eleven centimetres,” she said.

“Over twenty metres.”

“How do you lift eleven centimetres of slip with a ship on it?”

Milli thought about it. He’d been thinking about it since the third measurement. He’d thought about it lying on his stomach in the dark with the water running to port.

“You don’t,” he said. “You don’t lift the slip. You lift the ship. Just the back end of her. Just enough. And you shim the way underneath while she’s up.” He paused. “With a lot of people.”

“How many people?”

“All of them,” said Milli. “And Salty.”

It took four days.

Not because it was complicated. Because it was heavy. Because the Patience weighed more than anything Milli had ever been near that wasn’t a building, and the tide came in twice a day and filled the bottom of the slip with water and you couldn’t work under her then, and the tide went out twice a day and left you three hours of mud each time, and three hours was not long enough to lift a ship.

So they did it in pieces. Three hours at a time.

Gabe and Salty and Azac and Hako on the long levers, under her stern, with timbers from the boatyard as fulcrums, because they had the arms. Jared on the shims, cutting oak wedges to Milli’s numbers. Deston in the middle, with his gold out, linking everybody’s mana, so that when the four on the levers heaved, they heaved with all thirty-five of them behind it, a little. Rachael watching all of them for backs. Newish counting.

Not a beat. Never a beat, now. Not since floor 8. Newish stood on an upturned barrel on the slip in the mud with her arms folded and watched the four of them set their shoulders, and waited, at no regular time, until it felt right, and then said one name.

“Salty.”

And Salty heaved, and the other three heaved with him, because they’d all been waiting for it, and the whole huge dark stern of the Patience came up off the slip a finger’s width. Creaking. Groaning. And Milli was under it on his back in the mud with a wedge and a mallet and his whole tail fluffed out to twice its size, and put the wedge in, and hit it, twice, precisely, and rolled out.

“Down,” said Newish.

They let her down. Onto the wedge. A finger’s width higher than she’d been.

“Again,” said Milli, from the mud.

It went on like that. For four days. A finger’s width at a time.

The tide-folk came and watched. Not all the time. They had their own work. The mud-diggers had the mud, and the fishing boats went out on the tide whether anybody was lifting a ship or not, and Ebba’s new baby needed walking up and down the rope walks at three in the morning, and nobody in Hythe stopped for anything. But they came, in the gaps. Stood along the harbour wall in a row with their arms folded and their tide-cords at their wrists and watched thirty-six stair-folk lift Corran’s ship off her slip a finger at a time, and didn’t say anything, and went back to work.

On the third day, one of them brought a barrel of beer down to the slip and left it there without a word.

On the fourth day, three of them came down off the wall and took a lever.

Jared was at the forge in the evenings.

There was a forge on the slip, at the back, under a lean-to: Corran’s own, gone cold for a year. Jared had lit it on the second night and hadn’t let it go out since. Milli went to find him there on the fourth evening, when the port way was level at last, and Milli had measured it four times, and the water ran down both sides of the slip at the same speed, and Marrit had walked the whole length of it twice with her hands in her pockets and said huh, which from Marrit was a speech.

Jared was making a rudder.

Not a rudder. The irons for one. Long flat bands of iron, as long as Milli’s arm, with holes punched along them and a curl at one end like a hook. Pintles and gudgeons. Milli had learned the words this week. The fittings that held a rudder onto the back of a ship, and let it swing. There were three sets already finished, on the floor of the lean-to, black and cooling.

And two more on the anvil.

“She’s got rudder irons,” said Milli. “Corran made them.”

“Corran made good ones,” said Jared, without looking up. He was turning a band in the fire with his tongs. He had the thorn-shield propped against the wall behind him, with the rose inside the rim, the way it always was. “These are better.”

“Why?”

Jared took the band out of the fire, put it on the anvil, and hit it. Three times. Clean. Then put it back in.

“Ice said,” said Jared. “On the ledge. It took the rudder off the Patience on the thirtieth day. Marrit steered with a sweep oar for a week.” He turned the band. “So I’m making it a rudder it can’t take.”

Milli looked at the irons on the floor.

“It’ll take something else,” he said.

“Yeah,” said Jared. Calm. “Probably. That’s what it does.” He took the band out and hit it again. “Then I’ll make that too.”

Milli sat down on an upturned bucket by the door of the lean-to and watched him work. He didn’t say anything for a while. He liked watching Jared work. Jared worked the way Milli measured things: without hurry, without waste, as if he’d already done it a hundred times in his head and was just showing the iron.

“Do you believe it?” said Milli eventually.

“Believe what?”

“What he said. On the ledge. The thirtieth day. Marrit with the oar.” Milli looked at his greased hands. “He said it like he’d seen it.”

Jared stopped.

He held the band in the tongs over the fire for a moment, not putting it in, not taking it out. The light of the coals came up red on his face, on the strand of crimson hair over his eye.

“I believe Ice,” he said.

“That’s not what I asked.”

“I know it’s not what you asked, man.” Jared put the band in the fire. “It’s what I’m answering.” He turned it slowly. “He told us he doesn’t know where anything is any more. Higu said, then tell us what you remember, and we’ll find out what’s true. So he told us what he remembers. And I’m making rudder irons.” He looked at Milli. “That’s all it is. That’s all it needs to be. Higu’s got a rock with two columns on it. I’ve got a forge.”

Milli didn’t say anything.

“Alice doesn’t ask,” said Jared, after a while. Quieter. “You noticed? She doesn’t ask him. Not once. Not on the ledge. Not at the tarn.” He hit the band. “She smiled at the tarn. When she saw him and Thea. She smiled like she already knew.”

“She does that.”

“She does that,” Jared agreed. “She does that with everything.” He almost smiled. “Doesn’t mean she did.”

Corran was slower every day.

Milli noticed. He noticed things. It was the cat in him, Hana said, or the engineer, or both, and he’d stopped arguing with her about which. He noticed that the old man got to his bucket under the bow later every morning. That on the third day he came down the harbour steps one at a time, with a hand on the wall, stopping twice. That on the fourth day Maigo was waiting at the top of the steps for him, without anybody asking him to, and walked down beside him, very slowly, saying nothing, with his hand under the old man’s elbow, and that Corran let him.

He noticed that Rachael looked at the old man every morning, the way she looked at everybody, and then looked away a little faster than she looked away from anybody else.

He noticed that Ice did too.

That was the thing. Ice came back from the Shoals every evening at the end of the tide with salt in his hair and a new cut somewhere and Thea and Xola walking a long way behind him, the way they had to, and the first thing he did, every single evening, before he took his shield off, before he looked at the rock, was look up the slip to the bucket under the bow.

To check.

And every evening, when the old man was there, something in Ice’s shoulders came down a centimetre.

Milli had measured enough things to know what a centimetre was.

He didn’t say anything. Nobody did. He watched Ice come up the slip on the fourth evening and look at the bucket, and the old man lift his mug, and Ice lift his hand back, and then go to the forge, and stand in the door of the lean-to watching Jared hammer his rudder irons with a face Milli didn’t have a measurement for. And Jared look up, and see him, and say it’s what you said, man, and Ice say I know, and not say anything else, and go away.

“He’s going to see her swim,” said Hana, that night, in the loft.

They were lying under the big nets, in the corner by the wall that was theirs, the way corners always became theirs, on every floor. Her head on his arm. Her tail over both of them like a blanket. The loft was dark and full of breathing and the sound of the tide coming in under the stilts, and across the room, by the door, he could see the shape of Ice lying between Gabe and the wall, and Thea’s shape next to him, and the lantern turned right down between them.

“Who?”

“Corran.” Hana’s voice was very quiet. “Two days. The big tide. He’s going to see her swim. Gabe said.”

“Gabe says a lot of things.”

“I know.” She was quiet. “Ice said he died in the spring. Before they came.” She didn’t say they. She said it the way you’d step round a hole in a floor. “Before the Patience was in the water. Marrit captained her. The first— Marrit captained her. So he never saw her swim.”

Milli didn’t say anything.

“So if he sees her swim,” said Hana, “that’s new. That’s something that didn’t happen.” She turned her head on his arm. “That’s true. That goes on the right side of the rock.”

“Two days,” said Milli.

“Two days.”

He thought about the old man coming down the harbour steps one at a time, with his hand on the wall. About the eleven centimetres. About four days of lifting a ship a finger’s width at a time, so that she wouldn’t stop halfway down the slip with her back breaking under her own weight.

“Then she’d better not stick,” he said.

The big tide came on the sixth day at noon.

The whole of Hythe came to the slip.

Milli hadn’t known there were that many people in Hythe. They came down off the rope walks and the stilted porches and up out of the boats, and stood along the harbour wall and on the quay and on the steps and on the roofs, hundreds of them, brown and weathered and barefoot with their tide-cords at their wrists. The mud-diggers didn’t dig. The fishing boats didn’t go out. For the first time since the thirty-six of them had come down off the hill, nobody in Hythe was doing anything except watching.

The big tide was the highest of the month. Marrit had explained it. The moon and the sun pulling the same way. The sea came further up the slip than it had come all year, green and full and heavy, right up under the Patience’s stern, so that she sat with her back end lifted on the water and her bow still on the timber. Waiting. Groaning very softly, the way wood groans when it’s being asked to hold something it isn’t used to.

And the ropes.

Eight of them, running from the bow and the sides of the hull down the slip and out along the quay, so that the people on them could pull her if she slowed. Thick as Milli’s wrist. Thirty-six climbers on them, and Marrit, and the three tide-folk who’d taken a lever, and then, without being asked, twenty more from the wall, who came down and took hold behind the climbers and stood there with their feet planted and said nothing.

Corran was on his bucket.

Not under the bow. They’d moved it. Maigo had carried it, and then the old man, very slowly, to the top of the quay, where he could see the whole length of the slip and the harbour beyond it. He sat there with a blanket round his shoulders and his mug in both hands and his milky eyes fixed on the ship, and he didn’t move.

“Two dogs left,” said Marrit.

She was standing by the hull with a long-handled maul. Her face was very white under the brown. The dogs were the last two props, she’d explained: dog-shores, short heavy timbers wedged at an angle between the slip and the ship, one on each side, holding her back. Everything else was gone. All the cradles and blocks and wedges that had held her on the slip for eleven years. There was nothing holding the Patience on the land now but the two dogs.

Knock them out, and she went.

“Both at once,” said Marrit. “Same moment. Exactly the same. Or she slews.”

“Slews?”

“Twists. Goes down the slip sideways. Off the ways.” Marrit’s jaw was tight. “And then she’s on her side in the mud, and that’s eleven years.” She looked at the port dog. It was jammed in at the bottom of the slip, under the curve of the hull, right where the way had been low. Where the shims were. Where the timber had been lifted and packed and lifted again, a finger at a time, for four days. “That one’s tight. The shims pushed it up. It’s tight as anything. It’ll take two blows. And whoever’s on it’s right under her when she goes.”

Milli looked at the port dog.

He’d known. He’d known since the third day, when the shims went in and he saw the angle of it change. He’d done the sum lying in the dark under the nets. The starboard dog would come out with one blow. The port dog wouldn’t. Not with a maul. Not with one person swinging. It would take two, or three, and by the third the starboard side would be gone and she’d be moving, and whoever was under her on the port side would be under her when she moved.

Unless they were very fast.

“I’ll take the port dog,” said Milli.

Hana didn’t say don’t.

He’d thought she might. He’d thought about it on the walk down the quay with the maul over his shoulder: that she’d stand in front of him and say it, the way she’d say it, very calm and very smooth, and he’d have to argue. And he’d thought about what he’d say. That he was the fastest. That he’d measured it. That he was the only one who knew exactly where the shims were, and exactly how the dog sat on them, and exactly where to hit.

She didn’t say it.

She came down the slip after him, through the crowd, with her crown on, and stopped in front of him at the bottom by the hull, and looked at him. At the maul. At the port dog, jammed in under the vast dark curve of the ship. At the water, green and full, lapping round his boots.

“You’re the fastest,” said Hana.

“I’m the fastest.”

“I know you’re the fastest.” Her ears were flat. Her voice was perfectly steady. “I’m marrying the fastest. I said yes.” She reached up and straightened his collar, which didn’t need straightening. “One blow?”

“No. It’s tight. Two.”

“And then?”

“And then I’m not there.”

Hana looked at him for a long moment.

“Okay,” she said. And went back up the slip to the rope, and took hold of it, and didn’t look back.

He watched her go.

Then he turned round and looked at the port dog, and the shims under it, and the hull above it, and the water, and the way the light came through the gap between the ship and the slip, and measured it all one more time. Where to stand. Where to hit. Where to go. Which way. How far. How fast.

Not the mud. The mud would hold his feet. The timber. Up the way. Toward the bow. Against the direction she’d move. So that she’d go past him, not over him.

He set his feet.

On the starboard side, Marrit lifted her maul.

Newish was on the barrel.

She’d dragged it to the top of the slip, by the quay, where everybody could see her. Every rope. Every hand. Marrit on the starboard dog, and him on the port, at the bottom, under the hull. She stood on the barrel with her arms folded and looked at all of it, and waited.

No beat. Never a beat.

He watched her. She wasn’t looking at him. She was looking at the water, and the ship, and the tide-folk on the ropes, and Corran on his bucket at the top of the quay. Waiting for whatever it was she waited for. The one. The moment when it felt right.

The whole of Hythe went quiet.

He heard the water lapping at the hull. He heard the ship groan. He heard a gull, far off. He heard his own heart. He felt the maul in his hands, the weight of it, the balance, the place on the dog where it would land.

“Milli,” said Newish.

He swung.

The maul hit the port dog at the bottom of its angle, exactly where he’d meant, and the dog shifted. A finger. Jarred loose from the shims. Not out.

On the starboard side he heard Marrit’s maul land, and the starboard dog go out with a crack like a branch breaking, and fall, and the whole ship move.

Not much. Not yet. A shiver. A long slow creaking shift above him, as all that weight that had been held on two dogs was suddenly held on one. His. The port dog groaned under it. The angle changed. It was going to go on its own. Now. In a second. With the ship coming down onto it.

He swung again.

He didn’t feel it land. He felt the dog go out from under the maul like a pip squeezed out of a fruit, and the maul go on through the space where it had been, and his arms go with it.

And then he was moving.

Up. Not the mud. The timber. Up the greased way toward the bow, against the direction the ship was going, with his boots sliding on the grease and his tail out straight behind him for balance and the whole vast dark curve of the hull coming past him on his right, moving, going the other way, down, so close that he felt the wind of it on his cheek. The tarred planks. The new paint. A rivet of Jared’s, going past his eye like a nail in a fence from a cart.

He didn’t stop until he was at the top of the slip, by the bow, which wasn’t by him any more. It was going. Going away from him down the slip. Faster.

He turned round.

The Patience was sliding.

Straight. Both ways. Level. Not leaning. Not slewing. Down the greased timbers into the sea with a long, deep, rising roar of wood on wood, and the eight ropes running out along the quay, and the people on them walking with it, letting it go, and the water coming up her sides. Her stern went into the sea and the sea came up white round it. Further. Faster. She was halfway. Two-thirds. The part of the slip where the port way had been low, eleven centimetres over twenty metres.

She slowed.

He felt it. Everybody felt it. The roar of wood on wood went down a note. The ship shuddered. Her stern was afloat and her bow was still on the land, and she was slowing, the way he’d known she’d slow, because the shims were good and the way was level but it was the end of the slip and the grease was thin there and she was heavy—

“ONE,” said Newish.

To everybody.

Thirty-six climbers and Marrit and twenty-three tide-folk pulled on eight ropes at once, at the same moment, like one hand.

And the Patience went off the end of the slip into the sea.

The wave came up the slip and soaked him to the waist.

He didn’t notice. He was watching her. Everybody was. She’d gone off the end of the timber in a great white burst of water, and gone down, her bow dipping deep, deep, so deep that someone on the wall screamed, and the harbour heaved up round her in a long green swell that ran out across the water and slapped against every stilt in Hythe at once.

And then she came up.

Slowly. Like something waking. Like the boats on the first evening, sitting up off the mud one by one when the tide came in. Her bow rose out of the water streaming, and the swell ran out from under her, and she rocked, once, twice, side to side, her three bare masts swinging against the sky.

And then she sat.

On the water. Level. Upright. Turning very slowly on the end of her eight ropes in the middle of the harbour, with the sea running off her sides and the sun on her new paint and her name across her stern in white letters. PATIENCE.

She swam.

The bell on the harbour wall rang.

Not once, the way it rang for the tide. Over and over. Somebody on the tower was ringing it with both hands, as fast as it would go, and the sound went out across the harbour and bounced off the cliff and came back, and the whole of Hythe was shouting. On the wall. On the quay. On the roofs. In the boats. Hundreds of people who hadn’t stopped for anything, all at once, shouting and stamping and banging on the rails.

Milli stood at the top of the slip, soaked, with the maul still in his hands, and looked for one person.

Not Hana. He knew where Hana was. He could feel her, the way he always could, on the third rope, with her crown crooked and her tail fluffed out to three times its size, shouting his name.

He looked for the bucket.

Corran was standing up.

Nobody had helped him. Maigo was right beside him, with his hand out, and hadn’t touched him. The old man had stood up off his bucket at the top of the quay by himself, with the blanket fallen off his shoulders onto the stone, and the mug fallen too, rolling, and was standing there with both his old hands at his sides, looking at the ship on the water.

He didn’t say anything.

His face didn’t do anything. It just looked. The way it had looked at the ship on the slip on the first day, when thirty-six stair-folk came round the end of the quay. The way you’d look at somebody asleep.

Except she wasn’t asleep any more.

Marrit got to him first. She came up the quay off the rope at a run, through the shouting, and stopped in front of him, and didn’t say anything either. She just stood there. And then the old man put out one hand, without looking away from the ship, and found her shoulder, and held it.

“She swims,” said Corran.

He said it very quietly. Milli was twenty metres away and he heard it anyway, under the bell, under all of Hythe shouting. The cat in him. Or something else.

“She swims,” said the old man again. “Eleven years.” His hand tightened on Marrit’s shoulder. “Look at her, Marrit. Look at her go.”

Marrit was crying. Properly. With her face screwed up and her mouth open, like a child, standing on the quay with pitch on her elbows and her grandfather’s hand on her shoulder. She didn’t try to stop.

“I’m looking,” she said. “I’m looking, Grandad. I’m looking.”

Milli looked away.

He looked down the slip, without meaning to, to the place at the bottom where the port dog had been. Where he’d stood. Where the shims were. Eleven centimetres. He’d lain there in the dark on the first morning with a bucket of water, and watched it run to port.

And then he looked across the harbour, and found Ice.

Ice was on the second rope, near the bottom, still holding it. Soaked to the chest from the wave. With Gabe on one side of him, and Thea on the other. He wasn’t shouting. He was the only one on the whole quay who wasn’t.

He was looking at the old man standing up on the quay with his hand on Marrit’s shoulder. And his face was doing something that Milli didn’t have a measurement for at all.

He never saw her swim, Hana had said. In the dark. So if he sees her swim, that’s new. That’s something that didn’t happen.

Thea had let go of her rope. She had her hand on Ice’s arm, just above the wrist. She was looking at him, not at the ship.

Milli looked away from that too. It wasn’t his.

Corran came down the slip to him afterwards.

It took a long time. Maigo walked beside him the whole way, with his hand under his elbow, and Marrit on the other side, and the old man stopped three times. But he came. All the way down the slip, on the wet timber, to where Milli was sitting on the end of the port way with his boots in the water and the maul across his knees, still getting his breath back.

The old man looked down at him with his milky eyes.

“You were quick,” said Corran.

“I measured,” said Milli.

The old man laughed. The thin dry laugh, like a rope through a block. It turned into a cough halfway through, and he had to stop, and Maigo held him, and Marrit’s face went tight. But he finished laughing. He made sure he finished.

“Eleven centimetres,” said Corran. “Marrit told me. Over twenty metres.” He looked at the port way, at the shims, at the place where the dog had been. “Three years I’ve known. Three years I’ve sat on that bucket knowing she’d stick.” He looked back at Milli. “And a stair-boy with a bucket of water and a bit of cord lies down in the mud in the dark and measures it. And then lifts her. A finger at a time.” He shook his head slowly. “Never had anybody lift anything for me. Not in eighty-nine years. Always did it myself.”

“You couldn’t lift this,” said Milli.

“No,” said the old man. “No. I couldn’t.” He was quiet. “I’m glad I lived to have somebody do it.”

He put his hand on Milli’s head. Between the ears. Briefly. The way you’d put your hand on a cat. Milli let him.

Then Corran turned round, and looked out across the harbour at his ship, sitting on the water at the end of her ropes with the sun on her.

“Three days to rig her,” he said. “And then she goes west.”

“Grandad—“ said Marrit.

“And I’m going with her,” said Corran. “On her first voyage. I built her. I’m going to sail her.”

“You can’t walk to the end of the quay—“

“Then I’ll not walk,” said the old man. “I’ll sit. Your stair-folk can carry me. They’ve got the arms.” He looked at Marrit. “You’ll be captain. You’ll have the wheel. I’ll sit on the deck on my bucket and tell you what you’re doing wrong.” He smiled. “Like always.”

Marrit stared at him.

Then she looked at Maigo, still holding the old man’s elbow. And at Milli on the end of the slip. And past them, up the quay, at thirty-six stair-folk standing about in the sun with ropes in their hands, soaked and laughing and watching. And at Rachael, among them, who’d heard. Who was looking at the old man. Who looked back at Marrit, and didn’t shake her head, and didn’t nod. Just looked.

He’s not ill. He’s just old.

“Fine,” said Marrit. Her voice cracked. “Fine. You stubborn old goat. Fine.”

“That’s my girl,” said Corran.

They rigged her in three days.

Milli had never rigged a ship. Nobody had, except Ice, and Ice was careful not to know too much, the way he was careful about everything now, saying I think it goes there when it was obvious from his face that he knew it went there. Marrit didn’t notice. Marrit didn’t notice anything except the masts, and the yards, and the miles of rope going up into the sky and coming down again through blocks with a squeal like a pig, and the sails, which came out of the sail loft in great brown rolls on the shoulders of tide-folk and went up into the rigging and were bent on, one by one, until the Patience stood in the middle of the harbour with all three masts dressed, and looked like what she was.

A ship.

They loaded her on the third day. Water and salt fish and biscuit and Ice’s sacks of things nobody else understood, which he’d been buying in Hythe all week with knives Salty made in the evenings. Jared’s rudder irons went on. Jared fitted them himself, hanging off the stern on a rope with a mallet in the water to his waist, and when he climbed back up onto the deck he was very wet and very satisfied and wouldn’t say anything about it. The thorn-shield went into the hold with the rose inside its rim.

They sailed on the fourth morning, on the tide.

The Patience didn’t wait. That was the thing about Hythe, and Milli had stopped being surprised by it. The tide turned at dawn and Marrit stood at the wheel with her tide-cord round her fingers and said now, and the tide-folk crew, seven of them, all hers, let go the ropes and hauled on others, and the sails filled, and the ship began to move. Whether anybody was aboard or not.

Calvin was not aboard.

He came down the quay at a dead run with his arms full of something wrapped in a cloth, shouting, as the Patience slid away from the end of the wall. The gap was a metre. Then two. Then more. Calvin didn’t stop. He reached the end of the quay at full speed and went off it with a scream and all four limbs going and the cloth bundle clutched to his chest, through the air over three metres of open green water.

Azac caught him by the back of the collar.

He’d been waiting at the rail. He didn’t look surprised. He just reached out over the side as Calvin came at him, took hold of his collar the way he’d taken hold of it on the cliff path, and hauled him in over the rail onto the deck in a heap.

“Bread,” said Calvin, from the heap. Breathless. Holding up the bundle. “Bread. Ebba’s mum made it. For the voyage. She said I could have the first loaf if I came back for it.”

“Calvin,” said Rachael.

“It’s still warm—“

Corran laughed so hard on his bucket by the mainmast that Maigo had to hold him upright for a full minute.

Marrit handed out the wax on the first night.

They were out of sight of land by then. Out of sight of the Stair, and Hythe, and everything. Just the islands, scattered to the west, black against the sunset, and the sea going on and on under a sky that went from gold to red to purple to a deep clear blue with stars coming out in it one at a time. The Patience ran before the wind with her sails full and the water rushing past her sides, and the deck moved under Milli’s feet in a long slow lift and fall that his stomach had got used to on the first afternoon and his tail hadn’t got used to at all.

Marrit came along the deck at dusk with a wooden box.

“One each,” she said. “Two, if you’ve got two ears. Which you have. Most of you.” She looked at Hana’s cat ears, and Aleaka’s fox ones, and Milli’s own, doubtfully, and gave them each four. “Over deep water, at night, you put these in. Everybody. You keep them in till dawn.”

Milli looked at what she’d given him. Small soft yellow balls of wax. Beeswax, warm from her pocket, smelling of honey.

“What for?” said Newish.

“So you don’t listen,” said Marrit.

“Listen to what?”

Marrit looked at her. Then out at the sea. At the dark coming up out of the east, and the long low swell, and the water under them, which was not green any more, the way it had been in the harbour and over the Shoals. It was black. It went down a very long way. You could feel it. Under the hull. Under everything.

“The Undertow,” said Marrit. “It sings, over deep water. At night. It sings and it calls you, and if you listen, you go over the side.” She said it perfectly flatly. Like a fact. Like the tide. “Ships have lost whole crews. Everybody listening. Everybody going over, one after another, in the dark, and the ship found in the morning with nobody on her.” She shut the box. “So you don’t listen. You put the wax in. Grandad’s done it for seventy years. I’ve done it since I was four. You do it too.”

Nobody argued.

Milli watched them take it. Deston, frowning at the wax, and then putting it in. Rachael, putting hers in and checking Calvin’s. Gabe. Salty. Xav. One after another, along the rail, in the dusk. Thea, looking at hers for a moment and then at Ice.

And Ice.

Ice had taken his from the box without looking at it. He’d rolled the four little yellow balls in his palm, once, the way you’d roll something you’d held before, and put them in his ears, two and two, without being told how, and gone back to coiling a rope.

As if he’d done it a hundred times.

Milli watched him do it. He didn’t say anything. He put his own in. It made the world go thick and soft and very far away, the sea, the wind, the creak of the rigging. His ears flattened against the wax on their own. He hated it.

He looked along the rail for Aleaka.

She was at the very back of the ship, at the stern rail, beside the wheel, where Marrit had gone back to steer. Standing very still in the last of the light with her hands on the rail and Tuppence on her shoulder and Sleet sitting pressed against her legs, hip-high, frost-grey. Looking down at the black water.

She had her wax in her palm.

She hadn’t put it in.

He had the middle watch, with Hana. Midnight to four.

The ship was very quiet. Everybody else was asleep, below, in hammocks in the hold, or on the deck under blankets, with the wax in. The tide-folk crew on watch stood at their posts without talking, wax in, eyes on the dark. Marrit had gone to sleep at last. Corran was asleep in a nest of sailcloth by the mainmast with Maigo sitting up beside him, awake, the way Maigo had sat up every night since the slip. The stars were very big. The sea rushed past.

He couldn’t hear any of it. Just the thick soft roar of his own blood, through the wax.

Hana was sitting on a coil of rope by the foremast with her knees up and her crown off, in her lap. He could see her face in the starlight. Calm. Smooth. She caught him looking, and smiled, and tapped her ear, and made a face. I hate it. He nodded. Me too.

He looked aft.

Aleaka was still at the stern rail.

She’d been there all night. He’d seen her when he came up for the watch, and she’d been there then, standing beside the night helmsman at the wheel with her hands on the rail, looking down at the wake. Sleet lying at her feet. Tuppence on her shoulder with her head tucked under her wing. She hadn’t moved.

He watched her.

And then he saw Sleet’s ruff go white.

Not all at once. The way frost comes on a window. From the roots. The thick pale fur round the young wolf’s neck stiffening, whitening, standing up, until it was a collar of white rime glittering in the starlight. Sleet had lifted her head. She wasn’t growling. She was very still, with her ears up, looking at the black water off the stern.

And Tuppence was awake. Out from under her wing. Standing on Aleaka’s shoulder, bolt upright, the white feather in her left wing catching the light. Not bobbing. Falls-wrens always bobbed. Tuppence bobbed in her sleep.

Tuppence wasn’t bobbing.

Milli stood up.

He thought about it for a second. Rationally. He thought about Marrit’s flat voice. If you listen, you go over the side. Everybody going over, one after another, in the dark. He thought about Hana on the coil of rope behind him. About the wax.

Then he reached up and took the wax out of his left ear.

Just the left.

The world came back. The sea, rushing. The wind in the rigging, singing very high and thin through a hundred ropes. The creak of the hull. A tide-folk sailor coughing, up the deck.

And under it.

Under all of it. So low that it wasn’t a sound so much as something in the planks under his boots, coming up through the soles of his feet, up his legs, into his chest. A long, long note. Lower than anything. Lower than the Breath under the Bastion, lower than the bell on the fifth wall. Rising. Very slowly. Over what felt like a whole minute. And then falling again. And then, after a long silence, rising again, in a different shape.

Coming up through the hull. Out of the black water. From a long way down.

It didn’t pull.

That was what he noticed. Rationally, with his heart going. He’d expected it to pull. It sings and it calls you, and if you listen, you go over the side. He stood on the deck with one ear open and listened to it, and waited to feel something reach up out of the dark and take hold of him and draw him toward the rail.

Nothing did.

It just went on. Rising and falling. Down there. In the dark. Over and over. Like somebody saying the same thing, very slowly, in a language with no words in it, again and again, to anybody who’d listen.

Behind him, Hana was on her feet. He felt her hand close on his arm, hard. He turned round. She’d seen him take the wax out. Her face had gone white. She was saying something he couldn’t hear through her own wax and his other ear, and pulling, and her eyes were enormous.

He shook his head. It’s all right.

He pointed aft.

Aleaka had turned round. At the stern rail. She was looking at him, down the length of the dark ship. She’d seen him take his wax out. She had her own wax still in her palm, the four small yellow balls, unused.

And her face was wet.

Not frightened. He’d seen Aleaka frightened, on the shelf above the Cataract with nobody able to see it. This wasn’t that. She was standing at the rail in the starlight with Sleet’s frosted ruff against her legs and Tuppence bolt upright on her shoulder, crying, quite quietly, with her mouth a little open, as if somebody had just told her something very sad and very important and she was still listening to the end of it.

She lifted one hand off the rail. Not much. Just a little.

And then she said something. To him. Across the deck. He read it on her lips, slowly, because she always spoke slowly, picking her way over the words like stones in a stream. He read it with his left ear open and the long low note coming up through the soles of his feet out of the black water.

It’s not pulling.

It’s asking.
