Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 2: One Floor Ahead

Chapter 83

The Day After

4,022 words · 17 min read

Nobody slept the first night in Evermorrow. Not one of them. Not even the town.

That wasn’t quite true. The town slept. Every window round the square was dark. Every shutter was closed. Every person in Evermorrow was in bed, asleep, the way they’d gone to bed every night for a hundred and fifty-three nights, expecting to wake up in the same morning. They didn’t know yet.

But the thirty-six didn’t sleep. And neither did Merle.

They came down out of the clocktower at a quarter past midnight in a long noisy line, the way they’d gone up. Out through the door at the bottom, past the caretaker’s empty stool, into the square. And stopped.

It was dark.

Real dark. The middle of the night. The four faces of the clocktower lit from inside like moons, and above them the sky, black, thick with stars, too many. The fountain splashing quietly. A cat crossing the cobbles on the far side, very fast, and vanishing down an alley. A smell of cold stone and woodsmoke gone out and something flowering in a window box. And silence. The deep thick silence of a town at night with nobody awake in it.

Merle walked out into the middle of the square and turned round in a slow circle, looking up.

“I’ve never seen it,” she said.

Her voice was very small in the dark.

“The square,” she said. “At night. Like this. After midnight.” She turned round again. “A hundred and fifty-three days. I’ve never once seen it after the twelfth stroke. I’d blink and it’d be morning.” She put her floury hands over her mouth. “It’s dark.”

Nobody said anything.

She walked across the square. To the fountain. She put her hand on the stone rim of it, and then in the water, and took it out and looked at it, wet, in the starlight. Then to the church steps. She sat down on them, and stood up again. Then to the butcher’s stall, shuttered and dark, and put her hand flat on the wood of the shutter. Then to the corner where the smith had his stall in the day, and the well, and the mouth of Lantern Street, black and narrow.

She was touching everything. Every single thing in the square. As if she needed to know it was all still there, after midnight, in the dark.

He watched her do it. Sitting on the edge of the fountain, with the lamp shield across his knees and his ribs still aching where the Hour had hit him, even after Rachael and Deston and the ember. Gabe was beside him. Gabe hadn’t moved from beside him since the clock room.

“She’s been waiting a long time,” said Gabe quietly.

“Yeah.”

“Longer than you.”

“Yeah,” said Ice. “A bit longer than me.”

The clock struck one.

The great bell, up in the belfry. One single deep note, rolling out over the dark town.

Merle stopped dead in the middle of the square.

She’d heard it ring a thousand times. Every hour of every day for a hundred and fifty-three days, from six in the morning to twelve at night, the same strokes. She knew every one. She’d told him so. She could tell you the time to the minute without a clock, just by which way the dog was running.

She’d never heard it ring one.

She stood in the middle of the square in the dark, with her floury hands at her sides and her face turned up to the tower, and laughed. Out loud. So loud it echoed off every shuttered window round the square, and somewhere a dog started barking, and somewhere a window opened and a voice shouted some of us are trying to sleep. And Merle laughed harder.

At two o’clock she laughed again. At three, she cried. At four, she was asleep, on the church steps, sitting up, with her head on Rachael’s shoulder. Rachael didn’t move for an hour.

He opened his window at about half past three, sitting on the edge of the fountain, because Alea told him to.

“There’s a window,” she’d said. “From the clock room. You didn’t read it. You were laughing too much.”

He opened it.

He looked at it. Then he looked at his hand.

There was something in it. He hadn’t noticed it arriving. A bell. Small, bronze, the size of a walnut, with a loop at the top for a cord and a tiny clapper inside. When he turned it over, it rang. Very faintly. A thin high note, like the smallest bell in the belfry.

All round the square, in the dark, he heard it. Thirty-five other tiny bells, being turned over in thirty-five other hands, ringing. People going oh. Calvin ringing his over and over until Rachael took it off him.

“One each,” said Alea softly. “Thirty-six.”

He rang his once more, very quietly, and put it in his vest pocket. Next to a candle from a tower on floor 2, and a blank card from a white room.

Then his level.

A little padlock. Beside the number. The same small grey lock he’d seen at ten and twenty-five, being patient.

“Forty-five,” said Alea.

“Forty-five.”

“Tier 4.” The fox looked at the padlock, then at him. “You know what I’m going to ask.”

“Hard,” said Ice.

“I knew you’d say that.”

“Then why did you ask?”

“So I could say I knew you’d say that.” She leaned against his arm. “Not tonight, though.”

“No,” he said. “Not tonight.”

He closed the window. The padlock could wait. It was good at waiting. So, it turned out, was he.

The sun came up a little after six.

It came up wrong. That was the first thing. It came up the way a sun comes up on a real morning, not on a morning that’s happened a hundred and fifty-three times. There were clouds. Low ones, grey and soft, coming in over the eastern hills. The light came through them in long pale bars instead of the clean gold it had been every single morning for months. And the birds started. But different birds, or the same birds in a different order, so that the song wasn’t the same song. And then, at about half past six, from the grey clouds, it rained.

In the morning.

Not at three. Not the short sharp shower out of a clear sky that had come across the valley from the west at three o’clock every afternoon for a hundred and fifty-three days. A soft grey drizzle, in the early morning. Coming down on the square and the fountain and thirty-six people sitting round it with bells in their pockets. And Merle, asleep on the church steps.

She woke up because her face was wet.

She sat up. She looked at the sky. At the grey clouds and the soft rain coming down at half past six in the morning.

“It’s raining,” she said.

Nobody said anything.

“It’s raining now,” said Merle. “It rains at three. It always rains at three. It’s half past six and it’s—“ Her voice cracked. “I don’t know when it’s going to stop.”

She sat on the church steps in the drizzle and put her face in her hands. And Ice understood that she was crying, and that it was the happiest he’d ever seen anybody be.

The town woke up.

It woke up slowly. Shutters opening, one by one, round the square. People coming out of their doors, into the drizzle, blinking at it. Looking up at the clouds with the faces of people who’d gone to bed expecting one thing and woken up to another and couldn’t quite say why it felt so strange.

And they remembered.

Not much. Not a hundred and fifty-three days. Just one. Just yesterday. Ice watched it happen, from the fountain. He watched the butcher come out and put his stall up. He looked round for the dog. The dog was there, brown and white, one ear up, sitting by the table, wet, with its head on one side. And the butcher looked down at it, and frowned, and said:

“You. Weren’t you the one that took my sausage? Yesterday?”

The dog wagged its tail.

The butcher looked at it for a long time. Then, slowly, as if he didn’t know why he was doing it, he took a sausage off the rail and threw it down to the dog. And went back to setting out his meat, shaking his head.

It wasn’t market day. That was the other thing. It had been market day every day for a hundred and fifty-three days. And this was the day after market day, which was a quiet day, and the stalls didn’t come out. Just the butcher and the baker and the bread. The square stayed half empty. The striped awnings stayed rolled up.

Dennis walked across the square to a door with a little cheese painted on the sign above it, and knocked.

It took a long time. Then the door opened, and the small sharp woman in the white cap looked out at him, in her dressing gown, with her hair down.

She looked at him.

“You,” she said.

Dennis stood very still.

“Six and a half,” said the cheesemonger. “Wasn’t it? Yesterday? You got me down to six and a half and a pot of chutney.” She narrowed her eyes. “I’ve been thinking about that all night. I don’t know how you did it. I never go under seven.”

Dennis didn’t say anything. Ice, at the fountain, saw his face, and had to look away.

“Well?” said the cheesemonger. “What do you want? We’re shut. It’s the day after market.”

“A wheel,” said Dennis. His voice had gone very high. “The hard yellow one. On credit. I’ll pay you tomorrow.”

The cheesemonger looked at him.

“Tomorrow,” she said.

“Tomorrow,” said Dennis.

She went back in, muttering. She came back out with a wheel of hard yellow cheese and put it in his arms. “Tomorrow,” she said, and shut the door.

Dennis stood in the drizzle outside the cheesemonger’s door, holding a wheel of cheese on credit. He started to cry. Loudly. The way Dennis did everything.

“The tab grows,” he said, to nobody. “Rae. Rae. The tab grows.”

He went to the bakery with Merle at seven.

She walked down Lantern Street in the drizzle with her arms wrapped round herself, not talking. He walked beside her. Halfway down, outside the blue door, she stopped. And looked at the bow window.

It was full of bread.

Yesterday’s bread. Loaves, golden and brown, and plaits, and rolls, the ones she’d baked yesterday morning, on the hundred-and-fifty-third morning, and put in the window. Still there. A day old. A little dry at the edges.

Every morning for a hundred and fifty-two mornings, that window had been empty when she came downstairs. And she’d had to fill it again.

It wasn’t empty.

She pushed the door. The bell went. She went in, and through, to the back. To the big floury table where she’d made the dough the night before, before she went up the tower. She’d left it in the bowl, under a cloth, the way she always did. It had always been gone in the morning. Back in the sack as flour, the bowl clean on the shelf.

The dough was still there.

It had risen. All night. It had risen and risen and risen, the way dough does when you leave it far too long. It had pushed the cloth up off the top of the bowl and come over the edge and was spilling down the side onto the table in a great slow pale tide.

Merle stood and looked at it.

“Yesterday’s still here,” she said.

He didn’t say anything.

“It’s ruined,” said Merle. “Look at it. It’s gone right over. I’ll have to start again.” She put her hand on it, on the great soft spilling mass of it. “I’ve never ruined a batch in my life. I couldn’t. It always went back.” She was laughing. She was crying. “Look at it. I’ll have to throw it out.”

“You’ll have to throw it out,” he agreed.

“I’ve never had to throw anything out.” She wiped her face with the back of her floury wrist and left a long white streak across it. “Oh, look at it.”

At ten past nine, the bell went over the shop door, and Mrs Penn came in.

She was a round elderly woman in a shawl, with a basket over her arm and a stick. Ice had heard about her knee every morning at ten past nine for twenty-six mornings, through the kitchen door. She came up to the counter, and looked at Merle, and frowned slightly.

“Merle, love,” said Mrs Penn. “Did I tell you about my knee? Yesterday?”

Merle looked at her.

“Yes,” said Merle. Her voice was barely there. “Yes, Mrs Penn. You told me.”

“Well, it’s the other one today,” said Mrs Penn.

Merle came round the counter and hugged her. Hard. For a long time. Mrs Penn stood in the middle of the bakery with her basket and her stick, being hugged, and looked at Ice over Merle’s shoulder with an expression of total bewilderment.

“Is she all right?” said Mrs Penn.

“She’s fine,” said Ice. “She’s just glad it’s tomorrow.”

They baked together at nine, because the dough was ruined and they had to start again.

He leaned on it. Push, fold, turn. Merle stood beside him at the big floury table and didn’t laugh once. She just watched his hands, and every so often put hers over them for a moment. Not to correct. Just to.

“What happens now?” she said, while the loaves were proving.

“I don’t know.”

“Good.” She smiled. “Neither do I. That’s wonderful. I don’t know what happens now.”

And at eleven she took the first batch out. Rosemary loaves, golden and cracked across the top, and gave them to him, wrapped in paper, still warm.

“Tomorrow’s bread,” said Merle. “Go on. Take it to them.”

He took it to the square. Thirty-six loaves in a basket. He gave them out one at a time, round the fountain, in the soft grey drizzle that hadn’t stopped. One to every person. And they sat round the edge of the fountain, all thirty-six of them, the way he’d sat there alone on the first morning. And ate tomorrow’s bread, the whole thing, all at once, with their hands.

It was exactly the same bread.

It tasted completely different.

Chris said it first, in the end. Somebody had to.

He’d been quiet all morning. Sitting a little apart from the others on the fountain, with his loaf in one hand and his notebook in the other, and his face doing the thing it did when he’d found a bug and didn’t want to report it.

“The respawn rule,” said Chris.

The fountain went quiet.

“On floor 1,” said Chris. “Deston’s party found out the hard way. If everybody doesn’t leave the floor within a day of the boss going down, it comes back.” He looked at the clocktower. “The Hour went down at midnight. If we’re all still here at midnight tonight—“

He didn’t finish. He didn’t have to.

Everybody looked at the clocktower. At the four faces, lit from inside even in the grey daylight. At the dark belfry above them, with thirty-six bells hanging silent in it.

“It’d come back,” said Calvin, slowly. “The Hour.”

“It’d come back.”

“And if the Hour comes back—“ Calvin stopped. He looked at the blue door at the end of Lantern Street, just visible from the corner of the square. “Does the day come back too?”

Nobody answered.

He looked at the clocktower, and he thought about Merle standing in the dark at one in the morning touching everything in the square. About a bowl of dough spilling over the edge of the table. About it’s the other one today.

“I don’t know,” he said. “I don’t know if it would. I’ve never done this before.” He swallowed. “I don’t want to find out.”

Drew stood up.

“Then we go,” he said. “Tonight. Before midnight. All of us. Up whatever stair the Hour was sitting on.” He looked round at them. “We don’t leave her in a loop. Not after this. Not for anything.”

“We’re thirty-one,” said Malcolm. “Thirty-two, some of us. Floor 8’s going to be—“

“Hard,” said Drew. “I know.”

“We’re not ready,” said Malcolm. He said it the way he said everything. Pessimistically. As if it was obvious. “We’ve never gone up a floor this low.”

Higu put her hand on Malcolm’s arm.

“We’re thirty-six,” she said, in her calm, slow voice. “We’ve never been thirty-six before.” She looked across the fountain at Ice. “We go up ready. Together. That’s what I said. That’s what ready means now.”

Malcolm looked at her. Then at Ice. Then he sighed, a long pessimistic sigh, and nodded.

“I’m going first,” said Calvin.

“You’re going first,” said Rachael.

Calvin turned round and stared at her. “You’re—you’re not going to say no you’re not?”

“You’re the scout who brings everybody home,” said Rachael. She didn’t look at him. She was eating her bread. “Everybody’s coming. So you’re going first.”

Thea found him on the bridge, in the afternoon.

He’d gone there to be on his own for an hour. He didn’t know why. He’d been on his own for a hundred and fifty-three days and he’d thought he’d never want to be again. But the square was full of people talking about floor 8, and he couldn’t breathe in it. So he’d walked out of town to the old stone bridge over the river, the one he’d crossed on the first morning, and sat on the parapet with his legs over the water.

The drizzle had stopped. The clouds were breaking up. Long bars of pale sun coming down through them onto the river, which was high and brown from the rain and going fast.

He heard her before he saw her. Boots on the stones of the bridge. Slow. And then she was there, at the end of the parapet, with the lantern in her hand.

She didn’t say anything. She came along the parapet and sat down on it, a little way from him. Not close. Not far. About the distance she’d sat from the worn place on the high terrace in the Red City, every evening, never in it, always next to it.

He didn’t say anything either.

They sat on the bridge and watched the river.

After a long time, she held out the lantern.

Across the gap between them, on the flat of her palm. The little glass lantern, plain, with the amber light inside it that wasn’t a flame.

“It’s yours,” said Thea.

He looked at it.

“You needed it,” said Thea. “On six. Higu drew a map. Eighteen times, from everywhere, and you couldn’t get past.” She was looking at the river, not at him. “And then once. In this light. You came down the hill with your head down and you didn’t look up till you were in it.”

He didn’t say anything.

“It’s yours,” she said again. “I’ve been carrying it since three. You cut it out of something’s mouth. It’s yours.”

“Keep it,” said Ice.

She looked at him then.

“I didn’t need it on six,” he said. “I needed you to have it.” He swallowed. “That’s not the same thing.”

Thea looked at him for a long time. Then, slowly, she took her hand back. With the lantern still in it. And put it down on the parapet between them, in the pale sun, so that its amber light lay on the old stone.

The river went by underneath them, fast and brown.

“I heard you,” said Thea.

He went very still.

“On six,” she said. “In the bowl. When it came down at me the second time. You stepped in front.” Her voice was quiet and level. “You said not this time.”

“I know.”

“I heard you.”

“I know,” he said again. “You wrote. i heard you.” He looked at the river. “I read it at midnight in the ringing chamber. On my own. I’d been carrying it round for a day without opening it.”

Thea didn’t say anything for a while.

Then her right hand came up and lay flat on her left shoulder. Over the jersey. Over a long pale scar that he couldn’t see and knew was there.

He looked down at his own left hand, on the parapet. At the long raised line across the palm.

“Not this time,” said Thea.

She turned her head and looked at him. Properly. With her bright blue eyes, with the star-shaped glints in them, the way she’d looked at him from the doorway of the bakery with the lantern in her hand. The way she’d looked at the slit of a helm, lying on the glass with her shoulder open.

“What was the first time?” said Thea.

The river went by.

He’d known she’d ask. He’d known since the bowl. Since i heard you. He’d lain in the middle of the ring of ropes for a week since then, knowing she’d ask, and trying to find what he’d say. He’d told Alea, on the floor of the ringing chamber. I’ll tell her not yet. And then I’ll tell her trust. And she’ll be furious with me.

He looked at her.

At her face, which he’d last seen in the first life on the hundredth floor, in the half-second before she stepped round him. Turned toward him. Her lips moving behind a ward. Three words, maybe four.

She was looking at him now. Waiting. On a bridge, in the sun, with a scar on her shoulder that matched the one on his hand.

He paused.

“Not yet,” he said.

Thea looked at him.

She didn’t look furious. That was the thing. He’d been ready for furious. He’d have been glad of it. She just looked at him, for a long, long time, on the bridge in the pale sun, the way she looked at a window she was reading. Words under the words. Taking it apart, very gently, all the way down.

And whatever she found at the bottom, he saw her decide something about it.

“…ok,” said Thea.

Two letters. The way he’d said it to Gabe.

He looked at her.

“When it’s yet,” said Thea quietly. “Whenever that is.”

He didn’t trust his voice. He tried anyway.

“You’ll be the first,” he said.

Thea didn’t say anything. She just looked at him a moment longer. Then she looked back at the river. And she didn’t move away. She stayed sitting on the parapet, a little way from him, with the lantern on the stone between them.

They sat on the bridge until the sun was low and the river had gone gold and somebody, far off in the town, started ringing a very small bronze bell over and over. Calvin, probably.
