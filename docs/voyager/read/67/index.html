Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 2: One Floor Ahead

Chapter 67

Axolotls Don't Scar

3,202 words · 14 min read

Xola had never left a mark on anybody in her life, and on the hundred-and-twenty-seventh morning she found out she’d left one on Thea.

She’d known it was coming. She wasn’t stupid. She’d been there on floor 4 when Malcolm closed Rui Xiang’s leg and it went pink, and gone by morning. She’d heard Kei say on six, it stays, at the bottom of the cistern, about the blood. She’d felt it under her fingers for three days, every time she put her hands on Thea’s shoulder. The wound remembering how to close, the way wounds did under her hands. And this one remembering it differently. Closing, but closing wrong. Not wrong. Just—not all the way back to how it had been.

She’d told herself it would go. By the morning. The way everything went.

On the hundred-and-twenty-seventh morning, she unwound the bandage in the grey light at the bottom of the pit, in the round room on the third ring. She looked.

It was a line.

Long and pale and raised. From the top of Thea’s left shoulder, where her neck met it, down across the shoulder and most of the way down her upper arm. Not red any more. Not angry. Clean, and closed, and healed. And there. Shiny and slightly pink at the edges, like a seam where two pieces of something had been joined.

Xola looked at it for a long time.

“No,” she said.

Thea opened one eye. She’d been half asleep on the cushions by the wall.

“No, no, no, no,” said Xola. She put both hands flat on the scar, the way she’d put them on a hundred wounds since floor 1. And she pushed. Not pushed. Asked. The way she always asked. Remember. Remember how you were before. Go back to it. Axolotls grow back whole legs. You can do one shoulder. Water came up round her fingers in little curls and went into Thea’s skin, and she felt the scar under her palms listen.

And stay exactly where it was.

“No,” said Xola. Her voice had gone high. “It’s supposed to go. It’s supposed to go back. I’ve never—I don’t scar things, Thea, I don’t—axolotls don’t scar, that’s the whole point of them, that’s the one thing they—“

“Xo.”

“—I can fix it, give me a minute, I just need to—“

“Xo,” said Thea. “Stop. Leave it.”

Xola stopped. She looked down at Thea, with her hands still on the shoulder.

Thea had both eyes open now. She was looking at Xola, not at the scar. Her face was very calm.

“It’s floor 6,” said Thea. “Kei said. Things stay.”

“It’s ugly.”

“It’s mine,” said Thea.

Xola stared at her.

Thea turned her head and looked down at her own shoulder. At the long pale line across it. She looked at it for a while, the way she looked at windows, the taking-it-apart way. Then she reached up with her right hand and touched it. Very lightly. Along the whole length of it, from her neck to her arm.

“He had one too,” she said.

Xola went still.

“On his hand,” said Thea. Quietly. Still looking at the scar. “His left hand. Right across the palm. From his thumb to the edge.” She traced her own, slowly. “He reached out. Toward my shoulder. When I was lying there. Before you got there. And his hand was right there, close, and I saw it. A line, like this one. Pink. New.” She swallowed. “And it was shaking, Xo. His hand was shaking so badly.”

Xola didn’t say anything. She couldn’t.

“He didn’t touch it,” said Thea. “He stopped. Right there. He just stopped. And then you came, and he took it back.” She was quiet for a moment. “I’ve been thinking about that hand for three days.”

Xola had been there for the first one. That was the thing about Xola and Thea and Ice. She’d been there at the very beginning, and it had been her fault, and she’d never once been sorry.

Thea was eighteen. Xola was nineteen. They’d been friends for a year, the internet kind, the four-in-the-morning kind. And there was a boy Xola knew from a tournament. They’d played in it together on the same team, a long weekend of a thing, years ago now. He was funny, and loud, and kind, and good at games in a way that made you want to watch him play. And Xola had thought, one night, for no reason she could explain, these two should know each other.

So she’d made a call. Three of them. And then she’d made an excuse halfway through and left the two of them in it.

They’d stayed in it till six in the morning. He’d played. Thea had sung. And then they’d done it again the next night, and the next. Every night for most of a year, him playing, her singing, Xola dropping in sometimes and dropping out again with a grin nobody could see.

Best thing I ever did, Xola had said to Thea, on floor 2, in Larchwick, in the snow. Thea had been sitting up at four in the morning with the face. The four-in-the-morning face, Xola called it, the one Thea got when she was thinking about something she couldn’t stop thinking about. Best thing I ever did, bringing him into that call. Second best was you.

It had passed. Of course it had. Things did. Thea had gone into her second year of studying and got tired, and the calls had thinned out, and then they’d stopped. And then it had been years, and then it had been a white room, and a blank name at the top of a list.

And now Thea had a scar from a thing that showed you what you wanted. And she’d been thinking about his hand for three days.

Xola sat on the floor by the cushions with her hands in her lap and looked at her best friend. At the four-in-the-morning face, at ten o’clock in the morning.

“Oh, babe,” said Xola.

“Don’t.”

“I’m not doing anything.”

“You’re doing the voice. The oh babe voice.” Thea shut her eyes. “I know what it sounds like.”

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You said oh babe.”

“I always say oh babe. It’s a greeting. It’s—“ Xola stopped. She looked at Thea’s face, with her eyes shut, and the scar on her shoulder, and her right hand still lying over it. And something in Xola’s chest that had been tight for three days, since a bowl of gold glass and a shout of STOP, gave way all at once.

She started to cry.

She didn’t mean to. She cried the way she did everything, loudly and all at once, with her whole face. Thea opened her eyes in alarm.

“Xo—“

“You ran into it.” Xola could barely get it out. “You ran into it, Thea. On purpose. I saw. I saw you see it. I saw you, you had the lantern up and you saw what it was, and everybody else was running at it like—like moths. Like stupid moths. And you could have stopped. You could have stood there and shouted. You did shout. And then you just—“ She waved both hands. “You just ran in. In front of Gigi. And it went through you.”

“I know.”

“I had my hands on your shoulder and it was coming out of you. All of it. I couldn’t—it wasn’t closing, it was so deep, and you were so white, and I thought—“ Xola stopped. “I thought, this is it. This is the one I can’t fix. On floor 6. In a desert. In front of a hole.”

Thea reached up with her good hand and took Xola’s wrist.

“You fixed it,” she said.

“It scarred.”

“You fixed it, Xo. I’m here. I’m talking to you. I’m being annoyed by your oh babe voice.” Thea’s mouth did something at the corner. “That’s fixed.”

Xola laughed. It came out wet and ridiculous. She wiped her face with the back of her free hand and smeared her eyeliner and didn’t care.

“Axolotls don’t scar,” she said. “It’s the one thing.”

“Well,” said Thea. “This one does now.”

Gigi came down at noon with water.

She came down the stairs from the second ring with a skin over each shoulder. She stopped in the doorway of the round room, and looked at the two of them on the cushions by the wall, Thea lying down and Xola sitting with her eyeliner everywhere. And she made a face. The bratty one. The one she’d been making since they were all nineteen.

“Why is she crying,” said Gigi.

“I’m not crying,” said Xola.

“You’re crying. Your face is all—“ Gigi gestured at her own face. “It’s gone everywhere.”

“It’s art.”

“It’s a crime.” Gigi came in and sat down cross-legged on Thea’s other side and put one of the water skins in Thea’s good hand. She didn’t look at Thea while she did it. She looked at the floor. “Drink that. Kei said. All of it.”

Thea looked at her for a moment. Then she drank.

Xola looked at the two of them. Gigi, sitting cross-legged with her silver hair hanging down and the dead blue flowers still in it, not looking at Thea. Thea, drinking, with her eyes on Gigi over the top of the skin.

Three days ago, in the cistern, Gigi had sat with Thea’s head in her lap all night, wiping her face, and not said a word. Xola had fallen asleep against the wall around two, with her hands on Thea’s arm. She’d woken up a little after three to the sound of two people talking very quietly. She hadn’t moved. She’d kept her eyes shut and listened. It showed me you. Staying up with me. Like before. And I should have stayed up. Some nights. And then nothing, for a long time, and when she’d opened her eyes a crack, Gigi had her forehead against Thea’s, and their hair was over both of them like a curtain.

Xola hadn’t said anything about it. To either of them. It wasn’t hers.

But she’d been waiting years for those two to be in the same room without it hurting. And they were sitting on either side of her now, on cushions in a pit in the desert, and it didn’t, quite. Not like before.

“Your flowers are dead,” Xola said to Gigi.

Gigi put her hand up to her hair.

“I know,” she said. “They died in the heat. On the first day.”

“Why haven’t you taken them out?”

Gigi didn’t answer for a second.

“Thea gave me them,” she said at last. Bluntly. Not looking at either of them. “Forget-me-nots. The ones I woke up with went brown on floor 1, the third day. And there was a bank of them by the river in Hearthstead, and she picked some and gave them to me and didn’t say anything, and walked off.” She shrugged, very stiffly. “So.”

Thea had stopped drinking.

Xola looked from one of them to the other. Then she reached out and took Gigi’s hand and Thea’s hand, one in each of hers. She held them both, very tightly, sitting between them on the floor, and didn’t say anything at all. Because for once in her life she couldn’t think of anything flowery enough.

It was Xola who asked, in the end. That evening. Because somebody had to, and nobody else would.

Gigi had gone up to eat. Thea was sitting up now, with her back against the wall and her shoulder bandaged loosely, looking at the lantern on the floor beside her. It was always beside her. She’d carried it since floor 3, and now she had it where she could see it all the time, even lying down.

“Thea,” said Xola.

“Mm.”

“What Kei said. In the cistern.” Xola kept her voice down. There were people in and out of the round room all the time. “The word. Hako’s word.”

Thea didn’t look up from the lantern.

“I know,” said Xola. “I know it’s a story. I know Drew said. I know it’s just the ten of us and the club. I know.” She twisted her hands in her lap. “But you didn’t look surprised. In the cistern. When Kei said it. I was watching you. Everybody else’s face went—“ She did something with her own face. “And yours didn’t. You looked like—“ She stopped.

“Like what?”

“Like when you used to get your exam results,” said Xola. “And you’d known for weeks what they’d be. And you’d been hoping you were wrong.”

Thea was quiet for a long time.

“Since floor 2,” she said at last.

Xola stared at her.

“I didn’t know what it was,” said Thea. “I didn’t have a word. Hako’s got the word. I just had—“ She looked at the lantern. “On floor 2, in Larchwick, I told Drew. It’s like he’s going somewhere he’s already been. Somewhere it went wrong. And he’s going back to fix it. I didn’t know what I meant. It was just a feeling. Every time I looked at that name at the top of the list.”

“Since floor 2,” said Xola.

“I wrote it down. In my book. In my alphabet. So nobody could read it.” Thea’s good hand had gone to the scar again. “Every floor, more of it fitted. The note. The storm. The chapel. The way Mother Tallow said a day early. And then on floor 4, on the high terrace, the place where he sat. Hyvred said it was soaked into the stone like a stain. And I put my hand on it, and I thought, he’s waiting. He’s waiting for us to come through that door, because last time—” She stopped.

“Last time what?”

Thea didn’t answer.

Xola looked at her best friend. At the scar on her shoulder, and her hand on it. At the lantern.

And then Xola understood.

Not the theory. She’d understood the theory at the bottom of the cistern, when Kei said it, along with everybody else. She understood the rest of it. The part underneath. The part that was making Thea’s hand press flat on the scar like she was holding something in.

“Oh,” said Xola. Very quietly. “Oh, babe. If it’s true—“

“Don’t.”

“If it’s true, then he’s done all this before. All of it. The tower. The floors.” Xola heard her own voice going high and couldn’t stop it. “And it went wrong. And people—“ She couldn’t say it. “And he was there. He was there for all of it. And then he came back. And he’s been up there on his own, a floor ahead, for a hundred and twenty-seven days, carrying all of that. Carrying all of us. However we—“ She stopped again.

“Xo.”

“He’s been carrying us dead, Thea.” Xola’s voice cracked right down the middle. “In his head. All of us. Every day. And then walking up to us and leaving us water with onion skins on it—“

She put her hands over her face.

Thea didn’t say anything for a while. Then Xola felt her move. Felt a good arm go round her shoulders and pull her in, carefully, on the side without the scar.

“I know,” said Thea, into her hair. “I know, Xo.”

“Is that why he—when he said not this time—“

“I don’t know.”

“You do know. You’ve got the face.”

Thea was quiet for a long time, with her arm round Xola and the lantern glowing amber on the floor beside them.

“He stepped between me and it,” she said at last, very low. “When it came down at me the second time. He didn’t block it. He put himself in the way. And then he said it. Not this time.” She was quiet. “Like somebody who’d been standing there before. In that exact place. And it had gone the other way.”

Xola went very still in her arms.

“The other way,” she said.

“I don’t know, Xo. I don’t know anything. It’s a story. It’s Hako’s word.” Thea’s arm tightened. “But I’ve been thinking about it for three days. And I think—“ She stopped. “I think last time, it was me. I think last time I was the one who got in the way. And it went through. And he was standing behind me, and he watched.” Her voice had gone almost nothing. “And this time he got there first.”

Xola didn’t say anything at all.

She sat there on the floor of the round room, at the bottom of a pit in a desert of broken glass, with her best friend’s arm round her. She thought about a call she’d made when she was nineteen. Three people. And then an excuse, halfway through, so she could leave the other two in it.

Best thing I ever did.

She’d never once been sorry. She wasn’t sorry now. But she held on to Thea very, very tightly, and cried for somebody she hadn’t talked to properly in years. Somebody who’d been carrying them all, up there on his own, and had a scar on his hand to match.

Later, when Thea was asleep, Xola sat up by the lantern and looked at it.

A little glass lantern, plain, with a light inside it that wasn’t a flame. Amber. Warm. Lights that lie look like what they are. He’d cut it out of something’s mouth on floor 3 and left it on a bench, lit. And Thea had picked it up and carried it everywhere since, without anybody asking her to.

Xola wasn’t a reader. Not like Kei and Hako, not like Chris with his notebook or Thea with her book in her alphabet. She didn’t think in stories. She thought in water, and in what things remembered, and in what they could go back to.

Axolotls could grow back whole legs. That was the thing about them. You could cut a piece off and it would remember how it had been, and grow back exactly that. No scar. No mark. As if nothing had ever happened.

She looked at Thea’s shoulder in the lantern light. At the long pale line she couldn’t heal.

Maybe some things weren’t supposed to go back to how they’d been. Maybe some things were supposed to stay. To show where something had gone through, and been survived. And to match.

She put her hand over the scar, very lightly, without any water in it at all.

“Okay,” she said to it, quietly. “Okay. You can stay.”
