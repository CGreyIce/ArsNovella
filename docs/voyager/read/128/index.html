Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 4: The Second Row

Chapter 128

Working On It

7,196 words · 31 min read

The thing about making something slowly, Salty had found, was that you had to keep not finishing it.

That was the hard bit. Not the work. He’d always been good at work. Give him a hammer and a hot bar and a thing to make, and he’d make it, fast, and it’d be fine. A hundred nails on floor 1. A hand-warmer for Yan, the same night, because Yan’s hands went white. Heavy black arrowheads for Wendy on floor 2. Spare knives on floor 10, by the dozen, in the gaps. A sword made in a hurry, Ressa had said, on floor 8, turning his greatsword over in her burned white hands. Yeah, he’d said. It’s what I do.

The hard bit was every single night, for a hundred and fifty-odd days, picking the thing up and looking at it and knowing it wasn’t done and putting it down again.

He was sitting in the hold of the Patience doing exactly that, on the night of the three hundred and fifteenth day, in the corner behind the water casks where Cursiffe sometimes wrote, with a shuttered lamp on the deck by his knee and the blade across his lap.

It was nearly done.

That was worse.

It had started with listening. That was the joke of it. Ressa’s first day.

Sit. Listen to the hammer.

He’d sat. Every night, on floor 8, for forty nights, in her forge under the second arch with the fall coming down outside the door like a wall of white noise and the great hammer going THUD every four seconds, walking down Ressa’s blade a finger’s width at a time. He’d sat on a stool with her small old hammer in his lap and listened. He hadn’t known what he was listening for. He’d thought, the first week, that it was a test, and if he sat long enough she’d let him hit something. She didn’t. The second week he’d thought it was a joke. The third week he’d stopped thinking about it at all, and just sat, and that was when he’d started to hear it.

The difference. Between a blow that went in and a blow that only went on. Between iron that was ready and iron that was only hot. The hammer sounded different when it was right. Not louder. Fuller. Like a word said by somebody who meant it.

On the last night, before they went up, Ressa had given him a bar.

Not hers. She’d been very clear. A plain grey bar of fall-iron, the stuff that came down the falls in the spring floods and got caught in the lake net and that the river-folk smelted in clay ovens on the shore. Your iron, she’d said. Not mine. I’ll not have a blade of mine walking about the world with somebody else’s name on it. And then, at the door, with the spray on her face and her green hair coming down: Second day. Fold. And, already turning back to her hammer: And the edge is the last day. Not before.

That was all. Fold. No how. No how many.

There hadn’t been a forge on floor 9 that would take it. He’d carried it up the stair in his pack, and across the Down, and up again, wrapped in a shirt, a grey bar the length of his forearm that weighed more than it had any right to, and every night he’d taken it out and looked at it and put it back.

And then floor 10. The Breath.

He didn’t like to think about the Breath much. It was too big. The crack in the floor of the world, roaring up and falling back, and the forge-folk at their hundred anvils round it, and old one-eyed Geir with his arms folded saying no. Gabe had lived down there. Gabe had needed it, for the Kiln-heart, for the shield. Salty had worked the bellows for him and Jared had held, the nail guild, three of them, every day.

And at night, when Gabe was at anvil nine and the Breath was high and nobody was watching, Salty had gone four anvils down, to fourteen, and folded.

He’d done it badly at first. He’d done it fast, because that was what his hands knew. Geir had come and stood behind him the second night and said no so many times that it stopped being a word. No. Too hot. No. You’re not listening. No. Again. And Salty had stood there in the roar with his face burning and Ressa’s small old hammer in his hand and thought about a stool under the second arch, and a fall outside the door, and the hammer going THUD, and listened.

And slowed down.

A fold a night. Some nights none. Heat it, draw it, cut it, fold it back on itself, weld it shut with blows that went in. Heat it again. Look at it. Put it down. Two hundred and twelve folds, in the end. Ressa had done two thousand. Ressa would have laughed at him. Ressa would have been right.

Geir had said hm once. On the last night. Standing behind him with his arms folded, looking at the bar on the anvil, which wasn’t a bar any more, it was a long flat thing with a grain in it like water going over stones.

Hm, Geir had said. And walked away.

Gabe had never said a word about it. Not once, in forty days, four anvils away. Neither had Jared. That was the nail guild. That was how it worked. You knew, and you didn’t say, and when the other one needed his tongs held, you held them.

Then floor 11. Hythe. Corran’s cold forge, which Jared had lit for the rudder irons. Salty had used it at night, after Jared went to bed, with the shutters closed, shaping. The long curve. The point. The thick back and the thin, thin inside edge. He’d told everybody he was making knives, and he’d made knives too, a few, in the evenings, to sell for Ice’s sacks of things, so it was true. It was just not all of it.

He’d hardened it on the last night before the launch. Brought it up to the colour Ressa called the inside of a cherry, in the dark, and carried it, glowing, out of the forge and down the slip to where the tide was in, and put it in the sea.

The sea had screamed. Steam, white, everywhere, up into his face. And when it cleared, the blade had been black and grey and hard as a stone, and he’d been standing knee-deep in the harbour at midnight shaking all over, and above him, at the top of the slip, a small folded shape had been sitting on a bucket in the dark.

Corran hadn’t said anything. Not then. He’d just sat there. And then he’d got up off his bucket, very slowly, and gone inside.

I’m not deaf, he’d said, on the deck, a week later. I’m old.

Salty had tempered it the next day, in the ashes of the forge, slowly, all afternoon, watching the colours run along it. Straw. Then bronze. Then a blue like a dragonfly’s back along the thick of it, and he’d pulled it out before the blue got to the edge. And then he’d wrapped it in oilcloth, and put it in the bottom of his pack, under his spare shirts, and it had come aboard the Patience with him on the morning tide, and he’d ground it every night since in the hold, on Corran’s stones, which Corran had told him to take. You’ll want those, he’d said, at the forge door, with his hand on Salty’s shoulder. Salty had thought he meant for knives.

He hadn’t meant for knives.

And now there was the ash.

It was lying beside him in the lamplight, unwrapped, finished. Corran’s first piece of the Patience. A haft as long as a man, pale and straight and smooth, with a slight swell where your hands would go and a slight taper at the end. Jago had lent him a drawknife and Jared had lent him a spokeshave and Salty had spent six nights shaving it down a curl at a time, so slowly that Jared, coming down for something on the fourth night and seeing him, had said, Man, I’ve seen glaciers, and then sat down on a cask and watched him for an hour without saying anything else.

The socket was fitted. He’d done that this morning. A collar of iron round the head of the haft, that Jared had made, and that had to go on hot so it shrank and gripped. Hot meant fire. The only fire on the Patience was Ottery’s stove.

He’d gone to the galley hatch at dawn and stood there.

Ottery had looked at him. The whole vast silent width of her, at the stove, with a ladle.

“Can I use your stove?” Salty had said. “For about ten minutes. Not for food. For a bit of iron. I won’t touch anything else. I’ll clean it after.” He’d taken a breath. “I’m asking first. Before. Not after.”

Ottery had looked at him for a long time.

“First,” she’d said.

And stood aside.

So the collar was on. It had gone on with a hiss and a smell of scorched ash, and gripped, and he’d quenched it with a cup of Ottery’s water, and she’d watched the whole thing from the corner with her arms folded, and when he’d finished and cleaned the stove top with a rag until it shone, she’d put a hand on his head, very briefly, the way you’d touch a dog that had done well, and gone back to her porridge.

And the blade was on the haft. Seated in the socket, pinned through with two iron pins.

It was nearly done.

All it wanted was the binding. And the edge.

He sat in the hold, on the night of the three hundred and fifteenth day, and looked at it, and didn’t do either.

The edge is the last day, Ressa had said, at the door, with the spray on her face. He hadn’t understood it for a long time. He thought he did now. You don’t put the edge on till it’s wanted. An edge waiting in a box goes dull. An edge put on the night before it’s needed is the sharpest thing in the world.

It wasn’t wanted yet.

He wrapped it up again. The oilcloth. The marline. He put the lamp out and sat in the dark for a while with his hand on it.

He’d nearly given it to Yan last night. In the bow, after the names. After three hundred and eleven, and the window, and Yan’s face. He’d been sitting against the foremast with it across his knees, he’d brought it up without thinking, and Yan had read out Known, and it will turn, and Salty had sat there with his hands on the oilcloth and thought, now. Now. Give it to him now.

And then Yan had read out keep an Edge, and looked so lost, and Salty had thought: not yet. It’s not wanted yet. You’ll know.

He didn’t know how he’d know.

He thought he would.

In the morning, the singer started to call.

Not the hum. The hum had been going for sixteen days, low and long, coming up through the planks. This was different. Higher. Salty had never heard anything like it. It came up through the deck and through his boots and into his teeth, and every tide-folk on the ship stopped what they were doing and looked down.

Aleaka was at the bow rail before anybody else. She’d been sleeping on deck. She was kneeling at the rail with her hands flat on the wood and her head tipped and her eyes shut, the way she did now, listening with the thing her window called Heed.

“It’s saying look,” she said.

“Look at what?” said Hana.

“I don’t know.” Aleaka’s eyes were still shut. “Ahead. Look. Look. Like when you’re pointing at something for a child.” She opened her eyes. “It’s excited.”

“It’s a whale,” said Calvin. “It doesn’t get excited.”

“It’s not a whale,” said Aleaka. “And it is.”

Kip was already going up the mainmast.

He went up the way he always went, like something being pulled up on a string, and was at the crosstrees in about four seconds and at the masthead in about six. He hung there with one arm round the mast and one hand over his eyes, looking west.

He didn’t say anything for a long time.

Then he shouted.

Salty didn’t catch it. Nobody on deck did, quite. It was too high and too cracked and the wind took the first half of it. But Marrit, at the wheel, went white.

“What?” she shouted up.

“WHITE!” Kip screamed. “It’s WHITE! The whole SEA! It goes WHITE! At the END!”

Salty went to the rail.

Everybody did. Thirty-six people and seven crew and Marrit, crowding to the starboard bow, which made the Patience lean slightly and Jago swear. Salty got a place between Gabe and Tiger and put his hands on the rail and looked.

Nothing. Sea. Grey-blue, endless, the way it had been for seventeen days.

And then, right at the very edge of it, where the sea met the sky, a line.

Thin. Very thin. Brighter than the sky. Like the line of light under a door. It went across the whole west, from one side of the world to the other, dead level, a white line at the end of everything.

“Oh,” said Tiger, beside him. Very small.

“That’s the Edge,” said somebody. Salty didn’t see who. “That’s the Edge, that’s where it goes over—“

Ice was further down the rail. Salty saw him. He was standing very still with both hands flat on the rail and his eyes on the line, and his face was doing a thing that Salty had seen it do on floor 10, at the Breath, once, the night Ice said you always quench too early to Gabe and then heard himself say it.

Thea was beside him. Her hand was on his back.

“Seventeen days,” said Higu.

She was standing on the aft hatch, by the rock. She had the chalk in her hand. On the left of the line, under REMEMBERED, from ten days ago: 2 MONTHS. OLD ROAD. NORTH.

On the right, under the question mark, she wrote: 17 DAYS. DEEP ROAD. WEST. (THE SINGER.)

Then she stood there with the chalk and looked at it.

“It was always there,” said Marrit. From the wheel. Her voice was very odd. “The deep road. Straight. It was always there. Three hundred years, and we went round. Two months round, every time, island to island, because the deep sang.” She looked down at the deck. At the hum. “And it was singing this way.”

Nobody said anything.

“I’m going to have a lot of things to say,” said Marrit, “when I get home.”

They saw the light at dusk.

They’d been sailing at it all afternoon, the line getting wider, brighter, until it wasn’t a line any more but a band, a white strip across the bottom of the western sky like the sky had a hem. You couldn’t look at it long. It wasn’t bright like the sun. It was bright like snow. Like a field of snow in the morning with the sun on it, so you had to squint. And it didn’t move. The sea moved. The sky moved. The white stayed where it was, at the end.

And just before it, black against the white, very small, there was a rock.

A single rock. Standing up out of the sea at the very end of everything, like the last tooth in a jaw. With a tower on it.

Salty saw it from the bow. Tiny. A pale finger. And as the sun went down behind it, into the white, and the sky went red and then grey, a light came on at the top of the finger.

Three short. And a long.

Salty heard Ice breathe in. Further down the rail. He heard it from ten metres away, over the sea.

“That’s it,” said Ice. Very quietly. “That’s the Last Light.”

Three short. A long. Dark. Three short. A long.

“Is it real?” said Wendy.

Nobody answered.

It wasn’t the same as the Teeth. Salty knew that much without anybody telling him. He’d seen the Teeth from Hythe, the first night, the light on the black reef, and even from that far it had looked wrong somehow, too bright, too clean, like a lamp in a painting. This didn’t. This flickered. The long one, at the end, wasn’t quite the same length every time. It was a little longer, once, and a little shorter, the next. Like somebody’s hand on a shutter. Somebody who’d been doing it for fifty years and was a bit tired.

“It’s real,” said Thea.

She said it very quietly. She was standing behind Ice with her hand flat on his back. She didn’t say how she knew.

Salty didn’t ask.

And then Hyvred, on the crosstrees, said, “Oh no.”

It was between them.

They saw it properly at first light, on the three hundred and seventeenth day, when the sun came up behind the ship and lit the sea ahead all the way to the rock. Salty had been at the rail since before dawn. He hadn’t slept. He’d lain in the bow with the oilcloth under his bedroll and listened to the hum and thought about the edge, and when the sky went grey he’d got up.

The sea in front of the rock was turning.

Not the sea everywhere. Just there. A wide band of it, smooth, glassy, too smooth, like the water at the top of a weir. It started about a mile ahead of the ship and went on, curving, for miles, round to the left and round to the right, so wide you couldn’t see the far side of it from the deck. A great flat wheel of water, turning, slowly. Left to right. Bending the swell as it went, so that the waves coming in from behind the ship bent sideways when they got to it, and slid, and went round.

And beyond it, in the middle of it, where the wheel turned round itself, the rock. The tower. The light, pale now in the day.

The Undertow was on the stair.

“It’s never been here,” said Marrit.

She’d come forward to the bow. All of them had. She stood at the rail with her own tide-cord wound round her hand.

“Never,” she said. “Not in any story. Not in Gran’s. Not in the song. It’s four days north of the old road. Always. It doesn’t come to the Edge.” She was shaking. “It doesn’t come anywhere. It’s where it is. It’s—“

“It moved,” said Chris. “Again.”

“It came round to the singer’s road,” said Higu. “And we went round it. So it came here.” She was very calm. Not calm. “To the end of the road. Where we have to go.”

“It’s on the stair,” said Drew.

Everybody looked at him.

“It’s guarding the door,” said Drew. “That’s what they do. The big ones. On every floor. It wasn’t guarding anything before. It was just there. In the north. Eating boats.” He looked at the wheel of water. “It’s a floor guardian now. It’s on the door. You can’t get to the stair without going through it.”

“That’s not where it was,” said Ice.

Very quietly. To the sea. Everybody heard.

“It was four days north,” said Ice. “It took the rudder. On the thirtieth day. It was never anywhere near the—“ He stopped.

Salty watched him stop.

He watched Ice’s face go white from the top down, the way Hana had said it did. On the coil of rope. Four seconds. He watched Ice hear himself. Telling the sea where everything had been. Telling it out loud, on the deck, in front of everybody and whatever else was listening. And he watched Ice not finish the sentence, and swallow, and put his hands flat on the rail.

“It doesn’t matter,” said Ice. “It’s this now.”

Higu went aft and wrote on the rock. Salty didn’t need to look to know what she wrote. He looked anyway, later. On the left: UNDERTOW: 4 DAYS N. TOOK RUDDER D30. On the right: IN FRONT OF THE LIGHT. ON THE STAIR.

And then nothing else, for a long time.

They had the council on the main deck at noon, round the mainmast, where the old man had said the names.

It wasn’t really a council. It was just thirty-six people sitting on a deck, and seven tide-folk, and Marrit, and Higu standing in the middle with the rock, because somebody had to stand in the middle and it was always Higu.

“We can’t go round,” said Higu. “It’s in front of the only rock. The Edge is behind the rock. There isn’t a round.” She looked at the wheel of water, a mile off, turning. “We can’t go through. We tried that on the three hundred and fifth day. It took the wind.”

“And the singer won’t go near it,” said Aleaka. “It’s frightened. It’s—“ She stopped, and her face did something. “It’s been frightened of it for a long time. Since its small one.”

“So we kill it,” said Calvin.

“So we kill it,” said Higu. “Yes. But we don’t know what it is. It’s at the bottom of a hole in the sea five miles across. We can’t get to it. Nobody can fight at the bottom of a hole in the sea.”

“Then make it come up,” said Gabe.

“How?”

Nobody said anything.

And then Yan stood up.

Salty watched him do it. He’d been sitting with the rest of the party, between Wendy and Cursiffe, with Corran’s cords on his arm and Suzu round his neck. He stood up, slowly, the way he’d stood up on the jetty on floor 8 when Aud held out the pole.

“It’s full,” said Yan.

Everybody looked at him.

“Hyvred said,” said Yan. “On the three hundred and fifth. It’s full.” He swallowed. “It’s full of them. The tide-folk. Their dead. Three hundred years of them. Everybody who ever went in the sea without anybody saying their name.” His voice was very steady. “I’ve seen it. Their lights go down. Corran’s started to go, before we said his name. It went north-west. It was going there.” He nodded at the wheel of water. “That’s what’s in the hole. That’s what it’s full of. That’s what it eats. That’s why it says more.”

The tide-folk had gone very still.

“And I can call them,” said Yan.

He held up his left arm. The cords on it.

“I know three hundred and ten of their names,” he said. “By heart. And who they were. And my window says, a light you know by name will hear you say it. It will turn.” He looked at the wheel. “Last night I said one. Kitto Sanderling. To the north-west, where it used to be. And something turned. A long way off. Under the sea.” He lowered his arm. “It’s closer now.”

Marrit made a sound.

“So I call them,” said Yan. “All of them. From the bow. And they turn. And if they turn, they let go of it. And it gets—“ He looked for the word.

“Lighter,” said Aleaka. Her eyes had gone wide. “Hungrier.”

“And when it’s hungry enough,” said Higu slowly, “it comes up. For more.”

“For us,” said Rachael.

“For the ship,” said Higu. “Yes.”

Nobody said anything for a long moment.

“That’s a terrible plan,” said Wendy.

“Yes,” said Higu. “It is.” She looked at the rock in her hands. “It’s the only one there is. So we’re going to do it properly.” She looked up. “Every one of us. The way that brings everybody back.”

And she started.

Salty had seen Higu plan before. On floor 6, for the Prism. On floor 9. She did it the way Ressa folded. A finger’s width at a time, not in a hurry, every piece in its place before she moved on. Who on the port rail, who on the starboard. Azac and Jared and Hako and Salty at the rail, shields, the wall. Drew and Chi in the waist, the second wall. Ice—

“Ice in the middle,” said Higu. “On the main deck. By the mast.” She looked at him. “Not at the rail. Not in front. In the middle. Where everybody can get to you, and you can get to everybody.”

Ice looked at her.

“In the middle,” said Higu.

“Yeah,” said Ice, after a moment. “Okay.”

“And Gabe on his right,” said Higu.

“Obviously,” said Gabe. He always was, now. He had been since floor 10. Hearthwall across Ice’s right side, warm, with its three flames.

Thea, beside Ice, didn’t say anything. But Salty saw her breathe out.

The healers spread through the ship. The archers up in the rigging, Hyvred and Winter and Aleaka in the tops, Kip running between them with arrows. The mages on the quarterdeck where they could see. Hana with her crown off already, turning it in her hands. Newish beside her for the count. Deston at the foot of the mainmast, linking. Cursiffe with a stack of river-ink pages, in the eel-oil that shed water, writing on them as Higu talked, so everybody would have one. And the tide-folk below, in the hold, all eight of them, battened down.

“No,” said Marrit.

Higu looked at her.

“I’m at the wheel,” said Marrit. “It’s my ship. I’m at the wheel.” She looked round at her crew. Pell, and Jago, and the Lusk brothers, and Morva, and Ottery, and Kip, who was twelve, at the mainmast’s foot, with his face set. “They go below if they want.”

Nobody went below.

Higu looked at them all for a long moment. Then she wrote something on the rock and didn’t say what.

“And Yan in the bow,” said Higu. “Calling.”

“Not on his own,” said Salty.

He said it before he knew he was going to. Everybody looked at him. He felt his face go hot.

“He’s not in the bow on his own,” said Salty. “If it comes up, it comes up for whoever’s calling it. That’s the bit it’ll be angry with. That’s the bit it’ll want.” He swallowed. “I’ll be in the bow.”

“You’re on the rail,” said Higu. “With Azac.”

“Then I’m on the bit of the rail that’s the bow.”

Higu looked at him. Then at Yan. Then at Wendy, and Tiger, and Cursiffe, sitting together on the deck, the five of them, the way they always sat, the way they’d sat in the Copper Spoon on the first night.

“Yan’s party in the bow,” said Higu. And wrote it on the rock.

“Tomorrow,” she said. “Noon. At the turn.”

He put the edge on at sunset.

He didn’t go down into the hold to do it. He thought about it and decided he was done with the hold. He took the oilcloth bundle up to the very front of the ship, to the root of the bowsprit, where Tiger lay along the spar most evenings, and sat down on the deck with his back to the bulwark and Corran’s finest stone in a bucket of seawater beside him, and unwrapped it.

The sun was going down into the white. The sky over the Edge had gone red, and then gold, and the white under it had gone gold too. The rock stood up out of it, black, with the light at the top just coming on. Three short. A long.

He laid the blade across his knees and started.

Slowly. Ressa’s way. The stone wet, flat, held at the angle she’d shown him on the last night with her burned white fingers on his. Along the thin inside edge of the curve, from the heel to the point, in one long stroke. And again. And again. Listening. The stone made a sound on the steel, a soft long hissss, and he listened to it change. Rough, at first. Then smoother. Then a sound like breath.

He didn’t hurry.

He felt people come. He didn’t look up. He heard Tiger first, the bare feet on the deck and then a thump as Tiger sat down, very close, cross-legged, with his tail curling round his own ankles, not saying anything, which was a thing Tiger had learned on floor 8 and still found very difficult. Then Cursiffe. Cursiffe sat further off, with his book shut, and didn’t open it. Then Wendy, who sat down right beside him, shoulder to shoulder, the way she’d sat on floor 2 when he hummed her song in the dark.

“Where’s Yan?” said Salty, without looking up.

“Aft,” said Wendy. “With Marrit. Saying the Seal Holm ones to Pell.”

“Okay.”

Hissss. Heel to point.

“He knows,” said Wendy.

“I know he knows,” said Salty. “He’s known since floor 8. He’s been not knowing it at me for a hundred and fifty days. It’s been very annoying.”

“He learned that from Gabe.”

“I know where he learned it.”

Hissss.

The light came up gold along the deck and lay on the blade, and he saw the grain in it. The two hundred and twelve folds. Like water going over stones. He’d never seen it so clearly. He thought it might be the light.

It’s good, he thought. He didn’t let himself think it often. He let himself think it now. It’s good. It’s the best thing I’ve ever made.

It’s the only thing I’ve ever made slowly.

He put the stone down.

He tested the edge on the hair of his forearm, the way Ressa did, and it took the hair off without him feeling anything at all. He sat and looked at the bald patch on his arm for a while. Tiger leaned over and looked at it too and said, “Oh,” very quietly, and leaned back.

“Tiger,” said Salty.

“Yeah.”

“The binding.” He held out a hank of tarred cord. The best Pell had. “Will you do it?”

Tiger looked at the cord. Then at him.

“The slow knot?”

“Neth’s knot,” said Salty. “Over the collar. Where the haft meets the iron. Ressa binds hers with leather. I want it in Neth’s knot.”

Tiger didn’t say anything.

He took the cord. He took the end of the haft, where the iron collar gripped it, and laid it across his own knees, and started. Over. Wait. Under. Wait. Through. A finger’s width at a time. Letting each turn lie down where it was going before he moved it. Salty watched his hands. Tiger’s hands, which were the fastest hands on the floor, on any floor, going so slowly you could hardly see them move.

It took him half an hour. He didn’t hurry once.

When he’d done, he pulled the last turn tight, and it held, and he sat looking at it. Then he put his hand on the haft above the binding, just for a moment, the way Neth had put her hand on his head.

“There,” said Tiger. “Now it’s mended.”

Salty didn’t trust his voice. He nodded.

Yan came forward at dark.

He came the way he always came along the deck, talking, half to Suzu and half to whoever was there. “—and Pell says Hob was a terrible singer, and the Lusk brothers say he was the best singer on Seal Holm, and I think,” said Yan, coming round the foremast, “I think they might have had two different grandfathers called Hob and nobody ever told them—“

He stopped.

Salty was standing up. He’d stood up when he heard Yan coming. He had it in both hands, upright, the butt of the ash on the deck and the blade at the top, curving over, catching the last of the light from the west and the first of the Last Light, three short and a long, and the lamp somebody had lit at the foremast.

It was taller than both of them. It curved over the top like a new moon.

Yan stood there.

Suzu, round his neck, lifted her small white head.

“So,” said Salty. His voice came out wrong. He cleared his throat and tried again, and it came out wrong again. “So, um. Aud said. On the jetty. Keep it until somebody makes you your own.” He swallowed. “And I said, working on it.”

“I heard,” said Yan.

“I know you heard. Wendy elbowed me.”

“I heard that as well.”

“Yeah.” Salty looked at the blade. Then at Yan. “So I was. Working on it.” He held it out. “It’s done.”

Yan didn’t move.

He stood there on the deck with Corran’s cords on his arm and his pale cyan eyes on the blade, and didn’t move, for so long that Salty started to think he’d got it wrong. That it was too much. That he’d made it badly. That it was too heavy, or the curve was wrong, or he’d—

And then Yan put out his hands and took it.

He took it the way he’d taken Aud’s pole on floor 8. Salty had been there. At the back. Yan had put out his hands and Aud had laid the pole across them and Yan’s face had gone like somebody hearing a word they’d known once and forgotten.

His face did that now. Exactly that. And then more.

He held it. He felt the weight of it, the balance, the swell of the ash where his hands went, Salty had shaped that for his hands, from memory, from three hundred days of watching Yan hold a staff. He turned it, slowly, and the light ran along the blade, along the grain, the folds, the water over stones, and down the inside edge, which was the sharpest thing in the world.

“Folded under the Breath,” said Salty, because he couldn’t not. He was talking too fast. He knew he was. “Two hundred and twelve. That’s not many. Ressa did two thousand. Fall-iron, from floor 8, she gave me the bar, she said it had to be mine. Hardened in the sea at Hythe. The haft’s Corran’s. The first ash he cut for her. He gave it me. He knew. He was awake, the whole time, the old—“ He stopped. “The binding’s Tiger’s. It’s Neth’s knot.”

Yan looked down at the binding.

He looked at Tiger.

Tiger looked at the deck very hard.

Oh, said something by Yan’s ear, that Salty couldn’t hear. And then, he knew, because Yan’s face did it: Oh, Yun.

And the window came up.

Salty saw it come. He saw Yan’s eyes go to the air in front of the blade. And Yan read it out, in the flat voice, but the flat voice didn’t hold, it went on the second line and he had to stop and start again.

Unnamed ]

“Unnamed,” said Cursiffe, from the deck.

“Ressa says,” said Salty. “Said. The one who carries it names it. Not the one who makes it.” He shrugged. His shoulders were shaking. He hoped nobody could see. “So. You name it.”

Yan looked at the blade for a long time.

He looked at it so long the light went out of the west completely and there was only the lamp, and the Last Light at the end of everything, three short and a long.

Then he looked up at Salty, and his face was absolutely serious.

“Working On It,” said Yan.

There was a short silence.

“No,” said Salty.

“Yes.”

“You can’t call it that.”

“I just did,” said Yan.

And the window changed. Salty saw it. He couldn’t see the window but he saw Yan’s eyes go to it, and Yan’s mouth start to go, at the corner, very slightly, and Yan read it out.

[ Working On It
Edge-blade · Unique ]

“It’s on the window,” said Tiger. In a voice of pure horror and delight. “It’s on the WINDOW.”

“Oh no,” said Wendy. She had her face in her hands. “Oh, no. Oh, Yan.”

“You said it,” said Yan, to Salty. “On the jetty. On floor 8. In front of a hundred river-folk and Aud. Working on it. And I pretended not to hear, for a hundred and fifty days, because you wanted me not to.” He held the blade. “So I want it to be that. I want to hear it every time. I want everybody to hear it.”

Salty opened his mouth.

He’d had something to say. He’d had a whole thing. He’d been going to say that you couldn’t name a Unique edge-blade folded under the Breath Working On It, it was stupid, it was the stupidest thing he’d ever heard, Ressa would kill him, Geir would come up four floors specifically to say no.

What came out instead was a laugh.

It came up out of him like the Breath. He couldn’t stop it. He stood there in the bow of the Patience at the end of the world and laughed until he couldn’t stand up, and sat down on the deck, hard, and kept laughing, and somewhere in the middle of it it stopped being laughing and turned into the other thing, and he didn’t stop that either. He sat on the deck with his face in his hands and his shoulders going and cried like he’d cried on floor 5 into his hands at the first sun, the whole thing, the forge and the hundred and fifty days and the stool under the second arch and the old man on the bucket at the top of the slip, all of it, coming out of him all at once.

Wendy sat down next to him and put her arm round him and didn’t say anything. She was good at that.

Yan knelt in front of him. He laid the blade across both their knees.

“Your light’s doing the thing,” said Yan. Very softly.

“Shut up,” said Salty, through his hands.

“It’s like a forge with the door open,” said Yan. “It always was. It’s never kept still. And right now it’s the brightest thing on the ship.” He paused. “Apart from one.”

Salty didn’t ask which.

He thought he knew.

Later, when the others were asleep, Ice came forward.

Salty was awake. Of course he was. He was lying in his bedroll in the bow with his eyes open and the blade lying on the deck between him and Yan, who was asleep with one hand on the haft, the way you’d sleep with your hand on a dog. He heard the footsteps and knew whose they were. You learned people’s footsteps on a ship.

Ice crouched down by the foremast. He didn’t say anything for a while. He looked at the blade in the lamplight.

“I saw you,” he said eventually. Very low. “On floor 10. At fourteen. I came down for Gabe one night, late, and you were four anvils along with your back to me, folding.” A pause. “I went back up. I didn’t tell anybody.”

“Course you didn’t,” said Salty.

Ice looked at the blade.

“It’s the best thing you’ve ever made,” he said.

“It’s the only thing I’ve ever made slowly,” said Salty.

Ice nodded. As if that was the same thing. Maybe it was.

He crouched there a moment longer. Then he said, even lower, so low that Salty only just heard it over the sea:

“Don’t let him go first. Tomorrow.”

Salty looked at him.

There was something in his face. Salty couldn’t have said what. He’d known Ice two years before all this, and three hundred and seventeen days of this, and he’d seen a lot of things in that face, and he’d never seen quite this. It wasn’t fear, exactly. It was like somebody standing at the bottom of a slip in the dark watching a ship they’d built go into the water, not knowing if she’d swim.

“He’s not going first,” said Salty. “I am. I always go first.” He shut his eyes. “You go and stand in the middle. Like Higu said.”

Ice didn’t say anything for a while.

“Yeah,” he said. “Okay.”

And went aft.

Salty lay in the dark and listened to him go. Then he listened to the sea, and the hum, and Yan breathing, and Wendy, and Tiger on the bowsprit with his tail hanging down, and Cursiffe, who snored very slightly, in a careful sort of way.

Ahead of them, past the wheel of water, at the end of the world, the Last Light went three short and a long.

He thought about Corran, on his bucket at the top of the slip, in the dark, watching him put a blade in the sea. Not saying anything. Getting up, very slowly, and going inside.

I’m glad I lived to have somebody do it, the old man had said to Milli. On the day she swam.

Salty didn’t know if he’d heard right, about the old man being under the ship. Following. Yan said so. Yan didn’t lie about lights. But Salty didn’t see lights. Salty saw iron, and fire, and the colour of a blade when it’s ready.

He lay in the bow and put his hand out in the dark, and found the haft. The first ash of the Patience. And left it there.

Tomorrow, he thought. To nobody. To the old man. We’re going to take her west. Like you said.

Working on it.
