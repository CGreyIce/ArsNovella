Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 4: The Second Row

Chapter 138

Rain

4,871 words · 21 min read

Everybody talked at once.

That was the first hour. After the wind. Thirty-six people on a salt flat at dawn beside a dead heron the size of a tower, and every one of them talking. Out loud. At the same time. About nothing. About everything. It didn’t matter what. It was the sound of it. Thirty-seven days of whispering close into somebody’s ear or not speaking at all, and then the wind came in off the edge of the world, and the Salt let go of their voices, and they all found out at once that they’d been holding thirty-seven days of talking in, and it came out.

Hyvred screamed at the sky.

Properly. With sound. All of it. The long ragged breath in, and then the scream, in capitals, the way she’d screamed at the sun on floor 5 and at the first green in perfect silence a fortnight ago. It went out across the white and came back off the Mast, three miles off, and she screamed again at the echo, and Bee laughed so hard she fell over.

Calvin wouldn’t stop saying hello. To everybody. To the heron. To the Mast. HELLO. And listening to it come back.

Hana sang. Badly. A song from home, one Ice half-knew, and Milli joined in, worse, and then Jared, and then half of Hana’s party and some of Deston’s, and nobody knew the second verse, so they sang the first one again, louder.

Salty sat on the salt with his greatsword across his knees and his head back and just breathed. Out loud. So he could hear it.

Ice stood in the middle of it and listened.

He’d forgotten. That was the thing. Thirty-seven days, and he’d forgotten what they sounded like. All of them. Together. Not one at a time, close, in a whisper. The noise of them. Gabe’s laugh, over everything, the way it always was. Deston saying Calvin in the voice he used for Calvin. Rachael telling somebody to sit down. Xola, somewhere, saying babe to Winter, who was telling her to stop. Chi’s voice, quiet and steady, under it all. Drew, saying somebody thank me, to nobody, for nothing.

He’d heard this noise for ten years once.

He stood there and let it go over him and didn’t say anything at all, because he didn’t want to miss any of it.

Thea found him an hour later.

He’d gone a little way off. Not far. Fifty metres, to the east side of the ring, where the trodden salt of the camp gave way to clean white. He was sitting on it with his knees up and the lamp shield beside him, looking at the long white body of the Preserved lying on its side in the morning light, and listening to the wind.

She sat down beside him with the lantern.

“You’re not talking,” she said.

“I’m listening.”

“I know.” She put the lantern down on the salt. “You’ve got a face.”

He looked at her.

“Since it died,” said Thea. “You’ve had a face. Since you stood up. You read something.” She was watching him the way she watched windows. Reading the lines underneath. “Under your window. Something came. Alea said something to you. You went white from the top down, and then you didn’t say anything.” She paused. “You went white from the top down at the Gate. And on the Teeth. And on the ledge on floor 11. It’s always something under your window.”

He didn’t say anything for a long time.

“Alea,” he said.

She told you about the dust, said Alea. On his knee. Small, the size of a cat. Eight days late, with a plan.

“I haven’t got a plan.”

You’ve got her, said Alea. That’s most of one.

He looked at Thea.

She was waiting. She didn’t ask. She’d learned that on a bridge in Evermorrow, what was the first time, not yet, okay, and she’d never once forgotten it. She sat on the salt with her hands round her knees and waited, the way Ina waited, the way everybody on this floor had learned to wait, on a step.

“It said no response,” said Ice.

Thea went still.

“After,” said Ice. “When it was dead. The line that comes under my window. Record 2 of 2, no response. I’ve had that one before. On the Teeth. When the painted one asked me and I said I’d never been there.” He looked at the heron. “That’s me. That’s it saying I didn’t do what the other one of me did. I didn’t stand up.”

“You didn’t stand up,” said Thea.

“No.”

“And then?”

He told her.

He made himself say it the way it had come. Exactly. Althea, record 2 of 2, no response. And then, smaller, the last one. Observing.

Thea didn’t move for a long time.

The wind lifted her hair off her face and put it back. Out past the heron, a long way west, past the Mast, at the edge of the world, the sky had gone a colour he hadn’t seen on the Salt before. Not white. Grey. Low and grey, along the horizon, like a line of smoke.

“Two of me,” said Thea, eventually. Very quietly.

“Yeah.”

“One who never knew.”

“Yeah.”

“And one who does.” She looked down at her hands. “And I didn’t step round you. So that’s two records, and the second one didn’t do what the first one did.” She was working it out. He could see her doing it. The way she’d worked out everything for nine floors, from the grey lines under windows. “So now it knows there’s two of me. The same as there’s two of you. It knows I’m not the one in the first record.” She stopped. “It knows I know.”

“Yeah.”

“And it’s watching.”

He didn’t say anything.

Thea sat with it.

He watched her sit with it, and he wanted to say a hundred things, and all of them were the same thing, which was stand behind me. He wanted to say it so badly his hands were shaking on his knees. He’d stood in the middle of a ring of eight tanks on one knee while a beak the length of a sled came down out of the sky at her four times, and he hadn’t stood up, and now it was over and it was watching her, and every single part of him wanted to stand up now, here, on the salt, and put himself between her and the whole of the tower, and never sit down again.

He didn’t.

He sat on the salt with his hands shaking on his knees and didn’t.

Thea looked at his hands.

Then she reached over and put one of hers on top of them. Both of his. The way you’d put your hand over a candle.

“Then let it watch,” said Thea.

He looked at her.

“It’s been watching you for twelve floors,” said Thea. “And you’re still here.” She looked out at the heron, at the long white neck lying across Gigi’s thorns, at the beak still stuck in the salt where it had gone in between her and Kei. “And it looked at me for two, and it sent that, and it put you on your own in front of it, and it gave me the exact moment. The exact one. I had the ward in my hand.” Her voice didn’t shake. “And I didn’t. And you didn’t. And Gabe did.” She was quiet for a moment. “It’s got two records of me, Ice. It can watch all it likes. I’m only going to be one of them.”

He didn’t say anything.

He couldn’t.

He turned his hands over, under hers, and held on.

She’s right, said Alea.

“She’s always right,” said Ice.

“I know,” said Thea, who couldn’t hear Alea. “Tell her I know.”

“Your light,” he said, later.

They were still sitting there. The sun was higher. The grey line along the western horizon had got thicker, and higher, and it wasn’t a line any more. It was a bank. Low dark cloud, coming in off the edge of the world behind the Mast, the first cloud he’d seen in thirty-seven days.

Thea looked at the small pale light at her shoulder.

It was there. The way it was always there. A ball of light, a system, hanging at her shoulder the way thirty-five of them hung at thirty-five shoulders across the camp. Ordinary. Steady. It hadn’t done anything since. He’d watched it all morning, out of the corner of his eye, and it had done nothing at all but hang there.

“It said don’t,” said Thea.

“I heard.”

“You heard?”

“I was right there.”

She looked at it for a long time.

“It’s never said anything,” said Thea. “Not since the white room. Welcome, Player. And quest text. And once, on six, that information is not available, Player, and it stopped. In the middle. For a second.” She held her hand out to it again, palm up. It didn’t come. “That was the first time I thought it might be—“ She stopped. “I didn’t know what I thought it might be.”

“Alea,” said Ice.

Alea was quiet for a moment, on his knee.

I’ve never heard one do that, she said. Not in ten years. Not once. Her tail moved. They’re not allowed. They’re built not to. They say what the tower gives them to say and nothing else, and they say it in that voice, and they don’t stop, and they don’t choose. She looked at the light at Thea’s shoulder. That one chose.

He told Thea.

And every one of them flickered, said Alea. At the same moment. Thirty-five. I felt it. Like a room full of people all breathing in at once, when somebody says something they’re not supposed to. She was very still. They’ve been on your side the whole time, I think. All of them. All the way up. Like I was. They just weren’t allowed to say.

And one of them said anyway.

He told Thea that too.

She didn’t say anything. She sat on the salt with her hand out, palm up, toward the small pale light at her shoulder. It hung there. It didn’t come to her hand.

But it flickered. Once. The way it had after the heart stopped. Like a candle when somebody breathes near it.

“Okay,” said Thea. Very softly. To it. “Okay. I heard you.”

The rain came at dusk.

He heard it first. They all did. They were back in the camp, at the still, where Ina had the pots going out of habit and nobody had the heart to tell him they might not need them. The bank of cloud had come in over the Mast all afternoon, low and dark, and swallowed the sun at four, and the light on the Salt had gone strange, grey and flat and very clear, so that you could see every ridge in the crust for a mile. The wind had dropped. Everything had gone still. The way the air goes still before something.

And then a sound. From the west. A long way off. Soft. A hiss, like fat in a pan, like the sea on shingle, getting louder.

He’d heard it on a hundred floors. He hadn’t heard it on this one. Not once. Not in either life.

I never saw it rain here, he thought. And didn’t say it. And didn’t need to.

It came across the Salt toward them like a curtain. Grey. A wall of it, from the edge of the world, over the Mast, over the long white body of the heron, over Gigi’s green at the edge of the ring, over the camp.

Over them.

He felt the first drop on the back of his hand.

Cold. Fat. It hit his hand and broke, and ran. And then another, on his face. And then everywhere, all at once, the whole sky coming down, on the salt and the sleds and the steel tent and thirty-six people standing in the middle of a dead sea with their faces up.

Nobody said anything.

For once. For the first time all day. Thirty-six people standing in the rain on the Salt in silence. Not because they had to be quiet. Because they didn’t have anything to say that was as big as it.

It drummed on the shields.

On the steel tent, on Chris’s kite shield and Azac’s and Jared’s thorn-shield with the rose inside the rim, leaned in together over the still. A roar of it. And under the shields, in the pots, Ina’s brine stopped steaming and started to fill, and run over, fresh water into salt water, and Ina took his hand off the crust and pulled his mask up and looked at his own fire going out, and laughed.

On the salt. He watched it. The drops hitting the crust and the crust going dark where they hit, darker, and then the whole white of it going grey round them, and wet, and soft. The ridges. The six-sided shapes in the salt, that Bee drew without looking, that had pointed at Thea for nine days. He watched them soften under the rain, and slump, and run together, and go.

On the heron.

He watched that too. Everybody did. The long white body lying on its side on the salt where it had fallen. The rain came down on it, and ran over it, and the salt on it ran. The plates. The crust. A thousand years of it, on its wings and its back and its long white neck, coming off in the rain. Slowly, and then not slowly, in sheets, the way snow comes off a roof when the thaw starts. Sliding off, and breaking, and going into the wet salt below as slush, as water.

And under it, the feathers.

They weren’t white.

He’d thought they were white. Old paper. A shell. Something that had been a colour once, a long time ago, before the salt. They’d looked white from the camp and white from close to, lying in Gigi’s thorns with its beak in the salt a metre from his knee.

Under the rain, they went grey.

Blue-grey. Soft. The colour of the sky over the sea on floor 11, at dawn, before the sun. The colour, he thought, of an ordinary heron. On a river. On Earth. Standing in the shallows on one leg. The rain washed a thousand years off it and it was just a bird. A very big bird. Lying on its side on the wet ground with its wings out and its long grey neck in a coil of brown-gold thorns, and its eye shut.

It wasn’t kept any more.

It was just dead.

“Oh,” said Yan.

Ice turned his head. Yan was standing at the edge of the ring, in the rain, with Working On It in his hand, the butt of the pale ash on the wet salt and the blade curving over his head, and Suzu round his neck with her small white head up. He was looking at the heron.

Not at it. At the place under its breastbone. Under the wing. The way Yan looked at things.

“It’s going,” said Yan.

Nobody said anything.

“It was in there,” said Yan. To Suzu, or to nobody. “The whole time. Its light. It couldn’t go. The salt kept it. Like it kept the birds.” The rain ran down his face and he didn’t wipe it. He didn’t sound sorry for the thing. He sounded the way Yan always sounded about what nobody else could see: interested. “A thousand years. In there. Waiting. It couldn’t go anywhere. It wasn’t allowed.”

He watched the place under the wing for a long time.

“It’s going now,” said Yan. “It’s going up. On its own. It doesn’t need walking.” He sounded pleased. “It’s just going. Like the ones on the floors below. Like a thread.”

Suzu put her head against his jaw.

Yan stood in the rain and watched something nobody else could see go up out of a dead heron into the dark grey sky, and Ice watched Yan watch it, and he thought about the kept birds round the Mast with their wings out, and the raker’s sled with the strap still on, and the boats, and everything the salt had held on to for a thousand years because it couldn’t let go. And the rain coming down on all of it.

“Bye,” said Yan. Up. Cheerfully, the way you’d wave somebody off at a gate.

Maigo went down that night.

In the rain. He took his shoe off and put his root-foot not in the pot, but in the salt. The wet salt, at the edge of the ring, beside Gigi’s green. He did it before anybody could stop him. Niko said Maigo and Kei said don’t and Chris started forward, and Maigo put his foot down on the crust, and his toes went soft, the way they always had.

And went in.

They didn’t recoil. They didn’t come up crusted white at the tips. They went down into the wet salt like roots going into a riverbank, and spread, and lay there, and Maigo shut his eyes and stood in the rain with his face up and his two leaves on his antler and water running down the bark of his cheeks.

He didn’t say anything for a long time.

“They heard it,” he said.

Everybody had come. They were standing round him in the rain in the dark, with John’s motes hanging over them and the lantern in Thea’s hand. Gigi in front, on her knees in the wet beside her green, with her hand flat on the salt.

“All of them,” said Maigo. “Down there. In the rings. The forest ones. The deep asleep ones.” He opened his eyes. “They turned over. Every one. All the way across. I felt it go across the whole floor, under us, like a wind going over grass.” His face did something wonderful. “They heard the rain.”

“Will they come up?” said Gigi.

“Not tonight,” said Maigo. “Not for years. Maybe a lot of years. They’re very deep and they’re very asleep and it’s only one night of rain.” He looked down at his foot in the salt. “But it’s not no any more, Gigi. It was no. For a thousand years. It’s not no now. It’s not yet.”

Gigi put her face in her hands. In the rain.

“And Seep,” said Maigo. Quieter. “The wells. The water under everything. The last of the sea.” He shut his eyes again. “It’s coming down into it. The rain. Through the salt. Slowly. It’ll take a long time. But it’s going down. Into the old water. For the first time.” He breathed out. “Tullah’s well’s going to go up a hand this summer. Instead of down.”

Nobody said anything.

Out on the white, a long way east, back the way they’d come, there was nothing to see in the dark and the rain. But Ice knew what was out there. A line of green points across the Salt, with thumb-high hedges round them, one every night, all the way back to a dead crown on the salt three days from Seep. And a town on a rock with a well going down a hand every summer. And a woman with her daughter’s name pricked into her palm.

He hoped they were standing in it.

He hoped Tullah had her face up.

They went up the next morning.

They had to. The rule said a day, and Higu wasn’t going to argue with it, and anyway there wasn’t anything left on the Salt to stay for. It had rained all night. In the morning it stopped, and the sky was clean and pale and blue, the first blue sky on the floor, and the Salt wasn’t white any more. It was grey and wet and shining to the edge of the world, with a thin skin of water over it in places that held the sky like a mirror, so that walking across it to the Mast was like walking across the sky.

The heron lay where it had fallen. Blue-grey. Very still. Gigi’s thorns had let go of its neck in the night, on their own, and gone back down into the salt along their root, and round the green point at the edge of the ring, where the thorns had come from, the rain had done something.

It was bigger.

Not much. Not the way things grew on floor 9. A hand’s width, now, where it had been a thumb. Fat jointed stems, three of them, green, red at the tips, like a little candelabra. And at the end of one, so small you’d miss it, a bud.

Gigi knelt by it for a long time.

She didn’t plant another one. She didn’t need to. She looked at it, and then up, at the Mast, and then back, east, along the line of her road, and then she got up and picked up the strap of the fourth sled, and pulled.

The ship was the same.

That was the strangest thing. The only thing on the floor that was. He walked across the wet grey salt toward it in the clean morning light at the head of thirty-six people, with Thea on his right and Gabe on his left, and it got bigger and bigger until it was the whole sky, and it was exactly the same.

A hull. Old dark wood, dried and split and crusted at the seams with salt, standing up on its keel on the flat of the dead sea as if the sea were still under it. One mast. One crosstree, near the top, with a sail on it that had been salt-stiff and was soft now, after the rain, grey, hanging down, moving a little in the wind. A ladder of rope, rotted, down the side. A hatch in the deck.

The first time, there’d been thirty-four of them. He’d climbed the side first, because he always climbed things first, and found the hatch, and gone down into the hold in the dark with a light, and found the stair.

He stopped at the foot of the ladder.

He didn’t climb it.

He stood there with his hand on the rotted rope, and Thea beside him, and Gabe, and all of them behind, and he looked up at the side of the ship, the way you’d look up at a door you’d been through before, a long time ago, on your own.

“Dec,” said Gabe.

“Yeah.”

“You going up?”

He looked at the ladder.

“No,” he said.

Gabe looked at him.

“Higu,” said Ice. Not turning round. “Who goes up first?”

There was a short silence behind him.

“Tiger,” said Higu. “Then Hako, with the rope. Then the parties. Then the middle.” A pause. “Then the rest.”

“The middle,” said Ice.

“You and Thea,” said Higu. “In the middle. Like on the rock.” Another pause. “It’s still on the rock, Ice.”

He took his hand off the ladder.

Tiger went up. Of course Tiger. Like something on a string, up the side of the ship in about four seconds, over the rail, gone. And then his face, grinning, upside down over the side. It’s fine. It’s dry. There’s a hatch. And Hako, with the rope. And then the parties, one at a time. Xav’s. Chris’s. Yan’s. Hana’s. Thea’s, without Thea, Drew at the back of it looking down at her from the rail. He stood at the foot of the ladder and watched them go up past him. Hyvred’s. Deston’s, last of the parties, Deston at the back with his hand on his shirt pocket, stopping at the foot of the ladder to look at Ice and not say anything and go up.

Then the middle.

Thea went first. With the lantern on her belt. He watched her climb, steady, hand over hand, and he didn’t put his hand on the ladder under her. He wanted to. He didn’t.

Then him.

Then Gabe, behind him, last, with Hearthwall on his back.

The hold was dry.

Dark, and dry, and very old. It smelled of salt and old wood and nothing else. Long and low, with the ribs of the ship curving up on both sides like the inside of a whale, and the light from the hatch coming down in a square, and in the square, dust, turning. Not grey. Ordinary dust. The ordinary kind.

And at the far end, in the dark, a door.

The way the doors always were. Plain. Round-topped. Wood, in a wall of wood, as if somebody had built the ship round it. And under it, a line of light.

He’d stood here once. In the first life. With a light in his hand and thirty-three people coming down the hatch behind him. He’d been first. He’d opened it.

He stood in the middle of the hold with Thea on his right and Gabe on his left and thirty-three people round them in the dark, and didn’t go to it.

Tiger was at the door. Of course he was. He’d got there first. He was standing in front of it with his hand flat on the wood, the way he’d stood at the top of the four hundred and twelve steps, waiting. He looked back at them all.

At Ice.

“Do you want to—“ said Tiger.

“No,” said Ice. “You.”

Tiger looked at him for a long moment, with his tail very still.

Then he turned round, and opened it.

The stair went up. Of course it went up. It always did. Narrow, and stone, and round, and up into the dark, with the cold coming down it and something at the top, a long way up, a faint sound. Not wind. Not sea. Something he couldn’t place.

Voices, said Alea, on his shoulder. Very quietly. A lot of them. A long way up.

He’d heard them once before. The first time. He knew exactly what they were.

He didn’t say.

They counted.

All of them, together, the way they’d counted every stair since the top of the first one. Newish started it, under her breath, and then everybody. One. The first step. Two. Thirty-six people climbing a stone stair out of the hold of a ship standing on a dead sea, counting out loud, their voices going up the stair ahead of them in the dark and coming back. Loud. Because they could.

He climbed in the middle.

Thea in front of him, a step up, with the lantern. Gabe behind him, a step down, with Hearthwall. He could hear Gabe counting. He could hear Thea. He could hear Calvin, a long way up, counting louder than anybody, and Hyvred in capitals, and Maigo slowly, a beat behind, and Ina, who counted the way he did everything, so quietly you had to know he was there.

He counted with them.

Four hundred and eighteen.

Four hundred and nineteen.

Four hundred and twenty.

And the top. A landing. A door. The way it always was.

Tiger was at it. He’d got there first. He stood with his hand flat on the wood and his tail round his ankle and didn’t open it. He waited. Until the last of them came up the last turn of the stair into the light under the door, Gabe, last, breathing hard, with Hearthwall on his back.

Then he looked at Higu.

Higu looked at the rock. She’d wiped it, Ice saw. At the bottom of the stair, in the hold, while everybody was climbing the ladder. Both sides. Clean. Ready.

“Go on,” said Higu.

Tiger opened the door.

And the sound came in.
