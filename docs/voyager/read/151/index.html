Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 4: The Second Row

Chapter 151

Witnessed

5,750 words · 25 min read

Thea had been reading under windows for three hundred and ninety-four days, and she’d never once been invited.

Every window she’d ever read, she’d read the way you read a letter that isn’t addressed to you. Quickly. Sideways. Looking for the words under the words while the words on top were still talking. There are thirty-five Players. And under it, faint, like a line pressed through from the page behind: something else. She’d learned to read that way on floor 1, and she’d never stopped. It had been her whole way of being in the tower. A girl at the edge of the room, reading the notes nobody meant her to see.

The door had opened for her.

All the way. On its own. As if somebody had heard her knock and come to answer it.

She stood in the doorway with the lantern, and looked down ten paces of grey stone toward a grey light, and understood that for the first time in three hundred and ninety-four days, something in the tower had written her a letter. And left it open on a desk. And stepped back.

She went in.

The lantern changed the passage.

She hadn’t expected that. She’d thought it would be like the Fen on floor 3, or the dunes on floor 6, where the lantern showed you a thing as it was and the thing was always worse. Here, it was the other way round. Outside the lantern’s light, the passage was grey. Smooth. Even. A grey that went on and on, without a mark, like the inside of a cloud. But in the circle of amber round her feet, the stone was stone. Old. Scuffed. Worn into a shallow dip in the middle by a great many feet going one way at dusk and the other at dawn. There was a crack across one flag. There was a dark stain by the wall that might have been spilt ink, a long time ago.

“Oh,” said Xola, very quietly, behind her. Close enough to grab. “It’s just a corridor.”

“In the light,” said Thea.

“In the light,” Xola agreed.

The amber in the glass leaned. Not forward, toward the hall. Back. Hard. Lying flat against the glass on the side toward the door, the steps, the bottom step, and the man sitting on it with a rock on his knees.

She didn’t look back. She kept walking. Behind Drew.

The hall was standing up.

Every clerk. All hundred and twelve of them. On their feet, at their long grey desks, in their rows, with their grey pens laid down across their open grey books. Not writing. Standing. And every one of them turned, toward the passage, toward her, with their plain faces, in the grey light that came from nowhere.

Nobody moved.

Thea heard Chi breathe in behind her. Slowly. Deliberately. The way Chi had taught all of them to breathe, on floor 10, at the Steward’s count.

“Keep walking,” said Drew. Very quietly. Not looking round.

They kept walking.

The aisle between the desks went straight down the middle of the hall toward the far end, wide enough for two, and the clerks stood on either side of it, row after row, facing in. Like a congregation. Like people who’d come to watch something go past. John’s mote went ahead of Drew, a small white light, low, and the grey faces turned to follow it and then turned back.

To her.

And as she walked, the lantern went with her. And its light went sideways, the way light does. Onto the nearest desks. Onto the nearest faces.

And they changed.

Not much. Not all of them. Only the ones the amber touched, and only while it touched them. But in the circle of the lantern’s light, as she passed, a grey face would go, for a moment, real. A man of about forty, with a bald patch and a burst vein on the side of his nose. A girl who couldn’t have been more than twenty, with freckles, and a chipped front tooth, and red rims round her eyes, as if she’d been crying for a long time and didn’t know it. An old woman with a strong jaw and a mole on her chin and hands knotted with ink, like Aldo’s. Brown eyes. Blue eyes. A scar through an eyebrow. Colour. In their cheeks, their hair, their lips.

People.

And then the light moved on, and they went back to grey.

“They’re people,” said Xola. It came out of her like something breaking. “Thea. Thea, they’re—“

“I know.”

“In the light, they’re just people—“

“Keep walking,” said Chi. Calm. Steady. From behind Xola. “Xola. Keep walking.”

Xola kept walking.

Halfway down the hall, on the left, in the fourth row, the lantern’s light went over a tall woman with brown hair pulled back off a plain face.

Thea saw her go real.

Sharp eyes. That was the first thing. Hawise’s eyes. Grey-blue, quick, too clever for the face round them. A narrow nose. A small white scar on her lower lip, like somebody who’d bitten it once very hard as a child. Ink on her right wrist, the way there was ink on Hawise’s. And her lips were moving. Silently. Over and over. The same short shape.

Haw. Haw. Haw.

Thea didn’t stop.

She wanted to. Everything in her wanted to stop, and turn the lantern full on the woman’s face, and hold it there, and say her name. Alys. The way Calvin said it. Across a field.

She didn’t. Drew had said: nobody stops. And Alys wasn’t hers. Alys was Hawise’s, and Calvin’s, and next week’s.

She kept walking. And behind her, in the fourth row, as the light went past and the woman went back to grey, Thea heard something. Very small. A soft, flat sound, like something light dropped on a stone floor.

She didn’t look back.

The lectern was where Rui Xiang had said it would be. At the far end, on a raised step, with the grey going on behind it into nothing. Tall. Grey. And on it the book, open. Bigger than a door. Its pages grey, its writing grey, lying open on the slope of the lectern like something sleeping.

The tall clerk was standing at it.

As they came up the last of the aisle, it moved.

Not toward them. Aside. It took one step down off the raised step, to the left, and stood there, beside the lectern, with its long grey hands folded in front of it and its plain face turned toward Thea. And left the book open. With nobody in front of it.

Drew stopped at the foot of the step.

“I don’t like that,” he said.

“No,” said Thea.

“That’s—it’s inviting you. That’s an invitation. Thea, that’s the exact thing a thing does when it wants you to—“

“I know.”

Drew looked at her. She saw his face do the thing. Thinking very fast. Deciding not to say most of it. And then, unlike every other time she’d ever seen him do it, he said it anyway.

“Is it bad?”

Thea looked up at the book.

“I’ll tell you in a minute,” she said.

And went up the step.

The letters stood still.

That was the first thing. John had said they slid. Rui Xiang had said he couldn’t see them from the pillars. She’d half expected to stand in front of the great grey book and find it unreadable, and have to go back down the step and out through the hall and tell Ice on the step that she’d failed.

She held the lantern up over the page. And the amber fell on it.

And the letters stood still.

Grey writing on grey paper, small and close, in lines. A hand she didn’t know. Even and patient and very old. And every letter of it stayed exactly where it was, and was a letter, and made a word.

“Babe.” Xola, at her shoulder. Close. “Babe, they’re moving. I can’t— they keep going—“

“Not for me,” said Thea.

“They’re going for me too,” said Higu, behind, with her slate. Her voice was tight. “I can see it’s writing. It won’t stay still. Every time I—“

“Only you,” said Chi. Quietly. From the foot of the step. She hadn’t come up. She was looking at Thea, not the book. “It’s only for you.”

Thea looked down at the page.

Only you.

She’d been reading under windows for three hundred and ninety-four days. Under the words. Under the lines. Reading the things the tower hadn’t meant anybody to read. And now, at the end of a hall that was longer than the hill, it had opened its book and stepped back and let her read the words on top.

Because she was the one who knew.

She started at the page it was open at.

It was the ones before.

She knew them at once. Cursiffe had written their page whole on Water Street, and Drew had chalked it on the slate, and she’d stood in the copy-room and read it with everyone else. But here it was again, in the tower’s own hand. Grey on grey.

Thirty. Up the well. Spring. Told.

And under it, thirty names. Names she’d never heard. Names she didn’t know how to say. Some of them were names like hers, names from home, short and ordinary, and some of them weren’t. And near the bottom, in the same grey hand as all the rest, the one name she did know.

Ruth.

And then the cries. One a year. The climbers stand on the thirteenth. The twenty-second. The thirty-first. All the way up. The ninety-ninth.

And then the next line, the next year: Nothing cried.

She turned the page.

Nothing cried.

She turned it again. And again. Page after page of it. One line a year. Nothing cried. Nothing cried. Nothing cried. Three hundred years of a clerk going up the Assize steps at the Reckoning, and opening this book, and standing there, and saying nothing, and coming down. Three hundred lines, on thin grey pages, one under another, each in the same patient hand.

She turned them faster. Her hands were shaking. She didn’t know what she was looking for. She did.

And then the pages stopped being nothing.

There were two of them. Facing. The left page and the right. Open together, the way the book had fallen open at the ones before, with the binding between them like a spine.

And at the top of both, in the same grey hand, the same three words.

The first year.

She held the lantern up. She read the left one first.

Thirty-four. Up the well. Autumn. Told: Aldo Pellow, teller, and the Count.

And under it, names.

Not in the grey hand. That was the first thing she saw. Every other line in the book had been in the tower’s own patient grey writing. But the names on the left page were in two hands. One of them grey. And the other, beside it, line for line, in faded brown ink: a big, loose, eager, slanting hand that went up and down a little, like a fence on a hill. As if the person writing it had been in a great hurry, and very happy, and couldn’t keep his hand still.

Aldo, thought Thea. He ran. All the way down the hill. Crying. Climbers, in my life.

She read the names.

All of them. One at a time. She couldn’t not. In Aldo’s hand and the Count’s, side by side, down the left page.

Gabe. Calvin. Deston. Azac. Yan. Salty. Hana. Jared. Chris. Winter. Ina. Hyvred. Gigi. Bee. Xav. Rui Xiang. John. Drew. Higu. Xola. Chi. Every one of them. Every name she’d sat at a fire with for three hundred and ninety-four days. In an old man’s happy, shaking hand.

Declan.

Althea.

Thirty-four.

Not Rachael. Not Dennis.

She knew why. He’d told her. On a rock at the edge of a tarn at the top of the tenth floor, with her feet in the cold water, he’d told her everything, in order, the way he said things, and she’d held his hand through it. Floor 4. Calvin. Rachael. Dennis. She’d known. But she’d never seen it. Thirty-four names on a page in an old man’s happy shaking hand, and two that weren’t there.

And her own. Althea. In Aldo’s writing. Second from the bottom.

She’d been here. She’d come up the well, in the autumn, and an old man had run all the way down the hill to meet her, crying. She’d lived in the Climbers’ House at the foot of the Wellgate. Everybody had been kind. Nobody had asked her anything. She’d stayed seven weeks, while the leaves turned on the hill road. She’d been happy, maybe. He’d said she was, sometimes, the first time. On forty-three she laughed so hard at Calvin she fell off a bench.

She’d been here. And she didn’t remember any of it.

She read on. She made herself.

Under the names, the cries. One a year. In the Count’s grey hand alone, now. At the Reckoning, the Count’s clerk cried from the Assize steps: the climbers stand on the— and a floor. Higher every year. Ten lines.

She didn’t read the floors. She couldn’t. Her eyes wouldn’t. They went past every one of them, down the page, to the last line. As if they already knew where it was.

At the Reckoning, the Count’s clerk cried from the Assize steps: the climber stands on the hundredth.

The climber.

Not climbers.

And under it, one word, on its own.

One.

She stood very still with the lantern held up.

And under that, so faint she’d never have seen it without the amber falling on it at exactly that angle, a line in a smaller grey hand. The kind of line she’d been reading under windows for three hundred and ninety-four days.

Declan. Chose.

And under that, smaller still:

Record 1 of 2.

She didn’t remember turning to the right page. She was just reading it.

Thirty-six. Up the well. Spring. Told: Hawise Brand, teller, and the Count.

And the names. In two hands again. The Count’s grey. And beside it, line for line, a quick round brown hand with a little flick at the end of every word.

Hawise.

All thirty-six. Every one. Rachael. Dennis. Calvin, with both his arms. She read every name, the way she’d read the left page, and her eyes went hot and she let them.

Beside Declan, in the grey hand, a short line:

Already told.

Beside Althea, in the grey hand, one word:

Observing.

And under all of them, small:

Record 2 of 2.

And across the bottom of both pages. Under the left and under the right. In the grey hand. One line, running across the binding, from one page to the other, as if it were holding them together, or pulling them apart.

One record does not match.

“Thea,” said Xola. Very quietly. “Thea, you’re crying.”

“I know.”

“What does it say? What does it—“

“Everyone,” said Thea.

She didn’t say anything else. She couldn’t. She stood on the step with the lantern up and her face wet and the two pages open in front of her. The left one. The right one. Both of them headed the first year. Both of them full of the same names. One in the autumn, in an old man’s happy shaking hand. One in the spring, in a teller’s quick round one. One that ended, ten lines down, with one. One that hadn’t ended yet.

And she understood what it was. What they’d come to read. What the clerk was going to stand on the Assize steps at the Reckoning and read out, every line, before the Magistrate and the whole of Tell.

Not a secret. Not a theory. Not a slip at a fire, or a man going white, or you always quench too early.

A list.

Thirty-four people who came up a well in the autumn, and were happy for seven weeks, and went up the stair under the Assize, and were cried on the steps once a year for ten years, higher every year, until the year the clerk said the climber, and then one.

Everybody in that hall would hear their own name read out on the left page. In an old man’s hand. In an autumn that hadn’t happened.

And then hear one.

The tall clerk moved.

It came back up the step. Slowly. To her side. Not close. It stopped an arm’s length away from her, on the left, where Xola wasn’t. And it held out its hand.

There was a pen in it. Grey. The Count’s.

Thea looked at the pen.

And a window came up. Not the tower’s usual kind. Not blue. Grey. Small and plain, floating over the page, over the line across the binding. As if the book itself had written it.

[ Reconcile.
Strike one. ]

She read it.

And then, because she was Thea, and had been doing it for three hundred and ninety-four days, and couldn’t stop, she read under it.

It was there. Faint. Pressed through, like a line from the page behind.

The count will be reconciled. One record will remain. The other will not have been.

She stood very still.

Will not have been.

Not will be crossed out. Not will be forgotten. Will not have been. As if it had never happened. As if nobody had ever come up the well in the autumn. As if an old man had never run down a hill crying. As if thirty-four people had never been happy in Tell for seven weeks, and gone up, and been cried for ten years, higher and higher, until there was one.

As if none of it had ever been told.

“Thea?” said Drew. From the foot of the step. “What does it want?”

She didn’t take her eyes off the window.

“It wants me to choose,” she said.

“Choose what?”

Thea looked at the left page. At Althea, in Aldo’s shaking hand. At One. At Declan. Chose.

“Which of us is real,” she said.

She could see it. All of it. Clearly. The way she’d seen the ward in her hand on the Salt, rewritten, turning in her fingers.

If she took the pen and struck the left page. One line, through the first year. Then it would be gone. The autumn. Aldo running. The thirty-four names. The ten lines of cries. One. Declan. Chose. All of it would not have been. And at the Reckoning, the clerk would stand on the Assize steps and open the book and read the disputed man’s two hands in full, and there’d only be one hand. Thirty-six. Up the well. Spring. And Declan, told. Like everybody else. Nothing to read out. Nothing for anybody to hear. No list. No one.

He’d be safe. The cord would come off. The secret would be safe forever, because there wouldn’t be anything left to be a secret. Not in Tell. Not in the tower. Not anywhere.

And every person on that page would not have been.

Not died. He’d carried that for ten years, the dying. He knew how to carry that. Not died.

Not have been.

The fire on nine. Gabe sitting up three nights on the moss, putting branches on, for somebody who didn’t come. Not have been. Calvin’s arm on floor 4, and Rachael holding it, and Dennis running to her shouting her name by the wall. Not have been. Drew in the last room, making it stand, for half a second. Deston’s gold going, ten seconds after him. Hana’s four seconds. The gate. Six of them. Gabe, still standing when the doors closed.

Her. Stepping round him. In the last ten seconds. Saying four words he didn’t hear.

Not have been.

He’d carried them. All of them. For ten years, and then for three hundred and ninety-four days on top. Every name. Every name, not tonight. He’d been giving them to her one at a time, on watches, at the stern of a ship, in the dark. One name a night. Like putting something away somewhere safe.

And the tower was holding out a pen and offering to make it so that there’d never been anything to carry.

She thought about Yan, learning three hundred and eleven names on a ship, so somebody would say them. About Corran. Put me at the end. Two Rs. About a teller on the Tellhouse steps every evening, saying the names of the dead, so that nobody in Tell would ever go without a name. About Cursiffe on Water Street, with his hands flat on a table, telling again a page that had been lost for three hundred years, so that a woman called Ruth could say thirty, nobody left down.

About Higu, on the roof, last night. Running her thumb down the chalk line on the rock.

The left one’s not wrong. It’s just somewhere else.

Thea looked at the grey pen in the clerk’s grey hand.

“No,” she said.

She didn’t take the Count’s pen.

She put the lantern down on the edge of the lectern, carefully, where its light would fall across both pages. And she reached into her coat. Into the inside pocket. And took out her own.

It wasn’t much of a pen. She’d had it since floor 1. She wrote her wards with it. Rewritten light, the text going across them faster than anybody could read. On floor 9 she’d written with it on the back of her own hand to keep a tally of the tips of somebody’s head. On floor 12 she’d turned a ward into a line with it and put it through the eye of a heron the size of a tower. It wrote in light. Faint amber, like the lantern. It always had.

She uncapped it.

“Thea,” said Xola. Very quietly. “Babe. What are you doing.”

Thea didn’t answer.

She leaned over the left page. Over the first year. Over thirty-four names in an old man’s happy shaking hand. Over ten lines of cries, and one, and Declan. Chose.

And beside One, in the margin, in her own upright, tidy, careful hand, that was nothing like Aldo’s and nothing like the Count’s, she wrote:

Told. — Althea.

The letters lay on the grey page and glowed. Faint amber. The only colour on it.

Then she turned to the right page. To thirty-six, in Hawise’s quick round hand. To Already told, beside his name. And beside that, in the margin, in the same upright hand:

Told. — Althea.

She capped her pen.

Two hands. That was the law. Nothing’s told in Tell unless two hands write it the same. The Count’s hand, and Aldo’s, on the left. The Count’s hand, and Hawise’s, on the right. And now hers. On both. The same word. In the same hand.

What if it’s both, Malcolm had said to Askew. Askew had told Hawise and Hawise had told everybody. What if what we write is true to the first hand and the second? Then they’re the same man.

They were.

She was the only person in either world who could say so. So she’d said it.

The hall didn’t make a sound.

She felt it. Behind her. A hundred and twelve people who’d given up their names, standing at their desks with their pens laid down, not breathing. She didn’t turn round. She looked at the tall clerk beside the lectern, with the grey pen still held out in its grey hand.

It wasn’t looking at her.

It was looking at the page. At the two small lines of amber, one on the left, one on the right. And its hand, the one holding the pen, was shaking.

She’d seen that once before. On floor 10, in the snow, in front of the Gate. The Steward’s hand, stopping on its page when Drew said my name’s Drew. And again, later, at thirty-six, when thirty-five people said his name for him. A grey hand on a grey page, not knowing what to do.

The tall clerk’s hand shook for a long time.

Then, slowly, it lowered the pen. Not to strike anything. To the page. To the place under her line on the left. And wrote, in the patient grey hand, one word.

Witnessed.

And then, on the right, under her other line, the same.

Witnessed.

It straightened. And looked at her, finally. With its plain grey face. Into her eyes.

And spoke.

“Where did you come from?”

Thea had heard that once. Not herself. She’d been standing behind him, at the Gate, with the lantern, when the Steward turned its face to his shoulder and asked him, very quietly, the first thing the tower had ever said to him that wasn’t a number. And he’d said Ice, the first time, on the pass. And at the Gate, at the very end, when the count stood, he’d said:

Them.

She looked at the tall grey thing in front of her. At the book. At two pages, both headed the first year. At her own name, in an old man’s hand, in an autumn she’d never seen.

“Same as him,” she said. “From them.”

The clerk looked at her for a long time.

Then it reached past her, and closed the book.

Gently. Both covers. The great grey pages folding down over the left and the right and the line between them. And the sound it made was very small, for something so big. Like a page turning in an empty room.

And behind her, all at once, a hundred and twelve pens began to move again. The sound of rain on a roof, a long way off.

They walked out.

Nobody stopped them. Nobody turned. The clerks were sitting again, at their desks, writing, all of them, with their heads down. As if nothing had happened. As if nobody had come.

All but one.

In the fourth row on the left, halfway down, a tall woman with brown hair was sitting at her desk with her hands flat on her open book, not writing. There was no pen in her hand. Thea saw it as they passed, on the grey floor beside her chair, where it had fallen. She hadn’t picked it up.

She wasn’t looking at them. She was looking at the lantern. Following it with her eyes, all the way down the aisle, the way you’d watch the last lamp go past a window at night.

Thea didn’t stop.

But at the door, at the end of the passage, at the very edge of the grey, she turned her head, just once. And the woman in the fourth row was still watching.

He was on the step.

Of course he was. Where she’d left him. The bottom one, with his back against the rail and the rock on his knees and Gabe on his right with Hearthwall. It was full dark now. The Wellgate was empty. Across the road, at the copy-room gate, thirty people were standing very still in a bunch with a single lamp, and nobody had gone to bed.

He stood up when he saw the lantern in the doorway.

He didn’t come up the steps. He stood at the bottom of them. And she came down. One. Two. Three. Four. And stopped on the last, so they were the same height.

She didn’t say anything for a moment. Neither did he.

Gabe got up. Quietly. He picked up Hearthwall and went across the road to the others without looking back, and took Drew and Xola and Chi and Higu and John with him, a hand on Xola’s shoulder, steering. And left them the step.

Ice was looking at her face.

“You’ve been crying,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Is it—“

“I read it.” She sat down on the bottom step. After a moment, he sat down beside her. She didn’t look at him. She looked at the lantern in her lap. “Both. The left page and the right. They’re both called the first year.”

He didn’t say anything. She felt him go still.

“It’s all there,” said Thea. “Everyone. In the autumn. Up the well. In Aldo’s hand.” She was quiet. “He wrote like he was running. Up and down. Like a fence on a hill.”

She heard him breathe in. And not breathe out for a long time.

“Thirty-four,” she said. “Not Rachael. Not Dennis. And all the rest. And me.” She swallowed. “And the cries. Ten of them. And then the last one. The climber. And then—“

“One,” said Ice.

“One.”

He put his hands over his face.

She let him. She sat beside him on the step and let him, the way she’d let him on the rock under the aurora. And when he took them away, after a long time, she told him the rest.

“It asked me to strike one.”

He turned his head.

She’d never seen his face do what it did then. Not in either life, she thought. Not even at the Gate. It went white from the top down, the way it went at the edge of things. But it didn’t stop there. It kept going. As if something had reached into him and taken hold of the one thing he’d been holding on to hardest, for longest, and he could feel its fingers.

“Which—“ he said. His voice didn’t work. “Thea. Which one did you—“

“Neither,” said Thea.

He stared at her.

“It said strike one. And under it, it said the other will not have been.” She held his eyes. “Not crossed out. Not forgotten. Not have been. Like it never happened. Any of it. Them. Me. The fire on nine. Gabe at the gate. Drew. All of it. All the names you’ve been giving me. One a night.” Her voice was steady. She made it be. “I wasn’t going to let it. Not to make you safe. Not to make anything safe. You don’t untell people. Not in Tell. Not anywhere.”

He didn’t say anything.

“So I wrote told,” she said. “Beside both. In my own hand. With my pen. Told, Althea. On the left, beside one. And on the right, beside already told.” She looked down at her hands. “Two hands. Theirs, and mine. On both pages. The same word.”

“Thea.” It came out of him in pieces.

“They’re still in it,” said Thea. “All of them. Every name. In Aldo’s hand. Nobody left down.”

And he put his head down on her shoulder, there on the bottom step of the Countinghouse, in front of thirty people at a gate across the road, and didn’t care who saw.

She put her arm round him.

“It wrote witnessed,” she said, after a while, into his hair. “Under mine. Both times. The clerk. Its hand was shaking.”

He didn’t lift his head.

“And it asked me where I came from.”

He went very still against her shoulder.

“The way it asked you,” said Thea. “At the Gate.”

“What did you say?”

She looked across the road at the copy-room gate. At thirty people with one lamp, waiting. At Gabe, with Hearthwall, at the front of them, looking not at her and not at Ice, but at the Countinghouse door, like a wall. At Xola, beside him, with both hands over her mouth. At Higu, drawing something on her slate in the dark.

“Same as you,” said Thea.

It was a long time before either of them said anything else.

The night was warm. Somewhere up the hill a dog barked. On the corner below the Tellhouse, under the dead lamp, a clerk stood with its book. It wasn’t looking at her. For the first time since the well-house, it wasn’t looking at her.

It was looking at the ground.

“If they read it out,” said Ice, at last. Quietly. Into her shoulder. “At the Reckoning. All of it. Every line.”

“I know.”

“They’ll hear their names. On the left. In the autumn. And then—“

“I know.” She was quiet. “I don’t know what they’ll hear, Ice. Nobody else could read it. Not Xola. Not Higu. Not John. The letters slid for them. They held still for me.” She looked down at him. “Only me.”

“And me,” said Ice.

“And you.”

He lifted his head, finally. He looked at her in the light of the lantern on her knees.

“I don’t know what they’ll hear either,” she said. “When it’s read out loud. Maybe it slides for them, the way it slid in the book. Maybe it doesn’t.” She held his eyes. “But it’s told now. Both of it. In my hand. Whatever they hear, it’s not a thing the tower can make not have been. And when they ask who writes him—“

She stopped.

He waited.

“I’ll write you,” said Thea. “True to both.”
