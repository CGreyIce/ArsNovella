Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 3: Party of One

Chapter 106

Hearthwall

5,112 words · 22 min read

The hundredth fold went into the Breath a little before midnight, and Gabe didn’t breathe the whole time it was in there.

He’d stopped noticing the roar days ago. You did. It was like the falls on floor 8, or the ticking in the Down; it was just the sound the world made. In, rising, a man’s height, two. Out, falling. And the bar in the tongs in the white of it, and the colour coming up through it, dark to red to orange to straw.

It looked different now. The bar. It had been grey iron with a red heart in the middle, the first day. Now there wasn’t a middle. The red had gone everywhere. Folded through it ninety-nine times, out and in and out, until every part of the bar had a little of the Kiln-heart in it, and the whole thing glowed from inside, even when it was cold. Deep. Dark red, like the inside of your hand held up to a lamp.

“Now,” said Geir.

Gabe ran it to anvil nine. Jared had gone up hours ago; Salty too. It was just him and the old man and the Breath. He put the bar on the anvil and hit it. Flat. And again. Fast, before the colour went, the way he’d learned. And folded it, end over end, the hundredth time, and hit it again.

And it was done.

He stood there with the hammer in his hand and the bar on the anvil going from straw to orange to red, and looked at it.

“Hm,” said Geir.

Gabe looked round.

Geir was standing behind him with his arms folded, the way he’d stood every day for ten days. Looking at the bar with his one eye. He didn’t say anything else for a long time.

“That’s the best fold I’ve seen at this anvil,” he said at last. “Since my master.”

Gabe didn’t know what to say. He didn’t say anything.

“Now you make it a shield,” said Geir. “That’s the rest of the night. Maybe the morning.” He unfolded his arms. “And you do that bit yourself.”

“Yourself?”

“A shield’s shaped by the one who’ll carry it,” said Geir. “Not the smith. Not the master. The one who’s going to stand in the wall with it. Nobody else puts a hammer on it from now.” He turned toward the stair. “I’ll see it in the morning.”

He went. Gabe heard his slow feet on the spiral stair, going up, and up, and gone.

He was alone with the Breath.

He put the bar back in the fire.

Xav came down about an hour later.

Gabe heard him on the stair. He knew it was Xav before he saw him; Xav walked like nobody else, quiet and even, the same speed all the way down. He didn’t look round. He was hammering. The bar had stopped being a bar. It was getting wide. Flat in the middle, thick at the edge, a long rough oval on the anvil, glowing, with the hammer marks going out across it in rings. He hit it again.

Xav came across the cavern and sat down on the warm stone beside anvil nine, where Malcolm sat to do his hands. He didn’t say anything.

Gabe hit it again.

He knew. He didn’t know how. He just did, the way you know the phone’s going to ring a second before it does. He’d known since the stair. He’d known, if he was honest, since an afternoon ten days ago, with a bar going dull in the tongs over the trough. He hit the shield again. And again. And put it back in the Breath, and stood at the crack with the tongs in his hands, in the roar and the white light, with his back to Xav.

“Don’t,” he said.

Xav didn’t say anything.

“I’m waiting for him,” said Gabe. “I decided. On eight. I’m not asking anybody. I’m waiting for him to tell me himself. When he’s ready.”

“I know,” said Xav.

“Then don’t.”

The Breath went up. Came down.

“I swore something,” said Xav. “On six.”

Gabe stood very still at the crack.

“At the top of a pit,” said Xav. Quietly. Steadily. The way he said everything. “In the middle of the night. Under the moon. To Winter. And to twelve other people. I swore that you’d hear it from me. First. Before anybody else. Before any slate.” He paused. “The day it was a fact.”

Gabe didn’t turn round. The bar was glowing in the tongs. Orange. Straw.

“Is it a fact?” he said.

“No.”

Gabe turned round.

Xav was sitting on the warm stone with his arms on his knees, looking up at him. His face in the light of the Breath was the face Gabe had known for years, since the first time Declan brought him round: a quiet boy from his poly class who leaned over very slowly and told you you were wrong, and was right. Steady. Waiting.

Except it wasn’t. Gabe could see it. It had been waiting for a hundred and twenty days, and it had run out.

“It’s not a fact,” said Xav. “It might never be. Not until he says it. And he said not yet. On seven. He said trust.” He looked down at his hands. “So I’ve waited. I’ve waited for it to be a fact. Every day. And it isn’t. And every day it isn’t, you’re standing on his right with your shield up, not asking anybody anything. And everybody’s worked out there’s a thing. Ina knows there’s a thing. Winter told him. Rui Xiang’s reading it off my face. Malcolm’s sitting at the top of that stair right now with two of everything because I asked him to and he doesn’t even know why.” He looked up. “And you’re the one I swore to. And you’re the last.”

Gabe didn’t say anything.

“I’m not going to tell you a fact,” said Xav. “I haven’t got one. I’m going to tell you what we think. Thirteen of us. What we’ve thought since floor 6. You can do what you like with it.” He paused. “Or you can tell me not to. And I’ll go back up the stair and keep waiting. With you.”

The bar in the tongs was going dull. Gabe could feel the colour going out of it, through the iron, into his hands.

You always quench too early.

He put it back in the Breath. He didn’t hurry. He set it in the white, in the roar, where it would hold its heat. Then he put the tongs down on the anvil, and came and sat down on the warm stone opposite Xav, with his back against anvil nine.

“He’s been here before,” said Gabe.

Xav went very still.

“That’s it,” said Gabe. “Isn’t it.” It wasn’t a question. His voice sounded strange to him. Flat. Calm. Somebody else’s. “He’s been here. All of it. The floors. The coat. The—“ He stopped. “He knew where the coat would be. He knew the ropes would come. He knew what I’d do with a bar of iron when it went dull.” He looked at Xav. “You always quench too early. I’ve been doing this for two hundred and forty days. He’s never once watched me. And he said always.”

Xav didn’t say anything for a long time.

“Yeah,” he said. “That’s what we think.”

He told it the way Thea had told it, on six. He said so. He said Thea said this, on six, at the top of the pit, I’ll say it the way she said it. And then he did.

That Declan had been here before. All of it. Not alone. With them. All thirty-six. That they’d come out of the white room together, the first time, and climbed it together, and it had taken years. Ten, maybe. That’s what Hako thought. Ten thousand hours. That something had gone wrong. On floor 4. The note: be careful, train longer on the 3rd floor, written by somebody who knew exactly what was on floor 4 and what it would do to people who weren’t ready. The Quiet, that Declan had killed on his own before any of them ever saw it. That went for Calvin first, in his trial, because Calvin ran ahead talking into the first square.

“Rachael and Dennis,” said Gabe.

“We think so.”

“Calvin’s arm.”

“We think so.”

Gabe shut his eyes.

That maybe it hadn’t stopped at floor 4. That maybe, Thea had said, all of us. One by one. On the way up. That he’d got to the top anyway. On his own. At the end. And that something there had given him a choice. And he’d chosen to come back. To the white room. To the first minute. A minute before any of them opened their eyes.

“So he could run,” said Gabe. With his eyes shut.

“So he could run,” said Xav. “And be a floor ahead. And do it all first. And leave us coats. And lanterns. And notes.”

The Breath went up and came down. In. Out.

Gabe sat against anvil nine with his eyes shut and his hands open in his lap, palms up, burned, and didn’t move for a long time.

He’d thought it would feel like being hit. It didn’t. It felt like a door in a house you’d lived in your whole life, that you’d walked past every day, opening. And behind it, a room you’d always known was there. Full of furniture. Full of everything. With the dust on it.

A hundred and fifty-three days, he thought. For one word.

He’d been so angry. On floor 7, in a bakery, with flour in the air. A hundred and fifty-three days, you absolute idiot, for ONE WORD. He’d hit him. And Declan had seen it coming and known every way to stop it and hadn’t moved.

It was the word you asked for.

He hadn’t understood. He’d thought it was Declan being Declan. Stubborn. Stupid. Carrying something on his own because he always did. He’d thought it was a hundred and fifty-three days.

It wasn’t. It was ten years and a hundred and fifty-three days.

He opened his eyes.

“How long has he known you know?” he said.

“He knows I know something,” said Xav. “Since seven. The fountain. I told him: when you’re ready, wait as long as you need, and then move once.” He paused. “He said I taught him that. I said: half of it. Somebody else taught you the waiting.” Xav looked at the Breath. “He said: yeah. Somebody else did.”

Gabe thought about that for a long time.

Ten years, he thought. Somebody else taught him the waiting. Ten years taught him the waiting.

“Does he know it’s thirteen of you?”

“No. I don’t think so.” Xav was quiet. “Fourteen, now.”

Gabe asked his questions. Not many. He didn’t have many. He’d never been the one who sat up with a notebook.

“Does Deston know?”

“No.”

“Calvin? Rachael?”

“No. Nobody in your—nobody in Deston’s party. Nobody in mine but me. Nobody in Yan’s. Nobody in Hana’s.” Xav shook his head. “Thirteen. And now you.”

“Does Thea know for sure?”

“She thinks she does. She knew first. Before any of us. She said she’d known since the white room, a bit. That he was going back to fix something.” Xav paused. “She hasn’t asked him. She asked him once, on seven, on the bridge. What was the first time? He said not yet. She said okay.” He looked at Gabe. “He said: you’ll be the first.”

“The first to know.”

“Yeah.”

Gabe nodded slowly. He didn’t mind that. He found he didn’t mind it at all. He thought about Thea at the back of every room with the lantern, and the way Declan’s thread pointed at her, the way Hyvred had said once it did. He didn’t mind that she’d be first. He’d have minded if it wasn’t her.

“And me?” he said.

Xav looked at him.

“In the—“ Gabe stopped. He didn’t know how to say it. “The first time. Before. What happened to me?”

“I don’t know,” said Xav. “Nobody does. He’s never said a word about any of us. Not one. Not even by accident. Thea thinks that’s on purpose.” He paused. “Rachael and Dennis and Calvin, that’s the note. That’s the Quiet. That’s the only bit we’ve got that’s anything like a fact. The rest is just—“ He spread his hands. “Him. Looking at us. On the jetty. At Calvin’s arm. Like he’s never seen two of them before.”

Gabe sat very still.

He was thinking about a hanging house on floor 9. Declan in a heap of blankets with his arm on the cover, and the ticking far below, and somebody sawing wood in the dark. And Declan’s voice, very quiet. If I’m ever late back. Anywhere. Any floor. Make a fire.

He’d said I’d make one every night. And Declan had turned his face to the wall.

“He asked me to make a fire,” said Gabe.

Xav looked at him.

“On nine,” said Gabe. “After the split. If I’m ever late back. Make a fire.” He was looking at the Breath. “He said it like he was asking me to do something I’d already done.”

The Breath roared up.

“I made him a fire,” said Gabe. Slowly. “Didn’t I. The first time. On nine. He got lost, and I—“ He stopped. He didn’t know. He’d never know. He knew. “And he didn’t come back for a while. And I made a fire. So he could find it.” His voice had gone. “And he never said. And then he came back here. And he asked me to do it again. Because—“ He couldn’t finish.

Xav didn’t say anything. He didn’t need to.

“And always,” said Gabe. “You always quench too early.” He looked down at his burned hands. “He watched me forge. For years. I must’ve—“ He stopped again. “I must’ve made him things. Shields.” He laughed. It came out wrong. “First thing I ever said on floor 1. With a nail in my hand. When I find him I’m making him a shield that doesn’t break. And I must’ve. The first time. I must’ve made him one. Years of me at an anvil, and him watching, and me making him a shield that doesn’t break.”

He looked at the lamp in the Breath. At the white.

“And he’s here,” said Gabe. “Starting again. From the white room. With nothing.” He swallowed. “So it broke.”

Xav put his hand over his mouth.

Gabe sat against anvil nine with his back to the iron and his burned hands in his lap and cried.

He didn’t try not to. There wasn’t any point. He cried the way he’d cried on floor 7 on his knees in the flour with Declan’s arms coming up round him like the heaviest thing in the world. Except there was nobody to hold this time. Nobody to hit. Just the Breath going up and down, roaring, and Xav sitting opposite him with his hand over his mouth, not saying anything, not touching him, just there.

He cried for a long time.

And somewhere in it, under it, he understood something else. The way you understand the shape of a room in the dark.

He wasn’t angry.

He’d thought he would be. He’d been angry for a hundred and fifty-three days on floors 1 to 7, so angry he’d spent all his money on iron. He’d been angry in the bakery. He’d thought, if he ever found out what the thing was, he’d be so angry he’d go up the stair and find Declan and hit him again.

He wasn’t. He sat there and looked for it, and it wasn’t anywhere.

There was only the room behind the door. Full of everything. Ten years of everything. And Declan, at the end of it, at the top of something, on his own, with all of them gone. Choosing to come back. To the first minute. And running out of the white room before any of them woke up, so he could get there first and leave them coats.

You couldn’t be angry at that. Gabe tried. He couldn’t. It would have been like being angry at a wall for standing there.

When he’d finished, he wiped his face on his forearm and got up.

Xav looked up at him.

“You’re not going to go and find him,” said Xav. Not a question.

“No.”

“Why not?”

Gabe went to the crack. He picked up the tongs. In the white of the Breath, the shield was still there. Glowing. Holding its heat, the way he’d left it.

“Because he said not yet,” said Gabe. “And he’s been carrying it on his own for ten years. And he’s got enough people who know things he hasn’t told them. And I said I’d wait for him.” He took the shield out of the fire. Orange. Straw. “And I’m still going to. Knowing doesn’t change that. I’m still waiting for him. Not for the thing. For him to be ready to give it me.” He carried it to the anvil. “That’s the only bit that was ever mine to wait for.”

He put it on the anvil and picked up the hammer and hit it.

Behind him, after a moment, he heard Xav get up. He heard him go to the bellows, the big leather ones at the side of the crack that Salty worked when the Breath was low. And take hold of the handles. And pump. Steady. The way Salty did.

“You don’t have to stay,” said Gabe, not looking round.

“I know,” said Xav.

He stayed.

Gabe shaped it all night.

He didn’t think about it much. He thought about it with his hands. Wide. Round. Not as round as the hold-folk’s; a little taller than it was wide, the way a shield sits when it’s been made for a big man to stand behind and lean on. Thick at the rim. Thicker on the left side than the right, a little, where it would go out past his own body into the space next to him. Where it would cover the man on his left.

He hammered the rim over. He hammered it again. Every blow went into it and you could feel it go in and stay. The glow inside the iron didn’t go out when it cooled. It went dim, and deep, and stayed there. Like a coal in a grate at the end of the night, that you know you can blow back up in the morning.

Around three, he made the boss.

He hadn’t planned it. He’d thought he’d do it plain. A plain round boss in the middle, the way the hold-folk did theirs. But he stood at the anvil with the hammer and the punch, looking at the middle of it, and thought about the lamp shield. The one he’d made on floor 3 for somebody who wasn’t there yet. With a lamp for a boss, because of a lantern on a bench at the top of a green hill.

He made this one a fire.

A small one. Raised. Worked up out of the metal from behind with the punch and the small hammer, a hand’s breadth across. Three tongues of flame, curling up round each other, the way flames go round each other in a hearth. Not a lamp. Not a light you carry. A fire. The kind you make on the ground, at the foot of something, in the dark, and sit beside, and keep going, and put another branch on, and another, for somebody who isn’t back yet.

The Kiln-heart glowed through it. When he held the shield up to the Breath, the three flames on the boss lit up from inside, dark red, as if they were burning.

Xav stopped pumping. Gabe heard him stop.

He didn’t say anything. Gabe didn’t look round.

He turned it over. On the inside, behind the boss, where the arm-strap would go, where nobody would ever see it but whoever carried it, he took the finest punch he had, and a small hammer, and wrote.

It took a long time. He wasn’t good at letters. He did them slowly, one at a time, square, the way the hold-folk cut names into the walls. Small. Deep.

STILL HERE.

Two words. The ones he’d sent. From floor 4, in a whisper, to somebody a floor away who’d never answered. Still here. Me too.

He looked at them for a long time.

Then he put the shield in the oil.

It went in with a roar of its own. Steam and smoke and a long hiss, and the oil boiling up round it, and the glow in the iron going dark, all at once, and then, slowly, under the oil, coming back. Dim. Deep. A coal in a grate.

He’d let it go dull first. All the way. Before he quenched.

He’d been told.

The window came up when he lifted it out.

He read it. He read it again.

“Hearthwall,” said Xav, behind him. Very quietly.

“I didn’t name it.”

“No,” said Xav. “But it’s right.”

Gabe put his arm through the strap. It fit. Of course it fit. He’d made it on his own arm all night. He lifted it. It was heavy, heavier than his old one, heavy the way the lamp shield was, the way a thing is heavy when it’s going to stop something. And warm. Through the strap, through his arm, into him. Not hot. Warm. Like standing near a fire on a cold night.

He held it up in front of the Breath. The three flames on the boss lit from inside.

And then he turned, and held it up the way the hold-folk held theirs on the wall. With the left side of it going out past him, into the empty air beside him. Covering nobody. Covering the space where somebody would stand.

“He’s going to cry,” said Xav.

“Yeah,” said Gabe. “Probably.”

Malcolm was asleep at the top of the stair.

Sitting up. With his back against the wall where the stair came out under the third wall into the cold, and his bag on his knees, and his head forward on his chest. Snow on his shoulders. He’d been there all night. Gabe stood at the top of the stair with the shield on his arm and the grey of the morning coming up over the low end of the pass, and looked at him.

Two of everything, Gabe thought. In case.

He crouched down. Malcolm woke. He looked at Gabe. Then at Xav behind him. Then at the shield. His eyes went to the boss, and the three flames, faint, glowing in the grey.

He didn’t ask anything. Malcolm never asked anything.

“You’re okay,” said Malcolm. Not a question. Looking at Gabe’s face.

“Yeah,” said Gabe. “I’m okay.”

Malcolm looked at him a moment longer. Then he nodded, and got up, stiff, and brushed the snow off his coat.

“Thanks, Mal,” said Gabe.

“I didn’t do anything.”

“You were there,” said Gabe.

Malcolm looked at him. Something went across his face. Then he picked up his bag, and nodded again, and went off down the street toward the barracks in the snow without another word. Xav watched him go. Then he looked at Gabe.

“He’s on the door,” said Xav. “Six to eight. With Thea.”

Gabe nodded.

“You don’t have to—“

“I know,” said Gabe. “I’m not going to say anything. I’m just going to stand there.”

He was on the barracks step, with Thea.

The door rota. Drew’s rock. Nobody watches alone. They were sitting on the stone step outside the barracks door in the grey, side by side, a little apart, with the lantern on the step between them. Thea had her knees up. Declan had his lamp shield across his knees and his sword beside him. Snow was falling, a little, straight down for once, because the wind had dropped.

Declan saw him first. He always did. He looked up the street, and saw Gabe coming down it in the grey, with Xav a little way behind, stopping, staying back. And he saw the shield.

His face did the thing.

Gabe came down the street. He didn’t hurry. He came to the step, and stopped. And Declan stood up. Slowly. With the lamp shield on his arm. And they stood there, in the snow, in front of the barracks door, looking at each other.

Declan was looking at the boss. At the three flames, curling round each other. Glowing, faintly, dark red, in the grey.

“Gabe,” he said. Very quietly.

Gabe didn’t say anything. He did what he’d come to do. He stepped up onto the step beside him. On his right. And lifted the shield, the way the hold-folk did on the wall, and set it, so that the thick left side of it went out past his own body into the space beside him. Across Declan’s right side. Covering it.

He felt Declan feel it. The warmth. Through the iron, into his arm.

“Geir says,” said Gabe, “a shield’s never for you.” He was looking out at the street, at the snow coming down. Not at Declan. “It covers the man on your left. That’s what a wall is. Nobody covers himself.”

Declan didn’t say anything.

“So it’s for you,” said Gabe. “That’s all. It’s called Hearthwall. It doesn’t go cold.”

Declan was looking at the edge of the shield across his arm. At the rim. He put his hand on it. His left hand, with the scar across the palm. He held it there.

“It’s beautiful,” he said. His voice had gone. “Gabe, it’s—“

“I know. I made it.”

Declan laughed. Very wet. Very quiet.

And then he looked past the shield. Past Gabe. Up the street, at Xav, standing a little way off in the snow with his hands in his coat. And Xav looked back at him. And didn’t look away.

Gabe felt Declan understand.

He felt it go through him, through the rim of the shield where Declan’s hand was on it. A stillness. The way the Grow had stopped ticking on floor 9. Declan turned his head, very slowly, and looked at Gabe.

“Gabe,” he said.

“I’m not asking,” said Gabe.

Declan’s hand had gone tight on the rim.

“I’m not asking, Dec.” Gabe kept his eyes on the street. “You said not yet. That’s fine. That’s still fine. I’m not asking you anything. Not today. Not ever, if you don’t want.” He swallowed. “Xav kept his promise. That’s all that happened. He promised I’d hear it first. From him. Before anybody. And I did.”

He heard Thea, on the step, very quietly, breathe in.

“I’m not going to say it,” said Gabe. “I’m not going to say any of it. That’s yours. You say it when you’re ready. You say it to who you want.” He paused. “I just didn’t want to be the last one in the room. That’s all. And now I’m not.”

Declan didn’t say anything. He stood there in the snow with his hand on the rim of Gabe’s shield and his face doing something Gabe had never seen it do. Not in the second row. Not in a thousand calls. Not in a bakery full of flour.

“There’s one thing,” said Gabe. “Just one. Then I’ll shut up.”

He made himself look at him.

“Next time you’re late back,” he said. “Anywhere. Any floor. I’ll make a fire.” His voice broke. He let it. “I’ll make one every night. Till you come in. Every night, Dec. However long.”

Declan’s face went.

He didn’t say anything. He couldn’t. Gabe watched him not be able to. Watched him stand there with the snow on his hair and his mouth open and nothing coming out of it. And then he put his forehead down. On Gabe’s shoulder. On the right one, the one above the shield. And stayed there.

He didn’t say you did. He didn’t say anything at all. He didn’t have to.

Gabe put his free arm round the back of his neck and held on.

On the step, Thea had picked up the lantern and turned round, so that she was looking the other way, up the street, at the snow. Giving them the step. Up the street, Xav had turned round too.

They stood there a long time. The snow came down straight, for once, very quietly, on the two of them and the two shields. The lamp and the fire. Overlapping.

After a while, Gabe started to hum. The one from the second row. The one they’d sung when they were thirteen and fifteen, with Declan getting the words wrong every single week, on purpose, until Gabe went purple.

Declan didn’t sing. He couldn’t. But Gabe felt him laugh, once, into his shoulder, wet. Which was the same thing.

Behind them, the barracks door opened, and Calvin came out yawning, saw them, said “Oh,” and went straight back in again and shut it. Very quietly. For once.
