Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 5: Everyone Comes Back Down

Chapter 191

For Now

6,363 words · 28 min read

Winter hated the Fen.

She’d hated it the first time and she’d hated it every single day of the forty-four they’d spent in it, and she’d been looking forward, in a small mean private way, all the way down two hundred and four steps from the Red City, to hating it again. It was warm. That was the main thing. Not hot, like the Glass, which at least had the decency to try to kill you honestly. Warm. Wet-warm, close, like the inside of somebody’s mouth. The air didn’t move. The water didn’t move. The sky was low and yellowish and sat on top of you like a lid, and everything smelled of mud and rot and green, and there were flies.

She liked cold. Cold was clean. Cold told you the truth about what it was going to do to you. The Fen lied about everything.

Tiger opened the door at the bottom, and the Fen came in.

“Oh, no,” said Winter, out loud, with enormous satisfaction.

Ina, behind her, made a small noise that was nearly a laugh.

The door came out where she remembered it. Of course it did. The ascension room on the green hill above the Black Mere: a small round stone room with a stone bench along one wall and an arch at the back with the stair in it, and a door at the front onto the hillside. It had been the last thing they saw of floor 3, going up, on the sixty-sixth day. All thirty-five of them crammed into it, with Calvin at the stair and Rachael’s fist in his collar, and Drew counting.

And it had been the place they found the lantern.

Winter came out of the arch into the round room and saw the bench, and stopped, because Thea had stopped.

It was just a bench. Stone. Worn smooth along the front edge by whoever had sat on it, over however long. Thea stood in front of it with the lantern in her hand, the Lure-glass one, the one that never went out, with its little amber flame steady behind the glass and its handle worn bright where her fingers went. She stood there and looked at the bench. At the place on it, near the left end, where on the thirty-seventh day, after the Lanternjaw, thirty-five people had come up the green hill soaking wet and exhausted and found a lantern sitting on the stone. Lit. Waiting. Amber on the handle, where somebody’s hand had been.

Ice had come out of the arch behind her. He was looking at the bench too.

Nobody said anything. The room was too small for thirty-six people and they were all trying to fit in it anyway, coming down out of the arch one at a time, and stopping, and seeing the two of them in front of the bench, and going quiet.

“I said she should carry it,” said Hana.

She said it from the back, near the arch, where she’d just come out. Not loudly. Hana’s voice was never loud unless she wanted it to be, and when it wasn’t it carried anyway, the way a bell carries.

“That day,” said Hana. “When we found it. Everybody was standing round it like it might bite. And I said Thea should carry it. I said it was the thing that makes lights tell the truth, and I thought you’d want her to.” She looked at Ice. “I didn’t know why I thought that. I just did.”

Ice didn’t look round. He was still looking at the bench.

“Yes,” he said.

That was all. Just yes. Hana went a bit pink and looked at Milli, and Milli put his arm round her.

And Thea put the lantern down on the bench.

She didn’t decide to. Winter could see that; she’d watched enough people do things they hadn’t decided to, this last year. Thea just bent and set it down on the stone, near the left end, where it had been, and straightened up, and took her hand off the handle. And stood back. And the lantern sat on the bench in the ascension room on the green hill, lit, the way it had sat on the thirty-seventh day with nobody there to see it but the stone.

Ice looked at it sitting there for a long time.

Winter had never seen him look at anything like that. Not even Thea. It was the way you’d look at a door you’d left open for somebody, a long time ago, in the dark, not knowing if they’d ever come through it, and then found, coming back, that somebody had come through it and closed it carefully behind them and kept the key.

“I didn’t know if anybody would find it,” said Ice. Very quietly.

“We found it,” said Thea.

“I left it for whoever came.”

“I know.” Thea picked it up again. The amber light swung, and steadied, and leaned. It always leaned toward him now. Winter had stopped noticing it was strange. “I came,” said Thea.

And she took his hand, with the lantern in her other one, and walked out of the ascension room onto the green hill, and he went with her.

Winter stood in the doorway and watched them go down the hill toward the black round water of the Mere, with the low yellow sky over them and the flies coming up out of the grass, and the little amber light going in front.

I came.

She was going to hate the Fen a lot less than she’d planned to, she thought. She was annoyed about it already.

Higu did the rock at the foot of the green hill, on the flat stone by the Black Mere where they’d all sat on the thirty-seventh day after the Lanternjaw, dripping.

Winter watched the Mere while she did it. It was still. Flat and black and round, with the reeds standing up round its edge and a heron on one leg at the far side. Nothing came up out of it. Nothing had come up out of it since the thirty-seventh day, Winter would have bet. Floors 1 to 3 didn’t keep their dead; the thing had gone to light in the water when the thirty-five killed it. There was nothing down there now but black water and whatever eels the Mere had in it.

“Ice,” said Higu. “What do you remember?”

He was standing at the water’s edge with Thea, looking at the Mere.

“Two weeks,” he said. He didn’t turn round. “In circles. No lantern.” He was quiet. “We never found the hill. Not for two weeks. We never found anything. We just went round.”

Higu wrote it. TWO WEEKS. IN CIRCLES. NO LANTERN.

“And the fourth night,” said Ice.

He didn’t say anything else about the fourth night. Winter saw Thea’s hand tighten in his. She saw Thea’s face, from the side. It didn’t do anything. That was how you knew.

Higu waited. When nothing else came, she wrote THE FOURTH NIGHT, small, and drew the line under it, and looked up for the middle.

Thea held the lantern up. Just a little. So the amber light went out across the black flat water of the Mere and lay on it, in a long gold line, all the way to the heron.

“There’s a lantern,” said Thea.

“That’s true,” said Higu.

THERE’S A LANTERN. Underlined twice.

They punted to Reedmoor in the afternoon.

The eel-fishers came for them. Winter didn’t know how they knew. Somebody on the hill, maybe, or a boy on a tussock with a long pole, watching the green hill the way the Reedmoor boys had always watched it, because the hill was where the Climbers came from and went to. Whatever it was, by the time the thirty-six came down from the rock to the channel at the foot of the Mere, there were punts waiting. Six of them. Long flat black ones, low in the water, with a fisher standing at the back of each one with a pole. They didn’t say anything. They just held the punts steady against the bank and waited for people to get in.

The Fen hadn’t changed either. Not like four. Four had stopped, like a clock. The Fen hadn’t stopped; it had just gone on being itself, the way it always had, without anybody needing it to. Endless reeds, taller than a man, rustling. Black warm channels between them, wandering, splitting, joining, going nowhere you could see. Drowned grey trees standing up out of the water with moss hanging off them in long beards. Herons. Flies. Heat haze over the open water, so the far reeds wavered. And under the water, things. Winter could feel them sometimes, through the bottom of the punt, the way you feel someone walk past behind you.

A mireling took a go at Calvin in the second channel.

It was nothing. It was a mireling. They’d killed hundreds of them on three, the first time, in the long weeks of standing in the channels up to their waists learning to be a line. It came up out of the black water beside Calvin’s punt, long and grey and sinewy, and wrapped round his ankle where it hung over the side, because Calvin always hung something over the side, and pulled.

Winter had an arrow on the string before Calvin had finished saying oh. She put it into the water a hand’s width behind the thing’s head, where the grey went thin, and the frost went out from the arrow into the black water in a white star, and the mireling stopped. Just stopped. Locked in a block of its own water, half out of the channel, with its long arm still round Calvin’s boot and its blind head turned up.

What you hit stays where it is.

Rachael pulled Calvin’s foot out of its grip and hit him on the back of the head with her glove.

“Feet in the boat.”

“It’s hot. I was cooling them—“

“In the boat.”

The fisher at the back of Winter’s punt looked at the white star in the water, and at the frozen thing in the middle of it, slowly going grey as the Fen’s warmth got into the ice. Then he looked at Winter. He didn’t say anything. He nodded, once, the way you’d nod at somebody who’d done a job you’d have done yourself, only better. And pushed off.

Winter found she didn’t hate that either.

The sun went down while they were still in the reeds. It went down on three the way it always had, the painted way, all at once, sliding down its rail behind the reeds so the sky went from yellow to orange to a sort of bruised purple in about the time it took to say so. And as the light went, the wisps came out.

Winter had been waiting for them.

They came up off the water the way they always had, at dusk, over the deep channels. Pale. Small. The size of a fist, maybe, each one, drifting, a little way above the surface, in ones and twos and then in dozens. On the forty-four days, the first time, Winter had learned not to look at them. Everybody had. You learned it the first night, or you learned it the hard way: the wisps became whatever light you most wanted to walk toward, and then you walked toward it, into the water, and the mirelings were waiting. The first night on three Bee had nearly gone in after one. Hako had held her. Nobody would ever say what Bee had seen.

Winter hadn’t looked. Not once in forty-four days. She’d kept her eyes on the boat, or the reeds, or the back of Ina’s head, and she’d never let herself find out what the Fen thought she wanted. She’d decided that was nobody’s business. Least of all a swamp’s.

She looked now. Because of the lantern.

Thea was in the front punt with Ice, holding it up. And in its light, the wisps were what they were. Winter had heard that a hundred times and never seen it, because on three, the first time, she’d been too busy not looking. While carried, lights that lie look like what they are. So she looked, and that was what she saw. Small pale lights. Faint and shy, a bit sickly, like marsh-gas, like a candle seen through dirty glass. Just lights. Floating over the black water, with nothing in them at all.

And they weren’t keeping their distance.

That was the thing. On the forty-four days they had. After the night the lights went out, Reedmoor said, the wisps had come back quieter, and kept their distance. They’d hung out over the deep water at dusk and never came near the walkways. Now they were coming. Slowly. Drifting in across the channels toward the front punt from every side, the way moths come to a window. Not at Thea. At the lantern. They came in out of the dusk and gathered round it in a loose ring, a little way off, small and pale and honest, and hung there, and went along with the punt, round it, keeping pace. Dozens of them. As if they’d been out there in the dark for a long time wanting something to tell the truth to.

Thea held the lantern up and looked at them.

“Oh,” she said softly. “They’re small.”

Ice, beside her, didn’t say anything. He was looking at them too. Winter, from two punts back, couldn’t see his face. She saw his hand go out, though, a little way, palm up, over the side, the way you’d put your hand out to a bird. One of the wisps came down out of the ring and hung over his palm for a moment. Faint and pale and nothing. Then it went back up.

“It’s what they are,” said Ice. “That’s all they ever were. Just that.” He was quiet a moment. “I didn’t know that. The first time. I never got to see.”

Nobody asked. Winter didn’t either. But she put it away, with the rest. I never got to see. She was careful with things like that. She had a lot of them now, and she’d told Ina, by the water on the night of the aurora, the one word she’d promised, fact, and nothing else, because that was the deal, and she kept her deals. She’d never put any of the rest on a slate.

And then, as the punts came round the last bend and Reedmoor came into view across the open water, Winter saw the other light.

Far off. Out in the middle of the Fen, a long way beyond the village, over the reeds. A small steady point of amber, low down near the water, not drifting. Not moving at all. It sat there in the purple dusk like a star that had come down to have a look and decided to stay.

“The tower,” said Hyvred, from the next punt. She’d seen it too. Hyvred saw lights before anybody. “The drowned one. In the middle. That’s—“ She stopped.

That was his. They all knew it was. Reedmoor had told them, the first time: on the thirteenth night, before any of them had come up, somebody had gone out to the drowned tower in the middle of the Fen and lit the lantern on top of it, by hand, and every wisp on the whole floor had gone out at once. The night the lights went out. Hyvred had seen his trail go out there and come back. They’d never been out to it. It was too far, and the channels to it were too deep, and nobody had wanted to go somewhere he’d gone alone.

Five hundred and eighteen nights. Winter did the sum without wanting to. It was still lit.

Ice was looking at it. Of course he was. He’d turned in the front punt to look at it across the dusk. And Winter, two punts back, could see his face this time, side on, in the amber of Thea’s lantern.

He looked like a man who’s been told that a candle he lit in a window once, for somebody who was never going to come, has been burning ever since, and that somebody saw it.

Reedmoor stood up out of the water on its stilts the way it always had. Reed houses, round, with pointed reed roofs, on long black poles driven into the mud. Plank walkways between them, a metre above the water, with rope rails. Punts tied up everywhere. Smoke from the smoking hall at the far end, the long one, the biggest building in the village, where the eels hung in rows in the dark of the roof and the smell got into your hair and never came out.

And the eel-woman on the walkway outside it, with her broom.

She was small and brown and exactly the same. Arms folded. Broom in one hand. Looking at them come in across the open water, thirty-six people in six punts with a lantern in the front and a ring of honest wisps round it, and not looking the least bit surprised.

“Told you,” she said.

That was all she said, to all of them. Told you. Then she turned round and went into the smoking hall, and they followed her in, because that was what you did.

Waddles got there first. He’d come off Chi’s shoulder at the bottom of the walkway and gone up it at a speed nobody would have believed a penguin could manage, and straight in through the door, and across the hall, and out of the back door onto the eel-woman’s own jetty, where a basket was hanging on a hook. He stood under it. He looked at it. Then he looked round at the eel-woman, and opened his beak, and made the noise Chi said meant I’m here to work.

The eel-woman looked at him for a long moment.

“Well,” she said. “You’d better get on with it, then.”

Waddles went in off the end of the jetty without a splash.

Chi made a sound and put her hand over her mouth.

The smoking hall was the smoking hall. Long and low and dim, with the eels in their rows in the dark under the roof, and the long table down the middle where they’d eaten every meal for forty-four days, and the fire at the end. The benches. The window Rachael had watched Dennis through, the day he went out to negotiate. The door.

And the nail.

Winter saw Deston see it. He’d come in near the front with Rachael and Dennis, and he’d stopped just inside the door and looked at the wall beside it. At a plain iron nail, driven into the post at about head height. With nothing on it.

He put his hand flat on his shirt pocket. The left one. Buttoned.

Forty-three days the note had hung on that nail. Deston had pinned it there himself, the twenty-third day, the night he read it out. No more pocket. And then on the morning they went up, the sixty-sixth day, he’d taken it down again and put it back in his pocket, because, he’d said to Gabe, it was a place to keep it.

He stood looking at the empty nail. Then he unbuttoned the pocket.

He took the card out. Winter had seen it once, on the twenty-third day, on the table in this hall. A small white card, his name on the front. On the back, in the grey of a fingertip, eight words and a letter. be careful, train longer on the 3rd floor - D.

Deston hung it back on the nail.

Just like that. He reached up and put the card on the nail by the corner, the way he’d done it the first time, and stood back. And the note hung there by the door of the smoking hall in Reedmoor, in the dim, beside a man who’d once read it out loud to thirty-four frightened people at that long table and told them what it meant.

Ice had come in. He was standing in the door, with Thea, and he was looking at it.

He’d never been in here. Winter knew that. She knew it the way she knew most things about Ice, from Hyvred, who’d told her everything about his trail, the first time, on floor 2, and never stopped. On three he’d gone round Reedmoor. He’d camped on a tussock in the reeds and never once come near the walkways, so his trail wouldn’t lead anybody here, so nobody would follow it. And then he’d gone up. He’d never seen the smoking hall, or the long table, or the nail.

He’d never seen where they stayed.

He stood in the doorway of it now and looked at his own handwriting hanging on a nail by the door, and Winter watched him understand, all at once, where it had been. While he was on four, alone, killing the Quiet in the first square, sitting every evening on the high terrace looking at a door. It had been here. On a nail. In a warm dim room that smelled of eels, with thirty-five people round a table under it, eating on a tab and training in the channels every morning up to their waists and coming back every night to this. Forty-three days. Because of eight words and a letter.

“I didn’t know where it went,” said Ice.

Deston looked at him.

“After,” said Ice. “I put it under your hand. And I went. I never knew where it went.” He was looking at the nail. “I thought you’d keep it in your pocket.”

“I did,” said Deston. “Then I didn’t. Then I did again.” He looked at the card on the nail. “It’s going back in. In the morning. It’s just—“ He stopped. He was a very honest man, Deston, and he took his time when he was finding the honest thing. “It should get a night here,” he said. “Where it did what it said.”

Ice looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded.

“Okay,” he said.

Winter turned away. She had to. She found she’d turned toward Ina, who was standing by the end of the long table with his mask pushed up, looking at the nail too. He looked at her. Neither of them said anything.

Fact, thought Winter. She’d told him by the water, the night of the aurora. The one word. He’d said okay and never asked what. And now here it was, hanging on a nail, a white card with eight words on it, and she wondered, standing in the smoking hall, if he’d always known that’s what she meant. If he’d known on the twenty-third day.

She thought he probably had. Ina knew things the way Hyvred saw lights. Without fuss. Without telling anybody.

And then there was the slate.

It was on the wall on the other side of the door. Big, the size of a small table, propped on two pegs. Too big to carry up a stair; that was why Drew had left it. He’d bought it in Reedmoor off the eel-woman’s brother on the twenty-fourth day and used it every morning for forty-two days and then, on the sixty-sixth, stood in front of it and looked at it for a long time, and left it.

It still said what it had said.

The eel-woman hadn’t wiped it. Winter didn’t know why. It must have been in her way, every day, for five hundred days; she must have walked past it a thousand times with her broom. And every line of it was still there. Drew’s square capitals. Chris’s columns of who’d done their trial and who hadn’t, all ticked. The rota for the channels. A list of the eight tanks. Somebody’s drawing of the Lanternjaw that was mostly mouth. SOMEBODY THANK DREW, in Newish’s writing, in the corner, with thirty-four ticks under it. And along the very top, above everything, in Drew’s biggest, most careful capitals, the ones he’d written on the fortieth evening when the group window came up and he’d made Gabe a promise:

THE THIRTY-FIVE (FOR NOW)

Gabe saw it at the same time Drew did.

“Huh,” said Gabe.

Drew didn’t say anything. He just walked across to it. He looked at it for a moment, the way he’d looked at it on the sixty-sixth morning, the last time. Then he licked his thumb, and reached up, and rubbed out FIVE (FOR NOW).

He did it carefully. It took a while; it had been there a long time. The eel-woman watched him from the fire with her arms folded and didn’t say anything either. When it was gone, Drew picked up the chalk off the ledge under the slate, the same stub, still there, and wrote in the gap, in the same square capitals, as carefully as he’d ever written anything.

SIX

THE THIRTY-SIX.

He stepped back. He put the chalk down on the ledge.

“Told you,” said Gabe. Not loudly. “That day. I said it should be thirty-six.”

“And I said when he came back we’d rename it.” Drew looked at the slate. “That’s a promise. I’m the general. I can do that.” He was quiet a moment. “We renamed the group on seven. In the window. This is the one I promised, though. This one.”

Gabe put his hand on Drew’s shoulder. Then he looked round at Ice, in the door, and grinned. The big one. The one that went all the way across his face.

“You’re on a slate, mate,” said Gabe. “In a swamp. You’re official.”

Ice laughed. He didn’t seem to expect to. It came out of him short and surprised, like Thea’s did now, and he put his hand over his mouth, and laughed again behind it.

And the eel-woman, at the fire, unfolded her arms.

“It’s yours,” she said. To Drew. “The slate. You didn’t say I could wipe it.” She sniffed. “Climbers always stay a while. And they always come back down. And they always want to see their slate.” She looked at Ice. “Never been short-changed by one yet.”

“I paid,” said Dennis, immediately, from the long table. “The tab. Day sixty-five. In full.”

“You did,” said the eel-woman. “And the penguin’s worked it off twice since, in my head.” She looked at Dennis. “Sit down. There’s eels.”

There were eels. There were always eels.

Winter went out onto the eel-woman’s jetty after supper, because the smoking hall was full of thirty-six people being loud and happy and the smell had got into her hair, and because Ina had gone out there a quarter of an hour before and not come back.

He was sitting on the end of the jetty with his legs over the black water and his mask on the planks beside him. Waddles was asleep in the eel basket on its hook above him. Far out across the Fen, low down over the reeds in the dark, the amber point of the drowned tower burned on. And round the jetty, a little way out over the water, a ring of small honest wisps hung in the dark, because the lantern was in the hall with the door open and they’d followed it as close as they dared.

Winter sat down beside Ina. Not close. She didn’t sit close to anybody. But not far either. About the width of his mask.

“You hate it here,” said Ina.

“I hate it here.”

“You’re smiling.”

“I’m allowed to hate things and smile. It’s in my rights.” She looked out at the tower. “He lit that. On the thirteenth night. Before any of us came up. And it’s still going.”

“Yes.”

“Five hundred and eighteen nights. Nobody’s been out to it. Nobody’s put oil in it. Nothing.” She frowned. “Lanterns don’t do that.”

“That one does,” said Ina.

They sat. The wisps hung round them, pale and small and true. The black water clucked against the stilts.

“He’s never been in there,” said Winter. She nodded back at the smoking hall. “In the hall. The whole time we were here. Forty-four days. He went round. So we wouldn’t follow him.” She was quiet. “And he never found it. The first time. He said. Two weeks in circles. They never found the hill. They never found anything.” She looked at the water. “So they never found Reedmoor either. Did they. They never had the eel-woman. Or the hall. Or the tab. Or the slate.”

Ina didn’t say anything. He was good at that.

“We found it,” said Winter. “We did. Not him. For once.” She didn’t know why that mattered so much. It did, though. It mattered so much it hurt somewhere under her ribs. “Everything else, he got there first. Every floor. Every thing. He knew where it was and what it did and how to kill it and how to stand. And this—“ She waved at the stilts, the walkways, the smoking hall full of noise behind them. “This was ours. We found it ourselves. And it was where we stayed, because of him. And he’d never even seen it.”

“He’s seen it now,” said Ina.

“He’s seen it now,” said Winter.

She’d spent her whole life hating being the last to know. She’d said that once, on floor 2, to Chris, sharing notes about Deston’s pocket. It’s the worst bug there is. And she’d spent the whole of the last year and a half on the other side of a door, knowing there was a fact, knowing she’d never be told what, and hating it, and keeping the deal anyway.

And tonight, for the first time, she’d watched him be the last to know something. A nail. A slate. A hall. Where they’d been, all that time. And he hadn’t hated it. He’d stood in the door and looked, and laughed, and put his hand over his mouth.

Then we’ll show you, she thought. Tomorrow. All of it. The channels where Azac learned to count. The forge where Gabe made your shield. The tussock where Calvin did his trial. The bit of walkway where Rachael hit Dennis with a fish.

She’d tell Chris. Chris would make a list.

“Ina,” she said.

“Mm.”

“When you said I was on the other side of the door. In the snow, on fourteen.” She didn’t look at him. “You said that was harder.”

“It is.”

“I don’t think I mind it any more,” said Winter. “Tonight.”

Ina looked at her. Then he looked back out at the tower, burning amber, far off over the reeds. He picked up his mask and turned it over in his hands, once, and put it down again.

“Good,” he said.

They sat on the end of the jetty until Waddles woke up in his basket and fell out of it into the water, and came up with an eel, and the whole smoking hall heard Chi laugh.

They showed him.

All of it, the next day, the thirty-five of them. Winter told Chris on the walkway at breakfast and Chris made a list, and Drew put it on the slate under THE THIRTY-SIX, and nobody asked Ice if he wanted to see it, because Winter had said not to ask. Just show him. He doesn’t do asking. He does following. So go in front.

So they went in front. For once.

Azac took him to the long channel where the eight tanks had stood up to their waists every morning learning to be a line, and showed him the post where he’d stood for his trial and not counted. Calvin took him to the tussock where he’d done his, at night, through the wisps, talking the whole way, look at my back, and brought everybody home. Gabe took him to the little forge at the end of the village, the one the eel-woman’s brother kept, and stood him in front of the anvil, and said, “Here. This is where I made it. Your shield. Every night for a month. I didn’t know who it was for,” and then, “That’s a lie. I knew,” and Ice put his hand on the anvil and didn’t take it off for a long time. Rachael showed him the bit of walkway where she’d hit Dennis with a fish. Dennis disputed the details. Hana showed him the reed house where she’d said she was resting, resting is a skill. Maigo showed him the drowned tree where he’d stood every morning in the warm, getting his leaves back. Niko showed him the step where he’d sat reading, at night, before he knew what he was. Higu showed him her map of three, still pinned up inside the smoking hall’s back door, and wrong in four places.

And Thea showed him the walkway at the edge of the village where she’d sat every evening on three with the lantern, the first week after they found it, looking out at the drowned tower. Winter didn’t hear what they said. She didn’t try.

He followed them all day. Everywhere. He didn’t say much. He looked at everything the way you look at a photograph somebody has kept of you, from a day you didn’t know anybody was taking pictures.

And in the evening, at the long table, under the slate, with the eel-woman at the fire and the note on its nail by the door, he cooked for them. Eel and onion. The way he’d cooked it on a tussock in the reeds on the twelfth night, alone, a long way from here, before any of them had come up. He’d never cooked it for anybody.

Winter ate it. It was very good. She’d never tell him.

They left on the morning of the third day.

Deston took the note off the nail before anybody else was up. Winter saw him do it; she was on the jetty, watching the mist on the channels. He took it down and put it in his left shirt pocket and buttoned the pocket, and stood with his hand flat over it for a moment, by the door. Then he went to wake Calvin.

Drew left the slate. THE THIRTY-SIX. Nobody wiped it. The eel-woman watched them go from the walkway with her broom, and Waddles stood on the end of her jetty with a last eel in his beak and looked at the basket on its hook for a long time. Then he looked at Chi. Then he put the eel down at the eel-woman’s feet, very carefully, and waddled down the walkway after Chi and got into her punt without being asked, and sat in the bottom of it facing backwards all the way to the first bend, watching the jetty. Chi cried and said it was the smoke.

“She’ll still be here,” said Higu. “When we come back up.”

“You don’t know that,” said Chi.

“On three it keeps,” said Higu. “Things here go on. I’ve watched.”

The punts took them south through the reeds, a long day, to the stair down to two. The wisps didn’t follow in the daylight. But that evening, just before the stair, when the sun slid down its rail and the sky went purple, Winter looked back.

Far behind them, out in the middle of the Fen, the drowned tower was burning. Amber. Low down. Steady.

And round it, small and pale in the dark, for the first time any of them had ever seen, was a ring of wisps. Dozens of them. Hanging round the tower’s lantern a little way off, the way they’d hung round Thea’s. Not lying. Not luring. Just there. Keeping it company.

Ice saw it too. He stopped on the stair-stone with his hand in Thea’s and looked back at it for a long time.

“Seventy-six,” he said. To Thea. Only to her.

Winter heard it anyway. She was standing right there.

She didn’t put it on a slate.
