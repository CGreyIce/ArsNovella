Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 3: Party of One

Chapter 109

Carry

5,426 words · 24 min read

Rachael didn’t cry. It wasn’t a rule. It was just a thing that didn’t happen, the way some people never get colds.

On Earth she’d been the one in the group who said the thing nobody else would say, flatly, in about four words, and then got on with it. That’s a bad idea. Worse. You bought what? People thought it meant she didn’t feel things. She’d let them. It was easier than the alternative, which was feeling things in front of people.

And then a white room, and a staff in her hand, and a light that said Cleric. And on the fourth day, on floor 1, her first heal. Calvin’s forearm, broken in two places by a ram with a scarred nose. She’d put her hands on it and felt what the bone was trying to do, and agreed with it, and it had knitted under her palms.

And then, about an hour later, sitting on her own on a rock above the valley, her own left forearm had broken. In two places. Exactly where his had.

Not really. There was nothing wrong with it. She’d looked. But it had felt broken. For about twenty minutes. Every splinter of it. The echo. The price. Whatever she closed in somebody else, she felt again in herself, afterwards, in the same place, all of it, and then it went.

She’d never told anybody. Not until floor 5, and only then because Declan was doing it to himself every day, and somebody had to say it’s not fine. It was hers to carry. You didn’t put it on the people you were healing. That was the whole point of healing them.

She’d carried every wound she’d closed for two hundred and fifty-seven days. Azac’s ribs, twice. Calvin’s arm. Jared’s arm, at the big pool, a week of it. And one night on floor 9, on a heap of green in the Down, with her hands on the leg of somebody who’d held a light up for eleven hours, she’d got the echo of the whole night at once, and sat down, and not been able to stand for an hour.

You don’t get another one this floor, she’d told him.

She hadn’t cried then either.

The cold had been different from the start.

The first night. The fifth wall. A boy called Tamm on his back in the snow, and Malcolm’s hands on him, and Malcolm’s face. She hadn’t been there; she’d heard. And then the next morning she’d gone down to the house for the wounded with Malcolm, and a big black-bearded soldier called Orm had held out his right hand with three fingertips gone white and waxy like candle-ends, and she’d put her hands round them.

And felt nothing.

Not nothing. She’d felt what the flesh was doing. It was doing what Malcolm had said. Trying to stop. Quietly. The way a lamp goes out. And there was nothing for her to agree with except that, and she wasn’t going to agree with that.

But when she took her hands away, about an hour later, her own fingertips had gone cold.

Not the echo. Not the way an echo came, afterwards, all at once, sharp, the shape of the wound. This was different. A slow cold. In the ends of her own fingers, where they’d touched his. As if a little of it had come off on her, the way flour comes off on your hands.

It lasted about an hour. Then it went.

She’d sat on the step of the house for the wounded in the cold sun and looked at her hands for a long time after it went.

Healing’s agreeing, she’d told Malcolm, on floor 4. Find what it’s trying to do anyway and agree with it. She’d been doing it that way for two hundred and fifty days. And there was nothing in the cold to agree with. It didn’t want anything. It wasn’t trying to do anything. It was just there, in the flesh, like a stone in a shoe.

But it had come off on her. A little.

And she’d thought, very slowly, sitting on the step: what if I didn’t agree with it. What if I just took it.

She tried on the third day. A soldier with a frostbitten toe, a young woman who’d caught a Rimed’s gauntlet across her boot on the wall. She put her hands round the white toe and didn’t try to heal it. She didn’t look for anything to agree with. She just reached. Into the cold. The way you’d reach into a bowl of water to take something out of the bottom.

And pulled.

It came. Not all of it. A thread of it. Cold, so cold it burned, going out of the toe into her fingers, up into her hand. She held on. She pulled more. The white on the toe went grey, then pink, very faintly, at the edge. And her own fingers went white. To the first knuckle. And hurt. Not like an echo. Like holding a handful of snow for an hour, all at once.

She let go.

The soldier’s toe was pink at the edge. Not all of it. A little. And Rachael’s fingertips were white, and it took two hours to wear off, and she sat on the step with her hands in her armpits in the cold and her teeth together so they wouldn’t chatter.

The toe kept. That was the thing. Ingrith came and looked at it in the evening and stood there for a long time. It had been going to come off. Now half of it was pink, and the half that was pink was staying pink.

What did you do, said Ingrith.

I don’t know yet, said Rachael.

She did it every day after that. A little. A finger at a time. She didn’t tell anybody. She found out how much she could take before it stopped wearing off.

Not much.

Her own body could throw off a little. A fingertip’s worth. Two. If she took more than that, it didn’t wear off. It stayed. And on the eighth day she took too much, from the hand of an old man who’d been on the wall forty years and had the Rimed’s cold in three fingers, and when she let go her own hand was white to the wrist. And it didn’t wear off.

It climbed.

She sat on the step of the house for the wounded and watched it climb. Slowly. Up past the wrist bone, into the forearm, a finger’s width an hour. And she knew what it was going to do, because she’d seen it on every bed in the long room behind her. Past the elbow by midnight. The shoulder by morning.

She didn’t tell anybody. She didn’t know what to tell them. She went to the Breath, at dusk, and stood as close to the crack as she could get, with her white hand held out to it, in the roar and the heat. And it did nothing. Ingrith had said. Not with warmth. Not with anything. The cold that came out of the Rimed didn’t go back where it came from. It just stayed.

She came back up the stair with her hand in her coat and her face very calm and went to the barracks, because she didn’t know where else to go.

And Gabe was there. On his bunk. Asleep, after a night on the wall, with his arm through the strap of the new shield, the one he’d made at the Breath, the one Declan had cried about on the barracks step. Hearthwall. It was lying across his chest, and even from the door she could feel it. Warm. Like a stove in a cold room.

It does not go cold.

She’d heard the window read out, the morning he made it. Calvin had told everybody. She hadn’t thought anything about it. She thought about it now.

She went across the room. She stood over Gabe’s bunk. He didn’t wake up. She looked at the boss of the shield. At the three flames worked up out of the metal, curling round each other, glowing faintly from inside, dark red.

She put her white hand on it.

It went.

All at once. She felt it go. Out of her hand, out of her wrist, out of her forearm, all of it, pouring out of her into the iron like water out of a tipped cup. Into the shield. And the three flames on the boss lit up. Bright. Red, then orange, then almost gold, for a second, like a fire somebody had thrown a log on. And then settled. Back to the dull red glow. And the shield was still warm.

It hadn’t gone cold. It had taken all of it, and not gone cold.

She took her hand off. It was pink. All of it. Fingertips to wrist. Her own colour.

Gabe was awake. He was lying on his back looking up at her, with the shield on his chest and her handprint melting off the boss in a little ring of frost that was already turning to steam.

“What,” said Gabe, “are you doing.”

“Nothing,” said Rachael. “Go back to sleep.”

“You just put your hand on my shield and it went bright.”

“Mind your own business.”

Gabe looked at the shield. Then at her hand. Then at her face. He’d known her for years; he knew what her face did when she was lying.

“Rach,” he said. “That was my business. It’s my shield.”

She stood there by his bunk for a long time.

Then she sat down on the end of it, and told him. Quietly. All of it. The toe, and the fingers, and the old man, and the hand going white to the wrist and climbing, and the Breath doing nothing. Gabe listened without saying anything. When she’d finished he looked down at the shield on his chest.

“It takes it,” he said.

“It takes it.”

“It doesn’t go cold.”

“No.”

Gabe was quiet for a moment.

“Then use it,” he said. “Whenever you want. I’ll be wherever you need me to be.” He paused. “You’re not doing this on your own any more, Rach.”

“I wasn’t doing it on my own.”

“You were literally doing it on your own. You didn’t tell anybody. You nearly lost your arm.”

“I didn’t nearly—“

“Rach.”

She shut her mouth.

“Don’t tell anybody,” she said, after a while. “Not yet. Not till I know how much I can do. I don’t want them—“ She stopped. “I don’t want Dennis fussing.”

“Dennis is going to fuss whatever you do,” said Gabe. “It’s his whole personality.” He lay back down. “Okay. Not yet. But I’m coming down to the house tomorrow with this. And the next day. And you’re going to take it a finger at a time and put it in here, and you’re not going to take more than you can carry. Deal?”

She looked at him.

“Deal,” said Rachael.

They did it for five days. Nobody else knew. Gabe came down to the house for the wounded every afternoon after he’d slept and sat on a stool in the long room with Hearthwall on his knees, and Rachael went from bed to bed, and took the cold out of people a finger at a time, a toe at a time, and when her own fingers went white she came back to the stool and put them on the three flames. And they went bright. And then settled.

Nine people kept fingers they’d have lost. One kept a foot.

Ingrith watched them do it for the first afternoon without saying a single word. On the second afternoon she cried, quietly, in the corner, with her small gentle hands over her face, and Rachael pretended not to see.

It was slow. It was always going to be slow. A finger’s worth at a time, because that was all Rachael could carry from the bed to the stool before it started to climb. More than that, and it would be in her wrist before she got there. More than that, and—

She didn’t think about more than that.

And then on the two hundred and fifty-seventh night the Rimed came down the cliffs, and she was kneeling in the doorway of the house with her hands on a hold-folk boy who’d been brought down from the fourth level with his ankle broken, and something came up behind her in the snow.

She’d known it was there. She’d felt it. The cold, on the back of her neck. And she hadn’t taken her hands off the boy, because you didn’t. You didn’t take your hands off them. That was the whole job.

And then she’d heard Dennis. Forty of him.

Dennis’s arm was in her lap.

She was kneeling in the snow in the doorway with it across her knees. White. Wrist to elbow. All the way round. Hard and waxy, like candle wax, and cold, so cold it hurt her through her trousers. And already, at the top, at the elbow, the white was going on. A finger’s width past the joint. Climbing.

Past the elbow by midnight. The shoulder by morning.

She’d tried to heal it. She’d known she would. She’d put her hands on it and looked for something to agree with and there’d been nothing, and her fingers had gone white anyway, because a little of it came off on her whether she wanted it or not.

The window had come up while she was looking at her fingers.

She’d read it. She’d scrolled down without thinking.

She looked at it now, in the snow, with Dennis’s arm in her lap and the bells still going overhead and Declan sitting in the street with his face in his hands and Deston’s gold thread warm in her chest.

Carry what you cannot heal.

All of it. Not a finger. Not a toe. A whole forearm. Wrist to elbow and climbing. More cold than she’d ever taken in five days put together.

She knew exactly what it would do. She’d felt a hand’s worth climb her own arm on the eighth day, a finger’s width an hour. A forearm’s worth wouldn’t climb a finger’s width an hour. It would run.

Do not put it down until it is safe.

And the only safe place on the whole floor was a shield, on the arm of a man, who’d been on the fifth wall when the bells went.

She looked up. Up the street. Past the house opposite, past the cliff, up the steep stairs of the third level into the snow, and the fourth level above that, where hold-folk were still fighting Rimed in the streets, and the long bare climb past the last houses to the fifth wall. At the top of everything. In the dark. Where the deep bell was ringing too.

It was a long way.

“Gabe,” she said. “Where’s Gabe?”

“On the fifth,” said Azac. “In the line. The Rimed are at the fifth as well. They came both ways.” He looked at her. “Declan ran. Gabe stayed. Somebody had to cover the gap.”

Of course he did, thought Rachael. And then: of course Declan ran.

“Okay,” she said.

She took the hard one.

She didn’t tell anybody what it was. There wasn’t time. She said “Deston, hold his arm up,” and Deston did, and she put both her hands round Dennis’s forearm. Flat. One at the wrist, one at the elbow. On the white.

And she didn’t heal.

She reached. Into the cold. All of it. The way you’d reach into a bucket of ice water to the shoulder. And pulled.

It came.

It came like a river. Not a thread. Not a finger’s worth. All of it at once, out of Dennis’s arm, out of the white, through her palms and into her, roaring, so cold it wasn’t cold, so cold it was a white silence going up her arms. Both arms. Wrists. Forearms. Elbows. She felt it go and she didn’t stop pulling. She pulled until there was nothing left to pull. Until the arm under her hands was soft. And warm. And pink. Dennis’s own colour. Dennis’s own arm, with the fingers moving.

“Rach—“ Dennis, from somewhere a long way off. “Rach, my hand, I can feel my—“

She stood up.

She didn’t know how. Her arms were white to the shoulder. Both of them. She could see it, in the light from the doorway, under her sleeves where they’d fallen back. White and waxy and hard, and at the top, at her collarbone, in the open neck of her coat, she could see it going on. Over the bone. Toward the middle of her chest.

Climbing.

“Rachael,” said Deston. His voice had gone. “What did you—“

“Calvin,” said Rachael.

Calvin was there. He was always there. He’d been there since floor 1, running ahead with his mouth going, and she’d had her fist in his collar for half of it, telling him to shut up.

“Gabe,” said Rachael. Her voice was very strange. Her teeth had started to chatter and she made them stop. “On the fifth wall. I need to get to Gabe. Before it gets—“ She looked down at her own chest. “Before.”

Calvin looked at her arms. At the white going over her collarbone.

He didn’t say oh my days. He didn’t say anything at all. His face went completely still, the way it never did, and he turned round.

“Look at my back,” said Calvin.

And ran.

She’d never run in Calvin’s wind before.

She’d heard about it. Everybody had. Slipstream, on floor 3, in the Fen, the night of his trial, go first, everyone behind you goes faster, nobody gets left. She’d been behind him for two hundred and fifty days, but she’d always had her fist in his collar, stopping him, not following him. She’d never once just let him go and gone after.

She went after.

It was like falling. Like running downhill, except it was uphill, up the steep stair from the third level, three at a time, in the snow. The air went fast behind Calvin, and she went with it, lighter than she’d ever been, her feet barely touching. Up. Past people. Past hold-folk coming down with spears. Past a house with a Rimed on its roof and Azac going under it, behind her, with his shield up, and his voice. And so she went up the stair, and the line held. She felt it land. She felt Deston’s gold thread in her chest, pulling, holding, warm. It didn’t touch the cold. It was just there.

“Look at my back,” said Calvin, ahead, over and over. “Look at my back, Rach, look at my back, just my back, I’m right here—“

She looked at his back.

The fourth level. The long halls. Fighting in the street, hold-folk and Rimed, snow and ice and shouting. Calvin went through it like a knife through cloth, round the edge, along a wall, under a cart, and she went after, in his wind, and nothing touched her. Up. Past the forges. Past the stables.

The cold went over her collarbone.

She felt it. She didn’t look. She didn’t need to. She felt it the way you feel the tide coming in round your ankles. Over the bone, and down, onto her chest. A hand’s width. Toward the middle. Toward the place under her breastbone where her heart was going so hard she could hear it.

Her legs started to go.

She didn’t stop. She made them not stop. She’d been making her body do things for two hundred and fifty-seven days that it didn’t want to: carry other people’s broken bones, other people’s cuts, a whole night of somebody else’s leg on a heap of green in the dark. She’d never once let it stop because it hurt. She wasn’t going to start on a stair.

The last houses. The bare rock. The long climb to the fifth wall in the snow, steep, with the deep bell ringing at the top of it and the sound of the fight on the parapet coming down. Calvin ahead of her, talking. Look at my back. The wind behind him. Her feet.

The white was two fingers from the middle of her chest.

She could feel her heart. She could feel the cold coming toward it. Slowly. Inevitably. The way the frost goes across a window.

She couldn’t feel her arms at all.

“GABE,” shouted Calvin, ahead of her. At the top of the stair. On the fifth wall. Into the fight. At the top of his voice, the way he’d shouted on the jetty on floor 8, and into a clock room on floor 7. “GABE. SHIELD. NOW. GABE—“

She came up the last steps onto the fifth wall and her legs went.

She went down on her knees. On the stones. Behind the line, in the snow. She couldn’t feel her arms. She couldn’t feel her chest. She could feel her heart. Banging. Very loud. And the cold, a finger’s width from it.

Do not put it down until it is safe.

She hadn’t put it down. She was still carrying it. She just couldn’t stand up.

Gabe came out of the line.

She saw him do it. Through the snow, in John’s white light. In the middle of the third company, shield up, overlapping the man on his left, holding. And he heard Calvin. And looked round. And saw her. On her knees. Behind the line. With her arms white to the shoulder.

He didn’t think. She saw him not think.

He stepped out of the line. Just stepped back out of it, with the gap he left already closing, the hold-folk on either side leaning in, covering it, Azac’s voice from somewhere telling them they would. And he came across the parapet at a run, through the snow, with the shield.

He went down on his knees in front of her.

He didn’t say anything. He didn’t ask. He took the shield off his arm and held it out. In front of her. With the boss toward her. The three flames, curling round each other, glowing dark red in the snow.

She couldn’t lift her arms.

She tried. They didn’t move. They were white to the shoulder, hard and cold and somebody else’s, and they hung at her sides and didn’t do anything.

“Rach,” said Gabe.

She leaned forward.

She didn’t have anything else. She leaned forward on her knees in the snow on the top of the fifth wall, and put her chest against the shield. Against the boss. The three flames. Over her heart. Where the cold was.

And let go.

It went out of her like a held breath.

All of it. All at once. Out of her chest, out of her shoulders, out of both arms, out of every finger, pouring out of her into the iron. Into the fire on the boss. She felt it go. A river going back where it came from. And the three flames under her breastbone lit up so bright that she saw them through her own coat. Red. Orange. Gold. White. Brighter than the Breath. Brighter than John’s lights. For one long moment the shield in Gabe’s hands burned like a star on the top of the fifth wall in the snow, and the hold-folk in the line turned their heads, all of them, and looked.

And then it settled.

Back to red. Back to the dull deep glow. Warm.

It hadn’t gone cold.

She was on her knees with her forehead on the rim of Gabe’s shield and her arms round it. She didn’t know when she’d put her arms round it. She could feel them. Her arms. Both of them. Warm. Her own. She could feel her fingers on the iron. Pink. She could feel her heart, slowing, against the boss.

The windows came up. She saw them through her own eyelashes.

She read them. She didn’t take them in. She knelt there with her arms round Gabe’s shield in the snow.

And she started to cry.

She didn’t know she was going to. It didn’t come the way she’d thought it would, if it ever came, which she’d stopped thinking about years ago. It didn’t come with a reason. It just came. Up out of the middle of her, out of the place under her breastbone where the cold had been a finger’s width away, all at once, the way the cold had come out of Dennis’s arm.

She cried with her forehead on the rim of the shield.

She cried for Tamm, who she’d never met, whose mother had stood on the step of the house for the wounded and asked if he was afraid. For Orm’s three fingertips, and the old man’s hand, and the forty years he’d stood on this wall. For every echo she’d ever carried, every broken bone and split skin and bitten calf and burned palm, two hundred and fifty-seven days of other people’s wounds felt again in her own body afterwards, on her own, on rocks above valleys and the ends of jetties and the steps of houses, and never once said, because you didn’t put it on the people you were healing.

For Dennis. Running down a street at her, forty of him, shouting her name.

She’d been carrying it so long she’d forgotten she was carrying it. And she’d just put it all down at once, on the top of a wall, in a shield that didn’t go cold, and there was nothing left in her to hold it up with.

Gabe didn’t say anything. He knelt in the snow and held the shield steady for her to cry on, and didn’t move.

Somebody sat down beside her. On the stones. On her left.

Calvin. Not talking. For once. For the first time she could remember, in two hundred and fifty-seven days, or before. Calvin sitting beside her in the snow on the fifth wall, with his knees up and his shoulder very nearly against hers, and not saying a single word.

She didn’t tell him to shut up.

Dennis came up an hour later, with Deston.

She heard them on the stair. Deston’s voice, low. Dennis’s, not low at all. I’m fine, Dest. I’m completely fine. Look. Pink. It’s moving. All the fingers. Look—

She’d stopped crying by then. Mostly. She was sitting with her back against the inside of the parapet behind the line, with Gabe’s shield across her knees because Gabe had put it there and gone back to the wall with Azac’s spare, and Calvin still beside her, still not talking. The Rimed had stopped coming. The snow had stopped. The fight on the fourth level was over; they’d heard the bell stop an hour ago.

Dennis came along the parapet and stopped in front of her.

He was holding his left arm up in front of him. Wiggling the fingers. Pink. All of them. His face was doing about four things at once.

“Look,” he said. “Look. Rach. It’s—you—“ He stopped. “What did you do?”

“Carried it,” said Rachael.

“Carried it where?”

She put her hand on the boss of the shield across her knees. The three flames. Warm.

Dennis looked at it. Then at her. At her face, which she knew was a mess. Which she’d never in her life let anybody see like this.

He didn’t say anything about it. That was the thing about Dennis. He talked more than anybody she’d ever met except Calvin, and he’d fuss about a blanket for a week, and he never, ever said anything about the things that mattered.

He sat down. On her right. On the stones. And held out his hand.

Not to hug her. He knew better. He’d known her for years and never tried. He held his left hand out on the snow between them, palm up. The one she’d carried. Pink.

The way Deston had held his out on the jetty on floor 8, for Declan.

She looked at it for a long time.

Then she put her hand in it.

Dennis’s fingers closed. Warm. Moving. Holding on.

“Rachael doesn’t cry,” said Rachael. Her voice was wrecked.

“Rachael’s allowed,” said Dennis.

She started again.

He didn’t say anything else. Neither did Calvin. Deston stood a little way off, with his gold thread warm in all four of them, and didn’t come closer. And they sat there, the three of them, against the parapet on the top of the fifth wall, with a shield across her knees that didn’t go cold, and waited for it to be morning.

Declan came up at dawn.

She saw him come up the stair. Slowly. Alone. With the lamp shield on his arm and snow on his hair, and his face grey, the way it had been in the street on the third level, sitting with his hands over it. He stopped at the top of the stair and looked along the parapet at them. At her, and Dennis, and Calvin, and their hands.

He didn’t come closer. He stood there.

She looked at him. He looked back. And his face did something she’d never seen it do, in all the years she’d known him. Not on Earth. Not on any floor. Not on the jetty, not in the bakery, not on the heap of green in the Down with her hands on his leg.

It looked like somebody who’d been holding something up for a very long time, and had just watched somebody else put it down.

He didn’t say anything. He nodded. Once. At her. And at Dennis’s hand in hers.

Then he turned round and went to stand in the line. On Gabe’s left. Where the warm shield would be when Gabe got it back.

Rachael watched him go.

She didn’t understand it. She thought about it for a long time, sitting there with Dennis’s hand in hers, and didn’t understand it. And then she put it away. With the echo. With the cold. With all the other things she’d carried and was going to have to learn how to put down.

You tell me, she’d said to him, on floor 8, on a lake ledge, at midnight. And I do it. And I get the echo.

He’d never told her anything. She’d never asked.

She thought, sitting there in the dawn, that maybe one day she would.
