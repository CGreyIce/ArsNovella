Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 5: Everyone Comes Back Down

Chapter 162

On the One

6,473 words · 28 min read

Newish had been counting since the white room, and nobody had ever once asked her to stop.

Not out loud. Not all the time. Just underneath. The way some people hum. Steps on a stair. Spoons going round a bowl at breakfast. Calvin’s boots on the floor of the Copper Spoon, which had a rhythm all of their own and the rhythm was wrong. She’d counted the Bellwether’s charges on floor 1, and the drips off the roof of the trapper’s hall on floor 2, and the beats of the Cataract’s tail, until the tower took the count off her and gave it to the thing in the water and she’d had to learn to throw it instead. One name. One beat. Calvin. And Calvin went.

She’d been counting the mowers for eight days.

You couldn’t not. That was the thing about the mowers. They were made of counting. Twelve of them in a line across a field, staggered like steps, and every one of them swinging the same long flat stroke through the base of the wheat at the same moment, right to left, swish, and the same small step forward after, step, and the whole line moving up the field together like one long animal breathing. It was the most beautiful thing she’d ever seen. She’d have liked to watch it all day. She’d have liked to stand at the end of the field with her arms folded and just count it.

Instead she was binding, because Annet Sallow had looked at her on the first evening and said you, and pointed, and that had been that.

On the ninth day, the line broke.

It was the heat. And the old men. And the Grey.

The Grey had taken two more fields in the night, the eighth night and the ninth: Mill Piece by the river, and the long narrow strip the gleaners called Tailend that went up beside it. Both of them cut, thank God, both of them stooked. But the stooks had still been standing in them, waiting to be carted, and in the morning they were grey. Rows and rows of little grey tents on grey stubble, perfectly still in the breeze, that fell in on themselves like dust when you touched them.

Nobody had said anything. Annet had stood at the gate of Mill Piece with her stick and looked at it, and cut a notch, and not crossed it, and turned round and walked up the lane. And everybody had followed her up the lane. Faster than yesterday.

So they were faster. That was the thing. Everybody was faster now, and everybody was tired, and it was hot. The hottest day yet. The kind of hot where the air over the stubble shook and the sky went white at the edges and the swifts stopped screaming because they couldn’t be bothered. Bramble Piece, they were cutting. A big field, on the slope above the village, south-facing, so the sun came down on it from dawn and didn’t let go.

Newish was three rows behind the mowers, binding, with sweat running into her eyes, when she heard it go wrong.

Not saw. Heard.

The swish. It had been twelve, all together, all morning. One sound. And then, about an hour after noon, it was eleven and one. One of the old men at the end, the one with the white beard down to his belt, was a fraction behind. His stroke came after the others’. Swish-swish. And his step came after theirs. And the man next to him heard it, the way you’d hear somebody singing flat next to you in a choir, and tried to wait for him, and then he was behind too.

Swish-swish-swish.

She stopped binding. She stood up with a half-tied sheaf in her arms and watched it come apart.

It went down the line like a crack going across ice. One man behind, then two, then the line wasn’t a line any more. It was twelve men swinging twelve blades at twelve different times, and stepping at twelve different times, and the stagger went out of it, and suddenly the man with the white beard was a whole stride behind the man next to him and the blade of the man next to him was coming round much too close to his legs.

“Hold!” somebody shouted.

They held. Twelve blades stopped at the bottom of their swings. Twelve men stood in the wheat breathing like horses.

Annet came across the field. Fast. With her stick. She didn’t shout. She looked at the line. At the white-bearded man, who was leaning on his scythe with his eyes shut. At the gap.

“Ezra,” she said.

“I’m all right.”

“You’re not all right. You’re seventy-one and it’s the hottest day in twelve years.” She looked at the line. “Change. Ned, take his blade. Ezra, bind.”

“I can bind,” said Ezra.

“I know you can bind. Bind.”

He went back to the binders. A younger man came up out of the binders and took his scythe. And the line started again. Swish. Step.

It was wrong.

Newish could hear it. She didn’t think anybody else could. The gleaners couldn’t, because they’d been hearing this line for fifty years and they heard what it should sound like. The passers couldn’t, because they were binding and carrying and sweating and they weren’t listening to anything except their own breath. But it was wrong. The new man was good, but he was young, and he swung faster than the old man had, and the line had learned the old man’s speed over forty years, and now it didn’t know which one to follow. They were together. Just. But they were trying to be together, all twelve of them, every stroke, and you could hear the trying. It made a sound like a cart with one wheel not quite round.

And it was slower. They were going slower than this morning. Because they were trying.

She looked at the field. At how much of it was left. Then she looked down the hill. Past the village. Past the river. To the bottom of the valley, where the gold stopped, and two fields lay grey and still and waiting in the heat.

Upbeat, said something in the back of her head. Her own voice. Put a beat under them. Everyone’s faster on the one.

She’d never done it for anybody who wasn’t one of the thirty-six.

“Annet,” she said.

Annet looked round.

Newish had no idea what she was going to say. She said it anyway. “Can I count them?”

Annet looked at her for a long moment. The way the gleaners looked at any question that seemed to them so obvious it hardly needed asking, and then, sometimes, behind that, the way they looked at one that had never been asked in a thousand years.

“Count them,” said Annet.

“The line. The mowers. Can I count it. Out loud.” Newish shifted the sheaf in her arms. “They’re trying to keep together and it’s making them slow. If somebody says when, they don’t have to try. They just go when I say.”

Annet didn’t say anything for a while.

“In my mother’s time,” she said at last, “there was a man called Abel Sowerby who used to sing for the line. At the end of the field. A low song. So they’d swing on it.” She looked at the mowers. “When he died nobody else could do it. That was forty years ago.” She looked back at Newish. “What would you sing?”

“Nothing,” said Newish. “I can’t sing. Ask anybody. I’d count.”

“Count, then,” said Annet. “And if it goes wrong, you stop.”

She put the sheaf down.

She walked up the field, past the binders, past the stooks, up through the stubble to the end of the line, where the mowers had stopped at the end of their row and were turning to go back down. She didn’t stand in front of them. She didn’t know where you stood. She stood at the end, a little apart, where she could see all twelve of them and they could all see her if they turned their heads.

Twelve old men and young men in white shirts with their sleeves rolled, sweating, leaning on their blades, looking at a passer girl with a staff on her back and straw in her hair and a face as red as a beetroot.

“Hi,” said Newish. “Sorry. I’m going to count. You go on the one. That’s all. You don’t have to listen to each other. Just me.”

Nobody said anything.

“It’s a thing I do,” said Newish. “It works. Probably. Ask Calvin.”

“Who’s Calvin?” said the young man who’d taken Ezra’s blade.

“I’m Calvin!” shouted Calvin, from three fields away.

The young man looked across the hedges at Calvin for a moment. Then back at Newish. His mouth did something at one corner.

“Go on, then,” he said.

She breathed in.

She didn’t count one, two, three, four. She’d tried that on floor 8, at the Cataract, and the tower had taken it and given it to the thing in the water, and it had nearly killed Jared. She’d learned since then. You didn’t give it the whole count. You didn’t give it the pattern. You gave it the one. Just the one. And the one went where you put it.

She watched the line. She watched the shoulders, the hips, the way the weight went back before the stroke. She found the place in the swing where the weight went back. And she put the beat there.

“One,” said Newish.

And something went out of her. Upbeat. She felt it go. It always felt like the moment in a song when the bass comes in: a floor under everything, suddenly, solid, that you could stand on.

Twelve blades went through the wheat together.

Not trying together. Together. One sound. Swish. Twelve men stepped.

“One.”

Swish.

“One.”

Swish.

She didn’t count the other three. She didn’t need to. She gave them the one, every time, at the bottom of the backswing, and they went on it, all twelve, and in between the ones there was just the field, and the heat, and the line moving up through the gold like one long animal breathing. Faster. Not much faster. A bit. The trying had gone out of it. They weren’t listening to each other any more. They were listening to her. And so they were together without having to be.

The young man at the end, Ned, who’d taken Ezra’s blade, laughed. Out loud. In the middle of the stroke. And didn’t miss the one.

At the end of the row they stopped and turned and looked at her.

Annet was standing at the bottom of the field with her stick. She didn’t say anything. She lifted the stick, an inch, the way Gaffer Tobin did. And put it down again.

“Again,” said Annet Sallow.

They called her the count.

Not Newish. Not the passer girl. The count. By the end of the day the whole of Haverley was doing it. The binders said it to each other at the bottom of the field: the count’s on Bramble, is the count coming to Long Close, we’ll want the count tomorrow on the far side. Old Ezra, binding, sitting on the stubble at the end of the afternoon with a sheaf in his lap, said to her as she went past, without looking up, “Abel Sowerby’d have liked you.” Which she thought was probably a compliment.

She counted Bramble Piece till the bell. She counted Long Close the next day, and Thistle Holme the day after, and the far side of the river the day after that. Her voice went. It went on the second day, the way it had gone at the Cataract, into a croak, and Rachael took one look at her at supper and put a hand on her throat and said stop talking, and she stopped talking, for an evening, which everybody said afterwards was the most remarkable thing that happened on floor 14.

The next morning the line was waiting for her at the gate.

They’d started without her before. They’d been mowing together for fifty years before she came out of a barn. But they were waiting at the gate of the far field in the grey of the morning with their blades on their shoulders, all twelve, and when she came up the lane with her staff, the young one, Ned, stood aside from the gate and held it open for her like she was the Reeve.

She went through it with her face burning.

So we’re special now, she thought. Great. Brilliant. Rude.

She counted.

They were faster. The whole of Haverley was faster. Not only because of her. Because of everybody. The thirty-six had been on the Acre for two weeks now, and they knew how to do it, not like gleaners, but well enough. Calvin’s bands held every time. Thea had stopped inventing new ways and started inventing faster ones. Salty and Gabe and Azac and Hako carried, and now Jared and Chris and Drew pitched, too, up onto the carts, when the carts came.

That was the next thing. The carts.

You didn’t just cut the wheat and stook it and leave it. Newish hadn’t known that. She’d thought stooks were the end. They weren’t. The stooks stood in the field for a few days, to dry, and then the carts came: great high wooden wagons, painted blue on the sides, gone grey with age, pulled by enormous slow brown horses with feathery feet. And everybody who could hold a fork stood in the stubble and pitched the sheaves up onto the cart, one at a time, over their heads, to somebody standing on the load who caught them and laid them, heads in, butts out, row on row, until the load was as high as a house and swaying. And then the cart went down the lane to the village, slowly, to the barns and the ricks, and came back empty, and you did it again.

And the Grey took whatever was still in the field.

Cut or not. Stooked or not. On the twelfth night it took Long Close, where the stooks had been standing two days, and in the morning there were grey tents on grey stubble from hedge to hedge. Annet stood at the gate and cut a notch and didn’t cross it.

“It’s not enough to cut it,” she said. To all of them. To the gleaners, standing in the lane. “It’s got to be in. Under a roof. Off the field.” She looked at the grey. “It doesn’t go under roofs. That’s the one thing. Cobb’s looked. It’s never once been under a roof.”

So they carted. All day and every day, as well as the cutting, as well as the binding. Whoever wasn’t mowing or binding was pitching. Newish counted the mowers in the morning and the pitchers in the afternoon. You could count pitching. It turned out you could count anything. One, and twenty forks went up at once, twenty sheaves went up onto the cart in one long gold flicker, and the man on the load laughed and swore and caught them all.

She lost her voice again. Rachael put her hand on her throat again.

“You’re going to talk yourself into a hole,” said Rachael.

“Mm,” said Newish, who wasn’t allowed to talk.

“I mean it. You’ve got two days left in you. Maybe three. Then you’re going to wake up and nothing’s going to come out.” Rachael’s hand was warm on her throat. Burden. Newish felt the rawness go out of her, into Rachael’s hand, and Rachael’s face went still for a second while it went in. “There. That’s one.”

“Rach—“

“Don’t.”

She didn’t.

The rain came on the fifteenth day.

Everybody saw it coming. You couldn’t not. It came up from the west in the afternoon, over the hills on the far side of the river: a long black line along the top of the sky, like a lid being slid across a box, with the sun still shining on the near side and the far side going dark. And under the black, between it and the hills, a grey smear. Hanging. Coming.

The gleaners looked at it, all at once. All over the field. Every face turned west, the way the swallows had sat on the barn facing south.

“Rain,” said Cobb. Very quietly.

“Is that bad?” said Calvin.

Cobb looked at him the way he’d looked at Drew when Drew asked if anybody had ever fought the bulls.

“On the stooks?” he said. “Wet? With the frost coming early, and no sun after to dry them?” He looked at the field. At the stooks standing in their rows, waiting for the carts. “They’ll sprout. In the ear. Green shoots out of the grain. And then they’re no good to anybody but the pigs.” He spat. “Rain on standing stooks is worse than the bulls. It’s worse than anything.” He looked at the Grey, down the valley, three fields from the village now. “Nearly anything.”

“How long till it gets here?” said Drew.

“An hour,” said Cobb. “Less.”

Drew looked at the field. Newish watched him look. She knew what he was doing; she’d watched him do it on every floor. Counting stooks. Counting carts. Counting forks and people and the distance down the lane to the barns and back. He was quick. He was the quickest of anybody at it except Higu.

“Not enough carts,” said Drew.

“No,” said Annet. She’d come up behind them without anybody hearing. “Three carts. Two down the lane and one here. Forty stooks still standing.” She looked at the sky. “We’ll get half. If we’re quick.”

“What if you didn’t use the carts?” said Gabe.

Everybody looked at him.

He was standing in the stubble with a fork in his hand and Hearthwall on his back, looking at the rows of stooks, and then up at the slope behind them, at the Thorne family’s big stone barn at the top of the field. Two hundred metres up the hill. With its doors open.

“It’s just carrying,” said Gabe. “Isn’t it. A cart’s just carrying. With a horse.” He looked round. At thirty-five people who’d spent fifteen days carrying, and a whole village of gleaners who’d spent their whole lives doing it. “We’ve got arms.”

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Calvin dropped his fork, and picked up a stook. A whole one. All eight sheaves, in his arms, against his chest, heads in his face. Staggered. Turned round. And ran up the hill with it toward the Thorne barn, shouting.

“LOOK AT MY BACK!”

It was the stupidest thing Newish had ever seen. It was the best.

Everybody did it. All of them. The passers first, because they were the ones who’d been told by Calvin, and then the gleaners, because they’d been watching the passers for fifteen days and had learned that when the passers did something stupid it usually worked. Everybody picked up a stook, or half a stook, or two sheaves, whatever they could carry, and ran up the hill to the barn with it, and threw it in through the doors, and ran back down. A long line of people going up the slope with gold in their arms and a long line of people coming down empty, passing each other, shouting, laughing. Salty with two whole stooks, one under each arm. Hako with one balanced on his head. Wren with one sheaf, as big as she was, dragging it, and Tiger beside her not carrying anything, not even pretending to, keeping up.

And Newish couldn’t carry anything much. Her arms were done. They’d been done since the third day. So she stood at the bottom of the slope, by the stooks, where everybody came down, and counted them.

Not one. Not for this. For this she counted names.

“Calvin. Salty. Gabe. Ned. Cobb. Rachael. Ezra—no, Ezra, sit DOWN. Azac. Jared. Thea. Thea, you’ve got it upside down. Wren. Tiger.” Over and over. Whoever was next at the stooks, she said their name, and they picked up and ran. And because she’d said it, they ran on it. Upbeat went out of her with every name, a floor under each one, and they ran up that hill faster than anybody should have been able to run up a hill with eight sheaves of wheat in their arms.

The black line came over the river.

The light went. The sun went behind it and the whole valley turned grey-green and strange, the way it does before a storm. The wind came up. Hard, sudden, from the west, cold, bending the hedges and blowing straw everywhere, and the first drops came with it. Big ones. Fat, heavy, far apart, smacking into the dust of the stubble and leaving dark coins.

Twelve stooks left.

“Hako. Chris. Drew. Annet—”

Annet Sallow picked up a stook and ran up the hill with it.

Eight.

Five.

The drops came closer together.

Three.

“Gabe. Jared. Calvin.”

One.

It was the last stook in the field. At the bottom, by the hedge, on its own. Newish was standing next to it. Everybody else was up the hill or coming down. There was nobody near enough to say a name to.

She picked it up herself.

It was heavier than she’d thought. Much heavier. Eight sheaves, and the straw pricked her arms and her face and went down the front of her shirt, and she couldn’t see over it, and her arms, which had been done since the third day, screamed. She turned round. She started up the hill. In the wind. In the first of the rain. On her own.

“Newish,” said somebody.

It was Calvin. Coming down the hill toward her, empty, running.

He didn’t take the stook off her. He fell into step beside her, on her left, and said her name again, louder, on the beat, the way she’d been saying everybody’s.

“Newish.”

And then everybody coming down the hill was saying it. Jared. Thea. Gabe. Salty. Ned and Cobb and Annet and Wren. They’d all heard Calvin, and they’d all worked out what he was doing, and they were saying it as they came past her, every one of them, on the beat: Newish. Newish. Newish. And it went into her like Upbeat went into them, a floor under her, solid, something to stand on.

She ran up that hill.

She threw the last stook in through the doors of the Thorne barn with both arms and fell over on top of it, and the sky opened.

It rained for three hours. Hard, the way it only rains at the end of a summer, straight down, so loud on the barn roof you had to shout. Everybody was in the barn. The whole of the field. Passers and gleaners, soaked, steaming, sitting on the heaped-up sheaves in the dark with the rain coming down outside the open doors in a grey wall and running off the eaves in ropes. Nobody said anything for a while. Everybody was too tired. Calvin was asleep on his face in the wheat. Wren was asleep on Tiger. Tiger was asleep on Salty.

Newish lay on her back on the top of the heap with her arms out, where she’d fallen, and looked up at the roof, and didn’t count anything.

Somebody sat down beside her.

It was Hana. She was soaked to the skin. Her lavender hair hung down in rats’ tails and her ears were flat with wet and her crown had slipped down over one eye. She didn’t seem to care. She sat on the sheaves with her knees up and looked out at the rain.

“That was very good,” said Hana.

“Calvin,” said Newish. Croaked. “Calvin did it. Saying my name.”

“I know.”

“He never does anything on the beat. He’s never been on the beat in his life.”

“I know,” said Hana. “That’s why it was good.”

They sat there. The rain came down.

Down the far end of the barn, by the doors, Milli was sitting on a sheaf on his own with his tail wrapped round his ankles, looking out at the rain. Not at anything. Just at it. He had the face he got when he was doing a sum in his head that wasn’t coming out. Newish had seen that face on him a lot this week. Every evening. Every time anybody said harvest-home.

She looked at Hana. Hana was looking at him too.

“So,” said Newish.

“Don’t.”

“I’m not doing anything. I’m lying here. I can’t even move my arms.”

“You’re about to do something,” said Hana. “I can see it in your face. Your face is the loudest thing on this floor.”

“My face is the loudest thing on any floor,” said Newish. “That’s not news.” She turned her head on the wheat and looked at Hana properly. “Are you going to?”

Hana didn’t answer for a long time.

“Annet said harvest-home,” said Newish. “Under the last sheaf. In the big barn. In front of everybody. If you mean to.” She paused. “And Milli said you had a date. In the spring. At home.” She waited. “And it’s not spring. It’s harvest.”

“I know what Annet said,” said Hana. Quietly.

“So are you going to?”

Hana looked at the rain.

“It was going to be at home,” she said at last. Very quietly. Not looking at Newish. “In the spring. Everything was—“ She stopped. “Everything was booked. We’d done it all. The two of us. Like a list. He made a list. Of course he made a list. With steps.” She almost smiled. “And then a white room.”

“I know.”

“And everybody who was going to be there isn’t.” Her voice didn’t wobble. Hana’s voice never wobbled. It just went very smooth, the way it did when she was holding something very still. “Not here. Not any of them. They’re all at home, at whatever time it’s stopped at, with their phones face down, and they don’t even know we’ve gone.” She looked at her hands. “It feels like doing it without them. Like going behind their backs.”

Newish didn’t say anything for a while. She lay on the wheat and listened to the rain.

She didn’t know what to say. That was new. She always knew what to say. Usually the wrong thing, very loudly, on purpose, so everybody laughed. She’d done it at every bad moment on every floor since the white room. She’d done it at the Cataract with blood on Jared. She’d done it on the Patience with the Undertow under them. She’d made herself the loudest, rudest thing in every room so that nobody had to be the quietest.

But Hana wasn’t laughing. And it wasn’t a bad moment. It was something else.

“Can I say something,” said Newish. “Not a joke.”

Hana looked at her.

“I can’t promise it’s not a joke,” said Newish. “It might come out a joke. That’s just my face.” She took a breath. It hurt; her chest was sore from the hill. “But. Hana. Everybody who was going to be there isn’t. Yeah. I know. That’s horrible. That’s the worst thing.” She looked up at the roof. “But everybody who can be there, is. Look.” She lifted one hand, which hurt, and pointed at the barn. At the people on the sheaves in the dark. At Calvin asleep on his face. At Jared, sitting against a beam, watching Hana with the face he always had for her, the steady one. At Aleaka, with Sleet curled round her and Tuppence asleep on her shoulder. At the gleaners. At Annet, standing in the doorway with her back to them all, looking out at the rain on her fields with her stick in her hand.

“Thirty-five of us,” said Newish. “And a whole valley full of people who’ve never been to a wedding with a cat at it. Two cats.” She dropped her hand. “And we’re not at home. We might never be. And we might be at home tomorrow and it’ll be nine past two and none of this’ll have happened. I don’t know. Nobody knows.” She swallowed. “But it’s happening. Right now. Here. And you two have been sitting at opposite ends of a barn every night for a week doing sums in your heads, and you’re going to get to the top of the valley, and it’s going to be harvest-home, and the bell’s going to go.” She looked at Hana. “And then it’s winter.”

Hana didn’t say anything.

“When the bell goes, it’s not yours any more,” said Newish. “Annet says. You leave it in the field.” Her voice had gone. She let it. “Don’t leave it in the field, Hana. That’s all. That’s the whole thing. That’s not a joke.”

Hana looked at her for a long time.

Then she put her hand over her face. Not crying. Hana didn’t cry, mostly. She just sat there with her hand over her face, on a heap of wet wheat in the dark, with the rain coming down outside, and breathed.

Then she took it away.

“Honey,” she said. Not loud. Not to Newish. Down the barn.

Milli’s head came up. All the way down the far end, by the doors, on his sheaf. His ears came up with it. He looked down the length of the barn at her in the dark, over thirty people’s heads.

He didn’t say what. He didn’t say anything.

He got up. He came up the barn, between the sleeping people and the heaped sheaves, carefully, stepping over Calvin, and stood at the bottom of the heap where Hana was sitting, and looked up at her, with his tail very still.

“Harvest-home,” said Hana.

Milli didn’t say anything for a moment. Newish watched his face. She watched the sum stop. Just stop, in the middle, the way a line of mowers stops when somebody shouts hold. And then something else, underneath, that didn’t need adding up.

“Yes,” said Milli.

“Is that a yes yes or a let me think about the steps yes.”

“It’s a yes yes.” He was quite red. “I’ve thought about the steps. I’ve thought about them every night for a week.” He swallowed. “There aren’t any. That’s what I worked out. There’s no list for this. You just go when the bell goes.”

Hana laughed. Very quietly. Behind her hand.

“Okay,” she said.

“Okay,” said Milli.

He climbed up the heap, which wasn’t easy, because it was wheat and it moved, and sat down beside her, on her other side, not Newish’s. And she put her head on his shoulder, wet hair and crooked crown and all. And he put his tail round her ankle.

Newish lay on her back on the wheat between the rain and them and looked at the roof and found that her face was wet. It was probably the rain. The roof was probably leaking.

“I’m not crying,” she said. To the roof. To nobody. “I’m counting.”

“Count what?” said Milli.

“I don’t know yet,” said Newish. “Shut up. I’ll think of something.”

Annet Sallow took it the way she took everything. Standing in the door of the Thorne barn with the rain stopping behind her and the evening coming out clean and gold and steaming over the wet fields, with her stick.

“Good,” she said. “Harvest-home.”

That was all. She looked at Hana and Milli, standing in front of her, wet, holding hands, with Newish behind them as if she’d brought them there on a lead, which she had. She looked at them for a long moment. At Hana’s ears. At Milli’s tail.

“I’ll need the women,” she said. And then, to Newish: “Not you. You’re the count. The count doesn’t make crowns.” And turned round, and walked out into the wet gold evening, and began shouting names across the field. Old women’s names, mostly. Newish didn’t know any of them. They all came.

The Grey took two fields that night.

Not one. Two. Thistle Holme and Bramble Piece, side by side, on the slope above the river, three fields from Haverley. Both of them had been stubble. Both of them had been empty since the hill, since the last stook went up into the Thorne barn in the rain. In the morning they were grey, hedge to hedge, perfectly still in the clean wind after the storm, steaming nowhere, wet nowhere, as if the rain had never fallen on them at all.

Two.

Annet stood at the gate and looked at it for a long time. Then she cut two notches on her stick, side by side, and didn’t cross either of them.

“Hungrier,” she said.

Nobody said anything.

“Well,” said Annet. She put the stick under her arm and turned her back on it. “It’s not under a roof. And it’s not getting under one.” She looked up the lane, at the village, at the barns, all of them full to the doors now, every one, with the last of Haverley’s harvest. “Haverley’s in.”

It was. Newish hadn’t known until Annet said it. The last of Haverley’s fields had come in in the rain. Everything was under a roof. The whole of Haverley’s harvest, in the barns and the ricks and the tithe barn, behind doors. And the Grey had stopped at the hedge of the last field it could reach and couldn’t come any further, because there was nothing left in the fields to take.

At the gate, the lads set Gaffer Tobin’s chair down, and the old man sat in it and looked at the two grey fields and at the full barns behind him.

He lifted his stick. An inch.

He held it there for longer than he usually did. Much longer. Newish counted it without meaning to. Eight. Nine. Ten.

Then he put it down.

“Up the valley, then,” said Gaffer Tobin. Very thin. Like paper. To nobody. To all of them. “Ashwell next. They’ll want us.”

“You’re not going up the valley, Gaffer,” said Annet.

“I’m going up the valley,” said the old man. “I’ve seen seventy-four in. I’ll see this one in. The whole of it. Not just ours.” He looked at her. “You’ll put me on a cart.”

Annet looked at him for a long time.

“I’ll put you on a cart,” she said.

They went up the valley that afternoon.

That was how it was done. Newish hadn’t known that either. Every village brought its own harvest in, and then, when it was in, it didn’t stop. It went up the valley to the next village and helped them bring theirs in. And then that village, when it was done, went on up with them to the next. All of them together. Further and further up the Acre, a bigger and bigger crowd, until at the very top, at the barn fields under the Long Barn, the whole valley was in one place, every village, every mower and binder and pitcher in the Acre, bringing in the last field together.

And then harvest-home.

“Every year?” said Calvin, walking.

“Every year,” said Cobb. “Since the first.”

“Even the years it’s not racing anything grey?”

Cobb looked at him. “It’s always racing something,” he said. “The frost. The rain. The bulls. It’s never not racing.” He walked on a bit. “This year it’s just got a face on it.”

They went up the lane out of Haverley in a long line. The mowers first, with their blades on their shoulders. Then the binders. Then the carts, empty, rattling, the big brown horses nodding, with the forks laid in the bottoms. And on the second cart, in his chair, roped in so he wouldn’t tip, with his blanket and his stick, Gaffer Tobin, sitting very upright, facing forward, up the valley.

Then the thirty-six.

Newish walked near the back, with Hana’s party. She was too tired to count. Her arms hung. Her voice was gone again; Rachael had told her not to use it until Ashwell and she was actually, for once, doing what she was told. Beside her, Aleaka walked with Sleet at her heel and Tuppence on her shoulder, and every so often, without seeming to know she was doing it, put her hand out sideways and touched Newish’s arm, lightly, like checking she was still there. Jared walked behind with his thorn-shield on his back, whistling something through his teeth. And in front of them, Hana and Milli. Holding hands. Not talking. Hana’s crown was on straight for once. Milli’s tail was up.

She looked back once. From the top of the lane. At Haverley, by the river, the grey roofs and the oak and the tower and the tithe barn with its doors shut now, full. And beyond it, down the valley, the Grey. Seven fields of it now. Eight. A long flat colourless stain across the bottom of the Acre from the old barn to the hedges below the village, very still, waiting.

It couldn’t go under a roof.

She turned round and went on up the valley, on the one, with everybody.
