The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 4: Threads

Chapter 109

His Father

3,573 words · 16 min read

He’d been not-thinking about it for three days.

He was good at not-thinking about things. He’d had thirty-four years of practice. You learned it early, when you were a baby with a grown man’s memories and no way to tell anyone, lying in a cot in a cottage, looking at the ceiling. You learned to put a thing in a room in your head and shut the door, and go and do something else. Fish. Mend a net. Feed the hens. Raise a daughter. And the door stayed shut, mostly, and you only heard what was behind it at three in the morning, when you couldn’t sleep, and then you got up and put the kettle on.

He’d been not-thinking about his father for thirty-four years.

That wasn’t true. He’d thought about him every day for the first ten. And then less. And then, the year Alea was born, holding her in a garrison room at three in the morning while Diwa slept, he’d thought about him so hard that he’d had to go out and stand in the snow until he could breathe again. Is this what it was like? Did you stand somewhere in the dark at three in the morning, the year I was born, and feel like this?

And then less again. Because it didn’t help. Because his father was in another world, and old by now, he’d thought, or gone, and there was nothing to do with any of it but carry it.

And then, last summer, the date at the bottom of his window. Three years. Not thirty-four. Three.

He’d stood on this jetty that night and done the sums over and over, and every time they came out the same. His father wasn’t old. His father wasn’t gone. His father was three years older than the last time Declan had spoken to him on the phone, and somewhere in a world on the other side of everything, he was getting up, and going to work, and coming home, on an ordinary weekday, right now.

He’d shut the door on that one too. It was the only way to live with it.

Gabe had opened it yesterday, under the sailcloth. With three words.

He’s okay, Declan.

They came for him in the morning.

He was on the step with his cup, watching the mist come off the water, the way he did every morning. Gabe came out of the cabin behind him, and stood there. And then Deston. And neither of them said anything, and they didn’t need to.

He put his cup down.

“Not here,” said Deston. “Can we walk?”

“Where?”

Deston looked up the lake road. At the low stone wall that ran along the side of it, between the road and the reeds, all the way from the shingle to the town. The wall Gabe had sat down on, three days ago, when Alea told him her name.

“There,” said Deston. “Up a bit. Where you can see the lake.”

They walked up the lake road in the morning, the three of them.

Not far. A couple of hundred paces. To a place where the wall ran along a little rise, and the reeds fell away below it, and you could see the whole of the lake laid out from end to end: the cabin and the jetty below, the shingle and the sailcloth forge, the black water going away north between the hills to where the mist was still lying on it in the far distance. The hens had followed them as far as the fence and stopped there, complaining.

He sat down on the wall.

He didn’t think about it. He just sat. With his feet on the road and his hands between his knees, and his back very straight, looking out at the lake.

Gabe made a small sound.

He looked round. Gabe was standing in the road, looking at him. And his face had gone very strange. And Deston beside him had the same look. As if they’d seen a ghost walk out of the reeds and sit down on a wall.

“What?” he said.

Gabe didn’t answer for a moment.

“You’re sitting like him,” said Gabe.

He didn’t understand. And then he did.

“He sat like that,” said Deston. Very quietly. “On a wall. After. Exactly like that. With his hands like that.” He sat down on the wall too, an arm’s length away. “Exactly like that, Declan.”

He looked down at his own hands, between his knees.

He hadn’t known. He’d never known he sat like that. It wasn’t his body. It wasn’t his father’s body. It was a body that had been born in a cottage on the other side of the world to a farmer who’d never been further than the market. It had nothing in it of anybody from Earth. Nothing. Not a bone, not a hair.

And it sat on a wall the way his father did.

Something came up in his chest that he didn’t have a name for.

He didn’t move his hands.

“Tell me,” he said.

Gabe sat down on his other side. Heavily. The wall shifted a little under him. And for a while none of them said anything, and they looked at the lake.

“You know most of it,” said Gabe, eventually. “Some of it. We were there. At the funeral. All of us, nearly. Group 4. Most of Group 6. Xav. Malcolm. Rachael and Dennis. Calvin, even. He’d only met you once, and he came.”

“I know,” he said. “Calvin said.”

“It was a week after,” said Deston. “After the call, after… A week. We’d all flown in, or driven, or whatever. It was a room. Just a room, with chairs. Lots of people in black. More people than you’d think. People from work. People from school. People I’d never seen in my life.” He swallowed. “Me and Gabe stood at the back. We couldn’t sit down. I don’t know why. We just couldn’t.”

“And your dad stood at the front,” said Gabe. “The whole time.”

He didn’t say anything.

“In a dark suit,” said Gabe. “It didn’t quite fit. Too big in the shoulders. Like he’d borrowed it, or bought it the day before, or hadn’t worn it in years. And he stood at the front, by the door, the whole day, and he shook every single hand.”

“Every hand,” said Deston. “Everybody who came in. Everybody who went past him to sit down. Everybody who came up after. He shook every hand that was held out to him. And he said thank you. Thank you for coming. To every one.”

“He asked after people,” said Gabe. “That’s the thing I remember. By name. He knew everybody’s name. He asked after their parents. Their jobs. Their studies. Whether they’d had a long drive. He asked me how I was. By name. Like he’d known me for years, Declan. I’d met him twice.” Gabe’s voice did something. “It was like a wedding. The way he did it. Like he was the host at a wedding, and it was his job to make sure everybody was all right. Not what it was.”

“He didn’t cry,” said Deston.

“Not once,” said Gabe. “All day. I watched. I couldn’t stop watching. Everybody else was in pieces. I was in pieces. Rachael had to go outside twice. Dennis had to hold Calvin up. And your dad stood at the front, in his suit, and shook every hand, and asked everybody about their parents, and didn’t cry once.”

He looked at the lake.

“No,” he said. “He wouldn’t.”

He’d known that before they said it. He’d known it in his bones, the way you know the shape of a room in the dark. His father had always been like that. A man who said very little and did everything. Who went to work, and came home, and sat in his chair, and got up in the morning and went again.

It wasn’t that he didn’t feel things. It was that he felt them so much he didn’t dare let any of it out, in case it all came out at once and he couldn’t stop it. So he held it. All of it. Like holding up a wall. And he shook everybody’s hand while he did it, so they’d be all right.

Declan hadn’t always understood that. He understood it now. In another world, in another body, thirty-four years on, he understood it completely.

“She was there,” said Deston.

He turned his head.

“The woman,” said Deston. “The one he’d been with. For a couple of years, before. She was there. All day. She stood next to him at the front, the whole time. She didn’t say much. She just stood there with her hand on his elbow. Not holding him up. Just… there. So he’d know.”

“Good,” he said.

It came out of him before he could stop it. A whole breath, in one word.

“Good.”

“And then later,” said Gabe.

He’d known there was a later. He could hear it in Gabe’s voice. The thing Gabe had carried up a river. The thing he’d said tomorrow to.

“Later,” said Gabe. “When it was over. When everybody was going. I went out the back, for some air. There’s a car park. Just a car park, with a low wall round it. And he was there.”

Gabe looked at the wall they were sitting on.

“On the wall,” said Gabe. “On his own. With his tie pulled loose. Sitting exactly like you’re sitting now. Hands between his knees. Back straight. Looking at nothing.” He breathed. “Not crying. Even then. Just sitting there. Like a man who’s been holding something up all day, and has finally put it down, and doesn’t know what to do with his hands now.”

He didn’t move.

He sat on the wall at the top of the lake road with his hands between his knees and his back straight, and looked at nothing, and felt the shape of his father’s body around his own, like a coat he’d been wearing for thirty-four years without knowing.

“I didn’t go over,” said Gabe. “I didn’t know what to say. I was twenty-four. I just stood by the door and watched him. And after a while…”

He stopped.

“After a while,” said Deston, gently, for him, “she came out.”

“She came out of the side door,” said Gabe. “And walked across the car park. And sat down on the wall beside him. And she didn’t say anything. Not a word. She just took his hand.” He swallowed. “And he let her. He let her take it. And the two of them sat there, on the wall, in the car park, looking at nothing. Together. In the evening. For a long time.”

“I went back inside,” said Gabe. “It wasn’t mine to watch. But I thought…” He stopped again. “I thought, good. That’s what I thought. Standing in the door. Good. Somebody’s got him.”

He sat there.

He didn’t cry. He noticed that. He noticed that he wasn’t crying, and that he wanted to, and that something in him was holding it, all of it, like a wall, the way it held everything. He noticed that he was sitting on a wall with his hands between his knees not crying, and that it was the most his father’s son he’d ever been.

He laughed.

It came out of him short and strange, and Gabe and Deston both looked at him.

“I’m doing it,” he said. “Aren’t I. Right now. I’m doing exactly what he did.”

“Yeah,” said Gabe. “You are.”

“Thirty-four years. Different body. Different world. Different everything.” He looked down at his hands. “And I’m sitting on a wall not crying.”

“You cried on the jetty,” said Deston. “Three days ago. In front of the whole town.”

“That was different.”

“It wasn’t,” said Deston. “You held it for thirty-four years. Then you let it go. All at once. In front of everyone.” He looked at the lake. “Maybe that’s what he did too. Later. When nobody was looking. On the wall, with her.”

He didn’t answer that.

He didn’t know. He’d never know. That was the thing. That was the thing that sat at the bottom of all of it. He’d never know whether his father cried, later, on a wall, with somebody holding his hand. He’d never be able to ask.

“Is he okay?” he said.

“He’s okay,” said Gabe.

“You said that yesterday. Under the sailcloth. Is he okay, Gabe? Not on the day. After.”

Gabe looked at Deston. Deston looked back.

“We saw him a couple of times after,” said Deston carefully. “Not often. Not much. He’s not…” He spread his hands. “You know what he’s like. He’s not a man you just drop in on. But we saw him. Gabe and me. And the last time, he was okay, Declan. He was older. He was quieter, even, if you can imagine that. But he was okay.”

“He was still working,” said Gabe. “He was still getting up every morning and going. Like always.”

“Of course he was,” he said. Almost a laugh.

“And she was still with him,” said Deston. “The last time. They were good together. You could tell. Not showy. Just… good. Quiet. She said something to him, and he nearly smiled.” Deston paused. “He nearly smiled, Declan. I saw it.”

He put his hand over his eyes.

He held it there for a while. On the wall. In the morning. With his other hand still between his knees.

“Okay,” he said, behind his hand. “Okay.”

“He’s okay,” said Gabe, for the third time. Gently. “That’s what we came to tell you. That’s the most important bit, so we’re telling you that bit again. He’s okay. Somebody’s got him.”

He felt her coming before he heard her.

He always did. Since she was small. A step on the road that he’d have known among a thousand. She’d come up from the cabin, past the fence, past the hens, who had followed her as far as the gate and stopped. She came up the lake road in the morning in her old coat, with Astra on her back and her ears forward, and her tail very still, the way it went when she was being careful.

She didn’t say anything.

She looked at the three of them on the wall. At Gabe and Deston, with their wrecked faces. At her father, sitting between them with his hand over his eyes and his other hand between his knees.

She didn’t ask.

She just came and sat down on the wall beside him, in the space between him and Gabe, which Gabe moved over without a word to make. She sat down close, the way she used to when she was small, so that their shoulders touched.

And she reached down, without looking, and took his hand.

The one between his knees. She took it out from between his knees and held it in both of hers, in her lap. Her cold hands. Her mother’s hands. Holding his.

She didn’t say anything at all.

He heard Gabe’s breath stop.

He heard Deston, on the other side, make a sound like something breaking very quietly.

He took his own hand down from his eyes. He looked at Gabe. Gabe was staring at Alea’s hands, holding his, in her lap. And his face had gone completely to pieces.

“That’s what she did,” said Gabe. Barely a voice. “That’s exactly what she did.”

Alea looked up. She didn’t understand. She looked from Gabe to Deston to her father, with her ears going back, uncertain.

“What did I do?” she said.

He couldn’t answer. For a while he couldn’t say anything at all.

Then he turned his hand over in hers, so that he was holding them too.

“Nothing,” he said. “Nothing wrong. You did exactly the right thing.” He held on. “You always do.”

And then, sitting on a wall at the top of the lake road, with his daughter holding his hand, in the morning, in front of his brothers, the way his father had sat on a wall in a car park with someone holding his, he let go of it.

Not all at once. Not like the jetty. Just slowly, the way the mist was going off the water below them, a little at a time. He sat there and let it go, and didn’t hold any of it up, and didn’t try to stop it. And Alea held his hand, and didn’t ask, and didn’t let go.

He thought, much later, that maybe that was what his father had done. On the wall. With her. After everyone had gone.

He hoped so.

He’d never know. But he hoped so.

They sat on the wall a long time.

Gabe told Alea, after a while, gently, what she’d done. About the car park, and the wall, and the woman who came out of the side door. Alea listened with her ears flat and her mouth pressed tight and her hands not letting go. When he’d finished, she looked up at her father for a long moment.

“Your dad,” she said. “My grandfather.”

He hadn’t thought of it like that. In thirty-four years, he’d never once thought of it like that.

“Yes,” he said.

“He’s alive.”

“Yes.”

“Right now.” She frowned, working it out. “Not old. Not like the king. Three years older than when you… than when.” She stopped. “He’s just. Alive. Somewhere. Right now. Today.”

“Yes,” he said.

Alea was quiet for a while.

“What’s he like?” she said. “You never told me about him. You told me about everybody. You told me about Gabe and Deston and Xav and Yan and Leaka and the one who shouts in capitals. You never once told me about him.”

He looked at the lake.

“He’s quiet,” he said, after a long time. “He doesn’t say much. He never did. He gets up every morning and goes to work, and comes home, and sits in his chair. He’ll shake every hand in a room so that nobody feels left out. He’ll stand at the front all day so that everybody else can sit down. He holds things up so other people don’t have to.” He breathed. “I used to think that meant he didn’t feel anything. I was wrong. It meant he felt everything, and didn’t trust himself to put any of it down.”

Alea looked at him.

“That’s you,” she said.

He didn’t answer.

“That’s you, Dad,” said Alea. “That’s exactly you. You just described yourself.”

Gabe laughed. Wetly. Deston laughed. And after a moment, so did he. Sitting on a wall, with his daughter’s cold hands round his, laughing, and crying, and not holding any of it up at all.

That night he opened his window.

He did it on the end of the jetty, on his own, late, after everyone else had gone up. Out of habit. And he didn’t look at the top of it, where his name was, and the tier, and the long line of skills. He didn’t look at the bar, which said fifty-four, and had said fifty-four since the night on the jetty, and would move again when it moved.

He went all the way to the bottom. Past everything. To the line Thea and Chris had found last summer, in their own windows, that he’d found in his.

The slow date. Earth’s date.

He looked at it for a long time.

Three years, and a little more. Getting on for two months since the summoning. The days crawled past down there. One of their days for every eleven of his. He’d done the sums so many times he didn’t have to anymore.

Somewhere, on that date, in a world on the other side of everything, it was a weekday. It was the middle of a morning, maybe. A man was getting up and going to work. Or sitting in his chair. Or sitting on a wall somewhere, with his hands between his knees, looking at nothing, not crying.

And somebody had him. Somebody came out of the side door and sat down beside him and took his hand, and he let her.

Good, Declan thought.

He thought it the way Gabe had, standing in a doorway in a car park three years ago. With his whole chest.

Good. Somebody’s got you.

He sat there for a long time on the end of the jetty, looking at the date. Then, not out loud, but very clearly, into the dark, to a man who’d never hear it, in a world he’d probably never see again:

I’m okay too, Dad.

I’ve got a daughter. She’s seventeen. She’s cleverer than me and she tells me so. She feeds the hens. She took my hand this morning on a wall, without anybody telling her to, exactly the way somebody took yours.

You’d like her.

She’d make you nearly smile.

He closed the window.

Behind him, up the shingle, a door opened. A step on the boards. A girl’s step, that he’d have known among a thousand. She came out to the end of the jetty in her blanket, and sat down beside him, and didn’t say anything, and after a while she put her head on his shoulder.

They sat there together, looking at nothing, in the dark.
