The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 11

Chapter 304

Room

5,205 words · 23 min read

Gigichi had been trying to grow a chair for nine days, and it kept coming up with thorns.

She did it in the yard behind the militia hall in the mornings, before anyone was about, where there was a strip of bare earth along the back wall that nobody used except Waddles, for sulking. She knelt on a sack and put her hand flat on the ground and thought, very clearly, a chair. Not a bench. She had a bench. A chair: one seat, a back, the kind of thing you’d put by a fire for somebody who’d come in cold.

And Briar came up out of the earth under her hand the way it always did, glad to be asked, a tangle of brown stem as thick as her wrist, and wound itself into the shape of a seat and a back, very nicely; and then put out thorns. All over. Long ones, like a dog rose gone wrong, curved and wicked and pointing out.

She’d stared at it the first morning. She’d thought she’d done it wrong. She’d done it again, the second morning, thinking a chair, no thorns, nobody wants thorns, and it had come up with more.

“It’s a very good chair,” Higu had said, on the fourth morning, from the back step, with her cup. “For somebody you don’t like.”

“Shut up.”

“I’m only saying.” Higu had come over and looked at it. She was small and calm and kept the list of everything, and had done since the hall, and she put her head on one side and looked at Gigichi’s chair the way she looked at everything, as if she were going to write it down later. “The bench doesn’t have thorns.”

“I know the bench doesn’t have thorns.”

“Why doesn’t the bench have thorns?”

“I don’t know. That’s the whole—“ Gigichi sat back on her heels. “I didn’t make the bench. It just came. I was just sitting there.”

“Mm,” said Higu, and reached out, and touched the back of the chair with one finger, to see.

It caught her. A thorn as long as a needle went into the pad of her finger and out again, and Higu said oh very quietly and took her hand back, and a bead of blood came up, dark and round, and ran down into her palm.

Gigichi had felt it go in. That was the worst part. She’d felt the thorn find the finger the way you feel your own tooth bite your tongue, and she’d felt the briar be pleased. Not here. That was what it was for. That was what it had always been for, since the autumn of the second year, since she’d first put her hand on the ground at Wrenhaven and a hedge had come up between her and everybody: not here, not me, go away. She’d been very good at it. It was the only thing she’d ever been really good at, in two worlds.

She’d torn the chair out of the ground with both hands, thorns and all, and thrown it over the wall, and gone inside, and not come out till dinner. Her hands had been scratched to the wrists. Her nose had bled, after, the way it did when she pushed too hard. She’d sat on her cot with a cloth under it and Higu on the end of the cot with a cloth round her finger, and neither of them had said anything for a long time.

“You weren’t sitting on it,” Higu had said at last.

“What?”

“The bench. You said you were just sitting there. You weren’t trying to make a bench. You were sitting on a stone waiting for somebody, and it came up under you.” Higu had looked at her finger. “You were trying to make a chair. For nobody. Nobody was waiting in it.”

Gigichi hadn’t answered. She’d thought it was a stupid thing to say.

She’d thought about it every day since.

The girl was brought in on the ninth evening, in the rain.

Gigichi didn’t see it happen; she heard about it, the way you heard about everything in Wrenhaven, in about a quarter of an hour, from four people at once. A fisherman’s girl, eight, from the huts at the bottom of the lake road. Lissa Fowler. A hook in her hand, two days ago, from a man on the river bank below the town who’d had a box of them and was giving them away. And now the cut had gone grey.

Grey.

Gigichi had read it in the channel two days before, the same as everybody. Rachael, from Kingsferry, plain and short the way Rachael wrote everything: a boy, a hook, a grey edge, a mend that went in and didn’t answer, a close that closed it in. A man on the steps with no thread. If anyone sees a cut go grey, don’t close it. Tell me first. And everybody had said that’s horrible and poor kid and Rachael’ll sort it, and Gigichi had read it and thought, privately, with her usual cheerfulness, well, that’ll be up the river by Thursday.

It was Wednesday.

Thea went straight to the infirmary. Of course she did. Thea always went straight to things; that had been true in a café on Earth and it was true now, here, with her Lantern and her Parse and her two years of reading the world’s own writing with her hands. She went down the hill in the rain with her coat over her head and Maren behind her, and the door of the infirmary shut behind them, and didn’t open again.

And Gigichi stood in the yard of the militia hall in the wet, looking at the strip of bare earth by the back wall where she’d thrown a thorny chair over it, and felt sick.

“She wants blankets,” said Xola, coming out of the hall with an armful of them, with Squiddles on her head under a fold of oilcloth, looking extremely put out. “Thea. She sent the Tallow boy. Blankets and the big kettle and the lamp oil and Chi’s good knife.” Xola held the blankets out. “Can you take them? I’ve got to do the kettle. And Chi’s gone up to the dairy for milk.”

Gigichi took the blankets.

The infirmary in Wrenhaven was four beds and a back room and a yard with a well in it, at the bottom of the hill where the lake road came into the town, with an elder tree by the gate that had been there longer than the town. Sister Odile ran it, and had for thirty years, and cried at goodbyes, and at hellos, and at most things in between; she was the softest person Gigichi had ever met and the least breakable.

The door was shut.

Not locked. Shut, with a chair against it on the inside, and Odile’s voice through it, gentle and immovable, telling somebody no, love, not yet, not till we know. And on the step outside it, in the rain, a woman.

She was sitting on the stone with her knees up and her arms round them and her face against her knees. She had no shawl on. She’d come out of the house without it, Gigichi saw, the way you do; she was wet through to the skin, her hair stuck to her neck, and she was shaking, and she wasn’t doing anything about any of it. She was sitting on the step of a shut door as close as she could get to it without being inside.

Ada Fowler. The mother. Gigichi knew her by sight: she gutted for the boats in the mornings and had a laugh you could hear from the jetty.

She wasn’t laughing.

Gigichi stood in the gate under the elder tree with an armful of blankets and the rain going down the back of her neck and had absolutely no idea what to do.

She was bad at this. She’d always been bad at this. Thea was good at it; Thea went straight in. Xola was good at it; Xola hugged people, Xola hugged everybody, Xola had once hugged one of Odette’s geese. Chi was good at it; Chi’s voice did a thing to frightened people that Gigichi had watched work on a whole queue of strangers at the Lists. Higu was good at it in her own way, which was to sit nearby and write things down until you felt better out of sheer boredom.

Gigichi said the wrong thing. That was what Gigichi did. She said the true thing, the sharp one, the one that came out of her before she’d looked at it, and people flinched, and she’d spent four years in this world learning to keep her mouth shut instead, and it still came out about one time in three.

She went and put the blankets down on the dry end of the step, under the eaves.

“There’s blankets,” she said.

Ada didn’t look up.

“You should put one on,” said Gigichi. “You’re soaked. You know you’re soaked, right? You’re going to make yourself ill, and then there’ll be two of you in there, and Odile’s only got four beds.”

There it was. One in three. She heard it come out of her mouth and wanted to put it back.

Ada lifted her head and looked at her.

She had the face you’d expect. White and wet and swollen round the eyes, and stripped right down to the bone of it, the way faces go when the worst thing has happened and then gone on happening for hours. And she looked at Gigichi standing there in the rain with her silver hair stuck to her face and her mouth open on the wrong thing, and she said, perfectly flatly, “They won’t let me in.”

“I know.”

“She’s eight. She’s eight and she’s in there and she’s cold, they said she’s cold, her hand’s cold, and they won’t let me in.” Ada’s voice didn’t rise. It went flat and flatter. “They say it might go to me. If I touch her. They don’t know. Nobody knows anything. Your friend doesn’t know. The one with the—“ She moved her hand at her own head, meaning Thea’s hair, or Thea’s eyes, or the way Thea looked at you. “She said she doesn’t know yet.”

“She’ll find out,” said Gigichi. “That’s what she does.”

“Then I’ll wait,” said Ada, and put her face back on her knees.

Gigichi sat down.

She didn’t decide to. She’d meant to go. She’d brought the blankets; that was the job; Thea had sent for blankets and the kettle and the oil and Chi’s knife, and the others were coming with the rest, and she could go back up the hill to the militia hall and be useful there, or not be, and nobody would think anything of it.

She sat down on the step instead. Not close. At the other end of it, under the eaves, on the dry bit, with the blankets between them and her back against the wet wall of the infirmary and her knees up, and her gold watch digging into her hip.

She didn’t say anything. She’d said enough.

The rain came down. It was the soft heavy kind that comes off the lake at the end of spring, warm, endless, that doesn’t so much fall as hang in the air and get into everything. It ran off the eaves in a line a foot in front of their faces. The elder dripped. Somewhere up the hill a door banged and somebody shouted for a dog. Behind them, through the wall, very faintly, she could hear Odile’s voice, low, and once Thea’s, and once a child, not crying, saying something she couldn’t hear.

Ada heard it too. Gigichi saw her whole body go toward the door. And stop, because the door was shut, and there was nowhere to go.

The step was stone. It was wet; the eaves didn’t quite reach it. Ada was sitting on the wet part, as close to the door as she could get, in a puddle, and she’d been sitting there an hour by the look of her, and she’d sit there all night.

Gigichi looked at her and thought: she’s got nowhere to sit.

It was a stupid thought. She was sitting. She was sitting on a step. But that wasn’t what Gigichi meant, and she knew it wasn’t, and she didn’t know what she meant; she only knew that there was a woman on the stone outside a shut door with her child on the other side of it, and the stone didn’t care, and the door didn’t care, and nothing in the whole wet world was holding her up except her own arms round her own knees.

She thought about a stone by the lake road. One evening last autumn, when he was away west. Sitting on it in the cold because she didn’t want to be in the yard with everybody being kind to each other, waiting for nothing. For nobody. For somebody who wasn’t coming back till the snow.

And something had come up under her.

She’d put her hand on the ground. She hadn’t meant to. She’d just put it there, the way you put your hand on the arm of a chair; and the ground had answered.

Her hand was on the ground now.

She hadn’t noticed. It was lying flat on the cobbles of the infirmary yard at the end of the step, by the elder’s roots, where she’d put it to sit down. Cold and wet. She could feel the stones under it, and the earth under the stones, and under the earth, the way you feel your own heart if you lie very still, the roots of the elder going down and out under the whole yard. Old. Slow. Thirty years of a tree outside a door where people waited.

She didn’t think chair. She didn’t think anything. She looked at Ada Fowler sitting in a puddle with her face on her knees, and she felt, very simply, the way you feel cold, that this woman needed somewhere to be. Not somewhere comfortable. Not somewhere nice. Just somewhere that was for her. Somewhere that said you can be here.

Here.

It came up out of the ground the way the bench had. Not fast. Not the way Briar came, glad and wicked and in a hurry. Slow. A root from the elder, as thick as her arm, grey-brown and smooth and wet with rain, lifting the cobbles beside the step one by one and setting them aside, very carefully, as if it didn’t want to make a mess; and curving up out of the ground at the door end of the step, right by the door, under the very edge of the eaves where the drip stopped.

Ada lifted her head.

The root came up and over and down again, in a low curve, the height of a step, the width of a woman. And then another came up behind it, thinner, and curved, and went along the back of the first one like an arm. A seat. With a back. Grey and smooth and dripping. Right up against the door, so close that if you sat in it you’d have your shoulder against the wood; under the eaves, out of the rain, out of the drip; facing the door.

It didn’t have thorns.

Gigichi stared at it. She’d stopped breathing at some point. She started again.

Ada stared at it too. Then she looked at Gigichi, at the other end of the step, with her hand flat on the cobbles and her mouth open.

“Is that—“ said Ada.

“It’s for you,” said Gigichi. Her voice came out strange. “I think. It’s—“ She didn’t know what it was. “You can sit there. It’s out of the wet. You’ll be right by the door.”

Ada looked at the seat.

Then she got up, slowly, stiff from the stone, and stepped across the puddle, and sat down in it.

She sat with her shoulder against the door. Gigichi watched her do it. She watched the woman’s whole body go into the curve of the root and the root take it, the back curving round her shoulders the way the bench’s back had come up round Gigichi’s on the night he came home; and she watched Ada put her hand flat on the wood of the door, very gently, as if she were putting it on her child’s back through the wall.

And Ada began to cry. Properly, for the first time. Not into her knees. Sitting up, with her hand on the door, with her face doing all of it at once.

Gigichi felt something go up in her.

It went up the way the bench had come up, slowly, from underneath, from the place her hand was on the ground, and through her, and set. Not a flame; she’d never been able to feel it like that, the way Thea said, a candle going up a step. More like a root finding water. A small sure thing, a long way down, taking hold.

In front of her face, in the rain, where only she could see it:

She looked at it for a long time.

Room. The other half of Briar. Not not here. Here. There’s room.

She didn’t post it. Not yet. She sat at the end of the step with her hand on the ground and her heart going, and let Ada cry, and didn’t say a single thing.

Jory Fowler came off the lake at midnight.

He’d been out on the far side with the night lines when they brought the girl in; nobody had been able to reach him until the boats came in. Gigichi heard him before she saw him: running up the lake road in the dark in his sea boots, which nobody can run in, with the rain coming off him in sheets and somebody’s lantern swinging behind him. He came in at the gate under the elder and saw his wife in a chair made of tree against the door of the infirmary, and stopped dead.

“Ada—“

“They won’t let us in,” said Ada. “She’s all right. She’s asleep. Odile says. They won’t let us in.”

He stood there, a big man in oilskins with his hands opening and shutting, looking at the door, and at his wife, and at the root, and at Gigichi on the end of the step, and not knowing where to put himself. Gigichi saw it. She’d never in her life been able to see that, before: where somebody wanted to be. She saw it now as plainly as she could see the rain. He wanted to be by the door, and he couldn’t bear to be by the door; he wanted to be near his wife and he couldn’t sit still near anybody; he wanted to stand, and walk, and stand, and he wanted something to hold on to while he did.

Her hand was still on the ground.

The second root came up at the other side of the door. Not a seat. A rail, waist-high, smooth, curving out from the infirmary wall and along the front of the step and back, like the rail of a boat. Something to hold. Something to walk along, and back, and along, with your hands on it, all night, and still be by the door.

Jory looked at it. Then he put both his big hands on it, and held on, and Gigichi watched his shoulders come down an inch.

She hadn’t thought it. She hadn’t planned it. It had come up out of the ground the way the first one had, from the elder’s roots, from the place under her hand, the shape of exactly what he needed; and she understood, sitting there in the wet with her silver hair in rats’ tails, that it hadn’t come from her at all. Not from her thinking. It had come from him. From where he wanted to be. She’d only been sitting there.

You were trying to make a chair, Higu had said. For nobody. Nobody was waiting in it.

She thought she might cry. She didn’t. She sat on the step.

The others came at about one, with the kettle and the lamp oil and Chi’s knife and a basket of things Xola had thought of, which was everything. They stopped in the gate under the elder and looked.

Gigichi had stopped noticing, by then. There were five of them now. Ada’s seat against the door. Jory’s rail. A low wide stool by the well for the old woman from the next hut, Ada’s mother, who’d come down at midnight in two shawls and sat on it with her rosary and not said a word; it had come up exactly the height of a woman with bad knees. A curve of root along the yard wall for Lissa’s brother, who was twelve and furious and wouldn’t sit near anybody, and who’d climbed onto it and sat with his back to everybody and his face to the lake. And a plain flat seat a little way off, in the dry under the elder itself, that nobody had sat in yet.

Gigichi hadn’t made any of them. She’d sat on the end of the step with her hand on the ground and watched people come in at the gate, and something had come up under each of them the shape of where they wanted to be.

Xola stood in the gate with Squiddles on her head and the basket in her arms and her mouth open.

“Gi,” she said. “Gi.”

“Don’t,” said Gigichi.

“I’m not going to say anything.”

“You’re going to hug me.”

“I’m not.” Xola put the basket down. She looked at the roots, and at Gigichi’s face, and her own face did several things. “Later,” she said. “I’m going to hug you later. You can’t stop me.”

“Watch me,” said Gigichi, and her voice cracked, and Xola very kindly pretended it hadn’t.

Chi took the kettle inside. Odile opened the door a hand’s width and took it, and the knife, and the oil, and looked out at the yard over Chi’s shoulder, at Ada in her chair against the door and Jory at his rail and the old woman by the well and the boy on the wall, and her soft old face crumpled up completely.

“Oh, look,” said Odile, and shut the door again, quickly, before she could cry on anybody.

Gigichi put it in the channel then. It seemed like the moment.

Higu didn’t post Everybody’s fine.

Gigichi waited for it. Sitting on the end of the step with the channel fading in front of her face. Higu had posted it every night for two years, at the end, the last line, whatever had happened: Everybody’s fine. When it was true and when it nearly wasn’t. It was the thing you waited for so you could sleep.

It didn’t come.

Gigichi looked up the hill toward the militia hall, where there’d be one lamp still lit in the loft window, and Higu at it with the list.

Then she looked at the shut door, and Ada Fowler’s hand flat on the wood.

No, she thought. She’s right. Not tonight.

Thea came out at the first grey.

The door opened, not far, and she came out sideways through the gap and shut it again behind her, very quietly, and stood on the step with her back against it, and closed her eyes.

She looked terrible. She’d been up all night with her hands on a child’s hand, reading it, and it showed: her face was grey with tiredness and her eyes were red and there was a smear of blood under her nose where it had bled and she’d wiped it with the back of her wrist. She stood there with her eyes shut for a long time, breathing.

Ada was asleep in her chair, with her shoulder against the door and her hand still on it. Jory was asleep on his feet at his rail, his forehead on his arms. The old woman was awake, by the well, with her rosary, and watched Thea with eyes like a bird’s.

“She’s asleep,” said Thea. Very low, to the old woman. “She’s all right. It hasn’t moved. It hasn’t gone past the wrist.” She opened her eyes. “Maren thinks she can get it out. When I’ve read it. I want to know what it is before anybody touches it.”

The old woman nodded once, and went back to her beads.

Then Thea looked round the yard.

Gigichi watched her do it. She watched Thea see the seat against the door, and the rail, and the stool, and the wall, and the root climbing up out of the cobbles and the cobbles stacked neatly to one side; and watched her face, which had been grey and shut, open, slowly, all the way, the way it used to in a café a long time ago when Gigichi had said something true without trying and Thea hadn’t expected her to.

“Gi,” said Thea.

“Don’t,” said Gigichi.

“You did this.”

“It came up. I was sitting here.”

Thea looked at her a long moment. Then she came along the step, under the eaves, and sat down.

There was a seat there. Gigichi hadn’t noticed it come. Right beside her own place on the end of the step. It had come up at some point in the night without her feeling it, a low curve of root against the wall, the width of one person, so close to where Gigichi sat that whoever sat in it would have her shoulder against Gigichi’s.

Thea sat in it. Her shoulder went against Gigichi’s. She let her head go back against the wet wall, and shut her eyes, and breathed out, a long breath, all the way down, as if she’d been holding it since the evening.

The seat had been for her. Gigichi saw that. It had come up for Thea in the night, the shape of where Thea wanted to be, before Thea had even come out of the door; and where Thea wanted to be, after a night with her hands on a child’s grey palm, was against Gigichi’s shoulder on a wet step.

They’d been best friends once. On Earth, a long time ago. And then they’d drifted, and nobody had done anything, and a summoning had put them in the same long room for two years and a half, and they’d been polite. And then a night last summer when Thea’s nose had bled on Gigichi’s blanket and Gigichi had held her wrist. And then this.

“Oh, shut up,” said Gigichi. To the seat.

Thea laughed. A small one, with her eyes shut.

He came at full light.

Gigichi saw him come down the lake road the way she’d seen him come down it a hundred times from the bench: not hurrying, a man walking to the end of a garden. No rod. He came in at the gate under the elder and stopped, and looked at the yard, at the family asleep in the roots and Thea asleep on Gigichi’s shoulder and the old woman by the well; and then at the door.

He didn’t go to it. He stood in the gate.

He’d have heard. Of course he’d heard. Everybody in Wrenhaven had heard by now, and somebody would have walked out to the cabin with it, or Alea would have come back up the road at midnight from the town and told him, or he’d just known, the way he knew things. A girl at the bottom of his road with a hook in her hand and the grey in it.

Gigichi looked at his face and couldn’t read anything in it at all. Which was what his face did when there was everything in it.

There was one seat in the yard nobody had sat in. The plain flat one in the dry, under the elder, a little way from everybody, that had come up at about midnight with no one in it, and Gigichi hadn’t understood what it was for.

It faced the gate. And the door. Both at once, from a little apart. So that whoever sat in it could see the road coming in and the door of the room where the child was, and wasn’t close enough to either to have to say anything to anybody.

He looked at it for a while.

Then he went and sat in it.

He sat with his elbows on his knees and his hands together between them, and looked at the shut door, and didn’t move. Gigichi had seen him sit like that once before, on the end of the jetty, on the night the letter came up the river. As if he were waiting for something that was going to take a long time and that he wasn’t going to leave.

She didn’t say you okay. Not this time. She didn’t need to. She knew the answer, and so did he, and so did the seat.

She left her hand on the ground.
