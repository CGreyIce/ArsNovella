The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 6: Nine

Chapter 179

Snow

7,266 words · 32 min read

Alea knew it had snowed before she opened her eyes.

It was the light. It came in through the loft window wrong, too white and too soft, through a red curtain with a gold fringe that hung crookedly and let a stripe of it in down one side. It lay on the rafters the way light lies on the inside of a shell. And it was quiet. Not the ordinary quiet of the lake before dawn, which was full of small things, water on the posts and reeds and the hens shifting in the yard. The other kind. The kind where everything has a blanket over it and is holding its breath.

She lay under her own blanket and listened to it for a while.

Then she got up and pulled the curtain back and looked.

White. All of it. The yard, and the woodpile, and the hen-house with three humps on the roof that were hens, very offended. The field going down to the water with the cut rows gone soft under it, and at the bottom of the field a ring of briar that had been white with frost for a week and was white with something thicker now, heaped on the thorns, and green still, inside, where Gigichi’s pumpkins had been. The long sailcloth roof over the forge on the shingle, sagging in the middle under the weight. The jetty, a white line going out into the black. And the lake. Still black, still open, but edged now all the way round with a thin grey lip of ice where the reeds were, and the snow falling into it and vanishing, and falling, and vanishing, without a sound.

The first snow. On the lake. She’d seen twelve of them. She’d never once seen one with a king asleep on the rug downstairs.

She’d turned eighteen on the fourth night he was here. The same week as last year, near enough; the king said it was getting to be a habit. He’d given her, at supper, in front of everyone, a whetstone out of his roll that had been his father’s, and gone red to the beard. Halvard had said, gravely, Many happy returns, which nobody in Wrenhaven had ever heard anyone say out loud, and then looked as if he wished he hadn’t, and then as if he didn’t.

She looked at the step.

Kestra was on it. Sitting where she’d sat every night for a week, with a blanket round her shoulders and a sword across her knees and a book on the boards beside her. There was snow on her. On her shoulders, on her hair, on the blanket, on the scabbard, a finger thick. She hadn’t brushed it off. She was looking at the lake.

Alea watched her for a moment. Then she got dressed, and buckled on the knife, left side, and took Astra off the hook, and went down the ladder very quietly past the king, who was asleep on the rug on his back with his mouth open and the big red hen asleep on his chest, and opened the door.

Kestra didn’t look round.

“You’re late,” she said.

They’d done it every morning that week. Kestra hadn’t asked. She’d just been on the shingle at first light on the first morning, with her sword, waiting, and said again when Alea came down, and Alea had done it again, and that was that.

She’d done it a year ago too. When the king came the first time. Every morning, in the militia hall yard, by the water trough, with PenguChi beside her and Waddles watching from the edge of the trough. Again. And then Kestra had gone south with the king and Alea had gone on doing it every morning on her own, on the shingle, saying again to herself under her breath, because it had seemed rude to stop just because Kestra wasn’t there.

Kestra had noticed. On the first morning this week. She’d watched Alea go through the first form, and then the second, and stopped her halfway through the third and said, who’s been saying again to you? And Alea had said me, and Kestra had looked at her for a long time and written something in her book.

They went down onto the shingle in the snow.

It squeaked under their boots. Alea had forgotten that. The first snow always squeaked. She stood on the shingle with the snow coming down on her ears, which she hated, and flicked them, and drew Astra off her back in one movement, the way Gabe’s grip let her, the way it fit her hand like a cup, and set her feet.

“Window,” said Kestra.

Alea blinked. Kestra never asked that first. Kestra asked it at the end, by the trough, the two of them comparing, with PenguChi.

She opened it anyway.

[ Alea · Tier 2: Tempered · Progress to Honed: 31% ]

Kestra looked at it. She’d always been able to see Alea’s; Alea had shown her, the first week, a year ago, and after that she’d just turned it round whenever Kestra looked.

“Thirty-one,” said Kestra.

“It was fourteen in the spring.”

“I know. I was there.” Kestra looked at the number a moment longer. “I was Drawn at seventeen. Tempered at twenty. You’re eighteen and a third of the way to Honed.” She looked at Alea, not at the window. “Don’t let it go to your head.”

“I won’t.”

“It’s already gone to your ears,” said Kestra. “They’re up.”

Alea put her ears down. They went up again. She gave up.

“Again,” said Kestra.

The first form was the cast. The second was the draw. The third was the one that sang, if you did it right, the long sweep that Astra liked best, where the air went past the flat of the blade and made a sound like a bowed string, low, and hung there.

She did them in the snow. All three. And again. And again.

Kestra didn’t correct her. That was new. Kestra had corrected everything, a year ago. Her shoulders, her hands, her elbows, the angle of her chin. Again. This week she’d hardly said a word. She stood a little way off on the shingle with her sword point-down in the snow and both hands on the pommel, and watched. Not Alea’s hands. Not her shoulders.

Her feet.

Alea felt her watch them. She’d felt it all week. It made her clumsy at first. Then it didn’t.

On the ninth time through, at the end of the third form, with the long low note still hanging in the air over the shingle and the snow going sideways through it, Kestra said:

“Stop there.”

Alea stopped. Astra’s point in the snow. Her weight on the back foot. Breathing.

“Look down,” said Kestra.

Alea looked down.

Her boots, in the snow. Planted. Square. Weight on the back one, the way her father had taught her and the king had taught her father and everyone had taught everyone, all the way back to a forge in Brakewater, probably. Nothing wrong with them. She’d done the form a thousand times. She knew where her feet went.

“Where are they pointing?” said Kestra.

Alea looked.

They were pointing at the jetty. Not at Kestra. Not at the lake. At the jetty, at the end of it, where the white line of the boards stopped at the black water and there was nothing at all, no rail, no post, just the last plank.

She looked at them for a long time.

“They always do that,” said Kestra. “At the end of the third form. Every time. This week, last year, in the yard at the hall. Wherever you are. You finish the third form and your feet go to the end of the jetty.” She looked at the jetty too. “Last year it was the north, from the hall. You couldn’t see it. They pointed at it anyway.”

“I didn’t know,” said Alea.

“Nobody does,” said Kestra. “That’s why I write it down.” She took her hands off her pommel. “Mouths lie. Feet don’t. Somebody told me that when I was twenty-one, and I’ve never once found it wrong.” She was quiet. “I didn’t look at my own for thirteen years. I found out at the top of your lake road, last week, that mine point here too.”

Alea looked at her.

Kestra’s face didn’t do anything. Kestra’s face never did. But her ears had gone pink, which Alea had never seen, and Kestra didn’t have the kind of ears that went pink.

“It isn’t a fault,” said Kestra. “In the form. I’m not telling you to fix it.” She looked at Alea’s feet in the snow. “I’m telling you because one day you’ll be standing somewhere you don’t know, in front of something you don’t know, at the end of the third form, and you’ll want to know which way’s home without having to think about it.” She lifted her sword out of the snow. “Now you know.”

Alea looked at her own feet for a long moment. Pointing at the end of a jetty where a woman had stood once, when half the planks were gone, and a man had sat every morning for nine years, and she’d knelt herself, a fortnight ago, in the dark, with a rod and a pike hook, and gone with something enormous when it turned.

“Again,” said Kestra.

Alea did it again.

The king was on the step when they came up.

In his shirtsleeves, in the snow, with a cup of tea in each hand and the red hen sitting on his foot. He held one out to Kestra and one to Alea. He’d made them himself. Alea could tell, because they were much too strong, and because her father was standing in the cabin door behind him with the kettle in his hand looking like a man who’d been told I’ll do it in a voice that didn’t take an answer.

“You’ll want to go before noon,” said her father. To the king. Quietly. “If it keeps on. The road over the ridge past Hethe closes in the second snow. You know it does.”

“I know,” said the king.

He didn’t move. He stood on the step with his tea and looked at the snow coming down on the lake, and the hen on his foot, and the forge on the shingle with the sailcloth sagging. Alea watched him look at it. She’d watched him look at things all week. He looked at things the way she imagined a smith looked at a blade: all of it at once, and then each part, slowly, as if he’d have to make it again from memory.

“She’ll cry,” said the king. “The red one. When I go. She did last time. Kestra wrote it down.”

“The big red one cried,” said Kestra, from the snow, without opening her book. “He pretended not to see. He saw.”

The king looked down at the hen on his foot.

“Captain,” he said, gravely. “I have to go and be a king for a while. It’s very dull. You’d hate it.” The hen looked up at him with one eye. “I’ll come back. In the frost. I’ve told everybody.”

Alea’s father made a sound in the doorway behind him.

“You’ve told everybody what?” he said.

“That I’m coming back,” said the king. “Next autumn. I wrote it to Gabe. I wrote it to Oswin. I told Halvard on the road and he said yes, Severin, the way he does when he’s already put it in a drawer.” He drank his tea. “I’m a king. I’m allowed to have next year in my mouth. Everybody else does.”

Her father didn’t say anything. Alea couldn’t see his face. She never could. But she saw his shoulders, in the doorway, with the kettle, and she saw the king see them too, the way the king could see everything, and not say anything about it.

Halvard came out last.

In his grey coat, buttoned to the throat, with his saddlebags over his arm, because he still wouldn’t let anyone carry them. He walked better than he had a week ago. Alea had watched him walk all week. The first night he’d got off his horse like a man folded the wrong way twice. This morning he came down the step into the snow like a man who had slept in a bed for seven nights and been fed terrible stew and taken out to the end of a jetty every evening, and was not sure what to do with any of it.

He stopped beside her. She’d been standing a little way off, with her tea, waiting, while the king saddled the horses himself because nobody could stop him.

He didn’t say anything for a moment. He looked at her the way he’d looked at her on the first night, at the bottom of the step. Not looking away. He never looked away. She’d decided on the second day that she liked that about him, and on the third that she’d tell him, and hadn’t.

“Alea,” he said.

“My lord.”

“Halvard.” He said it quite simply. “I’m told nobody in this house calls anybody my lord. The king’s been informed of it several times.”

Something happened in the corner of her mouth.

“Halvard,” she said.

He nodded, as if that were settled. Then he looked down at the snow between them, and she could see him deciding something. She’d seen her father do it a thousand times. You couldn’t see his face, but you could see him decide. Halvard was easier. She could see his.

“You asked me something,” he said. “On the third night. At the table. While he was out on the jetty.”

She had. She’d been ashamed of it afterwards. She’d asked it the way you’d ask a thing at a table when the lamp’s low and you’ve had a cup of the king’s tea, which was strong enough to take paint off a door. What does it look like? His face. When I’m talking. And Halvard had looked at her across the table for a long time and said it isn’t mine to give, and she’d said I know, sorry, I know, and gone red to the tips of her ears and gone up the ladder.

“It isn’t mine to give,” said Halvard. “It still isn’t. It’s his, and he’d give it to you himself if he could. He can’t. Nobody knows why. Nobody under the sixth tier can hold it, and nobody’s ever been able to tell me why it’s the sixth and not the fifth or the seventh, and I’ve asked.” He was quiet. “But I’ll give you one thing. Not his face. Something I saw.”

Alea stood very still in the snow.

“Your mother,” said Halvard. “At Cinderhold. In the infirmary tent. Nearly twenty years ago.” He looked at the lake. “He came in with a cut. He always came in with a cut. He was the most cut boy on the whole border, and she stitched every one, and argued with him the whole time she did it. And every time he came in through the flap of the tent, with his arm wrapped up in his shirt and that scowl on him, she looked up from whatever she was doing.” He was quiet. “And her face did a thing. For about a heartbeat. As if she’d been waiting all day. And then she put it away and said you again and told him to sit down.”

Alea didn’t breathe.

“On the fourth night,” said Halvard, “you told the king about the pike. For the fourth time. At the table. And the king pretended he hadn’t heard it, and asked you about the hook.” He looked at her. “And your father’s face did that. The thing hers did. For about a heartbeat. And then he put it away and went on mending the net.”

The snow came down between them.

“That’s all,” said Halvard. “That’s the one thing.” He shifted the saddlebags on his arm. “I thought you ought to have it. Since you’re the only person at this lake who can’t see it, and the only one it’s for.”

Alea opened her mouth. Nothing came out.

She put her hand on her chest instead. Flat. Over her heart.

Halvard looked at it for a moment. Then he put his on his. And then, after a moment, he did something she hadn’t seen him do all week. He smiled. Not much. A thin grey man in a grey coat in the snow, smiling, very slightly, as if he weren’t sure he was doing it right.

He was doing it right.

They went before noon.

The king on his big plain bay, with the leather roll behind the saddle a good deal lighter than it had come. Halvard on his tall quiet horse that wouldn’t do anything without being asked three times. Kestra on her grey, with snow on her shoulders still, because she hadn’t brushed it off, and three packhorses on a string. Up the lake road, between the white field walls, slowly, in the falling snow.

The red hen cried.

She actually did. Alea had thought Kestra was exaggerating; Kestra never exaggerated. The hen stood in the middle of the lake road with her feathers fluffed out and made a noise Alea had never heard a hen make, a long low creaking wail, like a door in a storm, and then ran after the horses. Up the lake road. In the snow. As fast as a hen can run, which was very fast and very silly. And the king reined in and turned in the saddle and looked back at her, and Alea saw his face, and then she had to go and get the hen.

She carried it back down the lake road in both arms, with its head under her chin, still creaking. It pecked her once. Not hard. She didn’t mind.

At the top of the lake road, by the square, the riders stopped.

Alea saw them stop. The three of them, small, against the white. And she saw one of them turn round in the saddle. Not the king. He’d already looked back, at the hen. Not Halvard. He was looking ahead, up the north road, very straight.

Kestra.

Kestra turned round in her saddle at the top of the lake road and looked back. Down the whole length of it. Past the field walls and the hedge and the cabin. At the jetty. At the step. At the shingle where they’d stood every morning for a week in the snow saying again.

Kestra never looked back. Alea knew that the way she knew anything about anybody: she’d watched her for a year. Kestra looked at what was in front of her, always, because that was where the king was going.

She looked back for a long time.

Then she turned round. And the three of them went over the top of the road into the square and were gone, and there was only the snow coming down on the lake road where they’d been, and the hoofprints, already filling.

Alea stood at the gate with the hen in her arms.

Her father was beside her. She hadn’t heard him come. She never did. He stood at the gate with his hands at his sides and looked up the empty road for a long time.

“He’ll write,” said Alea.

“He said he would.”

“Not is he well.”

“No,” said her father. “I told him not to write that. He asked what he should write instead.” He was quiet. “I said anything. He said how are the hens.” He looked at the hen in her arms. “I said they hate me. He said good.”

Alea laughed. She didn’t mean to. It came out of her into the hen’s feathers.

The hen pecked her again.

Group 1 came down in the afternoon, with a war on.

Alea heard it before she saw it. She was in the cabin with her father, at the table, cleaning Astra, and he was mending the net he’d been mending all week and never finished because people kept coming and talking to him, and the noise came down the lake road like a flock of birds. Shrieking. Xola’s shriek, high and delighted, and Gigichi’s voice going you absolute and something Alea didn’t catch, and a thump.

She went to the door.

The five of them were coming down the lake road in the snow. Not walking. Fighting. Gigichi in front, scowling, with a snowball in each hand and snow all down her back where somebody had put it. Xola behind her, shrieking, with Squiddles on her shoulder gone bright pink with excitement and flinging snow with two of his arms, which was cheating and everybody said so. Thea with her hands up, laughing, saying I’m not playing, I’m not playing, and getting hit in the back of the head by Higu, who was walking very calmly at the back with her notebook in one hand and a snowball in the other and an expression of perfect innocence.

And PenguChi.

PenguChi wasn’t fighting. PenguChi was standing in the middle of the lake road, quite still, with her hands clasped in front of her and her golden eyes very wide, watching Waddles.

Waddles had found the snow.

He’d been in snow before. Last winter, every day. But he’d never, apparently, been in the first snow, on a slope, going down to a lake. He stood at the top of the field below the road and looked at the long white slope going down to the water, and then he lay down on his front, flat, and pushed off with his feet.

And went.

All the way down the field. On his stomach. Through the cut rows of the pumpkin field, fast, faster, a small round yellow shape skimming down the white slope like a stone across a pond, with his wings out flat behind him and his beak up, past the end of Gigichi’s hedge, across the bottom of the field, over the edge of the shingle, and into the lake with a small neat splash.

He came up. He shook his head. He swam to the shore, and climbed out, and waddled all the way back up the field, very slowly, very seriously, to the top. And lay down. And went again.

“Oh,” said PenguChi, very softly, with both hands over her mouth. “Oh, baby.”

The snowball fight stopped. Everybody turned round and watched Waddles go down the field. Xola was crying. Of course she was. Gigichi had dropped both her snowballs. Higu had her notebook open and was writing very fast. Thea had both hands over her mouth too, laughing into them, and the snow coming down on her pink and violet hair.

Alea’s father came out of the cabin door behind her.

He stood on the step and watched a penguin go down his pumpkin field on its stomach into his lake, for the third time, in the first snow. Alea couldn’t see his face. She looked at it anyway. She’d given up not looking, a long time ago.

Then she remembered Halvard. For about a heartbeat.

She looked at his shoulders.

They’d gone. All the way. The way they’d gone at the gate at Cinderhold, Yan said, under a wall of old soldiers with their hands on their chests.

He laughed. Out loud, on the step. The real one, that she’d heard perhaps a dozen times in her life. And Gigichi heard it from the road and turned round, and saw him, and saw where Alea was standing, at the bottom of the step, in front of him, unguarded.

And threw a snowball at her.

It hit her in the ear. The left one. Square inside, full of snow.

Alea stood there with snow in her ear and her mouth open.

“Chi 1,” said her father, from the step, in English, very gravely.

“She was open,” said Gigichi.

Alea put Astra down against the step, very carefully. And bent down. And picked up two handfuls of snow. And went up the lake road at a run.

The hens came for her father at about the fourth snowball.

Alea didn’t see it start. She saw the end of it. She was behind the woodpile with Xola and Squiddles, under siege from Gigichi and Higu, who were behind the hen-house and had apparently made a pact, and she heard a noise from the shingle, and looked round.

The big red hen had come out of the yard.

She hadn’t come out all afternoon. She’d sat in the hen-house since the king left, in the dark, making the creaking noise at intervals, and refused grain, and pecked Xola, who’d gone in to comfort her. And now she’d come out. And she’d seen him. Standing on the shingle by the forge, alone, with his hands in his pockets, watching a snowball fight in his yard. Without a king anywhere to stop her.

She went for him.

She went for him the way she’d gone for him every day of Alea’s life. Low and fast and furious, across the snow, with her neck out and her wings half open and the other two coming out of the yard behind her in a line. And Alea saw her father see her coming. And look round. For the woodpile. Which had four girls and a squid behind it.

He looked at the jetty.

He went down the jetty. Fast. Not running. He never ran. But fast. Along the white boards in the snow, with three hens after him in a line, all the way to the end. And stopped there. On the last plank, with the black water in front of him and three hens behind him, on a jetty in the snow, with nowhere at all to go.

He turned round and faced them.

The red hen stopped. Two planks off. And looked at him.

Everybody had stopped. All five of them. Alea, behind the woodpile. Every snowball in every hand.

Alea’s father stood on the end of his jetty in the first snow with three hens in front of him, and looked down at the big red one for a long moment.

“He’ll be back in the frost,” he said. In the kingdom’s tongue. Very quietly. To the hen. “He said so. He’s told everybody.”

The hen looked at him.

“I miss him too,” said her father.

And the hen sat down.

On the jetty. Two planks off. In the snow. She fluffed her feathers out and sat down, and the other two sat down behind her, in a line, and the three of them looked at him. Not attacking. Not anything. Just sitting there, on the end of his jetty, the way you’d sit with somebody.

He didn’t move. He stood on the last plank with his hands in his pockets and looked at three hens who’d hated him for nine years sitting in the snow in front of him.

Then, very slowly, he sat down too.

On the end of the jetty. Two planks off from the hens. With his legs over the black water.

Nobody threw a snowball for a long time.

“Higu,” said Xola, at last, in a whisper, behind the woodpile. “Higu, are you writing this down?”

“Obviously,” said Higu.

Rowan came down at dusk with the letter.

Alea was in the kitchen, soaked through, steaming in front of the fire in a blanket, with snow melting out of her ear. The others had gone back up the hill an hour ago, wet and loud, with Waddles asleep inside the front of PenguChi’s coat and Gigichi saying it was a fair throw all the way up the lake road to anybody who would listen. Her father was still on the jetty. With the hens. He’d been there an hour. None of them had moved.

Rowan knocked. Three times. She knew the knock.

He stood on the step in the snow with his big grey head bare and a letter in his hand. Brown paper, folded and sealed, gone soft at one corner where it had got wet.

“The carrier,” he said. “Came in this afternoon, over the ridge, ahead of the snow. Three weeks late; he always is, in the autumn. It’s been sitting in a sack at Hethe.” He held it out. “It’s got Wrenhaven on it. And the lake girl. And then somebody’s put the fox one, by the water underneath in a different hand, I think at Hethe, because they couldn’t make out who the lake girl was.”

Alea took it.

She knew the hand before she turned it over. Big and leaning, with a blot on the W the shape of a crab.

“Sorrel,” she said.

“I don’t know,” said Rowan. “It just says the lake girl.” He looked at her face. He was one of the only people in Wrenhaven who looked at her face when he talked to her and not at her ears. He’d always done it. “Is it good?”

“It’s the other one,” said Alea. “She sent two. The same letter. One to Ashby, one here. In case.” She looked at the crab. “People always come back the way they went.”

Rowan nodded slowly, as if that made perfect sense, which from a man who’d waited sixty days with the cups out it probably did.

“Then you’ve read it,” he said.

“I’ve read the other one.”

“Ah,” said Rowan. He looked at the letter in her hands. “Odette copies things. Out, for the council. When it’s important, she copies it twice, in case one goes in the river.” He put his hat on. “She says you never get the same letter twice. Not if you write it by hand. She says it’s the second one that tells you what the person meant.”

He went back up the lake road in the snow.

Alea stood in the door with the letter.

She read it at the table, under the lamp, in her blanket.

It was the same. Nearly. The same big leaning hand, the same blots, the same crossings-out. Lake girl, I got yours. The bucket, and the crab-blot, and about my dad. The squall off the Gullhaven bar. The boat coming back on the morning tide with nobody in it. I bought her back. NEXT YEAR. She leaks like a basket.

She’d read the other one nine times. She knew it nearly by heart. She read this one slowly, line by line, the way Halvard read things, the way Kestra watched feet, looking for where it was different.

It was different in small ways. A word here. Very instead of really. A blot in a different place. Sorrel had written the gulls think I’m mad in one and the gulls think I’m cracked in the other. Alea liked cracked better.

And near the bottom of the first page, it was different in one large way.

In the Ashby letter there was a place scrubbed out. So hard the paper had gone thin. She’d held it up to the lamp at Annis’s table and seen the shape of a word through it. Since. And nothing else; Sorrel had scrubbed out the rest before she’d finished the line.

In this one, it wasn’t scrubbed out.

It was there. In the same place. Near the bottom of the first page, after you said you’d tell your mum about me, so I thought I’d tell my dad about you. A line, in the big leaning hand, with no blots on it at all, as if she’d written it very slowly and very carefully and then stopped and looked at it for a long time before she went on.

Nobody’s said his name since. Not on the quay. Not at home. Not the guild. They all say “your dad” and “him” and “the boat”. It’s like he’s a thing that happened. His name was Tobyn.

Alea looked at it.

She looked at it for a long time, under the lamp, with the snow coming down outside and the fire ticking and her ear still wet.

Sorrel had written it twice. And scrubbed it out once. On the first copy, the one she’d sent the long way round through Ashby, she’d got as far as since, and stopped, and taken it all out, so hard the paper went thin. And then she’d sat down and written the whole letter again, the second copy, for Wrenhaven, for the lake, and come to the same place. And left it in.

It’s the second one that tells you what the person meant.

Alea put the letter down very carefully on the table.

Then she got up, and put her boots on, and went out.

Her father was still on the end of the jetty. With the hens.

He looked round when she came down the boards. He didn’t say anything. He moved over, a little, on the last plank, and the hens shifted and glared at her and didn’t get up.

She didn’t sit down. She stood beside him at the very end, on the edge, in the dusk, with the snow coming down on the black water and vanishing into it, the way it had all day. With her feet pointing where Kestra said they always pointed. Here.

“Sorrel’s dad,” she said. “Off the Gullhaven bar.”

“I remember.”

“Nobody’s said his name. Since. On the whole quay. Two years. Not the guild. Not her mum. They say your dad and him and the boat.” She held the letter in both hands against her chest, through the blanket. “She scrubbed it out of the first letter. She left it in the second.”

Her father didn’t say anything. She felt him not say it. The way you feel weather.

Alea looked out at the lake.

It wasn’t the sea. She knew that. Sorrel’s dad had gone over the side into the sea, a long way south, off a sandbar she’d never seen, in a squall at night, hauling. This was a lake in the north. Fresh water. Black and still, with the first snow on it and a lip of ice coming in round the reeds. It wasn’t the same water. It wasn’t anywhere near the same water.

She’d asked her father once, when she was small, where the lake went. And he’d said down the Wren to the river, and down the river to Kingsferry, and out past Kingsferry into the sea. All of it? she’d said. Eventually, he’d said. Water goes where it’s going. It takes its time.

She put one hand flat on the snow on the end of the jetty. The last plank. The way she’d put it on the water a fortnight ago, after the pike.

“Tobyn,” said Alea.

Out loud. To the lake. In the snow.

Her father went very still beside her.

“Your daughter’s called Sorrel,” said Alea. To the water. “She came at me like a pike, in Valcrest, at the Lists. With your hooks. She pulled me over by my own sword. She got second. She bought your boat back with it.” Her voice did something. She let it. “She’s called it Next Year. It leaks. She goes out past the bar in it every morning before the guild boats, and talks to you, and the gulls think she’s cracked. She told you about me. So I’m telling you about her.”

The snow came down.

“She’s going to lose,” said Alea. “Next year. To me. I just thought you should know. So you can tell her it’s coming.”

She stopped.

She didn’t know what else to say. She stood on the end of the jetty with her hand flat on the snow and her eyes stinging, and the lake didn’t say anything, because lakes don’t. The snow went on falling into it and vanishing.

Her father put his hand on her shoulder.

He didn’t say anything. He just left it there. Warm. It was always warm.

And on the plank behind them, the big red hen got up, and walked very slowly past him, round him, to the very edge, and stood there beside Alea at the end of the jetty, looking at the water too. And didn’t peck anybody.

Her window opened on its own, on the way back along the jetty.

She didn’t ask it to. It just came up in front of her face in the dusk, white and plain, the way it did sometimes when something moved and wanted her to know.

She looked at it.

It moves when I put something down, her father had said, to Higu, on the end of this jetty. Not when I do something. She’d heard Higu tell it to the others, after, in the militia hall kitchen, and she’d thought it was only true for him. Because he was the Black Blade, and old, and strange, and everything about him was only true for him.

She hadn’t put anything down. She’d picked something up. A name. Off a page. Somebody else’s, that she’d never heard said.

She looked at the thirty-two for a long time.

Then she thought: no. That’s not it.

She’d put something down too. She’d been carrying it since Ashby. Since a posting house kitchen with Annis frying onions, and a scrubbed-out word held up to the lamp, and I thought there was nowhere for her to go. She’d been carrying a girl on a quay, a long way south, talking out loud to the sea every morning, on her own. With nobody to say the name back.

Somebody had said it back now.

She closed the window. She went up the shingle to the cabin with her father beside her and three hens behind them in a line, in the snow, not attacking anybody.

She wrote back that night.

In the loft, under the crooked curtain, by a candle, on her knees on the floor with the paper on the lid of her chest, because there wasn’t a table up there and she didn’t want to write it at the kitchen table where her father would see her face doing whatever it was going to do. He’d see her shoulders anyway. She didn’t mind that.

Sorrel,

I got the other one. The Wrenhaven one. It came over the ridge today in the first snow, three weeks late, with “the fox one, by the water” written on the back by somebody at Hethe who couldn’t work out who the lake girl was. Everyone here knows.

It’s different from the Ashby one. You said “cracked” in this one instead of “mad”. Cracked is better.

And you left it in.

I said it. Tonight. Off the end of our jetty, in the snow, with my hand on the boards. Out loud. “Tobyn.” I told him you came at me like a pike with his hooks. I told him about the boat. I told him you’re going to lose next year, so he can tell you it’s coming.

It isn’t the same water. I know. It’s a lake. But my dad says the lake goes down the Wren, and the Wren goes down to the river, and the river goes out past Kingsferry to the sea. All of it. Eventually. He says water goes where it’s going and takes its time.

So it’ll get there. Maybe next year. Maybe off your bar, some morning, when you’re out in NEXT YEAR before the guild boats, talking. Somebody said his name. That’s all. It’ll get there.

Our hens sat with my dad on the end of the jetty today for an hour. They’ve hated him for nine years. Nobody knows why they stopped. I think I do. I’m not telling you. You’ll see.

Next year.

Alea. (the lake girl) (the fox one, by the water)

P.S. I’m thirty-two percent to Honed. I’m telling you so you’re frightened.

P.P.S. Is it Tobyn with a y? I spelled it with a y.

She read it over. Once. She didn’t change anything. She folded it, and sealed it with a drop of candle-wax and her thumb, because she didn’t have a seal either, nobody in this house had a seal, and wrote on the outside, in the big careful letters she’d learned at the kitchen table with her mother watching:

Sorrel. The boat-hook girl. Gullhaven quay. Ask for NEXT YEAR.

And put it on the sill, under the curtain, for the morning.

She didn’t sleep for a while.

She lay under her blanket with the candle out and looked at the stripe of white light coming in down the side of the crooked curtain. The snow had stopped. The sky had cleared. There was no moon, but there didn’t need to be; the stars were out, all of them, the wrong stars, and the snow took their light and gave it back, the way snow does, so that the whole white world outside was lit from underneath, bright enough to see every post on the jetty.

She got up, after a while, and pulled the curtain back, and looked.

The lake, black, with the stars in it, and a thin bright rim of ice all the way round. The field, white. The hedge at the bottom of it, white, heaped with snow, with green inside where nobody could see. The forge on the shingle under its sagging roof. The jetty going out into the black, a long white line.

And on the end of it, a lamp. And a man sitting beside the lamp with his legs over the water.

He was always there. She’d known he would be. She’d looked out of this window at that lamp on the end of that jetty every night of her life that she could remember, and it was always there, and she’d always gone back to sleep.

She didn’t go back to sleep.

Because there was somebody else. Coming down the lake road from the square, in the starlight, on her own. Small, wrapped up, with a lantern in one hand and something flat and square under her other arm, held against her chest. Walking very carefully on the snow, the way you’d walk if you’d thought about every step before you took it. Past the gate. Past the cabin. Not stopping. Down the shingle, to the jetty.

Higu.

With her book.

Alea watched her go out along the jetty, in the snow-light, a small dark shape with a lantern on a long white line, all the way to the end. And stop. And sit down, a little way off from the man with the lamp. Not close. Not far. The way she’d sat there once before, a fortnight ago, on the morning of the pike, and asked him something, and he hadn’t answered.

Alea let the curtain fall.

She lay down under her blanket in the dark, and looked at the stripe of pale light on the rafters, and thought about a question that hadn’t been answered, and a girl who’d come back down a lake road in the snow at midnight to ask it again.

Ask again in the spring, Gigichi had written, on a scrap of paper, under her pillow. Alea knew; Gigichi had told Xola, and Xola told everyone everything.

Higu hadn’t waited for the spring.

Higu never did.
