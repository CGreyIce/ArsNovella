The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 4: Threads

Chapter 106

Okay. Again.

4,106 words · 18 min read

Xav woke before anyone else, the way he always did.

Grey light in a small window. A low ceiling of old silver timber. The smell of woodsmoke and fish and, very faintly, hedge. Gabe asleep beside him on the floor with his mouth open and a girl’s blanket over his feet, a blanket with a fox stitched on one corner in red wool. Deston curled up on the other side of Gabe with his face in his elbow, the way he slept on buses.

The hearth was warm. The kettle was on the hook beside it, dented, wired, still faintly ticking as it cooled.

The door was open.

Xav lay there for a minute and listened. Hens, outside, complaining about something. Water against posts. And under it, so faint you’d only hear it if you’d been listening for it for nine years, on a hundred thousand rounds, through bad headphones: somebody sitting very still, on purpose, not making a sound.

He got up and went out.

Declan was on the end of the jetty.

Of course he was. He was sitting where he’d sat all night, in the place at the very end, with his legs over the water and the rod held out in front of him and the line going down into the grey. He hadn’t been to bed. Xav could tell by the set of his shoulders. He’d sat up the rest of the night on his own, after the rest of them went in, and put the kettle on again at some point, because there was a cup beside him on the boards, and then come back out here to watch the light come up on the lake.

Xav walked out along the jetty. He didn’t try to be quiet. There wasn’t any point. You couldn’t sneak up on Declan in a game and you obviously couldn’t sneak up on him here.

“Morning,” said Declan, without turning round.

“You didn’t sleep.”

“I’ll sleep later.”

“Gabe’ll hit you again.”

“Gabe’s asleep.” Declan moved along the boards, without looking, to make room. “Sit down. You’re making the fish nervous.”

Xav sat down.

They didn’t say anything for a while.

That was the thing. That had always been the thing, with Declan. Everybody else Xav had ever played with filled the silences. Talked between rounds, talked during rounds, talked about the round after the round was over. Declan talked more than any of them, most of the time. Too loud. Laughing in the middle of his own sentences. But in the last round, in the last thirty seconds, when it came down to the two of them, he’d go completely quiet. And so would Xav. And they’d sit there on comms not saying anything, two people in two rooms across a city, waiting, and it had been the most comfortable silence of Xav’s life.

This was the same silence.

He hadn’t known how much he’d missed it until he was sitting in it again.

The lake was pale grey and very still. There was mist on it in long low strips, lying on the water, and the sun wasn’t up yet but the sky behind the hills at the east end had gone the colour of the inside of a shell. The hills went up dark on every side. There was a heron standing in the reeds at the edge of the shingle, on one leg, not moving, waiting too.

“That bird’s been there an hour,” said Declan.

“It’s good.”

“It’s the best on the lake. It’s been there every morning for six years. I’ve never once seen it miss.” Declan didn’t look away from the line. “I’ve thought about asking it for lessons.”

“Want to try?” said Declan, after a while.

He held out the rod.

Xav looked at it. It was a plain thing. Long, dark, a bit worn where hands went. A reel near the bottom, wooden, with the line wound on it, and a cork. It looked like a fishing rod. It looked like exactly the kind of fishing rod you’d see leaning against the wall of any cabin on any lake in the world.

He knew what it was. He’d heard Group 3’s version of the Redmarch, in the channel, line by line, in the autumn. A man walking across the red with a rod over his shoulder. A hook going out a hundred paces. A demon reeled in through the air like a fish. One stroke of the rod’s shaft.

“Is it going to cut my hand off,” said Xav.

“Only if you ask it to.”

“I’m not going to ask it to.”

“Then no.” Declan put it in his hands. “Don’t swing it. Just hold it. The line’s already out. All you do is wait.”

Xav held it.

It was heavier than it looked, and lighter than he’d expected, both at once, and when his hand closed on the worn place on the grip he felt something in it. Not aura. Not anything he had a word for. Just a sense, very faint, of something enormous lying quietly asleep inside the wood, the way you feel a dog asleep in a dark room before you see it.

“It knows you,” said Declan.

“How?”

“I don’t know. It just does. It’s never let Rowan hold it.” Declan leaned back on his hands. “Don’t look at the line. Everybody looks at the line. Look at the water. Look at the whole lake. The line’ll tell you.”

Xav looked at the whole lake.

He found, after a minute, that he was good at it.

He’d have known that, if he’d thought about it. It was the same thing. It was exactly the same thing as holding an angle. You picked your place, and you settled, and you stopped wanting anything, and you let everything else in the world get bored before you did. The mist moved on the water. The heron stood on its one leg. Xav breathed out and let his shoulders come down and sat on the end of the jetty with the rod held out in front of him, and stopped being in a hurry for the first time since he’d woken up on a stone floor a year and a half ago.

Beside him, Declan watched him do it.

“Huh,” said Declan, quietly.

“What.”

“You’re better at this than me.”

“No, I’m not.”

“You are. You were always better at the waiting. I just started first.” Declan smiled, a little. “I’ve been telling Alea that for years.”

The sun came up.

It came up over the hills at the east end all at once, the way it does over water, and the mist went gold and then white and then started coming apart, and the whole lake lit up from one end to the other. The heron in the reeds moved, once. Came up with something silver. Swallowed it. Went still again.

“Nice,” said Declan.

“It waited.”

“It always waits.”

Xav watched the water.

“Inferno,” he said.

He didn’t know why he said it. It just came out. The way things came out, sometimes, in the last thirty seconds.

Declan didn’t ask what he meant. Declan never had to ask what he meant.

“The final,” said Declan. “Banana.”

“You held it for a minute and a half.”

“I know. You told me yesterday.”

“I’m telling you again.” Xav kept his eyes on the water. “You had the AWP. I had a pistol and forty-one health and I was in the car, at the bottom, and I wanted to go so badly I could hear my own heart. And you said wait. On comms. That’s all. Nothing else. For a minute and a half. And I waited. And they got bored. They came round the corner one at a time, the way you said they would, the way you always said they would, and you took the first one and I took the second one, with a pistol, from the car, with forty-one health.” He breathed. “That was the first round I ever won on my own. In my whole life.”

“You didn’t win it on your own. I was there.”

“I know. That’s what I mean.”

Declan didn’t say anything.

“I’ve won about four hundred since,” said Xav. “Here. In the Hollow. In a yard. On a quay, waiting nine days for a barge. Every single one of them, I hear you say it.” He swallowed. “Wait. That’s all. Nothing else.”

The line moved on the water. A tiny ring, spreading. Then nothing.

Xav didn’t move. Didn’t strike. Didn’t look at it.

“Good,” said Declan, very softly.

“What did you say,” said Xav.

It had taken him nine years to ask. He’d thought about it, on and off, for all of them. At three in the morning, on comms, in the last round, in the gap before Declan moved. That low mutter under his breath, too quiet to catch properly, a handful of words in a language Xav had never had a name for. He’d asked once, at nineteen. What’s that you say? And Declan had laughed and said you’ll find out, and never told him.

And then a yard in Duskhollow, in the summer, in the dew. Petra touching her own mouth as if it belonged to somebody else. My mouth doesn’t know the shapes.

“Before the last round,” said Xav. “Every time. And on the Redmarch, Petra said. Before you moved. You said something.”

Declan was quiet for a moment.

Then he said it.

Not under his breath, this time. Out loud. Clearly. In the morning, on the end of the jetty, to the lake.

“Audentes fortuna juvat.”

Xav didn’t move.

“It’s Latin,” said Declan. “Obviously.”

“Obviously.”

“Virgil. More or less. I don’t even remember where I first saw it. I just remember thinking it was the best thing I’d ever read.” He almost laughed. “It means fortune favours the bold.”

Xav thought about that.

“You waited,” he said. “Every round. Every single round. For nine years. You never pushed once. You held banana for a minute and a half.” He turned his head and looked at Declan for the first time since he’d sat down. “That’s not bold. That’s the opposite of bold.”

“No, it isn’t,” said Declan.

He looked at the lake.

“Anybody can run round a corner, Xav. That’s easy. Your heart’s going, your hands are going, everything in you wants to go, so you go. That’s not bold. That’s just giving up on waiting.” He shrugged. “Standing still. That’s the hard part. Standing still while every single thing in the world is coming straight at you, and your whole body’s screaming move, and you don’t. Until it’s time. That’s the boldest thing there is.” He was quiet a moment. “I said it to remind myself. Every time. Before I moved. Be bold. Stand still. Fortune likes that.”

Xav looked at him.

“Petra thought it was a prayer,” he said. “For eight years. To a god nobody here knew. And then she thought it might be a joke.”

“It was both,” said Declan. “Most things I said were both.”

The line went tight.

Not a ring this time. A pull. Sudden and hard and heavy, the rod bending in Xav’s hands, the reel ticking, something down there in the cold dark water taking hold and going. Xav’s whole body wanted to go with it. Wanted to haul, wanted to stand up, wanted to do something.

He held still.

He held still for one heartbeat. Two. The rod bent almost double. The line hissed off the wooden reel.

And then he pulled, and it came off the hook, and the line went slack, and whatever it had been went back down into the dark with his whole morning’s patience in its mouth.

Xav sat there holding a limp rod.

Beside him, Declan had both hands over his face and was shaking.

“Don’t,” said Xav.

“I’m not.”

“You are.”

“That was the pike,” said Declan, through his hands. “That was the pike. I’ve been trying to catch that pike for eight years. You had it on the line for two seconds.”

“It was my first try.”

“I know.”

Xav looked at the slack line, lying on the water.

Then he reeled it in. Slowly. The way he’d watched Declan do it. Checked the hook. It was fine. It was still baited, somehow. He held the rod out again over the end of the jetty and let the line go out, and settled, and looked at the whole lake, and let his shoulders come down.

“Okay,” said Xav. “Again.”

Declan took his hands off his face.

He looked at Xav for a long moment. Something moved in his face. Xav couldn’t hold on to it, the face. Nobody could. Everyone said so. His eyes slid off it like rain off a window. But he didn’t need the face. He’d never needed the face. He knew exactly what it was doing, from the shoulders, from the breath, from the way Declan went still beside him on the boards. The same way he’d always known, through a headset, from two words, whether Declan was about to laugh or about to cry.

Both.

“Yeah,” said Declan. His voice wasn’t quite right. “Okay. Again.”

Petra came down the lake road at mid-morning.

On her own. Before any of the others. Xav had known she would. She’d have been up at dawn in whatever bed Group 1 had found for her above the bakery, and she’d have done her standing in some backyard in Wrenhaven with the bakers watching her, an hour, two. And then she’d have buckled on her sword and walked down the lake road alone, because that was how Petra did things. Without telling anybody. Without asking.

He felt Declan notice her before he saw her.

It wasn’t anything Declan did. He didn’t turn. He didn’t move. But the stillness changed. It went from the stillness of a man fishing to the stillness of a man who has just heard a step on a road a quarter of a mile away and knows exactly whose step it is.

Xav looked round.

She’d stopped at the bottom of the road, where it ran out onto the shingle. Where Xav had stopped yesterday. She was standing very straight, the way soldiers stand, with her sword at her hip and her hands at her sides, looking along the jetty at the two of them on the end of it. She didn’t come any further.

She waited.

Of course she waited. She’d taught Xav to wait. She’d never once, in more than a year of dawns, moved first.

“That’s Petra,” said Xav.

“I know,” said Declan.

He stood up.

He did it slowly. He put the cup down on the boards. He looked at Xav, and at the rod in Xav’s hands, and said, “Don’t let it get away again,” and then he turned round and walked back along the jetty. Barefoot on the grey boards. Past the nets. The whole length of it.

Xav didn’t follow him.

He sat on the end with the rod in his hands, and watched, and held very tight to the thing Declan had asked him in the night not to say.

Declan stopped in front of her.

Xav was too far away to see their faces, properly. He didn’t need to. He could see their feet. He’d learned that from Petra too. Feet don’t lie. Watch the feet.

Hers were planted. Square. A soldier’s feet, a swordswoman’s feet, set for anything.

His were bare, and still, and set exactly the way they’d been set on the end of the jetty all morning. Easy. Settled. Waiting for nothing.

He said something.

Xav didn’t hear it. The wind was the wrong way. But he saw Petra’s whole body change. He saw her rock on her planted feet as if somebody had shoved her, gently, in the chest. He saw her hand come up off her sword and go to her mouth.

He found out later what it was. Petra told him herself, that evening, on the shingle, in a voice he’d never heard from her: very small, very young, the voice of a girl of twenty-three.

Hello, girl from the ditch.

That was all he said, at first.

Hello, girl from the ditch. As if he’d seen her last week. As if there’d been nothing in between. Eight years, and a war, and a whole life. And then, while she stood there with her hand over her mouth, unable to say anything at all:

You wouldn’t put your sword down. You were at the end. Your hands were shaking so hard you couldn’t have hit anything with it. All night. You wouldn’t put it down.

You remember, Petra had said. Barely.

I remember all forty of you, he’d said. You were the one at the end.

Xav watched her try to say it.

He’d known she had something to say. She’d had it for eight years. He’d watched her carry it all the way up the river, ten days on a barge, in silence, at the bow, beside Sabine, the two of them like a pair of cats on a wall. She’d never told him what it was. He’d never asked.

He watched her say it now.

He couldn’t hear it. He didn’t try. It wasn’t his. He watched her stand very straight on the shingle with her hands at her sides again and say it the way a soldier gives a report: steadily, all the way through, without stopping. It took a long time. It was clearly something she’d said to a wall, to a ceiling, to a yard at dawn, a thousand times, and it was clearly coming out differently to how it ever had before.

He found out later what it was, too. Not all of it. Enough.

Forty of us walked back to the fort. Thirty-eight are still alive. Twenty-six are still soldiers. Two married each other. One’s a priest in Valcrest and prays for you every morning, and she doesn’t know your name, so she just says “the man at the ditch.” One has six children. I’m a guard in Duskhollow, and I taught your friend to stand still.

Thank you.

That’s all. That’s what I came to say. I came to see who taught him, and to say thank you. And I’ve done both now.

Declan didn’t say anything for a long time.

Xav watched his feet. They didn’t move. They didn’t shift. But his shoulders came down, very slightly, the way they’d come down on the end of the jetty last night when Gabe put his arm round him.

Then he said something else. And Petra laughed, suddenly, startled, out loud, the first time Xav had ever heard her laugh, and wiped her face hard with the back of her hand.

Then she asked him.

Xav saw her ask. Saw her lift her hand again and touch her own mouth, very lightly, the way she had in the yard at Duskhollow. As if it belonged to somebody else. He knew exactly what she was asking.

What did you say? Before you moved. Every time. What were the words?

Declan looked at her.

Then he said it. Out loud, the way he’d said it to the lake an hour ago. And this time the wind had come round, and Xav heard it across the water, clear as anything. Three words, in a dead language, on a shingle beach in another world.

“Audentes fortuna juvat.”

Petra went completely still.

Xav watched her mouth move. Silently. Trying the shapes. Au. Den. The shapes her mouth hadn’t known, for eight years, the sounds she’d tried and tried to remember in the dark. He watched her find them. One at a time. He watched her mouth close on the last one and stay closed.

She turned her head. She looked straight past Declan, along the jetty, to where Xav was sitting on the end of it with the rod in his hands.

She didn’t ask. She didn’t have to.

“Fortune favours the bold,” Xav called. Across the water. “It means fortune favours the bold.”

Petra looked at him for a long moment.

“So it was a joke,” she said.

She said it to Declan. But Xav heard it. Everyone on the lake heard it. Petra’s voice, when she meant it to, could carry across a parade ground.

“And a prayer,” said Declan. “Both.”

He offered to teach her.

Xav heard that part too. The wind stayed round. He heard Declan say it, simply, the way you’d offer somebody a cup of tea.

“Stay a while,” said Declan. “If you can. I’ll teach you.”

Petra looked at him.

“I came to see who taught him,” she said.

“You’ve seen,” said Declan. “Now stay.”

“What would you teach me?” said Petra. “I’m Reaching. I’m thirty-one. I’ve been standing still in yards since I was twenty-three. I know how to wait.” Her chin came up. “I learned it from you. In a ditch.”

“I know,” said Declan. “Xav says you never move first.”

“Never.”

“Good,” said Declan. “I’ll teach you when to.”

Petra didn’t say anything.

Xav, on the end of the jetty, felt something go through him. Something cold and bright, like a door opening. He knew what that was. He’d heard Declan say it, a hundred times, to a hundred different people, in a hundred different games. Never quite the same words. Always the same thing. You’re not bad. You’re just waiting too long. I’ll show you the moment. Once. Then it’s yours.

“For a while,” said Petra slowly.

“For a while.”

“Yes,” said Petra.

They came back along the jetty together.

Petra first, because Declan stepped aside and let her go first, and she walked the whole length of the grey boards with her sword at her hip and her face perfectly still and her eyes very red. She stopped beside Xav at the end. She looked down at him. At the rod in his hands. At the line going down into the water.

“You’re fishing,” she said.

“I’m waiting.”

“Is there a difference?”

“Apparently not,” said Xav.

Petra looked at him a moment longer. Then she sat down on the boards beside him, very straight, with her sword across her knees, and looked out at the lake. And after a while her shoulders came down too.

Declan sat down on Xav’s other side, in his place.

The three of them sat in a row on the end of the jetty in the sun.

Xav held the rod. He looked at the whole lake. He thought about the ditch, and the infirmary wall, and the thing he knew that the woman beside him didn’t. The other half of the night that had saved her life. He felt it sitting in his mouth, heavy, like a stone. He’d carry it. It wasn’t his to put down. It was Declan’s, and Declan had decided, and that was the end of it.

It isn’t hers. It was never hers.

He’d watched her say thank you. He’d watched Declan take it. That was the right ending to that story, and it was the only one she’d ever get from him.

The line moved on the water.

A tiny ring. Then a pull. Then the rod bending in his hands, hard, heavy, something down there in the cold taking hold and going, and every part of him wanting to go with it.

He held still.

Beside him, he felt both of them hold still too. Declan on one side. Petra on the other. The two people who’d taught him how. Not moving. Not breathing. Waiting with him.

Wait, said Declan, on comms, nine years ago, in a room across a city. That’s all. Nothing else.

Xav waited.

And then, at exactly the right moment, the moment he’d never in his life been able to explain to anyone and had always, always known, he moved.

It wasn’t the pike.

It was a perch, about the length of his hand, very indignant, flapping on the boards and glaring at all three of them. Petra looked at it with polite professional interest. Declan looked at it and started laughing so hard he had to lie down on his back on the jetty with his arm over his eyes.

Xav unhooked it, carefully, and looked at it, and dropped it back over the edge into the lake.

Then he baited the hook again. And held the rod out over the water. And let the line go down. And settled.

“Okay,” said Xav. “Again.”
