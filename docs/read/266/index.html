Chapter 266

The Same Hour

5,594 words · 24 min read

It was always the same hour.

Yan had worked that out on the third night, sitting on the knoll in the dusk with his back against the cairn and the channel open in front of him and Suzu cold and tight round his neck; and he’d checked it every night after, for twelve more nights, the way Chris would have, because it was the kind of thing you didn’t believe until you’d checked it. Every night at Thornwall, at dusk, the whole width of the kingdom away, a girl on a tower wrote they’re moving. And every night at Cinderhold, an hour east of the fort on the edge of the red, at the same minute, the cold under the grave lifted its head.

Not woke. He’d have known if it woke. It lifted its head, the way a dog asleep by a fire lifts its head when another dog barks three farms off; and it listened, all night, west; and at first light, when Hyvred wrote going back, it put its head down again.

Fifteen nights.

It was asleep now. Deeper than he’d ever felt it. He sat on the knoll in the snow at dusk on the third day after, with the channel shut and his back against the cold stones of the cairn, and felt for it with the part of him that felt for things, down through the snow and the green under the snow and the red under the green; and it was there, a long way down, a shape like a ring with lines in it, lying in the dark under Diwa’s grave with its head on its paws.

Not listening. Not any more. Not since the last dawn.

Still asleep, said Suzu. In his head. Cool and low.

“Still asleep.”

Then come in. You’re cold.

“In a minute.”

You always say a minute, said Suzu, and didn’t say anything else, and didn’t move from his neck; which was how he knew she was as glad as he was to sit there a little longer and feel it not move.

It had started in the gate passage.

He hadn’t been at the grave the first night. He hadn’t known to be. He’d been sitting on the step of the east gate passage in the dusk with his staff across his knees, watching Rhea cut flagstones, because that was what Rhea did now, and somebody ought to watch.

She’d moved in off the yard at the first frost. The yard was packed earth, and packed earth went hard as iron when the red froze, and Rhea said that a stroke into iron-hard ground was a stroke into nothing, it didn’t go anywhere, it just jarred. So she’d taken her post and her sword into the passage under the east gate, which was flagged, big old grey flags laid when the fort was built, worn into a dip down the middle by forty years of boots; and she cut at the post there every dusk until it was too dark to see the edge.

She cut the way she’d cut all summer. Slow. Both hands. Rhea, who’d never done anything that wasn’t hard, cutting as if the post might break if she wasn’t careful; and as the sword came up, her light went down.

He watched it go. He’d watched it go all year. Out of the middle of her, where it had always sat roaring like a bonfire in a wind; down through her legs, through her boots, into the stone. A thick slow column of warm gold, going down into the flags the way water goes down into a floor.

And stopping. At the floor. Spreading out a little under her feet, the way water does when it reaches stone.

She cut. The sword landed on the post with the sound he’d heard her make on it all year: low and heavy and short, like a bell dropped onto a flagstone. The post shook in its iron shoe.

Rhea breathed out. Brought the sword up. Again.

He’d stopped telling her what it looked like. She’d stopped asking. She knew he could see it, and she knew he’d tell her if it changed, and it hadn’t changed since the summer: down, and stop, and spread. A floor. She was pressing on a floor, every dusk, as hard as she could, slowly, and the floor didn’t give.

He opened the channel, because it was dusk, and because for a week it had been dusk at Thornwall with a thing at the mouth of the pass, and you looked.

And Suzu went rigid.

The whole length of her at once, round his neck, the way she’d gone rigid the first time he’d stood on the rise; her head came up off his collarbone and her tongue came out and stayed out, and he felt it before she said anything, because he felt everything she felt, and it came up through the stone step under him and into the bones of his legs.

East. An hour east. Under the knoll. Something lifting.

And in the same breath, in the gate passage, Rhea’s sword came down.

Her light went down with it. Down through her legs, through her boots, into the flags, a thick slow column of gold; down to the floor.

It didn’t stop.

It went through. A hand. No more than a hand. Like water finding a crack and going into it. He saw it go, gold, down past the place where it had stopped every dusk for a year, a hand’s depth into the grey; and the stroke landed on the post with a sound that wasn’t a bell at all.

It was a crack.

Not the post. The flag. The big old grey flagstone a hand in front of her right boot, worn into a dip by forty years of the garrison going out and coming in; it split, with a sound like ice going on a pond, from one edge of itself to the other. A line. Clean.

Rhea stood very still with her sword on the post.

She looked down at the flagstone. At the crack in it, a hand in front of her boot, where nothing had touched it.

“What’s it look like,” she said. Not a question. Not turning round.

He’d stopped telling her. He’d learned it from Ice, on the shingle, watching him not tell people things: if you told somebody it stops, they heard stops, and they pushed on it, and they lost whatever it was. He’d kept it for a year. But Suzu was rigid round his neck and something under the knoll an hour east had lifted its head, and he found he didn’t want to keep anything any more.

“It didn’t stop,” said Yan.

Rhea didn’t say anything for a long time.

“How far.”

“A hand.”

She looked at the crack. Then she brought the sword up again, slow, both hands. Her light went down. To the floor. And stopped. And spread. And the stroke landed on the post like a bell on a flagstone, and the flag in front of her boot stayed exactly as cracked as it had been and not one hair more.

“Again,” said Rhea, and brought it up.

It stopped again. And again. She cut until it was too dark to see the edge, and it stopped every time; and when the dark came down at last over the red and Teague came out with a lamp to say supper was on, she stood in the passage with her sword down and her breath going up white and looked at the crack in the floor for a long time.

“Once,” she said.

“Once,” said Yan.

“Why once.”

He didn’t know. He thought he might. He didn’t say it.

He went to the grave the next night.

Rhea came. She didn’t ask; she was at the east gate at dusk with her sword on and her coat buttoned when he came down with his staff and Suzu, and she fell in beside him, and that was that. Nobody went out on the red after dark without a sword, the commander said. Rhea said she was a sword. Salty came too, because Salty went where Yan went when it was dark, the way he went where Wendy went the rest of the time; and Wendy stayed on the wall, counting.

It was an hour. Out of the gate and down the burial track between the stones in their long rows, grey with frost, some with names on them and most not, and on past the end of them onto the knoll. The ash at the top, bare now, with its leaves down. Liraun’s seedling a hand from it, a stick in the frost with a leaf still on it, which it shouldn’t have had. The cairn. And the black stone at the head of the cairn, with her name on it.

He sat down with his back against the cairn. Salty sat down beside him. Rhea stood, a little way off, at the edge of the knoll, facing east, with her hand on her hilt.

He opened the channel.

And under him, under the knoll, under the grave, the cold lifted its head.

He felt it come up. Not far. Not near. The way you feel someone in a room behind you turn round. A ring with lines in it, a long way down in the dark, that had been asleep since the first night the black stone stood on the cairn; lifting, and turning, a hair, west. And listening.

It hears, said Suzu.

“Hears what?”

She didn’t answer for a long time. She lay very tight round his neck with her head up and her tongue out, tasting the dusk.

The other one, she said. In the west. It hears the other one.

“Is it the same thing?”

It’s the same shape, said Suzu. And then, because he waited, and because she never lied to him: I don’t know if it’s the same thing. I know it’s the same shape. It’s listening to it the way you’d listen to somebody saying your name in the next room.

He sat there in the dusk with his back to the cairn.

He’d known it was the same shape. He’d known since the rise: a ring with lines in it, burned into the ground, and the same ring, faint, asleep, under the grave. He’d read Hyvred’s letters about the valley. He’d read Thea’s reading in the autumn, through Hyvred’s eyes, the thirteen rings of people and the fourteenth being written in holes. He’d known. He’d filed it, the way you file a thing you don’t want to look at.

It was listening. At the same hour. Every night.

“What do we do?” said Salty.

“Nothing,” said Yan. “Sit here.”

Salty thought about that.

“Okay,” he said. And sat there.

He looked down at her.

She’d never said that. Not in three years. Not to anybody but him. She didn’t talk to the others; she couldn’t, they couldn’t hear her, and she didn’t want them to. That was the first night.

They did it every night after. Nobody said they would. Nobody said let’s. It was just that at dusk, at Cinderhold, when Hyvred wrote they’re moving, the five of them stopped whatever they were doing, the wall and the yard and the forge and the knoll, and wrote. Wendy counted. Salty said eat. Maigo said they were there, in capitals. Niko said here. Yan said Suzu said goodnight. Every night. Fifteen nights.

It wasn’t anything. They knew that. It didn’t hold anybody’s wall up or stop anybody’s ribs breaking or keep a single lamp in Thornwall lit. Wendy said that, on the fifth night, at supper, at Oakes’s table, very quietly, with her spoon in her hand: it isn’t anything. And Salty said it’s not nothing, and went on eating; and Wendy looked at him for a long time and then went on eating too.

Yan read it on the knoll with his back against the cairn and the cold lifting its head under him and Rhea at the edge of the knoll facing east. He read it twice. Then he put his hand up to his neck, where Suzu was, and left it there.

Good, said Suzu.

The flagstones kept cracking.

Not every night. Rhea cut at dusk in the gate passage every night of the fifteen before she came out to the knoll, and most nights her light went down and stopped at the floor, the way it had all year. But not all of them. On the fourth night it went through, a hand, and a second flag split. On the seventh, a third. On the tenth, two at once, side by side, so that there was a line of cracks down the middle of the passage now like a seam in ice.

Always at the same minute. Always at they’re moving. Yan didn’t have to check the channel. He felt the cold lift its head under the knoll an hour east, and he knew without looking that in the passage under the east gate a flagstone had just gone.

The quartermaster was furious. Forty years, those flags. Laid when the fort was laid; there wasn’t a mason this side of Ashby who could cut new ones to match. The commander came down on the eleventh day and stood in the passage with his hands behind his back and looked at the seam of cracks for a long time, and then at Rhea, and then at the quartermaster; and said leave her, and went back up the stair.

She asked Yan on the twelfth night. On the knoll, at the edge, with her back to him and her face to the red.

“Is it me,” said Rhea. “Or is it that.”

She meant the cold. She knew about it. They all did; he’d told the commander and Aldous on the second night, and Thea in the channel, in one line, because Thea needed to know everything that was the same shape as the valley. The cold under the grave lifts at dusk. Same minute as yours. And Thea had written back noted. don’t go near it, and Higu had written WEST, which meant she’d put it on the page.

Rhea knew. And she’d stood at the edge of the knoll every night for twelve nights with her sword on, an hour east of a fort with a seam of cracks down the middle of its gate passage, and not asked. Until now.

He thought about it. He thought about lying. He’d have done it, a year ago; told her something that wasn’t quite a lie, the way Ice did, so that she’d keep cutting. It’s you. It’s all you.

“I don’t know,” said Yan.

It was true. He didn’t. He’d watched it every night. Her light went down, and the cold lifted its head, and the floor gave; in the same breath, every time, so close together that he couldn’t tell which came first. Whether something under the red was pulling on everything that went down into the ground at that hour, the way the call pulled on the lonely. Or whether it was just her. Pushing on a floor for a year, slowly, as hard as she could, and the floor finally giving, a hand at a time, and the hour a coincidence; the way you might hear a door slam in the next room at the same moment you drop a cup.

“I don’t know,” he said again.

Rhea stood there, facing east.

“Then it’s me,” she said.

And didn’t say anything else, all night. And the next dusk she was in the gate passage with her sword, cutting, slow, both hands; and Yan sat on the step and watched her light go down, and thought that was probably the most Rhea thing he’d ever heard anybody say.

The last night, he didn’t sleep.

He didn’t know why. Nothing was different; Hyvred had written they’re moving at dusk and the cold had lifted its head and he’d sat on the knoll till full dark with Rhea and Salty and come back. He’d gone to bed. He’d lain in his bunk in the barracks with Suzu on his collarbone and the stove ticking and listened to Salty breathe, and Wendy on the other side of the room counting under her breath in her sleep, which she did now; and he hadn’t slept.

At four he got up.

Salty was awake. Salty was always awake when he got up; Yan had never once in three years got out of a bunk in the dark without Salty sitting up on the other side of the room and reaching for his boots. Niko was awake too, on the bunk by the door, lying on his back with his eyes open, the way he lay when he was listening to something nobody else could hear.

“The frost’s loud,” said Niko. To the ceiling.

They went out together. The three of them, and Suzu, out of the east gate past the sentry, who knew them, down the burial track between the stones in the dark. No Rhea; she was on the wall. No moon. Stars, very hard and very small, and the red going away east under them, black, with frost on it.

Niko walked with his head down. He’d been able to see the frost-letters for a long time: little shapes that came up on the red at night where the frost grew, like writing, that almost made words and then went. Nobody else could see them. He’d said once that they went too fast to read, like something trying things. He walked down the burial track now looking at the ground on either side of it, at the frost on the red, and didn’t say anything; but Yan saw his hands open and close at his sides.

They came up onto the knoll in the dark.

The cold was up.

Higher than any night. He felt it the moment his foot touched the knoll: not lifting its head, the way it had every night for fifteen, but up. Standing. A ring with lines in it, a long way down in the dark under Diwa’s grave, standing up on all its feet and facing west, as still as a hare in a field. Listening so hard that the whole knoll seemed to be holding its breath. The bare ash. The seedling with its leaf. The cairn and the stone. The frost on the grass, very white, very thick, as if it had been growing all night.

Yun, said Suzu.

“I know.”

He sat down with his back to the cairn. Salty sat down next to him. Niko stood at the edge of the knoll, facing west, the other way from Rhea’s way, with his hands open at his sides.

They waited. He didn’t know what for.

The sky went grey in the east, behind them, over the red. Very slowly. The stars went out one at a time. The frost on the knoll went from black to grey to a kind of blue, and you could see the burial track going back toward the fort between its stones, and the fort, small and dark, with one lamp on the east gate.

He didn’t open the channel. He’d thought about it. He hadn’t.

And the black iron in the cairn rang.

By itself. Nobody touched it. Nothing touched it. The spike that stood in the old hole at the head of the cairn, the black half of a sword that burned demons, that had stood in that hole for eight years, and gone, and come back to it: it rang, once. The low note he’d heard it make for Alea on the tenth, coming up out of the iron and down into the knoll and out across the red; very low, very pure. He felt it in his back, through the stones. He felt it in his teeth.

And the cold went down.

All at once. Not lying down, the way it had lain down when Astra was laid beside the stone; not putting its head on its paws. Down. The way something goes down that’s been standing a long time and has been told to sit. Past where Astra had put it. Past where Ice had put it, the first time, sitting against the cairn with the sword across his knees and his palm on the earth. Down and down into the dark under the red, so far that for a moment Yan couldn’t feel it at all; and then he could, a long way off, very small, very still.

Not listening. Not any more.

Niko made a sound at the edge of the knoll.

“They stopped,” he said.

He was looking at the frost. At the red going away west from the foot of the knoll, grey-blue in the first light, white with frost. Yan looked too. He couldn’t see the letters. He’d never been able to.

“All of them,” said Niko. “At once. They were trying all night, faster than I’ve ever seen them, all over the red, like it was trying to say something before it ran out of time. And they stopped.” He turned round. His face was very white. “Like somebody’d put a hand flat on a table.”

It heard him, said Suzu.

Very quietly. In his head. She’d gone loose round his neck, all at once, the way a rope goes loose when somebody lets go of the other end.

“Heard who?”

She didn’t answer. She didn’t need to.

“From there?” said Yan. “From Thornwall? That’s—“

Everything heard him, said Suzu. Everything that’s that shape. Everything listening west. It all heard him. She put her head down on his collarbone. Even me, Yun. I heard him from here.

He opened the channel.

That was three days ago.

It snowed at Cinderhold the day after, the first of the winter; the red went white from the wall to the edge of the world, and the burial track went white between its stones, and the knoll. Yan went out at dusk every evening anyway, with Salty and Rhea, and sat with his back to the cairn and felt for it. It was there. A long way down. Asleep, with nothing at all lifting.

He thought about his mother.

He did, most days, at some point; it came up the way the cold came up under the knoll, at its own hour. A kitchen table. The post under the salt so it wouldn’t blow away, and her hand flat on the top of the pile, feeling the edges of the envelopes. Knowing most of them by the shape. Waiting for him to read her the rest.

He’d been her eyes. That was the thing he’d come back to, every year, doing the sum in the dark. Not all of her eyes; she’d known most of the pile with her hand. Just enough of them that she didn’t have to sit at the table alone with the rest.

Three months there, now. A little over. He didn’t know who was reading her the post. He didn’t know if anybody was. Not yet isn’t never. She went round his neck, the long way. You’d tell anybody else that. Tell yourself, Yun. It’s the same words.

He sat there with his window open in the dusk and read it. Locked. He thought about a hand on a pile of post, and a hand on a table in a fort, and a hand flat on an oak post inside an arch every morning, tomorrow. He thought that most of the people he knew were holding something they couldn’t put down, and that most of them weren’t strong enough to carry it, and that they carried it anyway, a hand at a time; and that he was one of them; and that that was probably allowed.

He shut it.

At the edge of the knoll, Rhea had drawn her sword.

She didn’t usually; not out here, not at dusk on the knoll, where there was nothing to cut. She stood facing east with it held in both hands, low, the way she held it in the passage. Yan watched her light. It went down. Out of the middle of her, through her boots, into the snow, into the green under the snow and the red under the green. To the floor.

And stopped. And spread.

He could see the floor now. That was the thing. He’d never been able to before; it had just been a place where she stopped. But he could see it tonight, in the dusk, from the cairn: a flat grey place a long way down under her feet, like the bottom of a well when the sun’s right overhead. It was closer than it had been in the summer. Not much. A hand. A crack’s worth.

“Well,” said Rhea, without turning round.

“It stopped.”

“Good,” said Rhea. She put her sword back. “Next year.”

She didn’t say what. She never did. She turned round and came back across the knoll through the snow toward the burial track, and Salty got up, and Yan got up; and the three of them and Suzu walked back to the fort in the dusk with the red white on either side of them and the one lamp burning on the east gate.

At the gate, he stopped and opened the channel. It was the hour.
