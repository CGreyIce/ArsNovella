The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 11

Chapter 301

How Are the Hens

5,877 words · 26 min read

The king had been in the west tower for five days, and Halvard had stopped expecting him to leave.

He had come up the stair on the first evening in his travelling coat, still wet from the road, an hour after he had stood in the Council chamber at the top of the old keep and said no to both motions in a voice that did not need to be raised. No to the thirty-five brought to Solmere at the thaw under the Crown. No to a girl’s cords taken out of her hands and given to the Chancellor’s clerks. He had not sat down to say it. He had not taken his coat off. Lord Vane had begun a sentence about the safety of the realm, and the king had looked at him until he stopped, and then gone out, and down the long gallery, and across the inner court in the rain, and up the hundred and forty steps of the west tower to the room at the top where his Advisor worked; and he had sat down in the chair by the window, and taken his boots off, and said, “I’ll stay here a bit, if it’s all the same to you.”

It was not a question. Halvard had known him for thirty years. He had brought up a second chair.

That had been five days ago. The king slept in the chair by the window with his coat over his knees, and woke before dawn, and went down to the farrier’s yard behind the royal stables and shod horses that did not need shoeing, and came back up at the second hour with soot on his hands and a pot of something from the kitchens, and read the Council’s notes as they came up the stair, and answered none of them. Kestra sat on the landing below with her back to the wall and her sword across her knees and sent the pages away. Sergeant Brask came up once with a message from Lord Vane, and stood in the doorway with his iron-shod staff, and looked at the king in his stockinged feet with a horseshoe nail between his teeth, and went down again without delivering it.

The study did not change. It never had. The long table under the east window, with Halvard’s papers on it in their squares. The map on the wall, the whole kingdom in faded colours: the blank inch in the south where the surveyors had never agreed, and the east, past the red of the Redmarch, black to the edge of the paper. The brazier. The shelf of ledgers, one for every year he had held the office, and nine before that, from the garrison.

And the drawer. Bottom left, in the old oak desk by the door, the one that stuck in wet weather. It stood open. It had stood open since the autumn before last, when he had carried its contents up a lake road and burned eleven of them in a lamp on the end of a jetty, with the man they were written to sitting on the boards beside him; and left the rest with him, one for every year, to be opened when they were due. There was nothing in it now but the smell of old paper. He had not shut it. He did not entirely know why.

The king had looked at it on the first evening, and then at him, and said nothing about it. That was the king.

The rider came on the ninth day after the letter left the lake, which Halvard did not know until afterwards, and at the third hour of the morning, which he did.

He heard her on the stair first. Not a page; the pages ran. This was somebody who had been in a saddle a long time and was climbing a hundred and forty steps on legs that had forgotten what steps were, slowly, with a pause on every landing. He heard Kestra stand up. He heard Kestra say something, and a woman’s voice answer, rough with road, by hand, to him, I was told by hand, and Kestra say, after a moment, go up.

She was young, mud to the thigh, with a post-rider’s leather bag across her body and a bay horse’s worth of the north road on her boots. She stood in the doorway and looked at the king in the chair, and at Halvard at the table, and did not seem to know which of them to bow to, and so did neither.

“The Advisor,” she said. “From Wrenhaven. By hand.” She took it out of the bag. “Mistress Hennet said it was on the Crown, my lord. Your order. She said you’d made it the first year and nobody’d ever used it, and I was to put it in your hand and nobody else’s, not a clerk, not the Chancellor, not—“ she glanced at the king, and swallowed, “—not anybody.”

“Thank you,” said Halvard.

He did not get up. He found he could not, quite. He held out his hand across the table, and she came and put the letter in it, and stepped back.

It was folded once. Not sealed. Cream paper, the good kind, the kind a girl bought for her father in a market because she liked the colour. On the outside, in a native hand that he knew better than his own, because he had had two years of it, answer after answer, every one of them beginning with the hens:

Halvard.

Solmere, the west tower.

He looked at it for a long time.

He had not written since the end of the winter. He had not asked anything. There was nothing in that hand on that paper that could be an answer, because there was no question outstanding; he had been careful, these last months, very careful, not to leave one. He had felt the thing coming down the river from Kingsferry since the thaw, in Cursiffe’s face and in Oswin’s silences and in a purse book that should not have existed, and he had stopped asking anything at all, even about the hens, so that whatever came would come when it chose and not because he had pulled on it.

And it had come. And it was not an answer.

“Go down to the kitchens,” said the king to the rider, kindly. “Tell them I said you’re to eat until you can’t. Then sleep. Kestra’ll find you a bed. He’ll want you again.” He nodded at the letter. “That’ll want an answer, and he’ll want it to go back the way it came.”

The rider went. Her boots went down the stair, slowly, one landing at a time.

Halvard unfolded the letter.

Halvard,

I pulled it out.

I didn’t know.

Tell the king.

You can ask me now.

A.

And under the one letter, a fish. A small one, in three quick lines and a dot for an eye, swimming left. Better than the one Halvard had drawn him, years ago, at the bottom of a note about hens, which had looked, Ars had written back, like a boot that had been told bad news.

He read it again.

He was aware of his hands. They had gone flat on the table on either side of the paper, the way they always went, and he had not told them to. He was aware of the king in the chair by the window, watching him and not saying anything, which the king did better than any man alive. He was aware of the fish.

I pulled it out.

He had known that. He had known it since the snow. Cursiffe had come up the west stair in the middle of the winter with a hand-sized book bound in calf that should have rotted three hundred years ago, and Oswin behind him white to the lips, and they had shut the door and told him: the two from the eleventh year of the Open-Handed, Aldemar’s two, who had asked every day for nine years to be sent home, and in the tenth year asked instead for a forge, and star-iron, and a road east. To hold the mouth. A word folded into the iron the way smiths in their world folded steel, over and over, until it held. And then the readers in Kingsferry with what they had found, the hook and the folds and the Kharvos woman’s song about a nail on a hill and a garrison boy with a red temper who had pulled it out. He had known all of it. He had put it in no ledger.

What he had not known was that Ars knew.

I didn’t know.

He had not known that either. That was the thing that took him by the throat. For eighteen years he had assumed, without once letting himself put it into words, that the boy had known what he was carrying. That he had walked into the black on purpose, for a reason, and found what he went to find, and come back with it, and refused to say. Halvard had built eighteen years on that. On a boy who knew and wouldn’t tell.

And there had been nothing to tell. The boy had not known. The boy had pulled a sword out of a hill because it was there and he was nineteen, and dragged it home on a strap, and stood under a gate with it; and when a frightened man asked him on the wall a week later what it was, he had said don’t ask me because he did not know, and could not bear to say so.

Tell the king.

You can ask me now.

Halvard looked up.

“Well,” said the king. He had not moved. “What does he say?”

He read it to him. All five lines. Then, because the king held out his hand, he gave it to him, and the king read it himself, slowly, with his lips moving very slightly on the short words, the way a man reads who learned late; and turned it over to look at the outside; and looked at the fish for a long time.

“He’s drawn you a fish,” said the king.

“He has.”

“It’s better than yours.”

“It is considerably better than mine, Majesty.”

The king turned the paper back over. He read it again. Then he folded it along its crease, very precisely, with his blacksmith’s thumbs, and held it on his knee.

“He’s never asked anybody for anything,” he said. “Not in all the years I’ve known him. Not when I gave him the border. Not when he gave it back and went off to a lake, and I rode all the way up there in my good coat, and he looked at me over a fishing rod as if I was a fence post.” He was quiet. “He’s never once asked me for a thing. And he’s asked you to tell me.”

“Yes.”

“Then tell me,” said the king.

So Halvard told him.

It took most of the morning. He told it in order, because that was the only way he knew how to tell anything: the purse book, shelf sixty-one, between the Wardens’ minutes and a treatise on carp; the two frightened people in the east wing three hundred years ago, and the paper the woman covered in circles; the forge, the star-iron, the road; the word in the folds, that Thea had read with her hands, the hall’s own language, a holding word, the opposite of the call. Stay. The readers in Kingsferry. The smith’s boy who had seen the folding and known it for a way of their world and not of this one. The song on the wharf. The hill. A month east, the readers’ letter said: weeks of black ground and no birds, and grass in rings round the hill that he had taken for the wind, and a day to pull it out; and the ground breathing out cold when it came.

The king did not interrupt once. He sat in the chair by the window with the letter on his knee and his stockinged feet on the boards, and listened, the way he listened at a forge to iron in the fire, with his whole face.

When Halvard told him about the folds, he looked down at his own hands.

“I had my hands on it,” he said. “When we cut it. At the lake.” He turned his right hand over and looked at the palm, at the old white scar across the base of the thumb. “It fought the chisel. Every stroke. I’ve cut a lot of iron in my life, and it doesn’t fight like that; it doesn’t care. I thought it was the star in it. I thought it was old and hard and didn’t want to be cut.” He closed the hand. “It didn’t want to let go.”

“No, Majesty.”

“Stay,” said the king. He said it the way a smith says the name of a joint, testing it. “That’s what it was saying. All the time I had the hammer on it. And I thought it was the iron being difficult.”

He was quiet for a while after that.

When Halvard came to the end, to the ground breathing out, the king got up. He went to the map on the wall, in his stockinged feet, and stood in front of it, and put one thick finger on the red of the Redmarch, on the little square of Cinderhold, and drew it slowly east, into the black. Past the last of the old surveyors’ marks. Past the ink. To the place on the paper where there was nothing at all, only the colour.

“About there,” he said. “A month, he said.”

“A month on foot. Nine days on a horse, before the horse died.”

“Nine days.” The king’s finger stayed where it was. “And it was holding something down. Out there. On its own. For three hundred years, with nobody knowing, and a boy of nineteen came along and pulled it out because it was there.” He took his hand off the map. “And then Kharvos came across the red, eight years after, with the long war years over and done, harder than it ever came in the long war. And they had a new thing they’d never done before. With a circle and a healer in the middle of it.” He did not turn round. “I’ve wondered every day for ten years why they came back when they did. Every day. I thought it was us. Something we’d done or not done. I thought it was me.”

Halvard said nothing. There was nothing to say that was true and also kind, and he had long ago stopped saying the other kind of thing to this man.

“It wasn’t me,” said the king. “It was him. At nineteen. And he didn’t know.” He stood there a moment longer with his back to the room. Then he turned round, and his face was perfectly ordinary, the face of a big tired man in his sixties with soot in the creases of his knuckles. “Well. He’s told me. That’s done.” He came back to the chair and sat down in it heavily and put his feet out. “The Council doesn’t hear a word of it.”

“No, Majesty.”

“Not Vane. Not anybody. The thirty-five, when he says. Not before.” He looked at the letter on his knee. “He’ll say. He’s started saying things.” He was quiet. “That’s new.”

“Yes,” said Halvard.

“You’ve got a question,” said the king. “Haven’t you.”

Halvard looked at him.

“Eighteen years,” said the king. “He’s written you you can ask it. I’m not daft.” He held the letter out across the space between the chair and the table, and Halvard took it. “I’ll go and shoe something. Ask him.”

He put his boots on, which took a while, and went down the stair, slowly, a big man on old knees, one landing at a time; and Halvard heard him say something to Kestra on the landing, and Kestra laugh, which she did perhaps twice a year.

Then he was alone in the study with the letter and the open drawer.

He had been forty-two.

That was the first thing, and he made himself look at it, because he had not looked at it in a long time. Not young. He had told himself for years afterwards that he had been young, and it had not been true; he had been forty-two, the strategist of the eastern garrison for four years, a court mage of the crown with a good coat and a high opinion of himself and a ledger for everything. Men. Arrows. Days. Demons, when they came. And one ledger that was not the garrison’s, that he kept in his own hand in his own room and showed to nobody: a boy.

He had found the boy at fifteen, on the parapet, at midnight, looking at nothing. What are you looking at? And the boy had shown him. A door in the air in front of his face that nobody else in the history of the kingdom had ever had, and a word in a language that nobody spoke. Halvard had sat down on the wall walk with his hands over his mouth and stared at it until dawn. And then he had gone to his room and begun a ledger.

He had written to Solmere about the boy within the month. Of course he had. It was his duty, and it was also the most interesting thing that had ever happened to him, and he had not been able to tell, at thirty-eight, where one of those ended and the other began. And Solmere had written back: watch. So he had watched. For four years. The tiers, when the boy crossed them. The skills, as they came. How long the boy slept, and what he ate, and who he fought, and how he fought them. Halvard had counted the boy the way he counted arrows. Very carefully. With great interest. And the boy had known; the boy always knew when he was being looked at; and he had never once said a word about it.

And then, one morning at the start of the summer, the boy had walked out of the east gate with a horse and a sword and a bag of bread, and not come back.

Halvard had written him in the garrison ledger at the end of the second month. Missing, presumed taken. East of the line. He had written it in his best hand, the way he wrote everything. The girl from the infirmary, the healer, with the baby on her hip, had come to his room the next day and asked to see the entry, and read it, and said no, quite calmly, and gone away again. She had not believed it for a moment. Halvard had. He had been very sure. He was good at counting things, and you did not come back from that far east; nobody had, in a hundred years.

And all that summer, in the room, at night, with the other ledger open in front of him, he had thought: I did this. Four years of watching. Four years of a court mage at his elbow with a pen, asking how he did that and how long it took and could he do it again. A boy with a temper and a door in front of his face and a man counting him. Of course he had walked off into the black. Halvard would have walked off into the black.

He had believed that for eighteen years. He had never said it to anyone. Not to the king. Not to the man himself, at a lake, on a jetty, in all those letters about hens.

And then the gate.

He had been on the wall because he was always on the wall at dawn. He counted the dawn in. It was a thing he did, at the garrison, a habit, the light coming over the black and the watch changing and the numbers going into the book. And he had looked down, and there had been somebody standing on the road outside the east gate, where nobody should have been, dragging something.

He had known him at once. Thinner. Burned black by the sun, with his hands in rags and his face gone strange, the face of somebody who had been alone a long time. And on his back, on a strap, a sword. Black. Longer than the boy was tall. The point had cut a line in the road behind him, all the way back out of the red, as far as Halvard could see.

And the ward had begun to sing.

It was a good ward. Halvard had laid it himself, that spring, on the iron of the gate: he had been proud of it. It did not sing. Wards did not sing; they held, quietly, and you felt them if you put your hand on the iron, a sort of hum, like a hive. This one sang. The moment the boy stopped under the gate, Halvard felt it come up through the stone of the wall into the soles of his feet and into his teeth, a high clear note he had never heard a ward make, and under it another note, lower, coming from the thing on the boy’s back. The two of them. As if they were talking. As if the iron in the gate and the iron on the boy’s back had recognised each other, and had something to say.

He had been more frightened in that moment than in all four years of demons.

He had not known what it was. He had only known that it was something no ledger had a column for, and that it was standing outside a gate he was responsible for, at dawn, with the whole garrison asleep behind him and a healer and a baby in the infirmary a hundred paces away. And that the boy holding it had looked up at him.

Open it, Halvard.

That was all he had said. Not please. Not it’s me. He had looked up at the wall with that strange alone face, and said his name, as if Halvard were the gate.

And Halvard had not opened it.

He had counted. Of course he had; he could not help it, even then; it was the only thing his body knew how to do with fear. He had stood on the wall with his hands on the parapet and the ward singing in his teeth and the boy looking up at him, and counted his own heartbeats, and not moved.

He knew the number. He had always known it. He had not written it in the ledger. He had written: Returned, east gate, dawn. Alone, on foot. Carrying a weapon of no known make. Admitted. In his best hand. And not the number. He had never said it aloud to anyone in eighteen years, and he was not going to say it now, even alone, even in his own head in his own tower. It was not a large number. That was the worst of it. It was not large at all. It was only longer than it should have been, by every heartbeat of it.

And then he had gone down off the wall and drawn the bar himself, with his own hands, because he could not order a guard to do a thing he would not do. And the gate had opened. And the girl had come out of the infirmary with the baby on her hip and put the baby down on the step and walked across the yard and hit the boy in the chest with both fists. And Halvard had stood by the open gate with his hands still on the bar and watched them sit down together in the dust.

A week later he had gone up to the wall at night, where the boy was on watch, and stood beside him a long time. He had not known what he was going to ask. Where did you go. What is it. Why do the dogs lie down when you walk past. He had felt the boy feel it coming.

Don’t ask me.

So he hadn’t.

He did not write that day.

He sat at the table with the letter in front of him until the light moved off the map and onto the floor, and then he got up and lit the lamp, and sat down again. The king came back up at dusk with soot to the elbows and a pot of mutton and barley and two spoons, and put the pot on the table beside the letter, and ate his half of it in the chair by the window without saying anything, and went to sleep.

Halvard did not eat his. He found, at midnight, that it had gone cold, and that he had a pen in his hand, and a sheet in front of him with How are the hens written at the top of it in his own hand from long habit, and nothing under it.

He wrote under it.

He wrote for a long time. He wrote the ledger, the other ledger, the one he had kept in his room; four years of a boy, counted. He wrote Solmere wrote back: watch. He wrote the summer, and the entry, missing, presumed taken, and the healer saying no. He wrote that he had believed, for eighteen years, that the boy had walked into the black to get away from him. He wrote the ward singing. He did not write the number. He wrote that he had not opened the gate, and that he was sorry, and that he had been sorry every day since, and that he did not know any longer whether he was sorry because he had kept a frightened boy standing under a gate or because he had been right to.

He filled three sheets. He read them through at the second hour of the morning with the lamp guttering, and they were all true, and every word of them was about Halvard.

He burned them in the brazier. One at a time. The king did not wake. Or did, and lay with his eyes shut, which the king could also do better than any man alive.

He did the same the next night. And the next. Four sheets, then two, then one that had only the hens and a line about the garrison ledger and a line about the number, and that one he held in the brazier for a long time before he let it go, because it was nearly right, and nearly was the worst thing a letter could be.

On the third morning the king looked at the brazier, at the fine grey ash in it, three nights deep, and then at him, and said, “He’ll have burned some too. I’d put money on it.” He scratched his jaw. “Before he wrote that.” He nodded at the letter, which had not moved from the middle of the table. “Five lines. That’s not a man who sat down and wrote five lines.”

“Majesty.”

“I’m only saying.” The king got up and put his boots on. “You’ve had eighteen years to write the four sheets. You don’t have to send them.” He went to the door. “He didn’t ask you to tell him anything. He said you could ask.”

The girl came up on the fourth day.

Not the rider. The fox girl, from the east wing: Aleaka, with her wide unreadable eyes and her small hands and the hawk on her bare arm, the furious bark-coloured one with the bent feather that would sit on nobody else. She had come up the west stair before, once or twice, with the hawk, because the hawk liked the top of the tower and would not settle anywhere lower. She did not come in. She stopped on the landing below the door, where Kestra was, and Halvard heard her stop. Heard her not come up.

He went to the door.

She was standing on the landing with one hand flat on the wall and her face turned up toward the study, with the hawk shifting its feet on her other arm, and her eyes very far away, the way they went. He had seen her do it once before, in the winter, in a corridor, and she had looked at him afterwards as if she had picked something up that did not belong to her and was trying to decide where to put it down.

She was looking at him the same way now. Only it was different.

“It’s lighter,” she said.

He did not ask her what. He had learned not to, with her; she would tell him or she would not, and asking made no difference. She stood there a moment longer with her hand on the wall. Then she took it away, and the hawk settled, and she nodded to him, once, and went back down the stair.

Kestra looked up at him from the landing with an expression he did not see on her often.

“What did she mean?” said Kestra.

“I don’t know,” said Halvard, which was true, and went back in, and sat down at the table, and took a fresh sheet, and wrote at the top of it, in his own hand, from long habit:

How are the hens.

He looked at it. Then he looked at the letter from the lake, at the five lines in the hand he knew better than his own, and the fish.

You can ask me now.

Not tell me. Not I’ll tell you. Ask.

He had spent four sheets every night on what he had to say, and the man at the lake had not asked him to say anything. He had been given a question, after eighteen years. One. He could spend it on whatever he liked.

And he found, sitting there in the afternoon light with the open drawer at his back, that he did not want to ask what it was. He knew what it was; Cursiffe had told him; it was a nail. He did not want to ask what was under it; the man did not know, and that was the whole of the letter. He did not want to ask about the gate. The gate was his. The king was right about that; the gate, and the number, were his, and he would carry them, and they were not heavy any more in the way they had been, because the boy had not known, and had stood under the gate not knowing, and had said open it to the one person on that wall who he thought might.

There was only one thing he had ever really wanted to know. On the wall, a week after, with the boy on watch beside him. Under all the others. Under what is it and where did you find it and why do the dogs lie down.

He wrote it under the hens. Four words. He did not burn it.

Why did you go?

He looked at it for a long time. It was a small question. It looked very small on the page, under the hens, with all that cream paper below it. He thought about adding something. I have always thought it was because of me. He did not. That was not a question. That was the four sheets again, and he had burned them, and he was not going to send a man at a lake eighteen years of a court mage’s guilt to carry on top of everything else he carried.

He signed it H.

And then, after a moment, because it seemed only fair, he drew a fish under the letter. It was a very bad fish. It was worse than the first one; it looked, he thought, like a boot that had been told the news and taken it hard. He looked at it and nearly laughed, alone, in the west tower, at the top of a hundred and forty steps, which he had not done in some time.

He folded it, and wrote A. on the outside, and The lake. By hand, and did not seal it.

The rider went north on the morning of the fifth day, rested and fed and with a new pair of stockings from the king’s own chest, which she had tried to refuse. Halvard took the letter down the hundred and forty steps himself and put it in her hand in the inner court, by the well, in the rain.

“By hand,” he said. “To him. Nobody else.”

“Yes, my lord.” She put it in the bag, against her body, under the oilcloth. She looked at him. “Mistress Hennet said, my lord. If there was an answer. She said to tell you.” She coloured. “She said he walked it down to her himself. The first one. Through the town. With his hand on his coat the whole way, like it might get out.”

Halvard stood in the rain.

“Thank you,” he said.

She went out under the arch, and he heard the bay horse on the cobbles, and then on the north road, and then not.

When he came back up, the king was sitting in the chair by the window with his boots off and his feet out, eating an apple, and looking at the open drawer.

“Well?” said the king.

“I asked him.”

“What?”

Halvard sat down at the table. He put his hands on it. They went flat on their own, and he let them.

“Why he went,” he said.

The king stopped chewing. He looked at Halvard for a long moment over the apple. Then he nodded, slowly, the way he nodded at a good weld.

“Good,” he said. “That’s the one.” He took another bite. “You didn’t tell him the number.”

Halvard looked up.

“You always count,” said the king. “You counted the dawn in every morning for four years at Cinderhold; the men used to set their watch by you. You were on the wall. You’ll have counted.” He chewed. “I don’t want to know it. Don’t tell me. That one’s yours.” He looked out of the window, north, at the rain. “I only wanted to know you hadn’t sent it to him.”

“I didn’t.”

“Good,” said the king again. He threw the apple core out of the window, which was a thing Halvard had asked him not to do for thirty years. “Shut that drawer, would you? It’s been bothering me for five days.”

Halvard looked at the drawer. Bottom left, the old oak desk by the door. Open, and empty, and smelling of old paper.

He got up and shut it. It stuck, because it was raining; it always stuck in the wet. He put his knee against it and pushed, and it went home with a small dry sound like a word.
