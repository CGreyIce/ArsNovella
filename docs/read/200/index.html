The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 7: Myths

Chapter 200

Spring on the River

7,624 words · 33 min read

For twenty-six years, Rachael had been the patient.

Not a patient. The patient. There was a difference, and nobody who hadn’t been one knew what it was, and she’d stopped trying to explain it somewhere around fifteen. A patient was somebody who got ill and then got better and went home. The patient was somebody the room was arranged round. Somebody people washed their hands before they touched. Somebody who knew the names of the night nurses on three wards and the exact sound of an IV pump about to alarm and how to say I’m fine in a voice that made a doctor go away. Somebody whose friends texted are you up for it? before every single thing, kindly, and meant it, and she’d hated them a little every time, kindly, and meant that too.

She’d been very good at it. It was a skill. Nobody gave you a window for it.

And then she’d woken up on the floor of a hall in another world, and an old man in grey had crouched beside her and laid two fingers on the inside of her wrist for about four seconds, and said that will hold, and she’d felt something in her that had been wrong since before she could remember go right, the way a dislocated finger goes back in, with a click you felt in your teeth.

That was two years and a half ago.

She still caught herself, some mornings, reaching for a mask that wasn’t there.

The seventh hour, and Sister Maud was on the step.

She always was. With a broom. Seventy-three years old, as small as Rachael and twice as fierce, in a grey habit with the sleeves pinned up and a face like a walnut that had decided long ago what it thought about everything. She said you could tell everything about the day from who came down the river street at the seventh hour and in what state. She swept the same four steps every morning whether they needed it or not, and they never needed it, because she’d swept them yesterday.

Rachael came round the corner of the river street with two of them, the way she had every morning since the night a hunter came through the river wall for her, the autumn before last. Rachael always with two. Sabine had said it, on a barge, last spring, and nobody had ever said she could stop, and Rachael had long since given up asking. Today it was Gabe, five paces behind her, with his big hands in his pockets and his hammer through his belt, near enough that she knew he was there and not so near she had to do anything about it. And Dennis, on her other side, a pace closer than Gabe because Dennis had never once in his life stood where he was told, humming.

The Wren was high. That was the first thing you noticed, coming round the corner, every morning since the thaw. The river at the bottom of the street brown and fast and loud with the spring coming down out of the north, all of it, every lake and stream and snowfield between here and the Greyspine, going past the quays in a hurry with branches in it, and once, last week, a whole hen-house, empty, upright, turning slowly round and round, that half of Kingsferry had come out on the wharves to cheer. The first barges of the year were in. The Patient Widow was in, with Dennis’s name on her papers and Old Haskett at the oar saying easy to nobody. The willows along the far bank had gone a green so new it looked wet.

It was spring. She’d never really had one, before. Not like this. Spring on Earth had been pollen counts and an inhaler and a window you didn’t open.

She stopped at the bottom of the step.

“You’re late,” said Maud, not looking up.

“It’s the seventh hour.”

“It’s the seventh hour and a bit.” Maud swept. “There’s a bargeman in the end bed with his hand caught in a winch. Brother Tamsin’s sitting with him. Brother Tamsin’s gone green.” She looked up at last, past Rachael, at the two of them behind her. At Gabe. At Dennis. Then back at Rachael. “And your tall one’s been on my step since the sixth hour, looking at the quays. He won’t come in and he won’t go away.” She went back to sweeping. “He’s in your way. Move him.”

Rachael looked up the step.

Deston was sitting at the top of it. Against the door-post, with his knees up and his long arms round them, a man of one metre eighty-five folded into the smallest shape he could make. He was looking down the river street at the quays. He’d been looking at them, she could tell, for an hour. He had the face he got when he was listening to threads, which was no face at all.

“Dest,” said Rachael.

He turned his head.

“They’re gone,” said Deston.

She knew who he meant. Everybody in Group 4 knew who he meant. You didn’t say it out loud on the street. You didn’t need to.

Deston saw threads. He’d seen them since the first week. Between everybody. Strangers who passed each other in a market had them, faint, a single strand of grey for a glance; mothers had them to children like ropes. Every person in the world, he said, was the middle of a web. And some of them weren’t. Some of them had faces and coats and voices and walked about the quays of Kingsferry buying fish and selling grain like anybody else, and had nothing coming out of them at all. Not a strand. Like a hole cut in the cloth in the shape of a person.

The threadless. He’d seen the first one in their first month, at the river gate. A man in a hat. He’d counted them ever since, the way Hyvred at Thornwall counted her rings. A green coat on the grain quay. A woman in a shawl. The man from the red-sailed barge. A woman with a basket of apples, whose apple Rachael had picked up off this very step in the autumn, through her sleeve, and thrown in the river.

Seven of them on the quays this winter. Deston had them by heart. Where each one stood, and when, and which way it was facing. Mostly, he said, they faced the infirmary.

“All of them?” said Dennis.

“All seven.” Deston’s voice was very flat. “I’ve been round every quay since the sixth hour. The grain quay. The salt quay. The fish steps. The south wharf. Under the bridge.” He looked at Dennis. “Can you hear them?”

Dennis stood very still at the bottom of the step, with his head a little on one side.

Dennis heard lies. He’d always heard them, he said, on Earth, faintly; here they rang, every one, like a cracked bell, so that Kingsferry with its thousand merchants lying to each other from dawn to dusk was, to Dennis, the most beautiful music in the world. And the threadless didn’t ring. They were the opposite of ringing. Where one of them stood, there was a hole in the noise of the port, a round silence the shape of a person, nobody inside it. He’d always been able to find them by the silence.

He listened for a long time.

“No,” said Dennis. Quite quietly, for Dennis. “No holes. Anywhere.” He shut his eyes. “It’s all bells. The whole port. Just people.”

“When?” said Rachael.

“This morning,” said Deston. “First light. I was on the roof.” He was always on the roof at first light. He listened to the gold thread, the one that went upriver to a lake, every morning; he said it was like checking a pulse. “I was watching the green coat. On the grain quay. He was where he always is, by the scales, facing this way. And then he stopped facing this way.” Deston looked at his hands. “He turned his head. North-west. Up the river. All at once, as if somebody’d called him. And I looked, and the shawl on the salt quay had turned her head the same way. The same moment. And the man under the bridge. All seven. All at once. North-west.” He swallowed. “And then they walked off the quays. Separately. Different ways. Not hurrying. Like people going home. And they didn’t come back.”

“Like Valcrest,” said Gabe. From five paces off. Very quietly.

Rachael didn’t say anything.

Like Valcrest. Thirty-nine of them, in the stands at the Lists, all week, counting. And then on the last night, all at once, every one of them walking out of every gate of the city at the same moment, separately, like they all heard the same bell, Hyvred had said. And a month after that, in the autumn, at Thornwall, thirty-nine people coming down the pass road one at a time and taking their places in the outermost ring in the valley, until the ring was full.

She stood at the bottom of Sister Maud’s step in the spring morning with the river going past loud and brown and the willows green on the far bank and everybody in the street going about their business, ringing like bells.

“That’s worse,” said Rachael.

Dennis looked at her.

“That’s worse,” said Rachael again. “When they were here, I knew where they were.” She hitched her bag up on her shoulder. “I could see the green coat from my window. Every morning. I knew which side of the street to walk on. I knew which quay not to go down.” She looked at the quays. “Now they could be anywhere.”

Nobody said anything.

“Well,” said Rachael. “I’ve got a hand in a winch.” And she went up the step, past Maud, past Deston, and in at the door.

She heard Maud, behind her, on the step, say to the three of them in the street in quite a different voice from the one she used on Rachael: “Well? Don’t stand there. She said she’s got a hand.”

The bargeman’s name was Corliss, and his hand was bad.

A winch on a grain barge at the salt quay, at the first light, with the river so high the barges were bucking against their ropes; a rope had jumped the drum and taken his right hand round it with it. Brother Tamsin had cut him free and splinted what he could and given him poppy and sat with him, and gone, Maud was right, a very pale green. Tamsin had already been Spark the week she came; he was twenty-four now, and kind and nervous and the best person in the building at sitting with someone who was frightened, and he could close a cut and knit a clean break and that was the edge of him. This was past the edge.

Rachael looked at it.

She’d learned to look, here. On Earth she’d been looked at. She’d lain in a bed while people in gloves held her arm to the light and frowned at it and said things to each other over her head as if she weren’t there. She’d decided, somewhere in the first week here, on the floor of this infirmary with a dying man’s blood on her hands and Tamsin peeling her fingers off the linen one at a time, that she was never going to say a single thing over anybody’s head as if they weren’t there, ever.

“Corliss,” said Rachael. “I’m Rachael. I’m going to look at your hand. It’s going to hurt. I’m going to tell you before every bit that’s going to hurt, and you’re allowed to shout, and swear, and tell me I’m a terrible person, and I’ll still be here when you’ve finished.”

Corliss, who was about fifty and had a face like a boot, looked up at her through the poppy.

“You’re the foreign one,” he said. “The one off the barge. The one they come for.”

“Yes,” said Rachael.

“Huh,” said Corliss. And held out his hand.

She put her palm over it. Not touching. An inch off. She’d asked first; she always asked first. Then, when he nodded, touching, very lightly, the way you’d put your hand on the side of a pot to see if it was hot. Healing was different. That was work. She’d never minded touching for work.

And she felt it.

She’d always been able to, a little, from the first weeks: a warmth in her hands near a hurt, like holding them out to a fire. It had grown. It was more than warmth, now. She could feel the shape of what was wrong under her palm the way you’d feel the shape of a key in a lock with your eyes shut: the three bones of the back of the hand that had gone, and how; the two fingers that had come out of their joints; the place where the tendon had torn and was trying, stupidly, bravely, to hold on.

“This bit’s going to hurt,” said Rachael. “Now.”

And put the joints back.

Corliss shouted. He swore. He told her, at some length, with real feeling, that she was a terrible person, and she said “I know,” and held on.

Then she shut her eyes, and wrote, with one finger of her other hand, in the air over his, small, in English, the word she’d been writing for two years.

Mend.

It took an hour.

It always took longer than people thought. In the ballads it was instant: the healer laid her hands on the hero and the wound closed and he rose up shining. Rachael had heard the one about herself, last summer, sung in a tavern in Valcrest by a girl with a lute who’d never met her, and it had been instant in that one too, and she’d had to go outside.

It wasn’t instant. It was slow and fiddly and very boring and it hurt. Mend was the word, and the word was the start; then you had to do it, a bone at a time, holding each one where it went and asking it to remember being whole, and waiting while it did, and then the next. She knelt by the end bed with her palm on the man’s ruined hand and her eyes shut and did it, bone by bone, while Corliss sweated and swore and then, about halfway through, went quiet and watched her face.

At the end, she sat back on her heels.

He moved his fingers. All five. Slowly. Then made a fist. Then opened it again and looked at it as if it were somebody else’s.

“Huh,” said Corliss.

“Don’t use it for a week,” said Rachael. “I mean it. Not the winch. Not a rope. Not even a cup. If you use it before then, it’ll go back to being what it was, and you’ll come back in here, and I’ll do it again, and this time I’ll do it slowly on purpose.”

Corliss looked at her.

“They say you’re hard,” he said.

“They’re right.”

“They say,” said Corliss, “the old woman on the step’s harder.”

“They’re right about that too.”

He laughed. It came out of him like a cough. He lay back on the bed and held his hand up in front of his face and opened and closed it, open and closed, and laughed, and then, rather to Rachael’s alarm, began to cry.

She didn’t touch him. She didn’t do that. She sat on the end of the bed, a little way off, near enough that he’d know she was there, and waited until he’d finished, and handed him a cloth, and didn’t say anything.

When she got up, Sister Maud was standing at the end of the ward with her arms folded, watching.

“You put the fourth one in crooked,” said Maud.

“I did not.”

“You did. A hair.” Maud came down the ward and looked at the hand without touching it. “There. It’ll ache in the cold. Twenty years from now he’ll curse you every winter.”

“Twenty years from now,” said Rachael, “he’ll have a hand to curse me with.”

Maud looked at her for a long moment, with her walnut face.

“Yes,” she said. “He will.” And went away down the ward.

Rachael stood by the bed and looked at her own hands, and at Corliss’s hand, opening and closing, and found she was smiling, and stopped.

Then, because nobody was looking, didn’t.

She took Gabe his lunch at noon.

She didn’t usually. Gabe ate at the forge, with Harl, out of a cloth. But she’d been on her feet since the seventh hour and she wanted, suddenly, very much, to walk somewhere in the sun that wasn’t a ward; and Dennis had been sitting on the infirmary step all morning in a patch of it like a cat, ready, so she bought two meat pies from the stall at the end of the river street and they walked along the south wharf to Rope Street with Dennis carrying the pies, because Dennis said if he didn’t carry them she’d only drop one in the river to make a point.

You heard Harl’s forge before you saw it. Everybody on the south wharf did. Two hammers, not one. A little one, quick and light, tink, tink, tink, like somebody tapping on a window; and after every tink, a great heavy DOOM that came up through the boards of the wharf into the soles of your feet.

The little one was Harl’s. The big one was Gabe’s.

They stood in the door.

Harl’s forge was a long low brick shed on the end of Rope Street with the river going past its back wall and its front open to the wharf, black inside, with a fire in the middle like a heart. Harl was at the anvil. Sixty, deaf in one ear, no eyebrows; he’d burned them off three times and given up. He had a little hand-hammer in one fist and a pair of tongs in the other, with something orange in the jaws of the tongs, and he was tapping it, tink, here, tink, there, very quickly, never looking up. And opposite him, across the anvil, in a leather apron with his sleeves rolled to the shoulder and his big arms shining, Gabe, with the sledge.

Tink. DOOM. Tink. DOOM.

Rachael watched.

She’d watched Gabe at a forge before. She’d watched him for two years, on and off, ever since Harl had handed him a hammer in their first winter. But she hadn’t watched him since the winter, and something had changed, and it took her a while to see what.

It was the waiting.

Harl’s little hammer touched the iron. That was all it did; it touched, tink, in a place, very lightly. It said here. And then Gabe’s sledge came down, on exactly that place, so hard the anvil rang and the sparks went up, and moved the iron. And then Harl touched it somewhere else, tink, here, and Gabe’s sledge was already coming, already on its way, before the tink had finished ringing; as if he’d known where Harl was going to touch before Harl did.

The striker doesn’t choose, Gabe had told her once, at supper. The smith chooses. The smith says here, and the striker hits it, and if the striker’s any good he’s already hitting it by the time the smith’s said it. You don’t think, Gabe had said. You just go where he goes. You make his hand bigger.

He was making Harl’s hand bigger. She could see it. The old man tapping, light and quick, and the young one following him so close there was no gap, the two of them moving round the anvil like one man with four arms.

The iron went dull. Harl put it back in the fire. Gabe leaned on the sledge, breathing, sweat running off him, and saw her in the door.

“Rae,” said Gabe. And grinned.

“Pies,” said Dennis, holding them up.

They ate them on the wharf, with their legs over the edge and the river going past under their boots, brown and fast and loud. Harl didn’t stop for lunch. Harl never stopped for anything. They could hear him behind them in the shed, tapping something on his own, tink, tink, and grumbling.

“You’re different,” said Rachael. “At it.”

Gabe looked at her with his mouth full.

“You used to hit it,” said Rachael. “You used to wait for him to say here and then hit it. Now you’re there before he says it.”

Gabe swallowed. He looked pleased, and then embarrassed about looking pleased.

“I’ve been practising,” he said.

“I can see that.”

“Every day. Since the barge came in. Since the letter.” He wiped his mouth with the back of his big hand. The king’s letter. Rachael knew; everybody knew. Gabe had read it out in the channel at seven in the evening in capitals, so loudly she’d heard him through two walls. It was nailed to the post inside Harl’s door now, cream paper with a thumbprint on it instead of a seal. Strike for me. I’ll tell you what we’re making at the anvil. “Harl says the king works fast. Faster than anybody. He says he struck for him once, at a fair, when the king was still a general and came through Kingsferry, and couldn’t keep up, and the king didn’t say anything, just slowed down for him, and Harl’s been ashamed of it for twenty years.” Gabe looked at the river. “So I’m practising. Harl’s doing it fast. As fast as he can. Every day. Making me catch up.”

“And can you?”

“Nearly.” He grinned. “He says by the autumn I’ll be able to strike for a king and not shame Rope Street.”

“And what are you making?” said Dennis. “At the anvil. In the autumn.”

“I don’t know.” Gabe said it easily. Then, less easily: “He said he’d tell me. At the anvil. Not before.” He looked at his hands. “I keep thinking. At night. What it’ll be. I keep thinking it’s a sword. And then I think, it’s the king, he made a headstone out of a sword, he made plough blades, it’s probably a gate hinge.” He laughed. “And then I think, it’s the tenth year. And we’re walking east after. To the grave. Me and Dest and Xav and him. And he’s asked me to strike.” He stopped. “And I don’t think it’s a gate hinge.”

Nobody said anything for a while. The river went past.

“You’ll be good,” said Rachael.

Gabe looked at her.

She hadn’t meant to say it. It had just come out, the way things came out of her more and more lately, with no edge on them. She heard it come out and thought about taking the edge back and putting it on, the way she’d have done a year ago. You’ll be adequate. You’ll be fine as long as you don’t drop the sledge on the king’s foot.

She didn’t.

“You’ll be good,” said Rachael again. “At the anvil. Whatever it is.” She looked at the river. “You’ve always been good at being the bigger hand. Ever since I’ve known you. You just never had anybody ask before.”

Dennis, on her other side, had gone very still. She didn’t look at him. She knew exactly what his face was doing. He’d be listening for the bell, and there wouldn’t be one, and he’d be trying very hard not to say anything about it, and failing.

“Oh my god,” said Dennis. “That was nice.”

“Shut up, Dennis.”

“That was genuinely, sincerely nice. To Gabe. On a wharf. In daylight—“

“I will push you in the river.”

“You’d have to touch me,” said Dennis happily, and she nearly did.

Gabe didn’t say anything. He sat on the edge of the wharf with half a pie in his hand and looked at the river with his face doing something complicated, and then, after a while, put his free hand down on the boards between them. Not on her. On the boards. Near enough that she knew it was there.

She left it there.

Emrys’s cord came in the middle of the afternoon.

She was back at the infirmary by then, at the long table in the back room where they rolled bandages, with Maud and Tamsin and two of the sisters. Emrys came in without knocking, which he never did, with his blue-and-silver cord in one hand and a strip of paper in the other. He was tall and grey, a court musician once in Solmere, the man who’d taught her group to chant spells like songs. He’d been hoarse since the night market, their first winter. When he was frightened his voice went entirely, and he had to whisper, and it was nearly gone now.

“From the cat girl,” whispered Emrys. “From Thornwall. Through Corvin’s cord. For you, Sister.” He held it out to Maud. “From the king.”

Maud didn’t take it. Her eyes were seventy-three years old and had given up small writing long ago; she had Emrys read her everything that came by the cord, and said a whisper was easier to argue with than a page.

“Read it,” said Maud.

Emrys read it. In his whisper, in the back room, with the bandages and the smell of vinegar and the river going past outside.

The king asks: every apothecary and every healer who can be spared, to Thornwall, by the fastest road. The call is stronger. The doses are made by one man and he cannot make enough. The apothecaries will brew; he will write. Healers for the called, and for him. Come now. S., by Corvin, by Hana.

Nobody in the back room said anything.

Rachael had read the channel. Everybody had. Three days of it. Drew standing in the road in front of twelve people, saying not here, and the whole call going back up the valley like a ball. Tiger going a hundred and fifty paces in one step for a boy in a red scarf. And this morning, at first light, from Hyvred, in letters so shaky they looked like they’d been written on a horse:

it turned round. it LOOKED. at me. for the first time

the king stood in front of me and opened up. ALL of it. and it looked at him instead

She’d read it in bed. Before the seventh hour. Before the step. Before Deston, sitting against the door-post, saying they’re gone.

She opened her window. Now. At the bandage table, with all of them watching. She scrolled back. She knew what she was looking for, the way you know what you’re looking for on a chart when you’ve seen the symptom before.

05:51 · Hyvred: it turned round. it LOOKED. at me. for the first time

And Deston, on the roof, at first light. He turned his head. North-west. All at once, as if somebody’d called him.

She shut it. She didn’t need to ask Deston what time. She knew what time first light was on the roof of the river-gate infirmary in the spring. She’d been awake for it every morning for a year.

The same minute.

“Send me,” said Rachael.

The back room went quiet.

Maud didn’t look up. She had the strip of paper from Emrys in her hand, and she was looking at it, though she hadn’t read a word of it, the way you’d look at a letter from somebody you’d known a long time.

“No,” said Maud.

“I’m the best you’ve got.” Rachael put the window away. “After you. I know I am. I’m better than Tamsin; Tamsin knows I am, Tamsin, tell her—“

“She is,” said Tamsin miserably.

“—and I can close a crushed hand in an hour and a cut in a breath and I’ve sat up with more people than anybody in this building except you, and I know what it’s like to be the one in the bed.” She heard her voice going up. She didn’t stop it. “They’ve got one man making doses. One. Kei. He’s not slept in a week, Hyvred says, and he’s the kindest person I’ve ever met and he’s going to kill himself doing it, and you’re going to send two sisters and Brother Ansgar who faints at blood—“

“I am,” said Maud.

“Send me.”

“No.”

“Why?”

Maud looked up at last.

“Because they’ve gone,” said Maud.

Rachael stopped.

“Your tall one,” said Maud. “On my step. This morning. Seven of them. Every quay. All at once, at the first light, turned their heads north-west and walked away.” She put the strip of paper down on the bandage table, very carefully, flat. “Where do you think they’ve gone, girl?”

Nobody said anything.

“Every hunter that’s ever come over that river wall,” said Maud, “came for you. You know that. I know that. The tall one’s counted them, and the funny one’s listened for them, and the big one’s stood at your back for two years. A healer’s the sweetest thing there is to whatever’s at the bottom of that pass. The old ones used to say so. I didn’t believe them, when I was a girl.” She was quiet. “Your fisherman believes them. The one with the rod. That’s why he sat down in the stands at Valcrest between you and the apple woman, in front of the whole city. He’d seen what happens to a healer they want. He’d buried one.”

Rachael didn’t say anything. She couldn’t.

“And now there’s a road swept down a mountain,” said Maud, “and a thing at the bottom of it calling people out of their doors, and seven of the things that watch you have turned their heads toward it and gone. And you’d like me to send you up the road after them.” She shook her head. “No. You’d be carrying the door with you. Every step. You’d be the door it wanted most.”

“That’s not your decision,” said Rachael. Very quietly.

“It’s entirely my decision,” said Maud. “It’s my infirmary. You’re my healer. I’m sending Sister Wenna and Sister Agathe and Brother Ansgar, who does faint at blood, but not until afterwards, which is the right time. And you’re staying here, on my step, where you’ve got two at your back and a river wall and a tall boy on the roof who’d know if a thing with no threads came within a mile of you.” She stood up. “When somebody who knows what they’re asking asks for you, by name, you’ll go. And I’ll pack your bag myself. Not before.”

She went out.

Rachael sat at the bandage table with her hands flat on it.

Tamsin wouldn’t look at her. Emrys was looking at her with his kind tired grey face, and she knew that if she looked back at him he’d say something gentle in his whisper, and she’d have to leave the room.

She looked at the bandages instead.

She went up to the roof at dusk.

The infirmary roof was flat, lead, with a low wall round it, where they dried the linen. Deston used it every morning; it was the best place in Kingsferry to listen to threads, he said, because it was high and there was nobody on it. There was nobody on it now. Only the linen, white, moving a little in the evening wind off the river, and the whole of Kingsferry going gold and grey below, and the river.

She stood at the wall and looked north-west.

Up the river. Past the bridge and the last quays and the willows, the long brown water coming down out of the north with the spring in it. Somewhere up there, a lake. A jetty. A man she’d known for nine years by a different face, who’d sat down between her and a woman with apples in front of forty thousand people and, when she asked him why, said because I could; and then just sat. And past the lake, north-west, a long way, a valley under a mountain she’d never seen, with a road going up it, and a girl who could see everything standing at the top of a tower, and a man making doses in a little room who hadn’t slept.

And seven people with no threads, walking.

She hated it. She’d hated it all afternoon, rolling bandages, setting a child’s wrist, sitting with an old man who was dying and knew it and wanted to talk about his garden. She’d hated it with a clean hard hate she hadn’t felt since Earth. Since a hospital bed and a nurse saying are you up for it? and her friends texting are you up for it? and everybody, everybody, deciding for her what she was up for.

She’d thought she’d left that in the other world.

She was very good at hating it. It was a skill.

Behind her, the door to the stair opened, and closed. Somebody stood just inside it. She didn’t turn round. She knew who it was by where he stopped: five paces off. Exactly five.

“Maud’s right,” said Gabe.

“I know she’s right.” Rachael didn’t turn round. “That’s the worst part.”

He didn’t say anything.

“Twenty-six years,” said Rachael. To the river. “Twenty-six years of people deciding what I could do. Can she go out. Can she go to the party. Can she have the window open. Is she up for it. Everybody very kind. Everybody washing their hands.” She breathed out. “And I came here, and I got better. Actually better. And for the first time in my whole life, I was the one who could do things. Not the one things were done for. The one in the chair by the bed. Not in the bed.” She heard her voice go and let it. “And now an old woman with a broom’s telling me I can’t go because I’m too precious.”

“That’s not what she said.”

“It’s what she meant.”

“No,” said Gabe. “It’s not.”

She turned round.

He was standing by the stair door with his hands in his pockets, still in his leather apron from the forge, with soot on his face and his big arms folded now, and that look he got. Not the forge look. The other one. The one he’d had at a lake, that first morning, when a stranger with a different face had sung the third part of a song they’d sung as boys, across the water, and Gabe had been the first one down the jetty.

“She said you’re the door it wants most,” said Gabe. “That’s not precious. That’s just true.” He shrugged. “Same as Declan. Same as the reason he sat down. He didn’t sit down because you couldn’t look after yourself. He sat down because he could see what you couldn’t, and he was bigger.” He looked at her. “That’s what you do. When you’re bigger. You don’t ask.”

Rachael didn’t say anything.

“You do it,” said Gabe. “In the ward. Every day. You stand between people and the thing that’s going to happen to them, and you don’t ask if they’re up for it. You just do it.” He was quiet. “You did it to that man’s hand this morning. Maud told me. She said you told him it’d hurt before every bit.” He shook his head. “Nobody ever did that for you. I know they didn’t. You told me once. On a bus. When you were nineteen.”

She’d forgotten that. A bus, in Singapore, at night, coming back from somewhere, Deston asleep on Gabe’s shoulder; and her saying, out of nowhere, looking out of the window, nobody ever tells you before the bit that hurts. And Gabe, who’d been seventeen and already very large and very quiet, saying I’ll tell you. And then never once getting the chance, because she’d never once let him.

“So that’s what Maud’s doing,” said Gabe. “She’s telling you before the bit that hurts. That’s all.” He looked at the river. “And you’re allowed to shout. And swear. And tell her she’s a terrible person. And she’ll still be here when you’ve finished.”

Rachael looked at him for a long time.

Then she laughed. It came out of her cracked and wet and not at all like her laugh, and she put her hand over her mouth to stop it, and it didn’t stop.

“You absolute bastard,” said Rachael. “You’ve been talking to Maud.”

“She talks a lot,” said Gabe. “When you’re not there.”

She didn’t go to Thornwall.

She wrote to it instead. That night, in the channel, sitting on the roof of the infirmary in the dark with the linen moving round her like slow white ghosts, and Gabe sitting against the stair door, five paces off, not saying anything, which was the thing Gabe was best at in two worlds.

She thought for a long time. She’d never been good in the channel. She said one line, mostly, under somebody else’s, and it was usually rude.

She shut the window.

She sat on the roof and looked north-west, at the dark, where the river came from.

Sabine was in the bakery doorway.

Rachael saw her from the roof, when she stood up to go down. Across the river street, in the deep doorway of the shut bakery opposite, the way she’d sat every night since the apple, on a stool, with her sword across her knees and a cup of something and her face like a closed ledger. Sabine had taken the job for the pay. She’d said so on the first day, to everyone, and she said it still, whenever anyone said anything that sounded like thanks. I’m paid. She’d sat in that doorway every night for half a year, through the end of the autumn and the whole of the winter, in the rain and then the snow and then the rain again. Rachael had said, once, in the autumn, at the window, you don’t have to, and Sabine had said not for this, and that had been all.

Rachael went down the stair, and out of the front door, and across the river street in the dark, and stood in front of the bakery doorway.

Sabine looked up at her.

“They’re gone,” said Rachael. “The seven. You’ve heard.”

“I’ve heard.”

“So you could go to bed. Tonight. If you wanted.”

Sabine looked at her for a long moment, over the cup.

“Not for this,” said Sabine.

Rachael stood there in the dark.

She’d have gone back in, a year ago. She’d have said suit yourself and gone back across the street and gone up to bed and lain there knowing Sabine was in the doorway and been angry about it, a little, the way she’d been angry at the nurses, and the friends, and everybody who’d ever been kind to her without asking. She’d have been very good at it.

She went into the doorway instead.

There was a second stool. There always had been; Sabine was a woman who planned for things. Rachael sat on it. Not close. The doorway was deep and the stools were at either side of it, a pace apart, more. Near enough that Sabine would know she was there. Not so near either of them had to do anything about it.

Sabine didn’t say anything.

They sat in the bakery doorway in the dark and looked across the river street at the infirmary, where a light was on in the end ward, where a bargeman called Corliss was lying with his hand on his chest, opening and closing it, opening and closing it, in the dark, because he couldn’t stop.

“Thank you,” said Rachael.

Sabine didn’t move.

“For the doorway,” said Rachael. “All winter. I never said.”

“I’m paid,” said Sabine.

“I know you are.” Rachael looked at the infirmary. “Thank you anyway.”

There was a long silence.

Then Sabine, without looking at her, held out the cup. Across the gap between the stools. Not far. Just far enough that Rachael had to lean to take it.

Rachael leaned. She took it. It was something hot and dark with a great deal of something in it that wasn’t tea, and she drank some, and coughed, and Sabine made a sound that in anybody else would have been a laugh.

She gave the cup back. They sat there.

At dawn she walked to the river wall.

The place on the far side of the river street where she’d thrown the apple, in the autumn, in the rain. The long low wall of grey stone along the top of the bank, with the Wren going past below it, high and brown and loud, and the morning coming up pale behind the willows on the far bank. She hadn’t slept much. She’d sat in the doorway till the third hour, and then gone up, and lain awake listening to the river.

Gabe was behind her. Five paces. He’d come out of Rope Street at the grey, the way he did every morning, to walk her to the step.

She stood at the wall and looked north-west. Up the river. Where everything was coming from.

She thought about Corliss’s hand. About nobody ever tells you before the bit that hurts. About a man with a different face, sitting down on a bench in Valcrest between her and the thing that wanted her, and saying because I could, and her saying sit there anyway. About an old woman with a broom saying not before.

She thought about being the patient. Twenty-six years of it. And two years and a half of not.

She thought she was going to be very, very good at this one too. Whatever this one was. Being the one people stood in front of, without minding it. Being the bigger hand, and the smaller one, both. She thought it would take her about as long as the last one had. She thought she’d probably hate it, a bit, for years, kindly, and mean it.

She thought she was glad to be here.

She’d always been glad. Since the first four seconds, on the floor of the hall, with an old man’s two fingers on her wrist and something going right. She was the only one of the thirty-five who’d never once wished it hadn’t happened, and the only one who’d never said a word against the Advisor, and she’d been ashamed of both, a bit, in front of the others, for two years and a half, as if being glad to be alive here were a thing you had to apologise for to people who’d lost more.

She wasn’t ashamed this morning. She stood at the river wall in the spring and was glad, plainly, all the way through, without a crack in it anywhere; and if Dennis had been there he’d have heard it not ring, and she found she didn’t mind if he did.

“Gabe,” said Rachael, without turning round.

“Yeah?”

She thought about it.

Five paces. Half a year of five paces, at her back in every street since the apple; and two years before that of near enough. Near enough that she knew he was there; not so near she had to do anything about it. She’d never once asked him to stand there. He’d just worked it out, on the river road out of Solmere, the first week, and never once got it wrong.

“Three,” said Rachael.

There was a pause behind her.

“Three what?”

“Paces.” She looked at the river. “You can stand at three. If you want.”

The pause went on for a long time. Long enough for a barge to come down the river past them, low in the water, with a man at the steering oar who lifted his hand to the healer on the wall, because everybody on the river knew who she was.

Then she heard Gabe move. Two steps. Big, careful, the way you’d step on ice.

And stop.

Three paces. Near enough that she could hear him breathing. Not so near either of them had to do anything about it.

They stood there, the two of them, at the river wall, at three paces, and watched the spring come down the Wren.

“Sister Maud’s going to say I’m late,” said Rachael, after a while.

“You are late.”

“I know.” She didn’t move. “Let her.”
