The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 6: Nine

Chapter 172

Letters

5,539 words · 24 min read

Annis was in the yard when they came over the bridge, and she put down the bucket she was carrying, and looked at them, and said, “Well. You brought her back, then.”

It was the twelfth day out of Cinderhold, in the late afternoon. The river was low and slow under the five arches of the bridge at Ashby, and the willows along it had gone yellow, and the town on the near bank had its chimneys going in the first cold of the autumn. And on the far side of the bridge, where the east road went out of Ashby and the north road came in, the posting house with the faded horn on its sign, and the yard, and the well, and a big grey-haired woman with flour to her elbows standing by it.

“I brought her back,” said her father.

Annis looked at Alea. At Astra on her back. At the pot in her arms, with the stick in it and its three leaves, gone mostly gold now, and one, very small, green, at the top, new. At the knife on her hip.

Her eyes stayed on the knife a moment.

“Back room,” said Annis. “Both of you. I’ll put a pan on.” She picked up her bucket. Then she put it down again. “And there’s letters.”

Alea’s ears went straight up.

“Letters?”

“Two,” said Annis. “Came in this last week. By the coast rider and the king’s. I’ve had them in the box on the wall.” She looked at Alea. “One’s for the lake girl. Care of the posting house at Ashby. Whoever wrote it can’t spell posting.” She looked at Alea’s father. “And one’s for you.”

Alea’s was in a hand she’d never seen and knew at once.

Big. Sprawling. Leaning to the right as if it were walking into a wind. Blotted twice on the outside where the pen had been pressed too hard. The lake girl, it said. Alea. Care of the posting hous at Ashby. Plese keep for her she is coming back that way people always come back the way they went.

She stood in the kitchen of the posting house with it in her hands, with the pot on the table beside her and Annis at the stove pretending not to watch, and didn’t open it.

“She sent two,” said Annis, to the stove. “The coast rider said. One to Wrenhaven, one here. In case.” She turned something in the pan. “Clever girl. People do come back the way they went. Most of them.” She didn’t look round. “He always did.”

Alea sat down at the end of the kitchen table, where she’d sat seven weeks ago with a borrowed pen and the posting house’s soft brown paper, writing I know yours hasn’t got one. She put the letter down in front of her. Flat. She looked at it.

Across the kitchen, at the door to the back room, her father was standing with his own letter in his hand. Unopened. Folded small and sealed in grey wax, with one word on the outside in a plain upright hand that Alea had seen once before, on a letter he’d read sitting in a gap in a wall with his legs over the drop.

Ars.

He looked at it for a long time. Then at her.

“Go on,” he said. “Yours first.”

She opened it.

It was two pages. Both sides. In the big leaning hand, with blots, and crossings-out, and one place near the bottom of the first page where something had been written and scrubbed out so hard the paper had gone thin.

Lake girl,

I got yours. The rider brought it to the quay and asked for the boat-hook girl like you said and everyone pointed. I have read it nine times. Dad would say that’s eight more than a letter needs. I told the whole quay you saluted a bucket. They don’t believe me. Nobody on the quay believes anything that happened at the Lists. I’ve stopped minding.

You said I write terribly. I do. You write terribly too. Your letter had a blot on it the shape of a crab. I kept it.

About my dad.

You said you thought about crossing it out. Don’t. I’m glad you didn’t. Everyone at home crosses it out. They go round it. They say “since” and stop. You didn’t go round it. It was the first time since that anyone just said it.

He went over the side in a squall two winters ago. Off the Gullhaven bar, at night, hauling. The boat came back on its own on the morning tide with nobody in it. The guild sold her to pay what he owed. That’s the part I never say.

I bought her back.

With the Lists money. Second place pays. Not much. Enough for a boat that leaks. She was called the Grey Gull. I’ve painted it out. She’s called NEXT YEAR now. Big letters, on the stern. The harbourmaster says that’s not a name, it’s a threat. I said yes.

She leaks like a basket. I’m mending her. I go out in her every morning before the guild boats and fish off the bar where he went over, and I talk to him. Out loud. The gulls think I’m mad. I don’t care. You said you’d tell your mum about me. So I thought I’d tell my dad about you.

I told him a lake girl came at me like a pike. He’d have liked that. He’d have laughed till he fell off the bar.

Alea stopped reading.

She sat at the end of the kitchen table with the letter in both hands and her ears gone flat and didn’t move for a long time.

So I thought I’d tell my dad about you.

At the stove, Annis had stopped turning whatever was in the pan.

She read the rest.

It’s cold here now. The herring are in. The guild says I’m too young to take a boat past the bar on my own. I take her past the bar on my own every morning. The guild says nothing about it because the guild master’s wife says if they stop me she’ll stop cooking for him.

I’m training. On the quay. With the hooks. Against the bollards, mostly, because nobody on the quay will fight me any more since the Lists. I’ve got a new thing. I’m not telling you what it is. You’ll see.

Next year.

You said your dad’s very good. I watched him on the sand at the end. I couldn’t look at his face. Nobody could. But I watched him look at you when Lucan read your box out, and I thought: that’s what my dad looked like when I pulled the Pemberton girl off her feet. Only bigger.

Did you tell her? Your mum. About me.

If you did, tell her the hooks were my dad’s. Tell her he cut them down for me when I was ten. Tell her I’m going to win next year.

Come to Gullhaven one day. Bring your dad. He can fish off our quay. The fish here are very stupid. He’ll be bored.

Sorrel. (the boat-hook girl)

(NEXT YEAR leaks. Did I say? It leaks.)

Alea put the letter down.

She sat there. The posting house kitchen was warm and smelled of bread and onions and the river. Annis had gone back to the pan. Her father was standing in the doorway to the back room with his own letter still unopened in his hand, watching her. She felt him watch. She didn’t look up.

“She bought her dad’s boat back,” said Alea.

“I heard,” said her father. Very quietly. “From here.”

“With the Lists money. She called it Next Year.” Alea’s voice did something. “She goes out in it every morning and talks to him. Off the bar where he went over.” She looked at the letter. “She told him about me.”

Her father didn’t say anything.

“I thought there was nowhere for her to go,” said Alea. “When I wrote. I thought, she hasn’t got a grave. There’s just the sea.” She put her hand flat on the letter. “There’s a boat. She goes out in it. Every morning.” She swallowed. “That’s where she goes.”

“It’s a good place,” said her father.

“It’s a boat. It leaks.”

“So did the jetty,” said her father. “When your mother stood on the end of it. Half the planks were gone.” He was quiet. “You don’t pick the place because it’s sound. You pick it because it’s theirs.”

Alea looked at him in the doorway.

Then she looked at the letter again. At the big leaning hand, and the blots, and the place near the bottom of the first page scrubbed out so hard the paper had gone thin. She held it up to the lamp. You could still see it, almost. The shape of the word that had been there before Sorrel decided not to write it.

Since.

She put the letter down, very carefully, and folded it along its folds, and put it in the inside pocket of her coat. Over her heart. With Higu’s moon chart.

“Your turn,” she said.

Her father opened his letter in the doorway.

He didn’t sit down. He broke the grey wax with his thumbnail and unfolded it and read it standing up, in the light from the kitchen, with the rod leaning against the doorframe beside him and the hammer in his belt. It wasn’t long. She could see that from the table. One side of a sheet. A few lines, in the plain upright hand.

He read it.

And then he read it again. And Alea, at the table, watched him do it, the way she’d watched him do everything all her life without ever being able to see his face. The shoulders. The breath. The hand holding the paper, which was quite still, and then wasn’t, and then was again.

He lowered it.

He didn’t say anything. He stood in the doorway of the back room at the posting house at Ashby, where he’d slept eight times on the way east with nobody, and looked at nothing.

“Dad?” said Alea.

He held it out to her.

She got up from the table and took it. She read it standing beside him in the doorway. It was very short.

Ars.

Senna put it into my hand on the gatehouse wall. I read it there. I could not come down for an hour. Then Severin found me and we sat on the forge step and did not hit anything for the whole afternoon, which I am told the castle has never seen.

I have done the sum again. All the way to the end, this time. It comes out where you said.

We will come. In the frost. He wants to see the hens.

So do I.

H.

Alea read it twice. Then she gave it back to him.

He took it. He folded it along its folds, very carefully, the way she’d folded Sorrel’s, and put it in the inside pocket of his coat. Over his heart. With the drawing, folded in four.

“He’s coming,” said Alea.

“He’s coming.”

“With the king. To the lake.”

“In the frost,” said her father. “He says.”

Alea looked at him.

“You asked him to,” she said. “In your letter. You said come to the lake in the frost, bring the king.” She’d read it, over his shoulder, in the infirmary, at dawn, before he sealed it. He’d let her. “And he said yes.”

“He said yes.”

“Has he ever been?” said Alea. “To the lake. In nine years.”

Her father was quiet for a long time.

“No,” he said. “Not once. He sent people. The king came. Maren came. He never came.” He looked at the folded letter in his coat, through the cloth. “He used to write, Rowan says. Every year. To Rowan. Is he well. Rowan wrote back he’s well. That was all.” He was quiet. “Eight years of is he well. And never once came to see.”

“He’s coming now,” said Alea.

“He’s coming now,” her father said. And then, very quietly, to the posting house kitchen, to nobody, in a voice she’d heard him use perhaps three times in her life: “So do I.”

At the stove, Annis put the pan down on the table between them with a bang. Bread and onions and something fried. And looked at the two of them, standing in the doorway of her back room with letters in their coats, and folded her floury arms.

“Eat something,” said Annis. “Both of you. You’re too thin for whatever that is.”

She wrote back to Sorrel that night.

At the end of the kitchen table, under the lamp, with a borrowed pen and the posting house’s soft brown paper, the way she had seven weeks ago. Her father was asleep in the back room. Asleep. She’d heard him go, the breath changing, through the door, within a minute of lying down, the way he’d gone every night since Cinderhold, as if sleep were a thing he’d been saving for nine years and was spending all at once.

She thought for a long time. Then she wrote.

Sorrel,

I got yours at Ashby. Annis kept it in the box. She says you can’t spell posting. She also says you’re a clever girl. I told her you are.

I’m writing this on the way home. We went to the grave. My mum’s. On the full moon. I’ll tell you about it one day. Not in a letter. It’s too big for a letter and I write terribly.

But I told her about you. Yes. At the grave, under the moon, out loud. I told her you nearly had me three times and I came at you like a pike. I told her your hooks were your dad’s. I’ll tell her the rest next year. That he cut them down for you when you were ten. That you’re going to win.

You’re not going to win.

She stopped. She looked at that. She grinned at the paper, alone, at midnight, in a posting house kitchen. And went on.

I’m glad about the boat. NEXT YEAR is the best name for a boat I’ve ever heard. My dad says you pick a place because it’s theirs, not because it’s sound. He says our jetty was half gone when my mum stood on the end of it and picked the lake.

My dad gave me a knife. It was my mum’s. She bought it for him and threw his soldier’s knife in the lake. I’m wearing it now. On my hip. I’m never taking it off.

I’ve got a tree too. In a pot. From her grave. A boy with antlers grew it. It’s going home with me to the lake. When it’s big you can come and see it. Bring the boat. You’ll have to row it up a river for three weeks. It’ll sink. I’ll pull you out with a pike hook. I’ve got a new hook now. A smith made it. It won’t bend.

I’m training too. I’ve got a new thing. I’m not telling you either.

Next year.

Alea (the lake girl)

(Tell your dad I said hello. Off the bar. Out loud. The gulls can think what they like.)

She folded it. She wrote on the outside, Sorrel. NEXT YEAR. The quay, Gullhaven. The boat-hook girl. And put it in the box on the wall with the others, and Annis, who hadn’t gone to bed and had been sitting by the stove the whole time pretending to mend a stocking, didn’t look at it.

The north road went out of Ashby at dawn.

Over the bridge, and up through the town, and out on the other side, north, the way it came down. A long pale line over the hills, very straight, the way the old roads were. Alea had seen it from the far side of the valley, seven weeks ago, coming down off the downs. His road. The one he’d come down eight times on his own, at the end of summer, to cross the bridge and go east.

She was on it now. Going the other way.

She’d thought it would be strange. She’d thought it would feel like walking backwards through a story. It didn’t. It felt like a road. A long one, going north over the hills, in the cold clear morning, with frost on the grass at the edges and the sun coming up low and gold on her right.

He knew everything on it.

She’d seen that on the way east, after Ashby: the milestone with the bite out of it, the well that tasted of iron, the farm with the dog. She saw it again now, going north. He touched the milestone, a mile out, without looking, the way you’d touch a doorframe in your own house. He filled the blue bucket at the iron well, for the tree, though there’d be a stream in an hour. He said hello, dog to the dog at the farm and the dog stopped barking and looked at him, puzzled.

And the thorn tree.

It was in a field on the right, going north. Alone. Very old, bent all one way by a wind from the west. He’d slept under it the first year, he’d told her, on the way east. In the rain. Because he didn’t want a roof.

He stopped in the road and looked at it.

She stopped beside him.

“Every year since,” he said, “I’ve walked past it going east, and thought about that night. And walked past it coming back, a month later, and thought about it again.” He was quiet. “It was the worst night I’ve ever spent anywhere.”

“Worse than the rise?”

“The rise was the worst night of my life,” he said. “That was the worst night I’ve ever spent. They’re different.” He looked at the thorn. “On the rise I had something to do. Under that I didn’t. I just lay there in the rain.”

Alea looked at the tree. Black, and bent, and old, and empty, in the frost, in the morning.

She put the pot down on the verge.

She climbed over the field wall. She walked across the frosty grass to the thorn tree, on her own, with Astra on her back and the knife on her hip. And stood under it, where he’d lain in the rain nine years ago with nothing over him but thorns. And put her hand flat on the trunk. The way Maigo put his hand on the ash.

“Thank you,” said Alea, to the tree. Out loud. “For keeping him dry.”

It hadn’t. It was a thorn tree; it couldn’t have. He’d been soaked to the skin, he’d told her. She said it anyway.

Then she came back across the field and over the wall and picked up the pot, and her father was standing in the road looking at her with the rod on his shoulder, and she couldn’t see his face, she never could, but she didn’t need to.

“Trees like being told things,” said Alea. “Maigo says.”

“Maigo would know,” said her father.

They walked on.

It got colder every day.

They walked north for ten days on his road, and the country changed round them the way it changed in the north in autumn: all at once, in the night. They went to sleep in the yellow and woke in the gold. Then the gold went brown. The leaves came off the trees along the road and lay in the ditches. The fields were stubble, cut, pale, with the stooks gone in. The mornings were white.

The first hard frost came on the eighth night, near Caddow.

She woke in the dark before dawn, by the embers of the fire, with her father’s coat over her feet again and his breath going slow and even on the far side, asleep, and the blue bucket upside down by the fire. And she knew before she opened her eyes. The cold. The particular cold of the first real frost in the north, that she’d woken up to every autumn of her life at the lake, that got into your nose and made it hurt.

She sat up.

The world was white.

The field they’d camped beside, the stubble, every cut stalk of it, white. The hedge. The road. The grass under her hand. All of it gone white and stiff and glittering in the grey before the sun, as if somebody had come in the night and dusted the whole country with powdered glass. Her breath went up white. The pot beside her, the earth in it, white on top, and the stick, and its leaves.

She looked at the leaves.

Three gold ones, and the new green one at the top. All white with frost. Stiff. She put her finger on the green one, very lightly. It was cold, and hard, like a leaf made of sugar.

“Oh,” said Alea. Very quietly. Frightened.

Her father was awake. She hadn’t heard him wake. He was sitting up on the far side of the embers, looking at her, and at the pot.

“It’s all right,” he said.

“It’s frozen.”

“It’s an ash,” he said. “From the red. From a tree that grew where nothing grows, out of a seed off the fort’s tree, and stood through the white winter on the knoll in the snow. It’s had worse than a frost.” He was quiet. “Wait for the sun.”

She waited for the sun.

It came up over the hills on the right, low and gold and enormous, the way it came up in the north in autumn. The light came across the white stubble field and lit every frozen stalk at once, so that the whole field blazed. And it came across the road, and the embers, and the blue bucket, and the pot.

And the frost on the leaves went.

Not all at once. Edge first. The way frost goes off a window when the sun comes round. The white going clear, and then going to water, and then going. The three gold leaves came back gold. And the green one at the top came back green. Softly. As if nothing had happened. As if it had been through worse.

Alea let out a breath she hadn’t known she was holding.

“First frost,” said her father.

“First frost,” said Alea.

She looked at the white field in the sun. The stubble. Pale and cut and glittering.

“The pumpkins,” she said.

“Half of them,” said her father. “Odette’ll say. The frost’ll have had half.” He stood up, stiffly, and stretched, and picked up the blue bucket. “She’ll be right. She’s always right.” He looked north, up the road. “Late pumpkins.”

“Both of us,” said Alea.

He looked down at her.

“Both of us,” he agreed.

They came into the Wren valley on the tenth day.

She knew it before she saw it. The smell. Water, and willow, and peat smoke, and the particular cold of air that’s come down off a lake. The road went over a long low hill, the last one, the way it always did coming home from Caddow, and she’d been watching for the top of it since noon, and she knew the exact place, the exact tree on the left, a crooked hawthorn, where you came over the brow and could see.

She stopped before it.

She didn’t know why. She stood in the road a few paces short of the top of the hill, with the pot in her arms and Astra on her back and the knife on her hip and her father beside her, and didn’t go on.

She’d always been the one at the top of the road.

That was what she was thinking. Eight times. Standing at Rowan’s gate at the top of the town with Rowan’s hand on her shoulder, watching him go up the lake road in the grey with the rod over his shoulder, until it went round the hill and he wasn’t there. And eight times, a month and a half later, in the first frost, standing at the top of the lake road with Rowan and Odette, watching him come down it. Alone. Nobody saying anything.

She’d never once come over this hill.

She’d never once been the one coming home. She’d always been the one waiting.

“Alea,” said her father.

She looked at him.

“I’m frightened,” she said. She didn’t know she was going to say it. “That’s stupid. It’s home. I’m frightened of coming home.”

He didn’t say anything. He didn’t tell her it was stupid, or that it was all right, or that there was nothing to be frightened of. He stood beside her in the road a few paces short of the top of the last hill, with the rod on his shoulder and the hammer in his belt and the blue bucket in his hand, and let her be frightened.

Then he put the bucket down.

And put his hands together, in front of his chest. Not palm to palm. Just the fingertips. And tapped them. Once. Twice. Three times. The way Hesper had shown her in the infirmary. The way her mother had stood at the flap of every tent on the border.

She’d never seen him do it.

She’d never once, in seventeen years, seen her father be frightened of anything, enough to count.

“Well, then,” said her father.

Alea laughed. It came out of her short and wet and surprised. She stood in the road with the pot in her arms and laughed at her father, the Black Blade, the Hero of the Redmarch, standing at the top of a hill in the Wren valley tapping his fingertips together like a healer at a tent flap.

“You’re frightened too,” she said.

“I’ve never once come home with anybody,” he said. “Not on this road. Eight times, coming down that hill on my own.” He picked up the bucket. “I don’t know how to do it with somebody. I’ve never done it.”

“Neither have I.”

“Then we’ll find out,” said her father.

They went over the top together.

And there it was.

The whole of the Wren valley, at once, in the late afternoon, gold and brown and white in the low sun. The river, a long silver line going away through the fields. The town on its hill, with its chimneys going, every one, in the cold, the smoke going up straight in the still air. The church tower. The square. The road going down through the town and out of the far side, and down between the hawthorn hedges and the stone wall and the reeds, to the water.

The lake.

All of it. Lake Ilse. Long and blue and still, very still, the way it only ever was by the north shore, in the cold, with the hills on the far side going up purple. And on the near shore, at the bottom of the lake road, small, with smoke coming out of its chimney because somebody had lit the fire for them, a cabin. A hen-yard. A field with pumpkins in it, orange, and half of them dark from the frost.

And a jetty.

Going out into the blue. Silver. Too many planks. With nobody on the end of it.

Alea stood at the top of the last hill and looked at it, and couldn’t say anything.

At the top of the lake road, on the edge of the town, by the militia hall, she could see something on the step. Small and brown. A basket. With nothing in it.

And at the top of the town, by a gate she knew, very small at this distance, somebody was standing. Big and grey, with a chain round his neck like a collar on a bear. Not waving. Just standing. Looking at the road over the last hill, the way he’d looked at it eight times in the first frost.

She saw him see them.

She saw him not move. She saw him stand at his gate at the top of the town and look at two people coming over the last hill, instead of one. A man with a rod on his shoulder. A girl beside him with a sword on her back and a tree in her arms.

And then she saw him turn, and go in. Quickly. Not running, because the mayor didn’t run. Into the house. And shut the door.

“He’s gone in,” said Alea. Puzzled. “Rowan. He saw us and went in.”

Her father looked at the house at the top of the town for a long moment.

“He’s getting the cups out,” he said.

They went down.

Through the fields, and over the little bridge on the river road, and up into the town at dusk. People came out. Of course they did. Every door. The baker with flour on his arms. The miller’s wife. The children, who ran ahead and shouted, and then stopped, and stared at the tree in her arms. Old Mabry from the council, who said well, well about eleven times. And at the corner of the square, on her stool, where she’d sat every day for thirty years, very small and very old and very straight, Mother Breck.

Alea stopped in front of her.

She didn’t decide to. She just found she had. She stood in front of the old woman on her stool in the square, with the pot in her arms, and looked down at her.

“We took it,” said Alea. “Your stone. Dad put it on the top. At the grave. Where it catches the light.”

Mother Breck looked up at her for a long time with her old wet eyes.

Then she reached out, slowly, with her dry old fingers like paper, and touched the edge of the clay pot. And then the tree in it. The green leaf at the top. Very lightly.

“Well,” said Mother Breck. “She’ll want feeding, that one.”

And went back to her onions.

They didn’t stop at Rowan’s.

Her father didn’t. He walked past the gate at the top of the town, with the rod on his shoulder, and didn’t look at it, and didn’t stop, and went on down the lake road in the dusk. And Alea, beside him, saw the door of the house shut, and a light in the kitchen window, and the shape of somebody sitting at a table with something on it.

“You’re not going in?” she said.

“Not yet,” said her father.

“He’s got the cups out.”

“I know,” said her father. “I said I’d knock. Like a stranger.” He looked down the lake road toward the water, and the cabin with its chimney going. “A stranger doesn’t come in through the gate with a bucket on his way home. A stranger goes home first. And washes his face. And puts the hammer by the door for the morning, and the tree by the water. And then walks back up the hill in the dark, on purpose. And knocks.”

Alea looked at him.

“You’ve been planning this,” she said.

“Since a kitchen in the summer,” said her father. “Since not tonight.”

They went on down the lake road between the hawthorn hedges and the wall and the reeds, in the dusk, with the frost coming down already on the stubble on either side. Past the militia hall at the top, where five girls were standing in the yard in the cold, very still, in a row, not coming out, not calling. Just standing. Watching them go by. And an empty basket on the step, with a stone in it.

Her father didn’t stop there either. But he looked at the basket. She saw him look.

“Tomorrow,” he said. To the basket. Very quietly.

And at the bottom of the lake road, in the last of the light, the hens came round the corner of the cabin in a body and saw him, and stopped, and stood there in the yard in a row with their feathers puffed out against the cold, glaring at him with the deep and patient hatred of creatures who’d been fed by the mayor for four months and had been perfectly happy with the arrangement.

“Hello,” said her father, to the hens.

The big red one lowered her head.

“Oh, no,” said Alea. “Not you too.”

“They only hate Gabe,” said her father. “Rowan says.”

The big red one went for him.

Alea put the pot down on the step of the cabin, very carefully, out of the way. And sat down beside it. And put her face in her hands, on the step of her own house, in the first frost, with the lake going dark in front of her and the jetty going out into it, and her father in the yard behind her being chased round the woodpile by three hens and not, she noticed, going up it.

And laughed, until she couldn’t breathe.

She was home.
