The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 3: The Anniversary

Chapter 62

Hook, Reel, Cut

4,204 words · 18 min read

On Earth, Yan had carried Ice in Apex.

That was how they’d always put it, the squad, and Ice had put it that way loudest of all. In Counter-Strike, Ice carried them: the AWP, the angles, the patience, the shot that came out of nowhere at the end of a lost round and turned it. In Apex, it was the other way round. Ice had been a disaster in Apex. Cheerfully, completely, a disaster. He ran into fights he couldn’t win and died in them and narrated his own deaths as he went, in a sports commentator’s voice, so that the whole squad would be laughing too hard to play. And Ice is down. Tragic. A promising career, cut short. And Yan would sigh, and go and get his banner, and bring him back, and then go and win the fight Ice had started, on his own, because somebody had to.

They’d won a tournament like that once. Ice had died in every single round of the final. Yan had carried him through all of them. Afterwards, on the call, with everybody shouting, Ice had said, very seriously, I was the reason we won. Trust. And Yan had laughed so hard he’d had to take his headset off.

He’d carried things all his life. That was what he did. His mother, mostly. And a squad. And a dead man’s banner, round and round a map at three in the morning, because the dead man was his friend and the game wasn’t over yet.

He’d never once, in all that time, been the one who got carried.

He felt it before Wendy screamed.

He was standing over Suzu with his hand out. That was all he had. He was still an Initiate, forty-three percent of the way to the first tier, with a feeling for candles and no way to do anything about any of it, and so he’d done the only thing left: put himself over Suzu, with the west one tearing at her, and held his hand out at it as if he could stop it by looking. He hadn’t decided to; there’d been nothing to decide. The west one hadn’t stopped. It had looked down at him with the place where its face should have been, and lifted one long grey arm.

And then the sea arrived.

That was the only way he’d ever be able to say it. He’d felt it once before, the night the cold one went out: a stillness so big he couldn’t see the edges of it, like trying to see the sea from inside it. He’d felt it again, faintly, worn into the grip of a black sword in a chapel. Old. Like the smell of someone on a coat.

It wasn’t faint now.

It came across the Redmarch from the north-west like a tide coming in across flat sand. Not fast. It didn’t need to be fast. It came in, and it kept coming in, and it filled everything. The red ground. The air. The fifty grey shapes in their ragged ring. The dying fire, and the soldiers on the ground, and Aldous on his knees, and the five of them. It came in round all of them and over them and through them until Yan was standing in it up to his neck, and then over his head, and he couldn’t breathe, and he didn’t need to, and he went down on one knee on the red without meaning to.

Every single one of the shapes stopped moving.

Not the way they stopped when they were counting. Not still-then-somewhere-else. They stopped the way an insect stops in a drop of resin. Mid-burst, some of them, with one long leg off the ground. They just stopped, all fifty of them, and stayed stopped, and Yan could feel their cold little candles flickering inside the stillness like flames in a jar with the lid screwed down.

Then Wendy screamed Salty’s name, and the east one jumped, and stopped too, for a different reason.

Yan saw the line.

He was the only one who’d been looking the right way. He’d turned his head toward the sea, the way you’d turn toward a sound. And he saw the line come out of the north-west, a long way off, very fast, so fine it was only a flicker in the sunlight. He saw it go past him, over his head, across the broken ring. He saw it touch the east one, in the air, in the middle of its jump, two paces from Wendy.

He didn’t see the cut. Nobody could have. It was just there, and then the thing was in two halves on the red, and Wendy was standing over them with her last arrow drawn.

And the line was still hanging in the air, perfectly straight, perfectly still, for one heartbeat. Then the man at the end of it reeled in.

Yan watched him do it.

He was a hundred paces off. More. Walking. He didn’t stop walking to reel; he did it as he came, unhurried, his right hand turning the little reel on the rod and his left hand holding the rod out in front of him at an easy angle. The line came back to him across the red, glinting, gathering itself, until there was a hook at the end of it, an ordinary iron fishing hook, small and dull, swinging gently from the rod’s tip.

He was a plain man. That was the first thing. Not tall, not short. A plain coat, brown, the kind a farmer wears. A pack on his back. Boots red to the ankle with the dust of the Redmarch. A face Yan couldn’t have described if you’d asked him, because it was the kind of face you didn’t look at twice, and Yan had looked at it three times already and couldn’t hold on to it.

He was the most frightening thing Yan had ever seen.

Not because of anything he did. Because of what Yan could feel. The sea was coming from him. All of it. Every drop of it, that had come in over the red and filled it up and pinned fifty demons in place like insects, was coming out of a plain man in a farmer’s coat, walking across the Redmarch with a fishing rod, as easily as a man breathes out.

The west one moved first.

The one over Yan. The one Suzu had been holding. It had stopped like all the rest, with its arm up over him, and now it turned its horned head slowly toward the man walking across the red, and Yan felt its cold candle flare, the way a cornered animal’s does. It was bigger than the scouts. Stronger. It could move inside the stillness, a little. It pulled its legs out of Suzu’s coils, and turned, and set itself, the way a bull sets itself.

The man cast.

Yan had watched people fish. Old men on concrete piers, on Earth, with buckets and radios. It wasn’t anything like that. There was no wind-up, no flourish. The man’s wrist turned, once, very slightly, and the hook went out. A long flat arc, low over the red, faster than an arrow. It went a hundred paces as if it were ten. It caught the west one high on the shoulder, tock, an ordinary small sound, like a hook catching on a branch.

And the man reeled.

The west one came off its feet.

It didn’t fall. It flew. Backward and up, off the red, toward him, as if it weighed nothing, as if it were a perch coming out of still water on a summer evening, all its long grey limbs flailing, a hundred paces of air in two heartbeats. And as it came to him, the man moved.

Once.

Yan saw that much, this time. The rod came round. Not the line, the rod: the shaft of it, the long plain wooden shaft, coming round in a short flat stroke, the way you’d swat a fly. And it went through the west one as if the west one were smoke. And the man took one step to the side, and the two halves of it went past him on either side and hit the red behind him and rolled, and he didn’t look round at them.

He was still walking.

The north one came at him.

The one with Salty’s greatsword in its side. It had been walking toward Salty and the black sword, slowly, and now it turned and went for the man instead. Not trotting. Running, on all fours, its long arms going like a dog’s forelegs, horns down, the hilt of Salty’s sword sticking out of its hip and swinging. It came across the red at him faster than a horse.

He didn’t cast. He just waited.

Yan watched him wait. He’d stopped walking. He was standing on the red with the rod held out in front of him, low, at that same easy angle, and the hook swinging at the end of the line, and he was perfectly still. Not stiff. Still the way water is still. The north one came at him across sixty paces, forty, twenty, and he didn’t move, didn’t move, didn’t move, and Yan’s whole body was screaming at him to move—

At five paces, he moved. Once.

The north one went past him on both sides. Salty’s greatsword fell out of it and clattered on the red.

The man looked down at the greatsword for a moment. Then he went on walking.

The south one ran.

It had been fighting Rhea, out past the dead fire, and Rhea had been losing. Yan could see her now, on her knees on the red, with her face covered in blood and her sword in both hands and the sheen on it guttering out. The south one had been standing over her. It wasn’t standing over her anymore. It had turned, the moment the north one went past the man in two pieces, and it was running. East. Back toward the gullies, back toward Kharvos, huge and grey and fast, not looking back.

It was a hundred and fifty paces away when the man cast.

It was two hundred when the hook caught it.

He reeled it all the way back. Two hundred paces, across the red, off its feet, through the air. Rhea watched it go over her head with her mouth open. And when it came to him, he moved once, and it went past him in two pieces, and he didn’t look round.

The fifty scouts were still standing there, pinned.

The man stopped walking. He was nearly at the broken ring now, thirty paces off, with the dead fire smoking between him and them, and he stood there and looked at the scouts, one after another, slowly. Yan saw him do it. He saw him turn his head and look along the whole ragged line of them, as if he were counting them, the way they’d counted the towers of Cinderhold all year.

Then he let them go.

Yan felt it happen. The sea didn’t go out. It just opened, somehow, in one direction. East. And fifty grey shapes came unstuck all at once, like a hand opening, and ran. Not in bursts. Not still-then-somewhere-else. Just ran, the way the scouts had run from the horns in the winter, scattering, in every direction, all of them going east, back across the red, falling over each other, scrambling up, running again.

The man watched them go.

His right hand turned the reel, very slightly, and the hook came up off the ground and swung out on the line. Yan felt it go. The line went out after the scouts, a long way, three hundred paces, four, faster than they could run, a thin bright flicker over the red, reaching for the last of them.

And stopped. And hung there in the air for a heartbeat, over the running shapes, and came back. Empty.

He’d let them go. On purpose. Yan understood that, kneeling on the red with Suzu cold and torn beside him. He could have taken every one of them. He’d let them go, so that they’d run all the way home to whoever had sent them, and tell it.

The Black Blade came for his sword.

And then, because he couldn’t help it, because it was what he was and what he’d been doing every day for a year, Yan looked at him.

Properly. Not at his face. Under it.

He’d learned how, in the winter. You could look at a person the way you looked at a candle, and see the flame. Or you could look past the flame, into the wick, and see the soul.

He’d spent a year learning what souls looked like.

Every soldier in Cinderhold. Every cook, every carter. Rhea, bright and loud and hot. Aldous, grey and patient and worn, like old stone. Every one of them was different, and every one of them was the same in one way, which had taken him months to notice and then he couldn’t stop noticing it: they had the same grain. The way wood from one forest has the same grain, whatever tree it’s cut from. They’d grown here. Their souls had grown here, out of this ground, under this sun, in these bodies, from the very beginning, and you could see it in them, the way you could see the forest in a plank.

And then there were the five of them. And himself.

He’d noticed it on the first night in the barracks in Solmere, before he even knew what he was looking at. Salty and Wendy and Maigo and Mashi had a different grain. So did he. So did all thirty-five of them, he was almost sure. Something about the way their souls sat in them, as if they’d grown somewhere else first, under a different sun, and been lifted out and set down here, in this ground, and hadn’t quite taken root. He didn’t know how else to say it. He’d stopped trying. He just knew it, now, the way you know your own handwriting. Earth. That was the grain. The grain of home.

The man standing thirty paces away on the Redmarch with a fishing rod had it.

The grain of home. Yan’s grain. Earth.

But it had taken. That was the thing that put him back down on both knees, with his hands flat on the red. It had taken root. All the way down. It had been lifted out of somewhere else and set down in that body a long time ago, a very long time, so long that the roots went all through him, into every corner, the way an old tree’s roots go through a wall. It was older than the body it was in. Much older. It had been somewhere before the body, and come here, and stayed, and grown, and grown, and grown, until it was the size of the sea.

Yan knelt on the red and looked at it, and couldn’t look away.

There was grief in it. Old grief, worn smooth, like a stone step worn hollow by a thousand feet. There was tiredness. There was a stillness so deep he couldn’t see the bottom. And under all of it, so faint he almost missed it, something warm. Something that, if he’d had to name it, he’d have called amused. As if, somewhere very far down, the sea was trying not to laugh.

He knew that. He didn’t know how he knew it. But he knew it, the way you know a song from the first two notes.

He didn’t say anything. He put it away. He’d think about it on his own, first. He always did.

But his hands were shaking on the red, and they didn’t stop for a long time.

Aldous went down first.

He’d been on his knees already, by the dead fire, grey and empty, with his book open in his hand. He saw the man come toward the ring. He stared at him. And then he put the book down on the red, very carefully, and put both hands flat on the ground, and bowed his head until it nearly touched the earth.

He didn’t say anything. He didn’t have to.

The soldiers who could still move did the same. Yan saw them do it, one after another. Sergeant Teague, on his side, with his arm opened from elbow to wrist and his face white, pushing himself up onto one knee with his good hand and bowing his head. Young Dace beside him. The others. Old men, some of them, who’d been on this ground eight years ago. They knew. They’d stood on the wall of Cinderhold the morning after, some of them, and watched a man walk out across the red alone with a sword on his shoulder.

And Rhea. Rhea, who could never resist a contest, who turned every drill into a fight and every fight into a wager, who’d never bowed to anyone in her life that Yan had seen. She was on her knees already. She looked at the man walking past her across the red, with the blood running down her face, and her mouth opened and closed. And then she laid her sword down on the red in front of her, and bowed her head.

The man stopped by Aldous.

“Don’t, Father,” he said.

It was the first time Yan heard his voice. It was nobody’s voice. A man’s voice, quiet, a little rough, with the accent of somewhere north. A voice Yan had never heard before in his life.

Aldous lifted his head. His face was wet.

“I watched you go,” he said. “From the wall. The morning after.”

“I know. I saw you.” The man looked out over the red, past the ring, toward the markers, a long way back. “You put them all up. All of them.”

“Somebody had to.”

“Yes,” said the man. “Thank you.”

He went on.

He stopped once more, by Teague. He went down on one knee beside him on the red, quite unhurried, and took the sergeant’s opened arm in his hands, and looked at it, and pulled the strap of Teague’s own sword-belt off him and wound it round above the elbow and pulled it tight. Teague made a sound through his teeth.

“Sergeant,” said the man. “You held.”

“Not long enough, sir.”

“Long enough.” He stood up. “Keep it tight. The Father will see to it when he’s got his breath back.”

He came on into the ring.

Suzu moved.

Yan had forgotten her. She’d been lying beside him on the red, full-size, torn along one side where the west one’s fingers had raked her, her white scales streaked with black. She’d been very still. Now she lifted her head, and turned it, and slid away from Yan across the red, slowly, toward the man.

Yan reached for her and didn’t catch her.

She went right up to the man’s boots. She lifted her head off the ground and tasted the air round him with her tongue, once, twice, the way she did with anything she wanted to know. And then she did something Yan had never once seen her do, in a year, with anyone but him. She laid her head down across the toe of the man’s red-dusted boot, and closed her bright green eyes.

The man looked down at her.

“Hello,” he said.

That was all. Just hello. Quietly. The way you’d say it to someone you hadn’t seen in a long time, and weren’t surprised to see.

On Earth, Yan had always translated for Suzu. He’d done her voice on stream, every day, for years. Hisses and all. He always knew what she’d say.

He didn’t know what she’d say now. For the first time in his life, he didn’t have the faintest idea.

Salty was still standing in the middle of the ring.

Yan didn’t know how. He’d seen the north one hit him. He’d heard the ribs go. He’d seen him get up anyway, and go for the three scouts with his bare hands, and lift the black sword off the red, which he couldn’t have done, and stand there with it, which he couldn’t have done either. He was still standing there now. Swaying. With the sword across his body, the grip in one hand and the blade resting on his other forearm, and his face grey and set, and blood on his chin. He hadn’t put it down.

The man came and stood in front of him.

Salty, short and square and enormous, with the black slab of iron across him. And the plain man in the brown coat, with the fishing rod in one hand. They stood there on the red, a pace apart, and looked at each other.

The man looked at the sword for a long moment. At Salty’s hands on it. At the scratched letters near the grip, crooked, one of them gone over twice.

Then he reached out and put his own hand on the grip. Above Salty’s.

Salty didn’t let go.

Yan didn’t think he could. He thought Salty’s hands had stopped being Salty’s a long time ago, out on the red, somewhere around the seventh stretch, and had been holding on by themselves ever since, because nobody had told them they could stop. The man seemed to understand that. He didn’t pull. He just stood there, with his hand on the grip above Salty’s, and waited.

“It’s all right,” he said quietly. “You can put it down now. You’ve carried it far enough.”

Salty’s hands opened.

The man lifted the sword.

One-handed. With the fishing rod still in his other hand. He lifted it off Salty’s forearm the way you’d lift a coat off a hook, and swung it up, and over, and settled it on his shoulder. The long black slab of iron, as long as a man, as broad as Wendy’s shoulders, that six soldiers and a winch had brought across the kingdom, that Salty had carried half a day and broken himself carrying. He put it on his shoulder like a bundle of sticks for the stove.

Like firewood, thought Yan. Aldous had said it in the courtyard. He carried it like firewood.

Salty stood there with his hands still out in front of him, empty, as if he didn’t know what to do with them.

Nobody said anything. Nobody moved. Fifty scouts gone, three hunters in pieces on the red, ten soldiers on their knees, a chaplain weeping, and in the middle of all of it, two men looking at each other across a pace of Redmarch, with a sword between them that had changed hands.

And Salty said it.

Of course it was Salty. It was always Salty. He never decided to say anything; he just said it, the way he lifted things, because it was there. He stood there with his empty hands and his broken ribs and the blood on his chin, and he looked the man in the face, and asked him the question he’d asked an empty grave on his own in the spring, and got told off for.

“Who was she?”

The man didn’t answer for a long time.

Yan thought he wasn’t going to. He watched the man’s face, which he still couldn’t hold on to. And under it, the sea, very still. He watched something move in it, far down. Not grief, exactly. Older than grief. Something that had been grief once, a long time ago, and had been carried so far and for so long that it had been worn into another shape.

Then the man said, “Her name was Diwa.”

He said it very simply. The way you’d tell someone the name of a street.

“She was a healer.”

Salty nodded slowly. As if that were enough. As if that were all he’d wanted.

“Diwa,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Okay,” said Salty.

And then his knees went, all at once, and Wendy was there, the way Wendy was always there, and caught him, and went down with him onto the red, and held him.

Yan knelt on the red with his hands flat on the ground and watched the man stand there with the sword on his shoulder.

He thought about Apex. About a dead man’s banner, round and round a map at three in the morning. About I was the reason we won. He thought about a squad. Five people, never more than a few paces apart. And about how, in every game they’d ever played together, somebody had to carry, and somebody got carried, and it had never once been him.

On Earth, Ice had carried them in Counter-Strike. Yan had carried Ice in Apex.

Here, on the Redmarch, on the eighth anniversary of the day it had turned red, a man none of them had ever met had walked across it alone with a fishing rod, and carried all five.

Yan didn’t say anything. He’d think about it on his own, first.

But he already knew.
