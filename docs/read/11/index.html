The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 1: Thirty-Four Winters

Chapter 11

The Hollow

2,471 words · 11 min read

Duskhollow existed because of a hole in the ground, and everybody in it knew it.

The town sat on a bend of the Wren a few days upriver from the ferry, a tangle of timber inns and outfitters and taverns piled on top of each other around a square, and every street in it ran downhill toward the same place: a wide stone arch at the edge of town, half-swallowed by ivy, opening onto a stair that went down into the dark. That was the Hollow. Old ruins, Garrick said, older than the kingdom, older than anyone’s records, and something in them bred monsters the way a pond breeds frogs.

“Shallow levels are for apprentices,” he said, on their first morning, standing at the top of the stair with his staff across his shoulders. “Rats the size of dogs. Crawlers. Things that bite and die. You’ll learn there. The deep levels are for people who are good, or people who are about to stop being anything.” He looked at the five of them. “You’re not going near the deep levels for a year. Possibly ever.”

“What’s in the deep levels?” asked John.

“Things that don’t die,” said Garrick, and went down the stairs.

For six weeks it worked.

That was the part Xav would think about afterwards. For six weeks it worked so well that it felt like cheating. Garrick took them down into the first ring of the Hollow every other day, through cold stone corridors lit only by the lantern on his staff, and showed them how a party survived down there. And every time, they already knew.

Azac in front, with a big round shield from the outfitters and a short sword he barely used. Malcolm just behind him, with a satchel full of bandages and salves and the pessimist’s instinct for where things would go wrong. RX ahead in the dark, quiet, with two knives, scouting the next turn and coming back to whisper what was there. John at the back, watching the light: where it fell, where it didn’t, where a shadow moved that shouldn’t. And Xav in the middle, calling it.

“Two crawlers, left wall,” RX would breathe, coming back out of the dark. “One big rat behind them.”

“Azac, hold the corner. RX, wait for the rat. Mal, stay on Azac. John, eyes on the back.”

And it would go exactly like that. The crawlers would come at Azac’s shield and break on it, and Xav would step in and finish them, and the rat would bolt and RX would be waiting.

None of them could use magic. None of them could use aura. They were Initiates with outfitter’s weapons and a few weeks’ training. But they’d been doing this together for years, in a dozen games, and it turned out that a lot of what made a party work had nothing to do with power at all. It was knowing where everyone was. It was calling early. It was trust.

Garrick watched them do it for six weeks with his eyebrows getting slowly higher.

“I’ve led parties down here for twenty years,” he said, one evening, in the tavern. “I’ve never seen Initiates move like that. Not once.” He looked at Xav over his mug. “Where did you learn it?”

“Games,” said Xav.

“You keep saying that.”

“It keeps being true.”

Every night in that same tavern they compared windows, the way they’d promised to. John was at ten, and swore the light down in the Hollow was “talking to him.” RX was at nine, and one of the grey slots on his skill page had started to show the faint outline of a word he couldn’t read yet. Malcolm was at seven and expected it to go down. Azac was at eight. Xav was at eight too, and didn’t mind. Slow was fine. Slow was how you learned a map.

Petra didn’t say anything. She came down with them every time, at the back, with her sword at her hip, and she never drew it. She just watched. Mostly, Xav had noticed, she watched him.

It was John who noticed the new tunnel.

They were in the first ring, on a corridor they’d walked a dozen times, and the light was wrong. John stopped dead in the middle of the passage.

“Hold on,” he said. “That wall. There’s light coming through it.”

There was. Xav could see it once John pointed: a thin line of grey along the bottom of the left-hand wall, where there had always been solid stone. RX went to look. The stone had cracked. Behind the crack there was a gap, and behind the gap there was a passage they’d never seen before, sloping down.

“Old collapse, probably,” said Garrick, frowning. “Happens. The Hollow shifts.” He held his lantern to the gap. “We’ll mark it and come back with—“

Something moved in the passage.

Xav would never be able to describe it properly. None of them would. It was low and long and the colour of the stone, and it came out of the gap so fast that the first any of them knew of it was the sound: a dry, scraping hiss, like a knife drawn slowly across slate.

“Back,” said Garrick.

Azac didn’t go back. He went forward. He did what he’d done in every game, every dungeon, every raid for three years since the person who used to stand there had stopped standing anywhere. He put himself between the thing and the rest of them, shield up, and planted his feet.

The thing hit the shield and the shield split.

It didn’t break. It split, clean down the middle, as if it had been made of paper, and Azac went backwards into the wall with a sound Xav would hear for weeks. Then the thing was on him, and there was blood, a lot of it, on the stone and on Azac’s arm and on his side, and Malcolm was shouting, and RX had his knives out and was going in, and Xav heard himself shouting no, no, RX, don’t—

Then there was a flash of something bright, and a sound like tearing silk, and the thing was in two pieces on the floor.

Petra was standing over it.

Her sword was out. Xav hadn’t seen her draw it. He hadn’t seen her move. She was simply there, between Azac and the gap in the wall, her blade held low, and along its edge there was a faint shimmer of light, and the light went past the edge, a hand’s width further than the steel, the way heat shimmers above a road. That was what had cut the thing. Not the sword. The light beyond it.

Aura. Real aura. The first any of them had ever seen.

“Reaching,” said Garrick, very quietly, as if naming it would help. “That’s Reaching tier. She can cut past her own edge.”

Petra didn’t look round. She was watching the gap in the wall.

“Get him out,” she said. “Now. There’ll be more.”

They got him out.

Malcolm packed the wound in Azac’s side with everything in his satchel and held it there with both hands while John and RX carried him, and Xav walked behind them with his useless sword and Garrick’s lantern, and Petra walked behind him, all the way up the stair, with her blade drawn the whole time.

There was a healer in Duskhollow, an old woman called Mother Annick who’d patched up adventurers for forty years and was not impressed by any of them. She took one look at Azac and sent everyone out of the room.

They waited in the corridor outside. Nobody said anything for a long time.

“I told you,” said Malcolm at last. His hands were still red to the wrist. He was looking at them as if they belonged to somebody else. “The first night. I told you it’d go wrong.”

“You did,” said Xav.

“I didn’t want to be right.”

“I know.”

RX was sitting against the wall with his knees up and his knives still in his hands. “I was going in,” he said. “Before she got there. I was going to go in at it.”

“I know,” said Xav again. “I saw.”

“It would’ve killed me.”

“Yes.”

RX put the knives down on the floor, very carefully, as if they might go off.

John hadn’t said anything at all. He was standing by the window at the end of the corridor, looking out at the evening light on the river.

“It’s not a game,” he said eventually, without turning round. “I knew that. I knew that. We all said it, the first night. But I didn’t…” He stopped. “There’s no respawn.”

Nobody answered him. There wasn’t anything to say.

Mother Annick came out after two hours, wiping her hands.

“He’ll live,” she said. “He’ll be in that bed a month and he’ll have a scar he can tell girls about. If it had gone in a finger’s width higher, he’d be dead.” She looked at the four of them. “Which of you put him in front of it?”

“He put himself there,” said Xav.

Mother Annick grunted. “They always do, the ones who stand in front,” she said. “Somebody should teach them not to.”

They went in one at a time. Azac was propped up on pillows, grey-faced and bandaged from his armpit to his hip, and when he saw Xav he managed something like a grin.

“Did I hold the corner?”

“You held the corner,” said Xav. His voice did something strange on the last word. “Idiot.”

“S’my job.”

“Not like that. Not at Initiate. Not against something we couldn’t see coming.”

“Somebody’s got to stand there,” said Azac, and closed his eyes. “He always did.”

Xav sat by the bed until Azac fell asleep. Then he sat there a while longer.

Petra found him in the tavern that night, alone at a table in the corner with a drink he hadn’t touched.

She sat down across from him without asking. It was the first time she’d done that.

“You called it right,” she said.

Xav looked up.

“Back,” said Petra. “Garrick said back, and you were already moving people back. You’d seen it before he spoke. The only one who didn’t listen was the big one, and he didn’t listen because he thought he was doing his job.” She turned her own cup slowly on the table. “You weren’t wrong. You were just at the wrong tier to be right.”

“That’s not much comfort.”

“It isn’t meant to be.” She was quiet for a moment. “Seven years ago I was a recruit on the Redmarch. My unit got cut off in the second night of the battle. Surrounded. Forty of us, in a ditch, with no officer, and nobody calling anything, and the demons coming over the top of the bank.” She said it flatly, as if reading it from a report. “Somebody came for us.”

Xav waited.

“One man,” said Petra. “Through the middle of them. He didn’t call anything either. He didn’t need to.” She looked at Xav, and there was that faraway look again, the one he’d seen at the waystation. “He waited. That’s what I remember. He stood at the top of the bank with forty frightened recruits behind him and he waited for them to come to him, and when they did, he only ever moved once.”

She drank, and set the cup down.

“You do that,” she said. “You don’t know you do it, but you do.”

Then she got up and left, and Xav sat there with his untouched drink and the strangest feeling crawling up the back of his neck, as if someone had just told him something very important in a language he didn’t speak.

Deston’s letter was waiting for him at the inn when he got back.

It had come up the river that afternoon, and the innkeeper had put it on the end of the bar, and Xav had walked past it twice without seeing it. He read it by the candle in his room, sitting on the edge of the bed.

It was long, and careful, and very Deston. It talked about Kingsferry, and Emrys’s singing, and Sabine and Dennis, and Gabe at the forges. And then, near the end:

I’ve been practising. The threads. I can see them most of the time now, if I look for them. They’re everywhere. Not just between us: between everyone. Strangers in the street have thin grey ones, just from walking past each other. A couple on the dock had one so thick I could almost touch it. I think the thickness means how close you are. How long. How much.

Gabe’s is still the thickest one I’ve got. Yours is close behind him.

I haven’t told anyone but you and Gabe and Emrys. You said write to you first, so I am.

Be careful down there. Declan would kill us both if anything happened to you.

Xav read the last line three times.

Then he took out paper and a pen, and sat for a long time with the candle burning down, trying to work out how to tell Deston about the Hollow. About the shield splitting like paper. About the blood on the stone, and Malcolm’s hands, and RX putting his knives on the floor. About Azac saying somebody’s got to stand there. He always did.

About a woman with a sword who’d said you do that, and gone away.

In the end he wrote it all down, plainly, the way it had happened. It took four pages. At the bottom he added one more line, and then sat looking at it for a long time before he decided to leave it.

We’re not playing anymore. I think we all needed to find that out. I’m sorry it was Azac who had to show us.

Down the corridor, he could hear Malcolm still awake, pacing. John’s door was open; he was sitting at his window, looking at the river. RX had gone out an hour ago and hadn’t come back, and Xav knew without asking that he’d gone back to the top of the Hollow stair to look down into the dark, the way you go back to look at a cliff you nearly fell off.

He sealed the letter, and opened his window, and looked at the numbers. Eight percent. Eight percent, after two months, against something that could split a shield like paper.

He closed the window, and blew out the candle, and lay in the dark thinking about a man seven years ago who’d stood at the top of a ditch with forty people behind him and waited.

He didn’t know why it felt like he’d heard that story before.
