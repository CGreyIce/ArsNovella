The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 3: The Anniversary

Chapter 61

The Last Stretch

5,825 words · 25 min read

Salty knew weights.

He’d known them before he’d known much else about himself. A bar, and what was on it. A plate, and how far it had to travel. How a thing wanted to sit in your hands, and how it wanted to fall, and the exact moment, halfway up, when it stopped being heavy and started being yours. He’d spent half his life in rooms full of iron on Earth, and come out of them shaped like this: short and square and enormous across the shoulders, all of it his own, nothing magic about it. Some people read. Some people played music. Salty picked things up and put them down again, carefully, and it had taught him most of what he knew.

It had taught him, for instance, that you couldn’t lie to a weight. You could lie to people. People let you. A weight didn’t care what you said about it. It just sat there and waited to find out if you were telling the truth.

He’d been thinking about that for six days.

The anniversary came up red.

Salty was awake for it. He hadn’t really slept. He’d gone into the chapel after supper and sat on the bench at the back, with the guard on the door pretending not to see him, and looked at the black sword on its trestles in front of the altar until the candles burned down. He hadn’t touched it. He’d touched it every day for six days, practising, out in the yard with the winch party and half the garrison watching, until he could get it onto his shoulder on his own and walk three hundred paces before he had to put it down. He hadn’t touched it last night. He’d just sat there.

Wendy had come in around midnight and sat down next to him and not said anything. She’d put her head on his shoulder. After a while she’d fallen asleep there, with her silent headphones round her neck, and he’d sat very still so as not to wake her, and kept looking.

He’d thought about the visit.

He didn’t, much. It wasn’t the kind of thing that did you any good. But last night, sitting there, it had come up anyway, the way things come up at two in the morning. The two of them had been going to fly out. Him and Wendy. Yan too, separately, later in the year. They’d talked about it for months, on calls, half joking and then not joking at all. Ice had sent Salty a list of things they had to eat. Eleven of them, with notes. He’d said they could do all eleven in one day if they were serious about it. Trust.

They hadn’t even booked the flights.

That was the thing Salty couldn’t get past, some nights. Not that they hadn’t gone. That they hadn’t even booked. They’d had the list. They’d had the dates, nearly. They’d just kept saying next month, the way you do, because there was always going to be a next month.

He’d sat in the chapel and looked at the sword that somebody had carried across the red alone, eight years ago, the morning after, and thought: that man didn’t wait for next month.

At dawn Wendy had woken up and looked at him and said, “You didn’t sleep.”

“No.”

“Idiot.”

“Yeah.”

She’d kissed him on the cheek, very briefly, the way she did when nobody was looking, and gone to get her bow.

The whole of Cinderhold came out again.

They lined the east wall this time, all of them, soldiers and cooks and the smith and the old woman from the laundry, in the grey light before the sun came up over the mountains of Kharvos. Nobody cheered. Nobody had cheered when the sword came in, either. They just stood there along the top of the wall, in a long silent row, and watched the cart go out through the east gate.

It was the same cart. The same great timber frame on its back, like the skeleton of a ship. The same six men of the winch party walking beside it, cleaner now, rested, with their corporal with the broken nose at the front. And on the bed of the cart, under its canvas, the sword.

Behind it came the escort. Aldous first, on foot, in his old black coat, with his book under his arm and his face very quiet. Then Rhea, in her mail, with her sword on her hip and her hair tied back hard. Then the ten soldiers Aldous had asked for, under Sergeant Teague, a grey Honed swordsman who’d been on the Redmarch eight years ago and didn’t talk about it. And then the five of them.

Maigo, barefoot, in his mustard hoodie, with his pixel sunglasses pushed up on his antlers and the new leaves on them stirring in the dawn wind. Yan, very tall and very still, with Suzu wound round his neck. Mashi, small and thin and calm, with a short sword at his hip and his hands in his pockets. Wendy with her bow. And Salty, at the back, beside the cart, with his own greatsword across his back and his hands empty.

They went out through the gate. Salty heard it close behind them.

He didn’t look back. He’d thought he might. He didn’t.

Aldous’s track ran east from the gate, between the markers.

It wasn’t really a road. It was the path Aldous had walked every day for two years after the battle, with a shovel and a book, and then every week for six more, and his feet had worn it down into the red until it was flat and hard enough for a cart. On either side, the markers went away in their long rows. Plain wooden posts. Names burned into the first few hundred, in Aldous’s careful hand, and then no names, only posts, and then posts that had fallen over and been set up again. A thousand of them. More. Aldous had stopped counting.

The cart went slowly. Nobody spoke.

The sun came up while they were still among the markers. It came up over the mountains in the east, red and swollen, and lit the whole Redmarch at once, and Salty, who’d been here a year and should have been used to it, stopped thinking for a moment and just looked.

It went on forever. That was the thing you forgot, inside the walls. Flat and rust-red and dead, with the grey grass standing up out of it in tufts that never grew and never died, and the old dry riverbeds cutting through it in long crooked gullies, and nothing moving on any of it. No birds. Nothing ever came out here, Aldous said. Not even the crows, after the first year. The ground was soaked too deep with what had happened on it. You could feel it through your boots, if you stood still long enough. Maigo said he could feel it all the time. Maigo said it hurt.

Maigo wasn’t talking this morning. That was how Salty knew he was frightened.

They came to the end of the markers an hour after sunrise.

The last row was the oldest. The posts there leaned at angles, grey and split with eight summers, and beyond them the ground broke up. The old riverbed came in from the north and spread out into a dozen shallow gullies, criss-crossing, with banks of red clay as high as a man’s waist and loose stones in the bottom. You could walk it. You couldn’t take a cart across it. Aldous had said so, in the spring. The winch can’t come out here.

The corporal stopped the oxen.

“This is as far as we go, Father,” he said.

“I know,” said Aldous. “Thank you.”

They didn’t need the winch this time. That was the strange part. The winch party stood back from the cart, all six of them, and folded their arms, and waited, because they’d seen it in the yard every day for six days and they knew what was going to happen.

Salty climbed up onto the cart.

He knelt beside the sword. Wendy passed him up the folded blanket, and he laid it across his left shoulder and the side of his neck, where the flat of the blade would sit, the way he’d worked out on the second day after the first time had taken the skin off. Then he got his right hand on the grip, up high, near the blade, and his left hand under the flat, a third of the way along, and breathed.

Slowly. That was the trick. He’d learned it on the first day, too. You couldn’t throw aura at it, the way he had at the table. If you threw it, it ran out halfway and you dropped the thing on your foot. You had to let it come up. From the bottom. Slowly, like water filling a jar.

He let it come up.

He felt it fill his legs, and his back, and his arms. The forge. That was what Yan called it. You’re like a forge. He felt it come up and hold and get hotter, and when it was as hot as it was going to get, he stood.

The sword came up with him.

It was heavy. It was always heavy. It didn’t stop being heavy halfway up, the way a bar did, and turn into yours. It never turned into his. It was somebody else’s weight, and it stayed somebody else’s, the whole time he was holding it. But he got it up, and over, and onto the blanket on his shoulder, with the grip out in front of him in both hands and the long black blade going away behind him like the beam of a house. And he stepped down off the cart, one foot, then the other, onto the red.

Behind him, very quietly, one of the winch party said, “Bloody hell.”

“Three hundred,” said Wendy, beside him. “Then down. I’m counting.”

“I know.”

“I’m counting out loud.”

“Yeah,” said Salty. “I know.”

They went down into the gullies.

Half a day.

That was what Aldous had said. An hour’s walk for a man on his own, across the gullies and out onto the open red beyond, to the low rise above where the river had once run. Half a day with the sword. More, maybe. It depended on Salty.

It depended on Salty. He’d thought about that a lot, too, in the last six days.

He walked. Wendy counted. Rhea went in front with four of the soldiers, picking the line through the gullies, where the banks were lowest and the ground was firmest. Aldous walked just ahead of Salty, so that Salty could watch his back and not have to look at the ground. Sergeant Teague and the other six soldiers spread out on either side. Yan and Mashi and Maigo walked in the middle with Salty, close, the way they’d walked on Earth, the way they’d always walked in every game they’d ever played together: five, in a stack, with nobody more than a few paces from anybody else.

At three hundred Wendy said, “Down.”

Salty put it down. On the sheepskin Aldous had brought, very carefully, the blade first and then the grip, the way he’d practised. He sat down on the red beside it with his forearms on his knees and breathed. Wendy counted to a hundred. Nobody else said anything.

At a hundred he got up and got it up again.

At the third stretch, he made three hundred and forty, because the ground was flat and the bank was low, and Wendy said, “Showing off,” and he said, “Yeah.” At the fifth, he made two hundred and sixty, and at the sixth, two hundred and ten. At the sixth, coming up out of the last of the gullies onto the open red, with the sun high and hot now and the sweat running into his eyes, he felt Maigo stop walking beside him.

“Down,” said Wendy. “Salty. Down.”

He put it down. He looked round.

Maigo was standing very still on the red, with his bare root-toed foot flat on the ground and his face turned south. He’d pulled his sunglasses down over his eyes. He did that when he didn’t want anyone to see what his face was doing.

“It’s flinching,” said Maigo.

Salty didn’t know what Maigo felt, exactly. None of them did. Maigo said it was like the ground had a heartbeat, and the heartbeat was hurt, and when something bad walked on it, it flinched. He’d felt it the night the scouts first came, a year ago, before anyone had seen them. He’d been right then. He was always right about the ground.

“Where?” said Rhea. She’d come back from the front. Her hand was on her sword.

“Everywhere,” said Maigo. “South. North. Behind. It’s…” He stopped. “It’s flinching everywhere.”

Yan had his eyes closed.

He stood there on the red, very tall, very still, with Suzu wound tight round his neck and his long pale hair moving in the wind, and his face had gone the way it went when he was feeling for the candles. Living things, he’d told them, in the spring. Little warm presences you could feel without looking at them. Salty was a forge. Mashi was two candles that kept almost touching. And the things that came across the red at night, the tall thin bent ones, were candles too. Cold ones.

“They’re in the gullies,” said Yan. His deep slow voice was very calm. “Both sides. They’ve been keeping pace with us since the markers.” He opened his eyes. “A lot of them, Father. More than came to the wall. More than I’ve ever felt.” He paused. “And three that are different. Bigger. Heavier. Like the cold one. Not as cold.”

Nobody said anything for a moment.

“How far to the grave?” said Salty.

Aldous looked east, across the open red, toward the line of dead trees on the horizon.

“We’re halfway,” he said.

“Right,” said Salty, and bent down, and got the sword up again.

“Lad,” said Sergeant Teague. “Lad, think.”

“I am thinking.” Salty settled the blade on the blanket. His shoulder was raw under it already, blanket or no blanket. He didn’t care. “If they want to stop us, they’ll stop us here or they’ll stop us there. Here’s no better. There’s at least the grave.”

“And if we go back?”

“Then they follow us back,” said Wendy, before Salty could. She’d nocked an arrow. She wasn’t drawing it. “And we’ll have walked half a day for nothing, and we’ll have to do it again next week with less sleep.” She looked at Aldous. “He’s right. I hate it. He’s right.”

Aldous looked at the five of them. At Salty, with the sword on his shoulder. At the south, where nothing moved, and the north, where nothing moved, and the long dead gullies behind them, where nothing moved either.

“Give it nothing,” said Aldous quietly. “Keep walking. Keep together. If they come, they come.” He turned east. “And if they come, Sergeant, the sword goes in the middle, and we stand round it. Whatever happens, they don’t take it. Not out here. Not on her ground.”

“Father,” said Teague.

They walked.

They came at the seventh stretch.

Salty had made a hundred and ninety paces. He remembered that afterwards: Wendy saying a hundred and ninety, and then not saying a hundred and ninety-one. Because the gully a hundred yards to their left had filled up, all at once, with tall thin bent shapes, coming up over the lip of the clay bank in a long ragged line, and the gully to their right had done the same, and when Salty turned his head, the open red behind them wasn’t open anymore.

He’d seen them from the wall, all year. Twenty at a time, fourteen, twenty-five. Shapes. Far off, in the dusk. He’d never seen them close, in daylight.

They were taller than men. Much taller, and very thin, the way a dead tree is thin, with long arms that hung down past their knees and ended in hands that were mostly fingers. They were grey, the same grey as the grass, and they moved in bursts: completely still, and then somewhere else, and then still again, too fast to follow. They didn’t have faces. Not really. They had the place where a face should go, and in the middle of it, low down, a mouth.

Forty of them. Fifty. He stopped counting.

And behind them, coming up out of the south gully slowly, not in bursts at all, the way a big dog trots, three more.

They weren’t thin. That was the first thing. They were the same height as the others, but they were heavy, deep through the chest and the shoulders, and their arms were too long even for that, and they had horns. Two each, curving back from where their ears should have been. They trotted out onto the red in a loose line and stopped, and lifted their heads, and Salty knew, without knowing how he knew it, that they were looking at the sword.

“Down,” said Wendy. Very quietly. “Salty. Put it down.”

He put it down.

“Ring!” Sergeant Teague was roaring it. “Ring on the sword! Shields out! Ring!”

The soldiers moved. They’d drilled it. Salty had watched them drill it, all week, in the yard, round the sword on its sheepskin: ten men in a ring with their shields out and their swords up and the black iron in the middle. They did it now, fast and tight, with Rhea at the south side, where the three big ones were, and Teague at the north.

Salty stood up and pulled his own greatsword off his back.

It felt like nothing, after the black one. That was the stupid thing. It was a big sword, longer than he was tall nearly, the kind that had made Rhea laugh at him the first week. You’ll take your own foot off. It felt like a stick. He stood at the east side of the ring with it in both hands and let the forge come up, all the way up, as fast as it would come, and felt the heat of it in his arms and his chest and behind his eyes.

Beside him, Mashi had drawn his short sword. He was standing very calmly, the way he always stood, with his weight on his back foot and his eyes moving. Not at the shapes. At the gaps between the shapes. He looked, for a moment, exactly the way he’d looked on Earth, playing CS, hunched forward at three in the morning with his headset half off, watching an angle.

Maigo had gone down on his knees. Both hands flat on the red.

Wendy had drawn.

Yan stood in the middle of the ring, beside the sword, with his eyes open now, and Suzu was unwinding from his neck. Salty saw her go, out of the corner of his eye: the little white scarf-sized snake sliding down Yan’s arm onto the red, and growing, the way she did, the way she’d done the first day in the summoning hall, until she was as long as a man was tall and as thick as Salty’s arm, and her bright green eyes were open, and she was hissing.

And Aldous, at the edge of the ring, beside Teague, opened his book. He didn’t look at it. He just held it open in one hand, and lifted the other, and began to chant.

Salty had never heard Aldous use magic.

He knew Aldous was a mage. Everyone knew. Bonfire, the fifth tier, the kind of mage who could put out an area spell, the kind armies wanted. But Aldous had been a chaplain, and then a gravedigger, and he’d spent eight years out here burying people and saying prayers over them, and in a year at Cinderhold Salty had never once seen him do anything with his magic except light the chapel candles with his fingertip, absent-mindedly, the way you’d flick a switch.

He was doing something now.

The chant was long. Old words, in the church tongue nobody spoke anymore, rising and falling, slow and heavy, like a hymn. And as he chanted, something came out from under his feet onto the red ground. A line of light. Gold, then orange, then fire: a thin line of fire running out across the red from the toes of Aldous’s boots, round in a great curve, round the whole ring of soldiers and the five of them and the sword in the middle, thirty paces across, until it met itself on the other side. And then it went up. Waist-high. A wall of fire round them on the bare red earth, hissing and roaring, burning on nothing at all.

The shapes stopped.

All of them, at once, at the edge of the fire. Fifty tall thin grey things, in a ring outside the ring, completely still.

“Steady,” said Teague. “Steady. Steady.”

Then the three big ones walked through it.

They didn’t jump it. They didn’t go round. They just walked through the fire, one on the south side, one on the west, one on the north, the way you’d walk through a curtain, and came out the other side smoking, with the fire still clinging to their grey hides in patches, and kept walking.

And the ring broke.

It didn’t break all at once. It broke the way things break in a fight, in pieces, too fast to follow, so that afterwards Salty could only remember bits of it, out of order, like a game you’d lost badly and were trying to replay in your head.

He remembered Rhea going for the south one. Her sword up, and the light running along the edge of it, the sheen she’d shown them in the spring, Gleaming, a thin bright line of silver on the steel. He remembered her cutting it across the arm, deep, and the black stuff coming out of it that wasn’t blood. He remembered the arm coming round anyway and hitting her, and Rhea going through the air, twenty feet, past the fire, and landing on the red and rolling and coming up on one knee with her face bloody and her sword still in her hand.

He remembered Suzu. A white blur, low on the ground, going for the west one’s legs and wrapping round them and pulling, so that it staggered, and two of Teague’s soldiers went into it with their swords while it was off balance.

He remembered Wendy’s arrows. Going up over the fire and coming down on the other side in a curve no arrow should have made, into the shapes outside the ring, one after another after another, because the shapes had started coming through the fire too now, where Aldous’s chant was faltering, and Wendy was putting arrows into every one of them that tried.

He remembered Maigo on his knees, with his hands flat on the red and his face screwed up behind his sunglasses, saying stay, stay, stay under his breath, and the shapes coming through the gaps in the fire stopping, for a heartbeat, with their long grey feet stuck to the ground as if the ground had grabbed them. Just a heartbeat. Long enough for a soldier to get a blade in.

He remembered Mashi in the gap between two soldiers on the east side, very calm, not moving, not moving, and then moving, once, when a shape came through exactly where he’d known it would, and the short sword going in under its long arm and out again, and Mashi stepping back into the gap as if he’d never left it.

He remembered Yan’s voice. Deep, slow, perfectly calm, the way it had been on a thousand Apex calls at three in the morning, calling it. Left. Two. Behind the gap. Wendy, high. Niko, your side, now. Salty. Salty, north, the big one, it’s coming for the middle.

It was coming for the middle.

The north one. It had walked through Teague’s side of the ring as if Teague’s side of the ring wasn’t there. Salty saw Teague go down, his shield split, his sword arm opened from elbow to wrist. He saw a young soldier called Dace go down beside him. And then it was in the ring, inside the fire, huge and grey and smoking, with its horns curving back and its long arms hanging, walking toward the sword on its sheepskin. Not toward Yan, standing beside it. Not toward any of them. Toward the sword.

Salty got there first.

He didn’t remember crossing the ring. He was just there, between the thing and the sword, with his greatsword up and the forge in him roaring, and he swung.

It was the best swing he’d ever made. He knew that even while he was making it. Everything in him went into it: the forge, all of it, all at once, the way it had gone into the table. Big and stupid and all at once. The greatsword came round in a long flat arc and hit the thing just above its hip and went in, deep, a hand’s width, two, into the grey hide, through whatever was under it, and stuck.

And stuck.

He pulled. It didn’t come out. The thing looked down at the sword stuck in its side, and then at him, with the place where its face should have been. And then one long arm came round, almost lazily, and the back of its hand hit Salty in the ribs.

He felt something go. Two somethings. He felt himself leave the ground. He felt the red come up and hit him, hard, all along one side, and roll him over, and he lay there on his back with the sky going round and no air in his chest at all.

He got up.

He didn’t know how. He couldn’t breathe, and the ribs on his left side felt like somebody had driven a cart over them, and there was blood in his mouth. But he got up, because the alternative was lying down, and he’d never in his life been good at lying down when there was something still to lift.

The ring was gone.

Aldous’s fire had gone down to a few low guttering patches on the red. Aldous himself was on his knees by one of them, grey in the face, with his book still open in his hand and his lips still moving, but no sound coming out. Half of Teague’s soldiers were down. Not dead. Salty could see them moving. Holding arms, holding legs, crawling toward each other. Rhea was on her feet beyond the fire, fighting the south one on her own, the sheen on her sword flickering now, going out and coming back. Suzu had the west one round the legs again, and it was tearing at her, and Yan was standing over her with no weapon at all, just standing there, with his hand out, as if he could hold the thing back by looking at it.

And the shapes were through.

Not at the people. That was the thing Salty understood, standing there swaying with the blood in his mouth. They didn’t want the people. They went round the people. They went past the soldiers on the ground and past Mashi in his gap and past Maigo on his knees, in their quick still-then-somewhere-else bursts, all of them, from every side, toward the middle. Toward the sword.

Three of them had it already.

They had it by the blade. Their long grey fingers were wrapped round the black iron, and the iron was burning them, Salty could see it, smoke coming up off their hands where they touched it, the way it had come up off the big ones in the fire. They didn’t let go. They were dragging it. Across the sheepskin and off the end of it onto the red, slowly, the three of them together, the way the winch had dragged it out of the grave.

“They don’t want us,” said Yan, somewhere. Very far away. “They want the sword.”

Salty didn’t decide. He never did.

He just went. Across the ring, three steps, four, with his ribs screaming and no greatsword in his hands, because his greatsword was still stuck in the side of the north one, which was walking toward the sword too now, slowly, with the hilt sticking out of its hip. He went at the three shapes with his bare hands and the forge coming up in him again from somewhere, from the very bottom, from somewhere he hadn’t known there was anything left. He hit the first one with his shoulder and it broke. Something in it broke, with a sound like dry wood, and it went down. He hit the second with his fist and it let go. The third one let go on its own.

And he bent down and got his hands on the grip of the black sword, and lifted it.

He didn’t know how he did that either.

He couldn’t have done it. Not with his ribs. Not with the forge running on nothing. He got his right hand on the grip, high, and his left on the flat, and he lifted, and it came up. Off the red. Across his body, the way he’d carried it across the courtyard, the grip in one hand and the blade resting on the other forearm, like a long heavy plank. And he stood there in the middle of the broken ring with it, swaying, with fifty grey shapes all round him, completely still now, and the big north one walking toward him with his own sword still stuck in its side.

He couldn’t swing it.

He knew that. He’d known it all week. He could carry it. He couldn’t use it. Nobody could, but one man. He could stand here and hold it, and that was all he could do. He stood there and held it.

“Come on, then,” said Salty.

He didn’t see the third big one until Wendy screamed.

It wasn’t a scream, really. Wendy didn’t scream. It was his name, very short and very sharp, the way she’d say it on a call when he was about to walk into something stupid. Salty. And he turned his head, with the sword in his arms, and saw.

The east one. The third one. The one he’d lost track of. It hadn’t come for the middle at all. It had gone round. Round the outside of the dying fire, behind Maigo, and Maigo hadn’t felt it because Maigo was feeling everything at once. And Wendy had seen it, because Wendy always saw things first, and she’d turned, and put herself between it and Maigo, the way you do. And she’d drawn her last arrow.

Her last arrow. Salty could see the empty quiver on her back.

It was ten paces from her. It was coming the way the one at Valcrest had come, Ina had said, in the channel: not running. Gathering itself. Low. Its long arms out wide on either side of it, and its horns down, and every line of it bent toward her. She had one arrow, and it was ten paces away, and then it was eight, and it jumped.

Salty was fifteen paces from her.

Fifteen paces, with the sword in his arms.

If you go, I go.

He let go.

He was already letting go. He’d decided before he knew there was anything to decide. His hands were opening on the grip, his weight was already going forward, out from under the black iron, and the sword was beginning to fall, and he was going to drop it on the red in front of fifty of them and run, and he was going to be too slow, he was too far, he knew he was too far, and he was going anyway—

And the thing stopped.

In the air. Halfway through its jump. Two paces from Wendy, with its long arms out, and its horns down, and its mouth open. It just stopped, as if it had hit the end of a rope.

And then it came apart.

It didn’t make a sound. That was what Salty remembered afterwards, more than anything. Not the way it looked. The silence of it. The thing hung in the air for one heartbeat, perfectly still, and then a line appeared across it, from one shoulder down to the opposite hip. Thin. Perfectly straight. As if somebody had drawn it with a ruler. And the two halves of it slid apart along the line, very slowly, the way two halves of a melon slide apart after the knife has gone through, and fell on the red ground at Wendy’s feet, and didn’t move again.

Wendy was standing there with her last arrow still drawn and her mouth open.

Salty’s hands had closed on the grip again. He hadn’t told them to. The sword had started to fall and his hands had just caught it, the way they caught everything, out of nothing but habit, and he was standing there with it across his body again, and he couldn’t feel his arms.

Something glinted in the air.

Over the place where the thing had been. A thin bright line, finer than a hair, hanging in the sunlight, perfectly straight. Salty followed it with his eyes. It ran away from him across the red, slightly upward, catching the light here and there along its length, a long way. Past the dead fire. Past the fifty shapes, which had all turned their faceless heads, at once, the way a flock of birds turns. Out across the open red to the north-west, a hundred paces, more.

To the end of a fishing rod.

There was a man there.

He was walking toward them across the Redmarch. Not running. Walking. A plain man in a plain coat, with a pack on his back, and a fishing rod in his hands, held out in front of him at an easy angle, the way you’d hold it on a jetty on a still evening, waiting for a bite. The line ran out from the tip of it, across a hundred paces of red, to the place where the thing had been.

Salty stood very still, with the black sword across his body, and looked at him.

He didn’t know what he felt. He couldn’t have told you. Something in the bottom of his chest, under the broken ribs, that had nothing to do with the forge.

Then the man reeled in.
