The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 1: Thirty-Four Winters

Chapter 26

The Council

2,720 words · 12 min read

The Council of Aurelan met in a long cold room at the top of the old keep, under a ceiling painted with the same gold suns as the summoning hall, and Hanarisu thought it was the least friendly room she had ever been in.

Twelve chairs stood on a dais along one wall, high-backed and carved, each with a lord in it. A thirteenth chair, larger and plainer than the others, stood empty at the end. Below the dais, on a bare stone floor, there was nothing at all. That, she understood, was where you stood when the Council wanted to look at you.

They stood there now, the five of them, in the clothes Oswin had found for them: plain and good and not too fine. Hanarisu in the middle. Milli on her left, his tail very still. Jared on her right, hands loose at his sides, standing exactly where Kestra would have stood. Aleaka beside him, pale, with her fox ears flat. Cursiffe at the end, calm and watchful, one hand resting over the place inside his coat where the little brown book lived.

Behind them, by the door, Kestra stood with her arms folded. Oswin hovered beside her, wringing his inky hands. And at the far side of the room, alone, in grey, the Advisor stood with his hands in his sleeves and his face giving away nothing at all.

Lord Vane rose from the fourth chair.

“My lords,” he said, smiling. “Thank you for indulging me.”

It was a good speech. Hanarisu had to give him that. She’d been listening to people talk for a living for years, and Vane was very, very good.

He spoke about the summoned with enormous warmth. He spoke about how precious they were, how gifted, how vital to the kingdom’s future. He spoke about the scouts on the Redmarch, counting the towers of Cinderhold. He spoke about the villages in the west, emptying into the hills. He spoke, with grave and gentle concern, about an attempt in Kingsferry by a stranger to lure away one of the summoned, a healer, a young woman, alone, by the river. That news had reached the capital a fortnight ago, and Hanarisu had been surprised and not at all pleased to discover that Vane already knew it.

“These are dangerous times, my lords,” he said. “And what have we done with our most precious resource? We have scattered it to the corners of the kingdom, in groups of five, with one guide and one guard apiece. A scholar. A musician. A surveyor. A retired adventurer.” He let that sit. “With respect to the Advisor, whose wisdom I do not question, I ask: is that protection? Or is that hope?”

There was a murmur along the dais.

“I propose,” said Vane, “that the summoned be placed under the care of the great houses of this kingdom. Each with a house behind them. Each with walls, and soldiers, and resources. Fostered, as we foster the children of our allies, so that they may grow strong in safety. And so that when the war comes, as it will, they will be ready.” He spread his hands. “Is that not what we all want?”

He sat down.

The murmur along the dais grew. Hanarisu watched it: who nodded, who frowned, who looked at the Advisor, who looked at the empty chair at the end. Seven of them, she thought, were already with Vane. Perhaps eight. Three were against. One was asleep.

The Lord Chancellor, at the far end of the dais, turned his head toward them.

“The Council will hear the summoned,” he said. “One of you may speak.”

Hanarisu felt the others turn to look at her.

She’d known they would. They’d talked about it the night before, round the fire in the prince’s sitting room, and Milli had said you’re the one who talks, and Jared had said you’re the one who watches, and Aleaka had said, very quietly, you’re the one who drew the line. Cursiffe hadn’t said anything. He’d just looked at her and nodded.

She stepped forward.

The warmth came up at once.

She’d known it would. It came up the moment she lifted her eyes to the twelve faces on the dais. That bright, soft, eager heat under her ribs, the same as in the archive, the same as at Vane’s banquet. Let me, it said. Let me out. Look at them. Seven, maybe eight, already with him. You could turn every one of them. You could turn Vane himself. One smile, and this is over.

She felt it press against the inside of her chest.

She thought of the Keeper’s puzzled face. She thought of Kestra’s little book, held out to her in a dark corridor. She drew her own line tonight.

She let it go back down.

It was harder this time. Much harder. It didn’t want to go. It went down slowly, sullenly, like an animal being pushed back into a cage, and when it was gone she felt cold and small and very, very ordinary, standing alone on a bare stone floor in front of twelve lords who could decide where she lived.

Good. That was what she wanted to feel. That was honest.

“My lords,” she said.

Her voice came out steady. It was her own voice, the one she’d used for years on a screen in front of strangers, and it had never needed magic to work.

“Lord Vane is right about almost everything,” she said. “These are dangerous times. There are scouts on the Redmarch. There’s something in the west. Someone tried to take my friend Rachael by the river in Kingsferry. I know. She wrote to me.” She glanced, very briefly, at Vane. “I’m surprised you knew so soon, my lord.”

Vane’s smile didn’t move. But something behind it did.

“He’s right that we’re precious,” she went on. “We’re the only thirty-five people in the world who can see exactly how far we’ve come and how far we have to go. He’s right that we’re vulnerable. Most of us are at fifteen percent of the first tier. I’m at fifteen. My husband’s at eleven. Our friend Thea, in Wrenhaven, is at eighty-nine, and she’s the furthest of any of us, and she still can’t light a candle.” She took a breath. “So he’s right. We’re weak, and we’re valuable, and that’s a dangerous thing to be.”

“Then you agree,” said Vane smoothly.

“No,” said Hanarisu.

The room went quiet.

“I don’t know where I’ll be next year,” she said. “Or if I’ll ever get home. Or if the way home is even real. I don’t know anything about this world, my lords, except what the people around me have taught me.” She turned, slightly, so she could see them: Oswin by the door, Kestra with her arms folded. “And the people who’ve taught me are a scholar who stays up all night reading so he can answer our questions. And a soldier who’s said maybe forty words to me in five months, and every one of them was true.”

She turned back to the dais.

“Lord Vane says a house would protect us. Walls, and soldiers, and resources. He’s right that a house has more walls than a guide and a guard. But a house would also own us.” She said it plainly, the way Kestra said things. “We’d belong to it. We’d fight its fights and want what it wants. And when the war comes, we wouldn’t be thirty-five people from another world trying to save this one. We’d be thirty-five swords in seven different houses, pointed wherever our fosters pointed us.” She paused. “I don’t think that’s what the Advisor brought us here for. I don’t think that’s what any of you want, either. Not really. Not when you think about it.”

Nobody on the dais spoke.

“We’d rather stay with the people who’ve been honest with us,” said Hanarisu. “That’s all. We’re grateful for the offer. And we’d rather stay.”

She stepped back into the line, between Milli and Jared. Her hands were shaking. She folded them in front of her so nobody would see.

Milli’s tail curled, very slightly, around her ankle.

The silence went on for a long time.

Then a door at the back of the dais opened, the one behind the empty thirteenth chair, and the air in the room got heavier.

Hanarisu felt it in her chest first, and then in her shoulders, and then behind her eyes. The same weight she’d felt in the summoning hall, five months ago, the day the king walked in: as if the whole room had sunk a few fathoms underwater. Along the dais, twelve lords stopped breathing at once.

King Severin came through the door and sat down in the thirteenth chair.

He’d been there all along, she realised. Behind the door. Listening. She didn’t know how long. The whole time, perhaps.

He didn’t look at the Council. He looked at the five of them, standing on the bare floor, one at a time, the way he had in the hall: weighing. When his eyes reached her, she felt it like a hand on the top of her head.

“Vane,” said the king.

Lord Vane rose at once. “Majesty.”

“Sit down.”

Vane sat down.

The king was quiet for a moment. Then he said, in his low rough voice that carried to every corner of the room without any effort at all:

“The summoned answer to their guides. And to me.” He paused. “Not to houses. Not to anyone who’d like to own them. To me.” He turned his head, very slowly, and looked along the dais at the twelve lords, one by one. “Is that understood?”

It was understood. Hanarisu watched it be understood, chair by chair. Vane’s face was perfectly composed. His hands, on the arms of his chair, were white at the knuckles.

“The Council is dismissed,” said the king.

The lords filed out quickly and quietly, the way people leave a room where something has gone wrong for them. Vane went last. He passed within two paces of Hanarisu on his way to the door, and smiled at her, pleasantly, the way he always smiled.

“Well spoken,” he said. “Truly.”

“Thank you, my lord.”

“I’ll remember it.”

He went out. She didn’t watch him go. She knew exactly what I’ll remember it meant. She’d heard it in a hundred chats, from a hundred people who’d lost an argument in public.

When she turned back, the king was standing in front of her.

She hadn’t heard him move. He was very tall, and very broad, and very old, and very much not old at all, and the weight of him in the air was so heavy that for a moment she couldn’t breathe. Behind her, Milli had gone rigid. Jared hadn’t moved, but he’d shifted his weight forward, very slightly. Aleaka’s ears were flat to her skull.

Only the strong may speak to the king. She was at fifteen percent. She opened her mouth and closed it again.

“You may listen,” said Severin. “You don’t need to be strong for that.” He looked down at her for a long moment. “Kestra writes to me about you.”

“I know, Majesty.”

“She wrote that you could have turned the Keeper. And Vane, at his banquet. And that you didn’t.” His eyes were very grey and very steady. “And today, in front of twelve men who were deciding where you’d sleep for the next ten years, you could have turned every one of them. I watched you not do it.”

Hanarisu didn’t say anything. She didn’t trust her voice.

“Most people,” said the king, “when they find a power like that, spend the rest of their lives using it and calling it something else. Persuasion. Charm. Diplomacy.” He was quiet for a moment. “I’ve known a great many of them. Some of them sat in those chairs.”

He turned to go. Then he stopped, and looked back at her over his shoulder.

“You drew a line,” he said. “Keep drawing it.”

And he went out through the door behind the thirteenth chair, and the weight went with him, and five people on a bare stone floor remembered how to breathe.

“I think,” said Oswin faintly, on the stairs going down, “that I need to sit down for about a week.”

Nobody argued.

They walked back through the castle in silence, the seven of them, Kestra at the back. At the door of the prince’s wing, Kestra stopped, and took out her little book, and wrote something in it. Hanarisu didn’t ask what. She thought she could guess.

Then Kestra put the book away, looked at Hanarisu for a long moment, and said, “Good.”

It was the second time Hanarisu had ever heard her say it. The first had been to Milli, in the practice yard, when he finally landed a hit.

There were letters waiting in the sitting room. The first replies to Aleaka.

She’d been watching the courier’s satchel every day for three weeks, and now here they were: one from Yan, at Cinderhold, and one from Kei, at Thornwall, and a thick one from Higu, in Wrenhaven, in very small neat handwriting. Aleaka gathered them up and held them against her chest as if they might fly away.

“Read them,” said Jared. “Go on. We just survived the Council. We deserve something nice.”

So Aleaka sat down by the fire and read them aloud, and Oswin, who had come in behind them and then not left, took out a pencil and the folded map from inside his coat, and began to add lines.

Yan’s letter was careful and slow, like Yan. He’d met Salty and Wendy and Maigo through games, he said; Mashi through a different game, years before. He’d met half the people in Group 7 through his own friends. He’d met Kei through someone. Then there was a gap, and a crossing-out, and then, through a friend we lost. Ice. Most of us did, I think. I don’t know if that’s what you’re asking.

Kei’s letter said much the same, in a different shape, with three rude comments from Hisui in the margins.

Higu’s was the longest. Five girls, all met through Xola, lines like spokes into a hub, and one short line across the rim that didn’t go through the middle: Higu to Gigichi, six years. And then, scattered through it, as if she hadn’t quite meant to put it in: a friend who’d modded her chat. Who’d raided Thea. Who’d played word games with PenguChi. Who’d sent Gigichi a message nobody else had thought to send.

Higu didn’t say he’s the one we all knew. She didn’t draw that line. But she’d written his name four times in three pages, and Aleaka read it aloud four times, and by the fourth time her voice had started to shake.

She stopped reading.

Oswin had stopped drawing. He was looking down at his map, at the knots and the bare places and the lines that ran in from every side toward the hole in the middle where nobody was standing. His pencil was hovering over it.

“Aleaka,” he said gently. “This friend. Ice. Was he—“

“He died,” said Aleaka. “Three years ago.” She swallowed. “In his sleep.”

Oswin looked at the hole in the middle of the map for a long time.

Then, very slowly, very carefully, he wrote a small mark of his own in the empty centre. Not a name. He couldn’t write the name; he couldn’t even read it. Just a little circle. An empty one.

He didn’t draw any lines to it. Not yet.

Hanarisu, by the fire, said nothing at all. She watched Jared look at Cursiffe. She watched Cursiffe’s hand go to the inside of his coat. She watched Aleaka sit with three letters in her lap and the name Ice written all through them, staring at the little empty circle in the middle of Oswin’s map.

And she kept her face pleasant, because she always did, and because for the first time since they’d come to this world, she was genuinely afraid that if she let it slip, everything she was holding down would come up at once.
