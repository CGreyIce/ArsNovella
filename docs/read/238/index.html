The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 8: The Third Seal

Chapter 238

Carry

4,745 words · 21 min read

After the lanterns had gone down the markers, Yan sat with his back against the cairn and his hand on the stone and watched the dark.

It wasn’t dark, to him. It never was, any more. He’d stopped being able to see the dark properly in the first winter, the way you stop being able to hear a clock once you’ve noticed it ticking; there was always something in it. Lights. Candles. The small warm flame in the middle of every living thing, which he’d learned to see on the wall of this fort with Aldous beside him in the snow, and had never since been able to stop seeing.

So he sat on the knoll in the dark and watched the lights.

Aldous’s, a pace away, at the foot of the cairn. Kneeling on the white cloth with his book shut in his lap and his lantern by his knee, turned low. An old man’s light: grey and patient and worn, like a stone step, and white at the heart. Yan had seen the white for the first time last summer, on this knoll, on the night of the ninth, the night the colours came. Pure, he’d thought, and then been embarrassed by the word, and then not been, because it was true.

Deston’s and Xav’s, a little way down the slope, by the ash. Side by side. Deston’s singing, if a light could sing; it went up and down very slightly all the time, like somebody humming under their breath. Xav’s very still, a lamp in a window with nobody moving behind it.

The ash’s. Big and slow and gold at the edges, filling the top of the knoll. And beside it, small, a spark: the seedling’s. And between them, since three mornings ago, a thread. Very fine. Gold-edged, spark-coloured. Going across a hand of earth from one to the other, under the grass. He could see it if he looked. He looked at it a great deal.

Suzu’s. Round his neck. Green. The same green she’d always been, since the first morning on the cold stone floor of the hall, before he’d known what green meant. Leaning toward him, all of it, the whole of her light, the way a plant leans on a windowsill.

And under all of them, faint, everywhere, so faint he’d learned to look past it, the grey.

Four hundred and eighty-one markers between the knoll and the fort. Fourteen hundred and twenty-three under them, Aldous said, with names and without. And under every one, if he let himself look, a sort of grey in the dark. Not a light. The place where a light had been. The way a wall stays pale where a picture’s hung for a long time and been taken down.

Not mine, Aldous had said, in the first month, on the wall.

He didn’t look at the grey. He kept his hand on the stone.

He’d had his hand on it since the rite.

He didn’t know why. He’d sat down against the cairn when the singing stopped and put his palm flat on the black iron beside him, on the plain east face, the back of the stone, the side without letters; and left it there. It was cold. It was always cold, Salty said, even at noon in summer. Cold the way the bottom of a well is cold, or the inside of a mountain.

And under the cold, a long way under, at the bottom of the spike, where the ash’s roots went down and closed round it like fingers, the other thing.

It had lain down. He’d felt it go, when Astra and the stone rang together and Alea stood there with her ears forward and didn’t move. Further down than last year. Further than in the spring, when Niko’s sword rang and it put its head back down after lifting it. Down, like a dog lying down when the house has gone quiet, all the way, with a sort of sigh.

He kept his hand on the stone to feel it stay down. That was all. He was tired.

He was always tired.

He’d tried, all year, to find out why. Not by asking. Suzu wouldn’t say, and asking made her sad, and he’d stopped. By looking. At his own light, which you couldn’t do, quite, any more than you could look at your own eyes; but you could see the edges of it, a little, if you held your hand up in front of your face at night and looked at the place where the light came out round your fingers. He’d done that a hundred times on the barracks roof. His own light, which he’d never been able to name. Not a colour. The others had colours. His had something else, that he’d stopped trying to name; it was like looking at the colour of water.

It was thinner than it had been. That was all he could tell. Since the ninth. As if somebody had taken a little off the top of it, very carefully, with a spoon, and it hadn’t grown back.

He’d felt it go that night. He’d thought it was the colours: the first time he saw them, Alea’s green and Aldous’s white and Suzu’s, all at once on this knoll under a full moon, as if he’d spent something to buy them. Maybe he had. Suzu had said enough, once, in the winter, to three elves, so loudly it went through a snake cord across the kingdom. And then nothing.

He sat with his hand on the stone and was tired, and didn’t mind, much, tonight.

He’d tried to hold one.

All year. That was the other thing he’d been doing, on the barracks roof, at the knoll, on the wall, while he was being tired. Since the ninth, since he’d started seeing colours, he’d wanted to know what you did with them. You didn’t just see a thing. Not in this world. Every skill anybody had ever had here, he’d watched it start as seeing and turn into something else: Thea’s Parse into Patch, Hyvred’s Scope into Range, Maigo’s stay into holding a hundred scouts by the ankles all night. You saw a thing and then you found out what your hands could do to it.

So he’d tried. With his hands. On the roof, in the winter, he’d held his palm out toward Salty’s light across the yard, the furnace that leaned toward Wendy, and tried to close his fingers round the colour of it. The way you’d try to close your hand round smoke. And nothing. It was too far. Colours weren’t things you could touch from across a yard.

So he’d asked Wendy.

He’d asked her because she wouldn’t make a fuss. She’d looked at him on the barracks step, in the snow, with her mismatched eyes, one brown and one violet, and her headphones round her neck, and said will it hurt, and he’d said I don’t know, and she’d said all right then, and held her hand out.

He’d taken it. Her colour was a cool deep thing, like the sky just after the sun’s gone, with a bright point in it, that was Salty, always, whichever way she was facing. And he’d tried to hold it. Not take. He was very careful about that. He’d promised Aldous on a wall three years ago, and the promise was in his hands now the way a habit’s in your hands, and he didn’t take. He tried to hold. To close his fingers round the colour where it came out of her hand into his, and keep it there.

It had gone through his fingers like water.

All of it. Every time. He’d tried four times, on the step, in the snow, with Wendy sitting there patiently with her hand in his, and every time he’d reached for it, the colour had slid out from between his fingers and back into her, the way water slides out of a cupped hand however tightly you hold it. And the fourth time, something had gone out of him instead. Not much. A breath. And he’d been sick in the snow behind the barracks step, and Wendy had held his hair out of the way without saying anything, and that night Suzu had wrapped herself round his neck so tightly he could hardly breathe and not spoken to him until morning.

He hadn’t tried again.

He’d thought about it. A great deal. He’d come to the conclusion, in the spring, sitting on the knoll with his back against this cairn, that you couldn’t hold a colour. That it wasn’t a thing you could do. That reaching for somebody’s colour was too close to the other thing, the thing Suzu did, the thing he’d promised not to do: that a colour was the outside of a soul, and you couldn’t put your hand on the outside of a soul without it being, somehow, a kind of taking; and his hands knew that even if he didn’t, and let go.

Maybe it’s just not mine, he’d thought. Like Hyvred’s lane wasn’t his, or Maigo’s.

He’d stopped trying.

He saw her coming up the markers a long time before she got there.

He didn’t see her. He saw her light. Green, small, a long way down the burial track in the dark between the rows; coming up. Alone. No lantern. Not fast. Setting her own pace.

The same green as Suzu’s. Loving. It had been the first colour he ever saw, last summer, on this knoll, and he’d known what it was before he knew how he knew.

He didn’t move. Neither did Aldous, at the foot of the cairn, though Yan saw his head come up a little. Down the slope, Deston’s light stopped humming for a moment and then started again, very softly, and Xav’s didn’t move at all.

She came up onto the knoll.

He saw her with his eyes, then: a fox girl in the starlight, with her ears up and her tail low and her hands empty, coming up over the top of the slope onto the green. She stopped when she saw them. Aldous on his cloth. Yan against the cairn. Then she saw Astra, on the grass, where she’d laid it, a hand from the stone; and her ears went back, and came up again.

She came across the green to it.

She didn’t pick it up. She stood over it, looking down at it in the starlight, the black blade on the grass beside the black stone it had been cut from, two halves of one thing, a hand apart. And Yan saw her put her fingertips together. Right hand on left. And tap them. Three times. Light.

He didn’t know what it meant. He’d seen Hesper do it. He’d seen Rhea do it, on the wall, this afternoon.

She knelt down.

On the grass. Between Astra and the stone. And put her hand out, not to the sword; to the stone. To the plain east face of it, the side without letters, the side Yan was sitting against.

And put it over his.

He didn’t know why she did it.

He asked her, afterwards, much later, on the walk down, and she said she didn’t know either. She said she’d come up the slope and seen him sitting there against her mother’s stone with his hand on it, grey in the face, the colour he’d been all year, and she’d thought: he’s tired. And she’d thought: Mum would have. And she hadn’t thought anything after that. She’d just done it.

She put her hand over his on the stone. Her palm on the back of his hand. Warm. Small. A swordswoman’s hand, hard at the base of the fingers, with a healer’s face, a woman at the sea had said.

And her colour came into his hand.

Not the way Wendy’s had. Not as if he were reaching for it. He wasn’t reaching for anything; he was sitting against a stone being tired, with his hand flat on cold iron. It came the other way. Out of her palm, into the back of his hand, through his hand, into his palm, the way warmth goes through a cup into your fingers when somebody fills it. Given.

Green.

He’d seen it a hundred times since last summer. He’d never felt it. It came into his palm between his hand and the stone, and sat there, and it was warm, and it had a weight. Not much. The weight of a bird. And it had a shape. That was the thing he hadn’t known. A colour had a shape, if you held it; the way a word has a shape in your mouth that you never notice until you say it in a language you don’t speak.

He held it.

He didn’t decide to. His hand did it, the way his hands had let go of Wendy’s on the step. They closed. Not his fingers; they were flat on the stone, under hers. Something in his palm, under his fingers, that he’d never known was there, closed round the green the way your hand closes round a small warm thing somebody puts into it in the dark, carefully, so as not to crush it.

And it stayed.

It didn’t slide through. It didn’t go back into her. It stayed in his palm, green, warm, the weight of a bird; and he could feel what it was made of. Not see. Feel. The way you can feel what a coin is by the edge of it in your pocket.

A lake. Very early in the morning, grey, with mist on it.

A man on the end of a jetty, with his back to her.

A kitchen, a long time ago, and a woman at the table with her letters on a slate, and a voice saying no, the other way round, love.

The sea. Grey and enormous and not caring.

A girl on a hard by some boats saying you look terrible.

A black stone on a green hill, and five lines in the dark.

And under all of it, so big it was the shape of the whole green and not just a thing in it, the reason it was green at all. He’d seen it last summer and called it loving and been a little embarrassed by the word. He wasn’t embarrassed now. He held it in his palm, and it was what it was, and the word was right.

One, he thought. He didn’t know why he was counting. Two.

It didn’t cost her anything.

He could feel that too. That was the thing he’d been most afraid of, on the step with Wendy, without knowing it: that holding a colour would take something from the person it belonged to, the way Suzu’s eating took something from whatever she ate. It didn’t. Alea knelt there with her hand over his and her colour sat in his palm and none of it was going anywhere. It was still hers. He was just holding it. The way you hold somebody’s coat while they put their boots on.

Three. Four.

It was still connected to her. He could feel the thread of it going back through his hand into hers. Not a thread he’d made; one that had always been there, that he’d never been able to see, between a person and the outside of their own soul. He wasn’t cutting it. He wasn’t pulling it. He was just holding the end.

Five. Six.

And he understood, kneeling there, or sitting there, with his back against her mother’s stone and her colour in his palm, why it had gone through his fingers on the step. He’d reached. He’d tried to hold the way you hold a thing you’re taking: from the outside, closing on it. You couldn’t. Not a colour. The colour didn’t let you. It knew, the way a hand knows, the difference between a hand closing round it and a hand being given something.

It had to be given.

Seven.

He’d never once been given one. Not on purpose. Nobody had ever known there was anything to give.

Eight.

Alea’s hand moved, very slightly, on the back of his.

Nine.

And it slipped.

Not through his fingers. Not like water. It went back. The way it had come, through his palm and the back of his hand, into hers, gently, the way a bird you’ve been holding gets restless and you open your hand and it’s gone. He didn’t let it go. It went. It had been given, and it went back to the one who’d given it, and that was right too.

Given, and given back.

He sat there against the cairn with her hand still over his on the stone and his palm empty and warm.

“What was that,” said Alea.

Very quietly. She hadn’t moved her hand.

“Your colour,” said Yan.

She looked at him in the starlight. Her ears were all the way up.

“I held it,” said Yan. His voice came out strange. Deep, the way it always was, but strange; like somebody else’s, a long way off. “For a bit. You put your hand on mine and it came. Into my hand. And I held it. Nine heartbeats.” He looked at their hands on the stone. “Then it went back to you.”

“I felt something go,” said Alea. “Out of my hand. Warm. And come back.” She thought about it. “It didn’t hurt.”

“No. It didn’t cost you anything. I felt that.” He swallowed. “I was afraid it would. I’ve been afraid all year.”

“What colour is it?”

He looked at her. He’d told her, last summer, on this knoll. He’d told her the first night the colours came. She’d forgotten, or wanted to hear it again.

“Green,” said Yan. “The same as Suzu’s.”

Alea looked at the small white snake round his neck.

“What’s it mean?” she said. “Green.”

“Loving,” said Yan.

She didn’t say anything for a long time.

Then she took her hand off his, slowly, and sat back on her heels on the grass between the stone and her sword, and put her hands in her lap, and looked at them.

“I didn’t know you could do that,” said Alea.

“Neither did I.”

He opened his window.

He didn’t decide to. It was there. The way it had been there on the knoll last summer, the night of the ninth, when the colours came, and on the wall the first winter when he found Kin.

He looked at it for a long time.

Seventy-eight. It had been seventy this morning. He’d looked, on the east stair, at the top, with his hand on the wall, breathing. Seventy for a month, since the summer, since the Lists. And now seventy-eight, and under it a line he’d never seen before, in the same plain white letters as everything else, greyed, the way Thea’s Handshake had sat grey under her bar for more than a year, the way Chris’s Trace sat grey under his.

He’d read Thea’s grey line in the channel the night it came, a year ago, when she wrote it out for everyone. And Chris’s, at the Lists. He’d thought, both times: that must be the worst thing. To know what a thing is. To have done it once, for a few heartbeats. And to be told not yet, in plain white letters, by your own window.

It wasn’t the worst thing.

He sat against the cairn and looked at Carry in grey, and it wasn’t the worst thing at all. It was a name. For a thing he’d done. That he’d thought all year he couldn’t do, that he’d thought wasn’t his, that he’d been sick in the snow trying to do. It had a name. It was his. It was just a little higher up than he could reach on his own, yet, the way a shelf is.

He knew what it was now. He’d felt the shape of it in his palm for nine heartbeats.

He’d get there.

You always did that, said Suzu.

Very quietly. In his head. So quietly he almost didn’t hear it, even there.

He looked down at her.

She’d lifted her head off his collarbone. Small, the size of a scarf, white in the starlight, with her green eyes on him and her tongue very still. She wasn’t looking at the window. She was looking at his hand. The one that had been on the stone, under Alea’s, that he’d taken off the iron now and was holding in his lap with the palm up, still warm.

“Did what?” said Yan.

She didn’t answer.

“Suzu. Did what?”

Held them, said Suzu. And then, as if she’d said too much, as if a door had been open in her that she hadn’t meant to leave open, she put her head back down on his collarbone, against his jaw, and closed her eyes, and was very still.

“When?” said Yan.

Nothing.

“I’ve never done it before. Tonight’s the first time. I tried with Wendy and it went through my fingers. I’ve never—“ He stopped. “When did I always do that?”

Nothing. Her tail came round two of his fingers, the ones on the hand in his lap, and held them. That was all.

He sat there with the question in his mouth and didn’t ask it again. When she didn’t want to say a thing she didn’t, and asking only made her sad, and he’d learned that on a cold stone floor three years ago and every day since.

But he thought about it. The rite. This afternoon. Aldous at the foot of the cairn, singing the old words that had always been a song; and the line at the end, the carrier’s line, that wasn’t in Aldous’s book, that the elves had sung over fourteen hundred graves in the winter. And his own voice, singing it, behind Alea, before he’d decided to. He hadn’t known it. He’d known it.

You always did that.

He didn’t know what it meant. He thought, sitting there, that he would. Not tonight. Some day. When she was ready to tell him, or when he was high enough up to see it for himself.

He put his other hand over the one in his lap, over Suzu’s tail round his fingers, and held it.

He noticed it after a while.

He didn’t notice it at first because he wasn’t looking for it. He’d been tired so long that he’d stopped noticing being tired, the way you stop noticing a sound. He just noticed, after a while, sitting against the cairn with Alea a pace from him on the grass and Astra across her knees now, where she’d picked it up at last; that he was looking at Deston’s light, down the slope, singing, and Xav’s, still, and Aldous’s, white at the heart. And the colours had come at once.

No moment. No wait. He looked, and they were there.

He held his hand up in front of his face, in the dark, the way he did on the barracks roof, and looked at the place where his own light came out round his fingers. The colour of water. He’d never been able to name it.

It wasn’t thinner.

It wasn’t thicker, either. It wasn’t what it had been before the ninth. But the place at the top of it, where something had been taken off with a spoon last summer and never grown back; something was in it. Not his. A little warmth. Green, at the very edge, so faint he’d never have seen it if he hadn’t just held the whole of it in his palm for nine heartbeats and known its shape.

She’d given it back. All of it. He’d felt it go.

And it had left something behind anyway. The way a hand leaves warmth in a cup.

He wasn’t tired.

Not not tired. He’d been tired a year; it wasn’t going to stop because of nine heartbeats on a knoll. But less. For once. He sat against her mother’s stone with his hand in his lap and was less tired than he’d been since the night of the ninth, and he didn’t know why, and he thought, maybe, he did.

The carrier pays, Aldous had read, in the winter, out of the elves’ song. The one who walks with the white. And Suzu had said enough.

Maybe it didn’t always have to be the carrier.

Maybe, sometimes, somebody gave something to the carrier, on purpose, for no reason, because he was sitting against a stone looking tired and her mother would have.

“Are you going down?” said Alea.

She’d been sitting a long time, with Astra across her knees and her ears turned toward the stone. Not talking. He hadn’t talked either. It had been the kind of not talking you could do with her, which he’d learned last summer on the road east: the kind her father did, that didn’t need filling.

“No,” said Yan. “Not yet. I’ll sit up. With Aldous.”

“Can I?”

He looked at her.

“Sit up,” said Alea. “With her. I didn’t, last year. I went down with Dad. I wanted to come back up all night and I didn’t.” She looked at the stone. “I came back for Astra. I don’t want to go back down yet.”

“It’s not mine to say,” said Yan.

“I’m asking anyway.”

“Then yes,” said Yan.

She nodded. She moved, a little, along the grass, so that she was sitting against the cairn too, a pace from him, with her back to the plain east face of the black stone and Astra across her knees. Not touching him. She didn’t need to.

At the foot of the cairn, on his white cloth, Aldous turned his lantern up a hair, without saying anything, so that it reached them both.

They sat there for an hour.

The moon came up. Late, a little lopsided, a few nights past full, over the black line of the mountains of Kharvos, very large and orange at first and then smaller and white. It laid a long pale light across the red, all the way to the knoll, and up the slope, and onto the cairn and the stone and the two of them sitting against it.

The ash’s leaves moved in it.

Yan watched them. And the thread under the grass, gold-edged, spark-coloured, between the ash and the seedling. And Deston’s light down the slope, which had stopped singing, which had gone very still, the way it did when Deston was listening hard to something a long way off. And Xav’s beside it. And Aldous’s, white at the heart, with his book in his lap and his hand, Yan saw, gone to his collar.

To the cord there. A plain knotted cord that he wore round his neck under his chaplain’s black, with a knot in it that was always cold, that ran, if you could see it, which nobody could but Deston and Hana, a long way west and south to a room in Solmere.

Aldous’s hand was on it.

Yan saw his light change. The way lights changed when something came into them from outside. A warmth, going into the white.

Aldous looked up.

Not at the stone. Not at the moon. At Yan. With the lantern turned up beside him, and his old face very still in it, and his hand on the cord at his throat; and in the moonlight his eyes were wet, and he didn’t seem to know it.

“Yan,” said Aldous.

“Father?”

“Hana,” said Aldous. As if that explained it. And then, because it didn’t: “On all seven. At once. She’s never sent on all seven at once to us.” He looked down at his hand on the cord. “From Thea. Through her. In Maren’s hand, it’ll be, at the other end. In mine it’s—“ He stopped, and read it again, the way you read a thing that’s come into your head in your own handwriting and you don’t believe it.

“Thea needs your eyes,” said Aldous.

Down the slope, Deston was already on his feet.
