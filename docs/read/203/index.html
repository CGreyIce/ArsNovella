The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 7: Myths

Chapter 203

Exact

7,186 words · 31 min read

Thea felt them come over the ridge at about four in the morning.

She was asleep. Then she was lying in the dark in the long room at the top of the militia hall with her eyes open, listening to something that wasn’t a sound.

She’d had that since the night on the jetty. Since the autumn, when she’d held two things for twenty heartbeats and said hello into the gap in the oldest thing in the world and something had come back. It wasn’t a sense, exactly. Nobody had a word for it. Higu had tried several in her notebook and crossed them all out. The nearest Thea had got was this: when something was written in that language, the one in the middle of the circle, the one under every window, she could feel it the way you can feel a phone buzz in another room. Not what it said. Just that it was there, and roughly where, and whether it was well.

She’d felt Oswin’s first leaf in the autumn, coming down the lake road in a board under the king’s arm. It had felt like a song somebody was humming slightly wrong. Mostly right. Three places where the note slid.

This was six of them.

She lay and listened. Over the ridge. Past Hethe, on the high road, coming down, slowly, the speed of a horse that’s been told not to hurry. Six of them, one behind the other, stacked, the way paper’s stacked in a case. And she listened to each one the way you’d listen to a choir warming up, one voice at a time, for the one that was off.

None of them were.

Not one. Not a slide. Not a hair. Six leaves of the oldest language in the world coming down a hill road in the dark, and every line in every one of them sitting exactly where it ought to sit, the way a note sits when it’s in tune and you stop hearing it as a note and start hearing it as the song.

She sat up.

“Higu,” she said.

Higu was awake. Higu was always awake when it mattered; Thea had never once known her not to be. She was sitting up in the next bed with her notebook already open on her knees and a pencil in her hand and the white bow in her hair slightly crooked from the pillow, looking at Thea in the grey light from the window.

“How many?” said Higu.

“Six.”

Higu wrote it down.

“And?”

Thea opened her mouth. Closed it. She found she was holding the blanket in both fists.

“Clean,” she said.

The rider came at noon. They’d been waiting since dawn.

All of them. Not on purpose. Thea had got up and dressed and gone down to the kitchen and put the kettle on, and then not made any tea, and sat at the long table, and somewhere in the next hour the others had all come down one at a time and sat at it too, and nobody had said why. Xola with Squiddles asleep on her head. PenguChi with Waddles, who’d been out on the trough board twice before breakfast and was damp. Gigichi, scowling, with her gold watch on the table in front of her, open, ticking. Higu with the notebook. And at about ten, Maren, in her guild coat, who came in and looked at them all sitting in a row in silence at the long table like people waiting outside a room where something was being decided, and didn’t ask, and sat down at the end with her own cup.

“Thea felt something,” said Higu, to her, without looking up.

“I gathered,” said Maren.

At noon the hall door opened, and a boy came in.

He was the same one. Thea knew him at once; they all did. The young one, nineteen or so, very solemn, very red in the face from the ridge wind, who’d come down the lake road in the winter with Halvard’s first letter and stood on the shingle at the land end of the jetty for a long time working up to it while the hens watched him. He was carrying a flat oak case under one arm, the size of a big book, with iron corners, and he was carrying it the way you’d carry a sleeping baby across a river.

He stopped inside the door. He looked round the hall. At the long table, and the six of them, and the penguin.

He looked very relieved about something.

“No hens,” he said.

“No hens,” agreed Maren.

“Thank the—“ He stopped himself. He went redder. He came across to the table and stood in front of it, and drew himself up, the way somebody stands who’s been told something more than once by somebody who frightens him. “I’m to put this into the hand of the girl with the code. Into her hand and no other. The Lord Advisor said.” He looked along the row of them. “He said I’d know her. He said she’d be the one who already knew I was coming.”

Thea stood up.

The boy looked at her. At her face, which was doing something; she could feel it doing it. And something in his own face settled, as if he’d been worried about this part for three weeks on the road and could stop now.

“That’s you,” he said.

“That’s me,” said Thea.

He held out the case. She took it. Into her hand. It was heavy, and cold from the road, and she could feel them inside it the way you feel warmth through a wall: six of them, stacked, quiet, exact.

“What’s your name?” said PenguChi, from the end of the table. Gravely. The way she asked everybody.

The boy looked surprised. Nobody had asked him, Thea thought. Not in three weeks, not in two journeys, not at Hethe or in the castle. He was a rider. You didn’t ask riders their names; you asked them where the letter was.

“Kit,” he said. “Ma’am.”

“Hello, Kit,” said PenguChi, and Waddles, in her lap, bowed, or fell forward.

Kit looked at the penguin. He looked at it for some time.

“I’ve another,” he said, at last, faintly, and took a second letter out of his coat. Small, square, cream paper, sealed in plain wax with an open hand. “For him. On the lake. Into his hand and no other.” His face had gone a slightly different colour. “Are the hens—“

“Yes,” said Gigichi.

She’d got up. She shut the gold watch with a snap and put it in her pocket and came round the table and stood in front of the boy, a head shorter than him, with her arms folded and her silver hair falling in her eyes and her scowl, and looked him up and down.

“I’ll take you,” she said. “They won’t touch you if you’re with me. The big red one might. Don’t look her in the eye.”

“Thank you,” said Kit, with enormous feeling.

“Don’t thank me,” said Gigichi. “I’m not doing it for you. I’m doing it so Thea can open that box without you standing there going red.” She jerked her head at the door. “Come on.”

They went. Thea heard Kit say, on the step, in a small voice, does she really look you in the eye, and Gigichi say only if you deserve it.

The door shut.

Thea put the case down on the long table.

Oswin had packed it himself. She could tell. Nobody else in the world would have packed six sheets of oiled paper like that.

Inside the oak there was a lining of soft grey felt, and on the felt, six sheets, each one between two leaves of thin tissue, each one flat and square and the colour of weak tea. On top, a folded note. Under the note, a second sheet of the same oiled paper, but smaller, covered in rows of tiny marks. And tucked down the side of the felt, where you’d hardly see it, a narrow strip with blue ink on it in a hand she knew better than her own.

She took the note out first.

It was in the kingdom’s script. Oswin’s careful, crabbed archivist’s hand, very small, very neat. She couldn’t read it. None of them could; they never had. She opened her window and let it read it for her, the way it read everything here, and the words came up in front of her face in English, plain and white.

Thea.

Leaves two to seven. Both sides. Six. The case holds six; the king made it to hold six, and said if I wanted it to hold more I could learn to make cases.

With the dividers. Every line. Edwy rules the margins and checks each line twice against the original, under a glass, with his own finger on the grey and mine on the copy. He trusts nothing. He is very good at trusting nothing. I think it is what he was for, before, and he has only moved it.

Cursiffe has put his marks in the margins. In blue. He says you will know.

The second sheet is the corners. In the first week, before I began, I copied the small mark in the top corner of every one of the forty leaves, all forty, onto one sheet, so I would know their order if anything happened to the box. Something happened to the box. The sheet survived because it was in my coat. I have sent you a copy of it. I do not know if it is useful. It seemed wrong to keep it.

I am on leaf twelve. I sleep six hours. The king checks.

Tell me if I am wasting a year.

O.

Thea read it twice. Then she read it out, in English, so the others could hear, because they couldn’t see her window; and when she got to the king checks, Xola started crying, quietly, into Squiddles.

“Sorry,” said Xola. “Sorry. It’s just. The king checks.”

“I know,” said Thea.

She put the note down. She took out the strip from the side of the felt.

Blue ink. English. Cursiffe’s capitals, a little shakier than they used to be, because his right hand had been in a fire in the winter and Rachael had said cold water, not ice, and it had healed, mostly.

THEA. MARGINS ARE MINE. WHERE THINGS ARE, NOT WHAT. EDWY’S TICKS ARE THE LITTLE RED ONES. HE’S LEARNING R. HE CAN DO IT LEFT-HANDED NOW. HE’S VERY PROUD. DON’T TELL HIM I TOLD YOU. — C.

P.S. I DIDN’T KNOW WHAT TO PUT ON THE BOX THIS TIME SO I PUT KEEP AGAIN. SMALL. INSIDE THE LID. IT’S HABIT NOW.

Thea looked inside the lid of the oak case.

There it was. Down in the corner, very small, in blue. KEEP.

She laughed. It came out of her before she could stop it, loud, in the quiet hall, and Higu looked up and nearly smiled, and Maren said “What?” and Thea said “Nothing. Cursiffe,” and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and took the first leaf out of its tissue and laid it flat on the table in front of her.

Leaf two.

Rings with lines in them. That was what it looked like, to anyone else. That was what it had looked like to her, the first time, in the autumn: rings, and lines going out of the rings and coming back, and small careful marks along the lines like the ticks on a ruler, all over the sheet, edge to edge, both sides. The same hand as the middle of the circle. The oldest hand. Grey lines on tea-coloured paper. In the margins, Cursiffe’s blue: ring, closed, five ticks. Line out at 3, back at 8. Same as leaf 1, top left, but bigger? And here and there, beside a line, a tiny red tick, two of them, side by side. Edwy. Checked twice.

She didn’t touch it. Not at first.

She looked at it the way she’d learned to look at the circle. Not hard. Not trying. Letting her eyes go soft and down, past the lines, into the place where her window lived, the way she’d written it to Niko once in a letter. Like you’re looking at somebody’s hands across a table.

Then she put two fingers on the edge of the paper.

And it parsed.

All of it. Top to bottom, edge to edge, one side and then, when Higu turned it over for her without being asked, the other. It went into her the way a clean file goes into a compiler: line after line after line, every ring closing where it ought to close, every line coming back where it ought to come back, every tick exactly where the tick before it said the next one would be. Her window didn’t stop. It didn’t go what?. Not once. It just read, and read, and read, smooth and quiet as water going over a stone, all the way to the last line in the bottom corner of the second side.

She took her fingers off it.

“Thea,” said Higu.

“No faults,” said Thea.

Her voice sounded strange to her. Very level. The voice she used to use on Earth at three in the morning when something finally built after two days.

“None?”

“None. Not one.” She looked at the leaf. “It’s exact.”

Higu wrote it down.

Thea took the next leaf out of its tissue.

Leaf three. Clean.

Leaf four. Clean.

She went faster. She couldn’t help it. She’d meant to go slowly, to take a rest between each one, the way she’d learned to with the circle, because parsing that language was like holding your breath and you could only do it so long. But each one came up into her window so smoothly that it didn’t feel like holding her breath. It felt like breathing. Leaf five. Clean. Higu turned each one over for her, without a word, and laid it back in its tissue, and passed her the next, and wrote a single word in her notebook each time, and Thea could see it out of the corner of her eye, the same word, five times, in Higu’s small neat pencil, down the page. Clean. Clean. Clean. Clean. Clean.

Nobody at the table said anything. PenguChi had her hand over Waddles’s beak, very gently. Xola had stopped crying and was sitting with both hands flat on the table and her gills standing out behind her ears, the way they did when she was holding something in. Maren, at the end, hadn’t touched her tea.

Leaf six.

Leaf seven.

She put her fingers on the last one, and it went into her like the others, line after line after line, and came out at the bottom corner of the second side, and stopped.

Clean.

Six. In a row. Not a hair out. Not one place, in twelve sides of the oldest language in the world, where a line went this way and should have gone that.

She sat back in her chair.

She found she was shaking. Not badly. Her hands, mostly. She put them in her lap.

“Six,” said Higu, and wrote it, and drew a line under it.

“Exact,” said Thea.

And then, because there was nobody in the hall but them and Maren, who’d seen her cry before, she put her face in her hands and laughed, or cried, she wasn’t sure which, for about a minute, while Xola came round the table and stood behind her chair with both hands on her shoulders and said Thea, Thea, Thea into her hair.

She’d done the sum in the autumn. In front of the king. A hundred and twenty places where her window would go what?. A hundred and twenty typos in forty leaves, three a leaf, because a reed pen held by a tired man at a long table under the lamps for eighty nights couldn’t be held still enough where the lines ran close. She’d said she’d read round some of them. Not all. She’d said the ones she couldn’t read round might be the ones that mattered.

She’d carried that sum all winter. It was the kind of sum that sits in the back of your head and does nothing and weighs a great deal.

And then a king had gone to a forge on the shingle of a lake and made a pair of dividers you could split a hair with, with a dead king’s open hand on the hinge, and a ruling pen with a screw in the nib, so that the line came out the same at the end as it went in at the start, whatever the hand was doing. And an archivist had used them. And a boy who’d poured oil over the leaves in the winter and set them on fire had sat at the same table all spring with a glass, checking every line twice, trusting nothing.

And now there weren’t a hundred and twenty. There were none.

Not one in six. Which meant—

“Which means it’s not luck,” said Higu.

She’d been watching Thea do the sum in her head. She always could.

“Six in a row,” said Higu. “If it were luck you’d get one. Maybe two. Not six.” She tapped the pencil on the page. “It’s the dividers. It’s him. It’s Edwy. It’s going to be clean. All forty.”

“All forty,” said Thea.

She said it out loud to hear it. It sounded enormous.

“All forty clean,” said Thea. “Every line exactly where the person who drew it put it. Three hundred years. More. And nobody’s ever had it clean. Not Aldemar’s scholars. Not anyone.” She looked at the six leaves on the table in their tissue. “Their copies were wrong. Their copies were wrong in three places a page, and they guessed, and they guessed wrong, and they stopped. And we’re going to have it clean.”

Xola’s hands tightened on her shoulders.

“Can you read it?” said Xola. Very softly.

Thea looked at the leaves.

“No,” she said.

It was Maren who asked the right question. Thea thought afterwards that it was always Maren, in the end. She’d been one of the Advisor’s seven since she was twenty-three, and kept an order she hated for sixteen months, and written down everything, and crossed out the things she wasn’t allowed to say, and she asked questions the way she wrote: exactly, and once.

“If you can’t read it,” said Maren, “what can you see?”

Thea thought about it.

“Shape,” she said.

“Show me,” said Maren.

So she did.

She got up. She went to the wall at the end of the long room, the plastered one by the stair where Higu had drawn the map of the lake road in the first winter and nobody had ever washed it off, and took a lump of chalk off the shelf. And she drew it.

Not the leaves. She couldn’t draw the leaves; nobody could, without the dividers. She drew what they were.

“One leaf,” said Thea, “you can’t tell anything. I said so to the king. It’s like one page of a recipe book in a language you don’t know. You can tell it’s a recipe. You can’t tell which one.” She drew a box. Then another beside it. Then another. “Six leaves, you start to see things repeat.”

She drew lines inside the boxes. Not the real lines. The shape of them.

“Every one of these six,” she said, “is the same thing over and over. Down the page. A ring, with lines in it, and ticks on the lines, and then a gap, and then another ring, the same shape, different ticks. And another. And another. Like rows.” She drew them. Little rings in a column, down each box, with gaps between. “Each one’s got the same frame. Same number of lines going out. Same places they come back. Only the ticks are different, and some of the lines inside.” She stood back. “That’s a list. That’s what a list looks like when you can’t read it. Same frame, over and over, different contents.”

“A list of what?” said PenguChi.

“I don’t know.”

“Things,” said Higu. “A list of things.”

“A list of things,” agreed Thea. “And they run off the end of leaf seven. Leaf seven’s last row doesn’t finish the list. It just stops, because the page stops, and the list goes on onto eight.” She tapped the chalk on the last box. “So it’s longer than seven. I don’t know how long.”

She went back to the table, and picked up the other sheet. The small one. Oswin’s corners.

Forty little marks. In four rows of ten. Each one copied, very carefully, from the top corner of one of the forty grey leaves in the vault three hundred miles south, in the first week, before he’d begun, so I would know their order. Cursiffe had written a number beside each, in blue. One to forty.

She’d looked at it on the table, when she first took it out, and thought that’s nice, and put it aside. It was only forty little marks.

She put two fingers on it now.

It parsed. Of course it parsed; Oswin had drawn it in the autumn with the dividers already in his hand, and they were very small. Forty marks. Forty little pieces of the language, each one a word, or the shape of a word. She couldn’t read any of them.

She didn’t need to read them. She only needed to see which ones were the same.

“There are two kinds,” said Thea slowly.

She held it up.

“Look. The first ones—“ She ran her finger along the first row, and onto the second. “These are all the same kind of mark. A ring, open at the top, with a tick in it. Different ticks. Same ring.” Her finger stopped, somewhere in the second row. “And then here they change. All at once. It’s a different mark. A line, going in, with something on the end of it. All the rest of them, to the end, to forty.” She looked at it. “Two kinds. One kind for the first part. Another for the rest.”

Nobody said anything.

Thea went back to the wall with the chalk. She drew forty boxes, small, along the plaster, in a long row. She drew a ring, open at the top, over the first ones. She drew it as far along as Oswin’s corners told her to, somewhere past the middle of the second row, and stopped. And over all the rest, to the end, to forty, she drew the other mark. A line, going in.

And then she drew arrows. Because Cursiffe had noted them in blue in the margins of all six leaves, without knowing what they were: little marks at the end of certain rows in the list, the same mark each time, that matched, exactly, a corner mark further on. Same as corner 23? he’d written, beside one. Same as 31? beside another. He’d put question marks. He was always careful to put question marks when he didn’t know.

Thea knew. She drew the arrows. From the boxes at the end of the row, the ones with the line going in, back into the list. Over and over. Every one of the later leaves reaching back into the first ones.

She stood back.

She looked at it for a long time.

“I said it to the king,” she said. “In the autumn. On the shingle. You can see the shape of instructions, the way you can see a recipe in a language you can’t read, because there’s a list and then there’s a method.” She heard her own voice go quiet. “I said it as a way of talking.”

She looked at the forty boxes on the wall. The rings at the start. The lines going in, after. The arrows going back.

“It isn’t a way of talking,” said Thea. “That’s what it is. That’s the whole thing. All forty.” She put the chalk down on the shelf, carefully, as if it might break. “A list. And then a method. And the method uses the list. All the way through. Forty pages.”

Gigichi came back in the middle of it. Thea didn’t hear her. She was just there, in the door at the top of the stair, with mud on her boots and her arms folded, looking at the wall.

“Kit gave him the letter,” she said, after a while, to nobody. “Into his hand. The red one looked him in the eye. He didn’t cry. Much.” She looked at the chalk. “What’s that.”

“It’s a recipe,” said Xola.

Gigichi looked at it a moment longer.

“For what?” she said.

And there it was. The question. Thea had felt it coming since the second leaf, since clean, sitting in the room with them like a seventh person at the table. Nobody had wanted to say it first. Gigichi said things first. She always had.

For what.

Everybody in the room knew what they wanted it to be for.

The circle in the floor of a hall in Solmere, fourteen rings wide. Thirteen of them written edge to edge. The fourteenth, at the very edge, with its right arc drawn as carefully as all the rest and left empty. A form with a blank box. The way home, that needed a Sun to draw, and nobody had ever been a Sun. I will spend this kingdom finding it, the king had said, at his own table, in Valcrest, in front of all of them.

And here was a list. And a method. In the same hand. Forty pages.

“I don’t know,” said Thea.

“You can guess,” said Higu.

Not pushing. Higu never pushed. She was only saying it so it would be said, the way she wrote things down so they’d be written: so that it was out, on the table, where everyone could see it, instead of sitting in the room like a seventh person.

“I can guess,” agreed Thea.

She looked at the wall.

She could. That was the terrible thing. She could see the shape of an entry in the list so clearly now. The ring, and the lines going out, and the ticks, and the places where the ticks were different from row to row. Six clean leaves. If she looked hard at the rows, at the differences between them, she could start to put meanings in. That’s a quantity. That’s a place. That one, the one that’s always the same three ticks, that’s probably a word like and. She could feel the guesses coming up in her, one after another, the way letters come up on a phone screen when you start typing and it thinks it knows what you mean.

And some of them would be right.

And some of them would be wrong. And she wouldn’t know which.

“Aldemar’s scholars guessed,” said Thea.

She said it quietly. To the wall.

“They had copies with three wrong places a page. And they looked at them and they could see the shape, the same as me, and they wanted it so badly, and they guessed. They put meanings in. They filled in the boxes they couldn’t read with what they thought should be there.” She folded her arms. “And then they wrote down their guesses as if they were the thing. And somebody after them read the guesses and thought they were the thing. And somewhere in there it all went wrong, and they stopped, and Aldemar wrote let no one burn them, and put them behind a door for three hundred years.”

“You’re not them,” said Xola.

“No,” said Thea. “Because I’m not going to do it.”

She turned round.

“I’m not guessing,” she said. “Not one. Not even the ones I’m sure about. Especially not those.” She heard her own voice go sharp, and didn’t stop it. “Patch only works when I can see what it was meant to be. I can’t see what this is meant to be yet. I can see that it’s a list and a method. That’s all. That’s true, and I’m writing down what’s true, and the rest of it stays blank until I can read it.”

“Thea—“ said Xola.

“No.” It came out hard. Much harder than she meant. “Don’t. Don’t ask me what’s on the list. Don’t any of you ask me if it’s the way home. I know you want it to be. I want it to be. That’s exactly why I’m not saying it.” She was shaking again. “If I say it, I’ll start seeing it. In every row. Whether it’s there or not.”

The hall was very quiet.

Xola’s eyes had filled. Her gills had gone flat.

Thea looked at her. And the sharpness went out of her all at once, the way it always did, and left her standing by a chalk wall with her arms folded and her heart going, feeling horrible.

“Sorry,” she said. “Xo. I’m sorry. That wasn’t—“ She unfolded her arms. “That wasn’t at you.”

“I know,” said Xola. She wiped her eyes. “It’s fine. You were right.” She sniffed. “You’re scary when you’re right. You always were. You used to do that on calls. At him.”

“I did not.”

“You did. When he was being stupid about sleep. He used to go yes, ma’am.” Xola laughed, and sniffed. “He liked it.”

Thea didn’t say anything to that. She didn’t trust herself to.

She turned back to the wall. She picked up the chalk again. And in the first box, over the first open ring, very small, in English, in capitals, she wrote it.

TODO.

She’d written it once before. On the circle. On the floor of an archive room in Valcrest, over the right arc of the fourteenth ring, the empty one, while Cursiffe watched: TODO. Because that’s what you write when you know something goes there and you don’t know what it is yet, and you’re not going to pretend you do.

Higu copied the whole wall into her notebook. The forty boxes. The two kinds of mark. The arrows going back. And the four letters in the first box, small.

“There,” said Higu, when she was done, and closed the book. “Now it’s written down. Now you don’t have to carry it.”

She went down to the lake at dusk.

On her own. The others knew not to come; they’d sat with her all afternoon in the hall while she put the leaves back in their tissue and the tissue back in the case and the case under her bed, and they’d talked about other things, deliberately, all of them, about Waddles on the trough board and the egg-woman’s boy and whether Gigichi’s tree had thirteen leaves now, until Thea had laughed properly. And then at dusk she’d got up, and Higu had looked at her and not said anything, and she’d gone.

He was on the end of the jetty. Of course he was.

The hens were round him. The big red one turned its head and looked at her as she came down the boards, and decided she was permitted, and looked away. Alea wasn’t there. Thea could see her light in the cabin window; she’d been at the Shiver all week, Gigichi said, falling in. There was a letter on the planks beside him, cream paper, open, held down with a stone. And a lamp, not lit.

She sat down beside him. On the last plank. Her feet over the water.

He didn’t say anything. He moved over a little, and went on fishing.

She’d sat beside him on this jetty a hundred times now. Since the winter, the second winter, the night he’d said hi at the bottom of the lake road and she’d heard his voice for the first time in three years. She still wasn’t used to it. She didn’t think she ever would be. You don’t get used to a dead friend sitting next to you with a fishing rod. You just stop being surprised by it, and the not-being-surprised is its own kind of thing.

She’d sung to him, on Earth. On calls, late, when he couldn’t sleep. He’d have something on his screen with the sound off, and she’d sing, whatever she was learning, badly, and he’d say that was nice in the voice he used when he meant it, and go quiet, and she’d know he’d gone to sleep with the call still running. She hadn’t sung here. Not once. Not to him. She didn’t know why.

“Six,” said Thea. In English. “Clean.”

He didn’t move. But his shoulders did the thing.

“I read the letter,” he said. He touched the cream paper on the planks with one finger. “Halvard. How are the hens. Oswin is exact. Severin checks. Edwy has started saying good morning to the Warden in the corridor and the Warden does not know what to do. H.” He was quiet. “Exact. He underlined it.”

“He was right.”

“Tell me.”

So she told him.

All of it. The six leaves, and the dividers, and Edwy’s red ticks, two by two. The rows. The frame, the same over and over, different contents, running off the end of leaf seven. Oswin’s corners, forty little marks in four rows, and the place in the second row where they changed. The arrows going back. Forty pages. A list, and a method, and the method reaching back into the list, all the way through. She told him the way she’d have told him on a call at three in the morning, when something finally built: fast, and with her hands, and laughing in the wrong places.

He listened. He always listened. He’d been the best listener she’d ever had, on Earth, which was a strange thing to know about someone who never let you see his face, and it was still true.

When she got to method, he went still.

Not the jetty still. She knew the jetty still; she’d watched it every evening for more than a year. The other one. The one she’d seen exactly once before, on the shingle in the autumn, when she said it parses in front of the king.

She stopped.

“And you can’t read it,” he said.

“No. Not at Candle. Not at Lantern either. Not for a long time.” She looked at the water. “Maybe not for years. It’s enormous. I can see the shape. That’s all.”

“But it’s real.”

“It’s real. It’s clean. It’s going to be clean all forty.” She swallowed. “It’s a list of things, and a way of doing something with them. In the same hand as the circle. The oldest hand.” She didn’t say the rest. She’d told the others not to. She wasn’t going to say it to him.

She didn’t have to. He’d read the channel every night for three years; he’d read her post from the archive room in Valcrest, the night she wrote it, fourteen rings. He knew what the right arc of the fourteenth was. He knew what everybody in the kingdom wanted a list and a method in that hand to be.

He was quiet for a long time.

Out on the lake a fish rose, a long way out, and the rings went out from it slowly over the dark water.

“Good,” he said.

She heard it. She’d known him since she was eighteen. She’d heard him say good a thousand times, in a thousand voices, on calls and on streams and at the bottom of a lake road, and she heard this one.

It was the true one. He meant it. It was good; he wanted it; he wanted them to have it, all thirty-five of them, a way home, written down in a clean hand. She could hear that.

And she could hear the other half of it too. Under it. The way you hear the rest under a note.

She sat with that. She didn’t know what to do with it. She’d never once, in all the years she’d known him, known what to do when he said a thing with two halves.

“Mostly,” said Thea.

He turned his head.

She didn’t look at him. She looked at the water.

“That’s what you meant,” she said. “Isn’t it. Good. Mostly.” She pulled her knees up. “I’m not asking. I’m just saying I heard it.”

He didn’t answer for a long time.

“Mostly,” he said.

The big red hen got up, and walked round behind him, and sat down on his other side, against his leg.

Thea laughed. It came out unsteady.

“Me too,” she said. “Mostly.”

He looked at her.

“I want to go home,” said Thea. “I want my mum. I want my bed. I want to finish my degree; I was two modules off. I want to sing at somebody who’s alive and not three hundred years old.” She was quiet. “And I’m sitting on a jetty next to you. And I don’t think you’re coming.” She heard her voice go, and let it. “You’ve never said. You don’t have to. You’ve got Alea. You’ve got a grave. You’ve got a whole life here, and you built it out of nothing after you died, and it’s good, I can see it’s good.” She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, the fingerless glove scratchy. “So, mostly. That’s all. Mostly.”

He didn’t say anything.

He put the rod down across his knees. He sat there beside her on the end of the jetty in the dusk, with the hens round them and the lamp unlit and Halvard’s letter under its stone, and after a while she felt something she’d never once felt, on Earth or here: his hand, on the back of hers, on the plank between them. Just for a moment. Warm. And then gone again.

“You used to sing,” he said.

She looked at him.

“On calls,” he said. “When I couldn’t sleep. You’d sing whatever you were learning. Badly.” There was something in his voice. She couldn’t see his face. She’d never been able to. She didn’t need to. “I never told you. I used to stay awake on purpose. To hear the end.”

Thea put her hand over her mouth.

“You fell asleep,” she said, through it. “Every time. I heard you. You snored.”

“I stayed awake to the end,” he said, “and then I fell asleep. Every time. It was the best part of the day.”

She didn’t say anything for a long while.

Then she took a breath, and sang.

Not a song. Not really. She didn’t know what it was; something she’d been humming all spring without noticing, a few lines, no words, the tune going up and coming down the way the lake went in and out of the reeds. Very quietly. Badly. The way she always had. On the end of a jetty in another world, to a man with a rod across his knees whose face she couldn’t see, while the light went off the water.

He didn’t say that was nice.

He lit the lamp. When she’d finished. He took the flint out of his pocket and struck it and lit the lamp on the last plank, and set the glass back on it, and the yellow light came up on the boards round them and on the hens and on the cream letter under its stone.

“Exact,” he said.

Thea laughed, and cried a bit, and didn’t mind.

She wrote to Oswin that night. Not a letter. A letter would take three weeks. She went up to the militia hall and found Maren in the kitchen and asked for the cord.

Maren’s was blue, with a white bead. She took it off her wrist and gave it to Thea without a word, and gave her a strip from the box Cursiffe had sent with the cords in the autumn, and a pencil, and went and stood at the window with her back to her, which was Maren’s way of leaving a room without leaving it.

Thea held the strip on her knee. She thought about what to put for a long time.

Then she wrote it, in English, which the cord would turn into whatever Hana’s half needed it to be, and Hana would read it in Solmere, and carry it three floors down to the vault, to an archivist at a long table under the lamps, with a boy beside him on a stool checking lines twice under a glass.

Oswin. Six. Clean. Not one fault in twelve sides. Exact.

It’s a list and then a method. Forty pages. The method uses the list. Your corners told me. Keep them in your coat.

I can’t read it yet. I’m not guessing. Not one.

You are not wasting a year. — T.

P.S. Tell Edwy his ticks are perfect. Tell him from me. Tell him R left-handed is very good.

She held it until it went warm, and then cool, and then it was only a strip of paper on her knee with nothing on it.

She gave Maren the cord back. Maren put it on her wrist without turning round.

At about midnight, in the long room at the top of the hall, with everybody asleep but Higu, the channel moved.

She shut her window.

She lay in the dark and listened to the oak case under her bed. Six of them. Quiet, exact, in tune, the way a note sits when you stop hearing it as a note. And far off, three hundred miles south, under a castle, in a vault, forty grey originals in a box with KEEP on the lid, and thirty-three of them still to copy, waiting for a man with a ruling pen and a boy with a glass.

She could wait. She’d learned it from somebody.

She went to sleep humming, very quietly, with no words. Higu, in the next bed, heard her, and wrote it down.
