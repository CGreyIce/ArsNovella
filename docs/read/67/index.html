The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 3: The Anniversary

Chapter 67

The North Road

5,133 words · 22 min read

Kestra wrote things down.

Not what people said. Anybody could write down what people said; the castle was full of clerks who did nothing else, and most of what they wrote was lies, because most of what people said was. Kestra wrote down what people did. Where they stood when the door opened. Which way they looked first. What they did with their hands when they thought nobody was watching. Who they stepped in front of without thinking, and who they stepped behind.

Mouths lied. Feet didn’t. She’d learned that at twenty-one, on the king’s guard, in her first month, from an old sergeant who’d since died, and she’d never once found it to be wrong.

She had a book for it. Plain, black, the size of her hand, in the inside pocket of her coat. She’d filled eleven of them in twelve years. The king read them. Not all of them, not every page; but he read them, at night, in his study, and sometimes in the morning he’d look at her across the yard and she’d know which page he’d read, and he’d know she knew, and neither of them would say a word about it.

In a year with the summoned, she’d filled most of a twelfth.

The red-haired one stands where I would stand. Nobody taught him. Somebody did.

The fox girl’s hawk will sit on no one else. She did not ask it to.

The cat girl let it rise tonight, and put it down again, in front of a room full of people who would never have known. She drew her own line.

She’d never written a single word about the Hero of the Redmarch.

There’d been nothing to write. She’d never seen him. Nobody she knew had seen him, except the king, and the Advisor, and a few old soldiers at Cinderhold who went quiet when you asked, the way old soldiers go quiet about anything. Everything else about him was songs. Kestra didn’t write down songs.

She’d said as much to Oswin, at dinner, in the first month, when he’d been going on about it again. The songs are better than the man. They always are. Oswin had gone red and spluttered, and Jared had laughed into his soup, and Milli had said what man? in his careful way, and Kestra had looked at all five of them, round the table, and thought nothing of it at all.

She thought about it now, quite a lot.

The king had sent for her ten days ago, at dawn, in the private yard.

He’d been doing his forms. He always was, at that hour, in a plain shirt with the sleeves rolled up and a practice sword, moving through the old patterns in the grey light like a man of thirty, though he was sixty-four. She’d waited by the wall, the way the Advisor did. When he’d finished, he’d come over and taken the towel off its peg and looked at her.

“I’m going north,” he’d said. “Privately. For a few weeks. You’re coming.”

Kestra had considered this.

“How many guards?”

“You.”

“Majesty, the Council—“

“Will be told I’ve gone hunting.”

“You don’t hunt.”

“Then I’ll start,” said the king, and almost smiled.

She’d waited. He hadn’t said anything else. He’d stood there with the towel round his neck, looking at her, with that face that had been a king’s face for so long that nobody, not even Kestra, could read it anymore.

“Why me?” she’d said at last.

“Because you think he’s a song,” said the king.

She hadn’t asked who. She hadn’t had to. He’d read the page, then. She’d known he had.

“I want someone beside me who’ll write down what she sees,” said the king. “Not what she’s heard. Not what she expects. What she sees.” He’d put the towel back on its peg. “I’ve had forty years of people who only ever wrote down what they expected. I’d like one honest book about this. Before I’m dead.”

Then he’d gone inside. And that night, she’d watched him go down to the castle forge, alone, the way he sometimes did when the Council had been very trying, and come back up an hour later with a long roll of dark leather under his arm, heavy, bound with straps. And he’d put it in his own rooms, and nobody else had been allowed to touch it.

Nine days north.

No banners. No heralds. No guard of honour, no outriders, no Council lords in their furs. Two to ride, two to carry, and nobody looking twice at either of them. Kestra had thought there’d be trouble with that. She’d thought somebody, in some inn, in some market town, would look up from their beer and see the king of Aurelan sitting in the corner with a bowl of stew, and stand up, and shout.

Nobody did. Not once.

It took her two days to understand why. They didn’t recognise him because they’d never imagined him like this. A king was a crown. A king was a procession, and a carriage, and a great many men in armour shouting make way. Nobody had ever told them a king could be a big grey-haired man in a plain coat, eating stew with his elbows on the table, asking the innkeeper about the price of barley. They looked straight at him and saw a farmer, or a drover, or somebody’s retired uncle.

The king let them. He seemed to like it.

He kept the pressure in, too. That surprised her more. She’d stood beside him for twelve years, and in twelve years she’d never once been in a room with him that didn’t feel, a little, like being underwater. The ninth tier did that. You couldn’t help it, at Ascendant; it came off you the way heat comes off a forge. She’d seen lords twice his size go pale in a corridor when he walked past, without knowing why.

On the north road, he kept it in. All of it. She could feel him doing it, sometimes, at an inn table or on a crowded bridge: holding it, the way you’d hold your breath. Not because he had to. Because he didn’t want to frighten anybody’s children.

She wrote that down. He keeps it in for the children.

On the fourth day, his horse threw a shoe.

They were coming through a village in the hills, a straggle of stone houses on either side of the road and a church and a well, and the big bay went suddenly short on its off fore, and the king swung down and lifted the hoof and looked at it and said a word Kestra had never heard him say in twelve years, and then laughed.

There was a smithy at the end of the village. A small one, with an open front and a smoking chimney and a boy of perhaps twenty at the anvil, thin and freckled, with a leather apron down to his knees. He looked up when they came in leading the bay, and saw two strangers in travelling coats, and said afternoon and what’s the trouble, the way you would.

He made a mess of it.

Kestra didn’t know anything about horseshoes. Even she could see he was making a mess of it. He was nervous, for some reason; perhaps it was the size of the man watching him, or the sword on Kestra’s hip. His hands were shaking. He burned himself twice. He got the shoe on the anvil and hit it and hit it again and it came out wrong, and he looked at it and went red to the ears.

“May I?” said the king.

The boy stared at him.

“May I,” said the king again, gently. “I used to do this. A long time ago.” He held out his hand for the hammer.

The boy gave it to him.

Kestra had never seen him like that.

She’d seen him at forms, at dawn. She’d seen him on the throne, and at the Council, and once, a long time ago, on a wall, with a sword in his hand, when something had come over it that shouldn’t have. She’d seen him tired, and angry, and grieving, and very occasionally amused.

She’d never seen him happy.

He rolled his sleeves up past the elbow. He took the boy’s apron, and put it on, and it was much too small for him, and he didn’t care. He put the shoe back in the fire and worked the bellows himself, slow and steady, watching the iron, not saying anything. And when the iron was ready, he took it out and laid it on the anvil and hit it.

It sounded different.

That was the only way Kestra could put it, afterwards, in her book. The boy’s hammer had gone clank. The king’s went ting. A clean high bright note, like a bell, and then another, and another, in a rhythm, steady as a heartbeat. The shoe moved under it like clay. It turned and curved and flattened and came right, under those great thick scarred hands, as if it had only been waiting for somebody to ask it properly.

He was humming. Kestra didn’t think he knew he was doing it. Some old tune, under his breath, in time with the hammer. A river song, maybe. Something from a long time ago.

The boy watched him with his mouth open.

The king shod the bay himself. Nailed it on, rasped it, set the hoof down, stood back. The whole thing took perhaps a quarter of an hour. Then he took off the apron and folded it and handed it back to the boy, and put two silver coins on the anvil, which was four times what it was worth.

“You’ve done this before,” said the boy faintly.

“Once or twice.” The king looked at him. “Slow down. You were hitting it before it was ready. Wait for it. Iron tells you when it’s ready, if you let it. The heat stops being angry.” He smiled. An actual smile; Kestra saw it. “And keep your thumb out of the way. You’ll want it later.”

They rode on. Kestra looked back from the end of the village. The boy was still standing in the open front of his smithy, with the apron in his hands, staring after them.

She took out her book, riding, and wrote: He was happy.

Then she looked at it for a while, and didn’t cross it out.

She asked him about the Hero on the sixth night.

They were in a hayloft above an inn yard in a market town whose name she’d already forgotten, because the inn was full and the king had said a hayloft would do, and she’d stopped arguing with him about that kind of thing on the second day. He was lying on his back in the hay with his hands behind his head, looking at the rafters. She was sitting up with her back against a beam and her sword across her knees, because somebody had to.

“What’s he like?” she said.

The king didn’t answer for a while.

“He’s going to disappoint you,” he said at last.

Kestra waited.

“You’ve heard the songs,” said the king. “Eight feet tall. Killed the general with his bare hands. Never sleeps. Never puts the sword down. Lives in a fortress on the eastern border with a thousand men who’d die for him.” He was quiet for a moment. “He’s going to be a fisherman.”

“An actual fisherman?”

“An actual fisherman. With a rod. And a jetty. And hens.” Something that might have been a laugh. “He grows pumpkins. He’s very proud of them. He’ll probably show you.”

Kestra thought about that.

“The songs were written by men who never stood next to him,” said the king. “I did. Several times.” He turned his head in the hay and looked at her. “You’ll be disappointed for about as long as it takes him to look at you. And then you’ll spend the rest of your life trying to work out what you saw.”

“He wrote to me,” said the king, later, in the dark. Kestra had thought he was asleep. “Did Halvard tell you?”

“No.”

“First letter in eight years.” The hay rustled. “He’s never asked me for anything. Did you know that? Not a title. Not land. Not a pension. I offered him half the east, after the Redmarch. A lordship. A seat on the Council. My own guard, if he wanted it.” A pause. “He said he’d rather have a lake.”

Kestra didn’t say anything.

“And now he’s asked me for something,” said the king. “The first thing. In eight years.” Another pause, longer. “He wants me to cut his sword in half.”

Kestra turned her head and looked at him in the dark.

“The black sword,” she said. “The one in the hall.”

“The one in the hall. He took it back. On the Redmarch, on the anniversary, out of the hands of the summoned who were carrying it. Halvard’s chaplain sent word.” The king’s voice was very quiet. “Half for his daughter. She’s reached the second tier. And half for the woman it stood over for eight years. A headstone.” He breathed out. “He wants it cut, and reforged, and folded, and made into a sword a girl of sixteen can swing. And a stone. And he wants it done by someone who understands that iron.”

“And that’s you.”

“That’s me.” The king was quiet for so long she thought that really was the end of it. Then: “I put my hand on it, in the hall. Before the summoning. When Halvard laid it in the circle. Do you know what it felt like?”

“No.”

“Like nothing I’ve ever touched,” said the king. “Somebody forged that iron who knew exactly what he was doing. And then somebody else used it for things no iron should ever have to be used for. For years. And it held.” His voice had changed. Kestra had heard it change like that only once before, a long time ago, on a wall. “I’ve wanted to know how ever since. I’ve wanted to put it in a fire and find out.”

“So this isn’t a favour.”

“Oh, it’s a favour,” said the king. “It’s the biggest favour anyone’s ever asked me. I’d have walked there.”

On the eighth day they came into the lake country.

Kestra had never been this far north. She’d been born in the south, in a garrison town, and gone to the capital at seventeen, and the farthest north she’d ever been was a hunting lodge in the hills where the old king used to go before he died. She hadn’t known it would look like this.

The land went soft. That was the only word for it. The hills rolled down into long green valleys, and the valleys were full of water: small lakes, and then larger ones, one after another, flat and grey and still under a wide pale sky, with reeds at the edges and birds she didn’t know the names of standing in the shallows on one leg. The road ran between them on causeways. The air smelled of water, and woodsmoke, and cut hay. Everything was quieter. The wind was quieter. Even the horses seemed to walk more softly.

The king rode with his face turned up to it and didn’t say anything at all for most of the day.

On the ninth morning, from the top of a long low hill, she saw the biggest lake yet. It went away north as far as she could see, grey and silver and absolutely still, with a dark line of pines along the far shore. And on the near shore, where a river came out of it and ran away south, there was a town. Timber and stone. Smoke from a hundred chimneys. A church, and a market square, and a long timber hall on a hill above the town with a yard in front of it. Boats on the water.

“Wrenhaven,” said the king. “And Lake Ilse.”

Kestra looked at it. She’d read the name in a hundred reports. The girl with the code-sight, Althea, who’d built the channel. The guide, Maren. The mayor, Hale, who’d refused treble the guard because, the king had said, he knew who was already there. A demon in two pieces in an orchard, and a fisherman.

It met a fisherman.

She took her book out of her coat, and looked at it, and put it back without writing anything.

They didn’t get through the gate unnoticed.

Kestra had known they wouldn’t, somehow. She’d felt it coming all morning. The town was too small, and too quiet, and the man waiting for them at the south gate in a mayor’s chain had clearly been waiting for some time.

He was enormous. A big grey bear of a man in his fifties, with the chain round his neck sitting on him like a collar, and a face that was trying very hard to look as if this were an ordinary day and not managing it at all. He saw the king, and the king saw him, and the big man went straight down on one knee in the middle of the road, chain and all, with a sound like a sack of grain hitting a cart.

“Majesty,” he said.

“Get up, Hale,” said the king. “You’ll frighten the horses.”

“He said you’d come.” The mayor got up, which took a while. His face had gone very red. “He said you’d pretend you weren’t coming, and then you’d come. He said I should be at the gate by the ninth morning.” He swallowed. “I’ve been at the gate since the seventh.”

“Of course you have.” The king swung down from the saddle and took the mayor’s hand and shook it, hard, the way you’d shake the hand of an old friend. “How is he?”

The mayor opened his mouth. Then closed it again. Then said, in a different voice, “Better, Majesty. I think. Better than I’ve seen him.”

The king nodded slowly. “Good,” he said. “Good.”

And then the town found out.

Kestra had been in the capital when the king came back from the Redmarch, eight years ago, with the war won. She remembered the noise. The whole city in the streets, roaring.

Wrenhaven didn’t roar. It did something stranger.

It stopped. All of it, street by street, outward from the south gate, the way a ripple goes out across still water. A woman at a window, shaking out a rug, stopped with the rug in her hands. Two men carrying a ladder stopped in the middle of the street, with the ladder between them. A baker stopped with a tray of loaves. Children stopped running. And everyone, one after another, turned and looked at the big grey-haired man in the plain coat standing in the road beside their mayor. And didn’t say anything. And then started to whisper.

It went up through the town like smoke. By the time they’d walked their horses as far as the market square, there were people in every doorway, at every window, in silence, watching.

Kestra looked up the hill.

The long timber hall. The yard in front of it. There was a woman in the yard, very straight-backed, who had obviously been in the middle of drilling somebody and had stopped with her arm still raised. A big man beside her, a soldier by the look of him, had dropped a bucket. And at an upper window of the hall, crammed into it, five faces.

Kestra knew them, though she’d never seen them. She’d read about them in a hundred reports. A girl with pink and violet streaks in her hair. A girl with a white bow. A girl with silver hair. A girl with a little yellow heart. And a girl with lavender hair melting into pink, with what appeared to be a squid sitting on her head.

All five of them were staring down at the market square. Not at the king. Not at Hale, or the horses, or the crowd.

At the lake road.

A thin woman in black with a ring of keys at her belt came across the square at speed, and dropped a curtsey so fast it was almost a stumble.

“Majesty. I’m Odette. City secretary. The town house is being aired, Majesty, we only had word on the seventh, the beds are—“

“I won’t need the town house,” said the king. “Thank you, Odette.”

Odette’s face did something remarkable.

“I’ll be staying by the lake,” said the king. “I’m told there’ll be a forge to build.” He took the long leather roll down from behind the pack horse’s saddle, and slung it over his shoulder himself, by the strap, the way a working man carries his tools. “Hale. Would you see to the horses?”

“Majesty.”

“And Hale.” The king looked at him. “Nobody follows us down.”

“Nobody will,” said the mayor. And Kestra, watching his face, believed him. Whatever else he was, he’d been told that before, by someone else, about the same road, and he’d made it stick for eight years.

The lake road ran down from the market square through a gap in the houses and out along the shore.

It wasn’t much of a road. Packed earth, and stones, with grass down the middle and reeds on the lake side and a long stone wall on the other, and beyond the wall, fields of stubble going up the hill. The lake lay beside it, flat and grey and silver, so still Kestra could see the clouds in it.

The king walked. Kestra walked behind him. Three paces, on his left, where she’d always walked.

Nobody followed them. She looked back once, at the end of the houses. The whole of Wrenhaven was standing at the top of the lake road in silence, in a crowd, a hundred people, more, the mayor at the front with his arms out a little at his sides, like a man holding back a flood. Not one of them had taken a step past him.

She turned round and kept walking.

The cabin was at the end of the road.

It wasn’t much of a cabin. That was the first thing she noticed, and she wrote it down in her head, because she’d been told so many things about the Hero of the Redmarch and not one of them had prepared her for this. Timber walls, weathered silver. A stone chimney, smoking. A shingled roof with moss on it. A garden on the south side, long beds behind a low fence, and in the beds, enormous orange pumpkins, a great many of them, sitting in the stubble like a row of fat contented men. A shed. A woodpile, neat as a wall. A hen-yard. And beyond the cabin, running out over the water on old grey posts, a jetty, with nets hung drying along it and a little boat tied up at the end.

That was all. A fisherman’s cabin on a lake. There were ten thousand like it in Aurelan.

He’s going to be a fisherman.

The hens came for them at the gate.

Three of them, in a line, across the yard, with their necks out and their wings half up, making a noise like a kettle. The one in front was big and red and evil-looking, and she came straight at the king with her head down, the way Kestra had seen men come at the king exactly twice in twelve years, and both of them were dead now.

The king stopped. He looked down at her.

The hen stopped too. Very suddenly. She stood there in the dust of the yard with one foot up, looking up at the king. Something passed between them. Kestra didn’t know what. The ninth tier, maybe, letting itself out, just a breath, for one heartbeat, for a chicken.

The hen turned round and went for Kestra’s boot instead.

Kestra was Reaching. The fifth tier. She’d stood on the king’s guard for twelve years. She had cut a man’s sword in half once, at a feast, in front of four hundred people, without drawing her own. She looked down at the big red hen attacking her boot with savage, methodical, personal hatred, and found, to her own astonishment, that she didn’t have the faintest idea what to do about it.

“She does that,” said a voice from the doorway. “She does it to everybody. Don’t take it personally.”

There was a girl in the doorway of the cabin.

A fox girl. Sixteen, perhaps. Fox ears, standing very straight up, and a fox’s tail, very still. She was standing in the open door with one hand on the frame, looking at the king, and at Kestra, and at the leather roll on the king’s shoulder. And her face was doing something that Kestra, who had spent twelve years watching faces, couldn’t read at all.

His daughter, thought Kestra. She’s reached the second tier. She looked at the girl’s shoulders, and her hands, and the way she stood. Square. Planted. Weight on the back foot, though she was only standing in a doorway. Somebody had taught her to stand like that. Kestra knew exactly who.

The girl looked at the king for a long moment. Then she stepped back out of the doorway, without a word, and turned her head, and looked at someone inside.

And a man came out.

Kestra had thought, all the way north, about what she’d see.

She’d thought about it every night, in inns and haylofts, lying awake. She’d thought about it on the road, watching the lakes go by. She’d tried to imagine him, and every time she tried she’d caught herself imagining the songs, and stopped, and tried again, and couldn’t. She’d decided, in the end, that she’d simply look, when the time came, the way she always looked. Feet first. Then hands. Then where he stood. Then the face, last, because faces lied.

She looked.

His feet were bare. He’d come up from the jetty; there was water on the boards of the step behind him, and his feet were wet, and his trousers were rolled to the shin. He was standing on the top step of his own cabin, at ease, with his weight on neither foot. Not planted, the way the girl was. Not ready. Just there.

His hands. One of them was empty. The other held a fishing rod. An ordinary rod, plain wood, with a reel, and a line, and a small dull hook swinging gently at the end of it. He held it the way a man holds something he’s held every day for a very long time, without thinking about it at all.

Where he stood. In his own doorway. Between the king and his daughter. Exactly there, and nowhere else.

Then his face.

It was a plain face. That was all she’d ever be able to say about it, afterwards, in her book, though she tried for an hour. A man of about the king’s height, or a little less, or a little more. Not young, not old. A plain brown coat over a shirt, open at the neck. A face you’d pass in a market and never look at twice.

She looked at it twice. Three times. She couldn’t hold on to it.

And she felt nothing.

That was what stopped her breath. She’d felt the king’s pressure for twelve years, in every room, the underwater weight of the ninth tier. She’d felt Reaching swordsmen on the practice ground, and Gleaming, and once, at a distance, a Shaped. You always felt them. Aura came off people at the high tiers the way heat came off a fire. You couldn’t help it.

She felt nothing at all from the man in the doorway.

Nothing. Like standing at the edge of a lake on a windless night, in the dark, when the water is so still you can’t tell where it starts.

And then she realised something else. The king’s pressure, which she’d felt every day for twelve years, which he’d been holding in for nine days on the north road for the sake of other people’s children: it wasn’t being held in anymore. He’d let it go. She’d felt him let it go, at the gate, for the hen. And it should have been rolling off him now, filling the yard, making the air go thick.

It wasn’t. It was gone. Quiet. As if it had walked down to the water’s edge and found something much deeper there, and lain down beside it, out of courtesy.

The two men looked at each other across the yard.

Nobody spoke. The big red hen had stopped attacking Kestra’s boot and was standing very still in the dust with her head on one side. The fox girl was standing just inside the door with both hands over her mouth. The lake lay behind the cabin, grey and silver and absolutely still. A long way off, at the top of the lake road, a hundred people were holding their breath.

“Severin,” said the man in the doorway.

His voice was quiet. A little rough. Nobody’s voice.

“Ars,” said the king.

And then the king of Aurelan unslung the long leather roll from his shoulder, and set it down on the step at the fisherman’s bare feet, and straightened up, and stood there in the hen-yard with his hands at his sides and a look on his face that Kestra had only ever seen once before, in a village smithy, four days ago, with a hammer in his hand.

“I hear you need a smith,” he said.

Kestra took out her book.

She did it without thinking. She’d been doing it for twelve years. She stood in the hen-yard with the pencil in her hand and the book open on her palm at a clean page, the way she always stood at the end of something that mattered, ready to write down what she’d seen. Not what she’d heard. Not what she’d expected. What she’d seen.

She looked at the man in the doorway. At his bare wet feet, and the rod in his hand, and his plain face that she couldn’t hold on to. At the king, standing in front of him, sixty-four years old and the strongest man alive, looking like a boy at his first anvil. At the fox girl with her hands over her mouth.

She looked down at the clean page.

For the first time in twelve years, Kestra didn’t know what to write.
