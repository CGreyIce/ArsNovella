The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 7: Myths

Chapter 190

Strangers at the Gate

4,714 words · 20 min read

Rhea had never once in her life been frightened of something she could see coming, and she was standing in the west gate of Cinderhold at dusk being frightened of a hill.

It was a perfectly ordinary hill. The first of the downs, where the road came up out of the south and over the top and down the long slope to the fort. She’d looked at it every evening for four years. She’d watched the relief come over it every autumn, and riders, and carters; and a wagon with five summoned and a chaplain in it, the first autumn; and a cart with a black sword on it, the summer after. She’d watched the man with the rod go over it going west in the autumn with his daughter and a tree in a pot. She knew every rut in that road and every thorn on its verges.

She stood in the arch of the west gate with her sword on her shoulder and her breath going up white, and looked at the top of the hill, and couldn’t make her hands stop doing the thing they did before a fight.

“Nothing yet,” said Teague, beside her.

“I know.”

“Little one says dusk.”

“I know, Teague.”

Teague didn’t say anything else. He’d been a sergeant for twenty years; he knew when to stop talking to somebody who was holding a sword. He stood beside her in the arch with his bad arm tucked into his coat and his good hand on the gatepost, and looked at the hill too.

The gate was open.

That was the first thing that was wrong. Rhea had served at Cinderhold four years, and the west gate had never once been open after dusk in all that time, and it was open now, wide, both leaves, with the bar standing against the wall inside and the road going out under the arch into the blue snow-light. Because a tree person with bells on his antlers had asked her to, at midnight, and she’d asked Aldous, and Aldous had looked at her over his book for a long time and said yes.

The young commander from Solmere had not said yes. He’d said a great many things, at length, in the commander’s room, about regulations and the Redmarch and what the king would say. And then Aldous had said, mildly, the king opened a door in the autumn that had been shut for three hundred years, for a reason, Commander. I think he’d understand a gate. And the commander had gone red and stopped.

So the gate was open. And Rhea was in it. Because it was her gate, and whoever came over that hill was going to have to ask her to come in. She’d said so. She’d meant it.

She’d also, she was beginning to suspect, been an idiot.

They’d all come.

She hadn’t asked them. She’d told Aldous, and Aldous had told Group 3, and Group 3 had told the fort without telling anybody, the way things got told at Cinderhold, and by an hour before dusk the whole of the west wall was full. The old ones first. Twenty-five of them, in a line along the parapet north of the gate, the way they’d stood in the autumn for the man with the rod. Old Ansel at the end of them, eighty, wrapped in three blankets, who’d been asleep in the north-west tower and been woken by a recruit and walked the whole length of the wall on his own to stand there. Then the young ones, along from them. The recruits from Ashby, sixty of them, in their new grey coats, very quiet, not knowing why.

And in the arch behind her, Group 3.

Rhea didn’t turn round to look at them. She didn’t need to. She’d known them two years and a half. She could hear Maigo’s bells, muffled in Nell’s scarf, going very slightly because he couldn’t keep still. Salty’s breathing, slow and heavy. Wendy’s bow, creaking, as she shifted her grip. Niko, who made no sound at all, which was how you knew he was there. And Aldous at the back with his book, praying under his breath, the old words, low.

And Yan.

He was a pace behind her left shoulder. With the scythe. And the white snake.

She didn’t turn round to look at him either. But she could feel him there. Everybody at the fort could, these days, if he was close: a kind of quiet, like the quiet in a chapel. Since the green. Since the night on the fallen tree. And the snake round his neck had been looking at the top of the hill since noon.

“She says soon,” said Yan, behind her. Quietly.

“You keep saying that.”

“She keeps saying it.”

Rhea didn’t say anything. She shifted her sword on her shoulder and looked at the hill.

They came over it as the light went.

Rhea saw them first. She’d have been ashamed if she hadn’t; she was on the gate. Three shapes, at the top of the road, against the last grey of the sky over the downs. Standing. Very still. As if they’d come to the top of the hill and seen the fort below them and stopped to look at it the way you’d stop to look at a view.

Then walking. Down the long slope. Toward the gate.

Not fast. Not slow. The way people walk who’ve been walking a long time and are going to arrive whether anyone likes it or not. One in front. Two behind, a little apart. All three in long coats the colour of lichen on a wall, grey and green at once, with hoods up, and pale staves in their hands, as tall as they were. No packs that Rhea could see. No horses. No cart. No lantern.

No swords.

She looked for the swords. She’d been looking for swords on people coming down roads for fourteen years. She knew where they’d be: hip, back, under a cloak, in a pack. There weren’t any. Three people walking down a road onto the edge of the Redmarch, in winter, at dusk, with nothing but sticks.

And tall.

She hadn’t understood how tall until they were halfway down the hill. Taller than Yan. Taller than anyone she’d ever seen except a man in a song with eyes like coals who didn’t exist. Not big, the way Salty was big; thin. Long. Like birches. The one in front walked with the stave in one hand and the other hand loose at her side, and Rhea knew, the way you know things about people you’ve just met in a fight, that it was a woman, and that she was old, and that she wasn’t frightened of anything in the world.

Behind Rhea, very quietly, Maigo made a sound.

“Look at the snow,” he whispered.

Rhea looked.

The road was white. The whole of it, from the gate to the top of the hill, a long smooth sheet of blue-white snow-light, unbroken, because nobody had come down it in three days. And the three of them were walking down it.

And not leaving tracks.

Not none. Rhea would swear to it, afterwards, to Teague, to anyone; not none. But hardly any. A faint mark, like the mark a bird leaves landing. Every step. As if the snow weren’t quite sure they were there.

“And the verges,” said Maigo. His voice had gone very small. “Look at the verges.”

Rhea couldn’t see the verges. It was too dark, and they were under the snow. But Maigo could. She heard him start to cry behind her, quietly, the way Maigo did when he was happy, and didn’t turn round.

They stopped at the gate.

Exactly at it. Exactly at the line in the road where the shadow of the arch began. As if somebody had drawn the line and told them where it was. The woman in front, and the two behind her, a pace back, either side. With their staves upright and their hoods up and their faces in shadow.

Rhea stood in the arch with her sword on her shoulder and looked at them.

She’d planned what she was going to say. She’d planned it all night. She was going to say who goes there, which was what you said, and then name yourselves, which was also what you said, and then she was going to look them up and down the way the old sergeant at Ashby had looked her up and down at sixteen, and say state your business at the king’s gate. And they’d state it. And she’d decide.

She opened her mouth.

The woman in front put back her hood.

And Rhea didn’t say anything at all.

She was old.

Rhea had known she would be. She’d known it from the way she walked. But knowing a thing and seeing it were different, and Rhea, who had seen a great many faces in fourteen years, most of them in fights, stood in the arch of the west gate with her mouth open and couldn’t make it close.

It wasn’t that the woman looked old. She didn’t. She looked forty, perhaps. Fifty, if you were being unkind. A long narrow face, very still, the skin pale and fine and faintly grey, like the bark of a young birch. Dark hair, nearly black, braided back from her face and down under her coat, very long. Ears.

Rhea looked at the ears.

Long. Longer than Yan’s. Not pointed the way Yan’s were pointed, up, like leaves; swept back, along the side of the head, fine and narrow, the way a hare’s ears lie flat when it runs. With small silver rings in them, three in each, going all the way up.

And her eyes.

That was where it was. The old. Pale, the colour of winter light through ice, and so still. Not cold. Rhea had seen cold eyes; she’d seen them on the red, on the bent grey things that came over at night. These weren’t cold. They were old the way a stone is old, or a hill. As if they’d watched a great many people stand in a great many gates and say name yourselves, and found it, every time, a little bit sweet.

The woman looked at Rhea.

At her face. Then at her sword. Then at her face again, at the scar across her scalp, pink, where a hunter had opened it on the red on the anniversary, a year and a half ago. Then at her hands on the hilt. Then at her feet.

She said something.

Rhea didn’t understand a word. It wasn’t the kingdom’s tongue. It was something low and soft and old, that went up and down like water over stones, with sounds in it Rhea didn’t have. It didn’t sound like a language people talked in. It sounded like a language people sang in.

Behind her, very slightly, somebody laughed.

Yan.

She’d never once in two years and a half heard Yan laugh at something he wasn’t supposed to. She turned her head. He had his hand over his mouth. His face did nothing, the way it always did nothing. But his shoulders.

“What,” said Rhea.

“Nothing,” said Yan, through his hand.

“What did she say.”

Yan took his hand away from his mouth.

“She said Gleaming,” he said. “She said young. She said very loud.” He paused. “I think it was a compliment.”

Rhea turned back to the woman in the road.

She understood, then. Not the words. The other thing. The summoned heard every language in the world as their own; she’d known that for two years and a half, the way you know the sky is up. Their windows did it. They heard the kingdom’s tongue as theirs, and the merchants’ tongues from the south, and the old words Aldous said over the dead. They’d hear this as theirs, too.

She didn’t. She stood in her own gate in her own country and was the only one in the arch who couldn’t understand what was being said about her.

She’d never once in her life been the one left out of a contest.

“Can you understand me?” said Rhea. Loudly. To the woman.

The woman looked at her for a long moment.

Then one of the two behind her stepped forward. The one on the left. A man, Rhea thought, though it was hard to tell; he was as tall as the woman and as thin, with the same birch-bark skin and the same long swept-back ears and his hood still up. Younger. Much younger, perhaps, if it meant anything. He looked at Rhea with his pale eyes and his mouth did something very slight at one corner.

“I understand you,” he said.

In the kingdom’s tongue. Badly. Slowly, with the words in the wrong places and an accent like a man who’d learned them off a trader in a market twenty years ago and not used them since. But the kingdom’s tongue.

“Good,” said Rhea. “Who goes there.”

“Three,” said the young one.

“Name yourselves.”

The old woman said something, softly, in the singing tongue. The young one listened, and turned back to Rhea.

“Names,” he said, carefully, “are not given at gates.”

Rhea looked at him.

“They are at mine,” she said.

There was a silence in the road.

Behind her, she heard Salty make a sound that might have been a laugh and might have been a prayer. She heard the old ones on the wall go very still. She heard Maigo stop crying.

The old woman looked at Rhea for a long moment with her winter eyes. Then she said something to the young one. Short. And the young one smiled. Not much. Properly, this time, all the way across his long narrow face, like a crack of light under a door.

“She says,” said the young one, slowly, in the bad kingdom tongue, “you are very loud.”

“I heard.”

“She says it is good. She says a gate should be loud.” He paused, and looked down at Rhea’s sword arm, and then at his own, which was long and thin and pale in its lichen sleeve and looked as if it might snap. “She says, for a name, what will you take?”

Rhea looked at him.

At the arm.

“Arm-wrestle me,” said Rhea.

They did it on a barrel.

There was always a barrel in the west gatehouse, for the watch to sit on. Rhea dragged it out into the arch, into the blue light, in front of the whole west wall and sixty recruits and twenty-five old soldiers and Group 3 and a chaplain who had stopped praying. And put her elbow on the head of it. And looked at the young one.

He looked at the barrel for a long time. As if he’d never seen one. Then he looked at the old woman, and she said something very short, and he put down his stave against the gatepost, very carefully, and pushed back his lichen sleeve.

His arm was thin. Rhea had arm-wrestled Salty every night for two years and lost by half a count most nights and won by half a count on some, and Salty’s arm was like the trunk of a small tree. She’d arm-wrestled a man with a rod for five weeks in the autumn and never once moved his arm a hair, and his had been like gripping a post driven into rock. This arm was like gripping a reed.

He put his elbow on the barrel. He took her hand.

His hand was cool. Not cold. The coolness of a stone in shade on a summer day.

“Teague,” said Rhea.

“Ready,” said Teague, who’d counted for her every night for four years. “Steady. Go.”

Rhea pushed.

She pushed the way she’d pushed every night for two years and a half. All of it. Her arm, her shoulder, her back, the whole of the fourth tier going down her arm into her hand in a sheen she could feel on her skin like heat. Gleaming. Salty said it was like being hit by a door. The man with the rod had said, once, very gravely, at the end of the fourth week, good.

The reed didn’t move.

She pushed harder. She felt the barrel creak under their elbows. She felt the old ones on the wall lean forward. She felt her own face go red and her teeth come together and the scar on her scalp go hot. The reed didn’t move. Not a hair. It was like the man with the rod again. Like pushing against something that wasn’t trying.

And then the young one smiled, and moved his hand an inch, quite gently, the way you’d close a door that was standing ajar.

And her knuckles were on the barrel.

Nobody said anything.

Rhea looked at her hand on the head of the barrel, under his. Then at his arm. Thin, pale, in its pushed-back lichen sleeve. Not a mark on it. Not a tremor. Then at his face. Smiling, very slightly, at one corner.

She’d lost to a reed.

She threw back her head and laughed.

She couldn’t help it. It came up out of her the way it always came up when she lost, which was rarely and always loud, and went out of the arch and up the wall and along the parapet so that the old ones jumped and one of the recruits dropped his spear. She sat on her heels in the snow by the barrel in the west gate of Cinderhold and laughed until her eyes ran.

“AGAIN,” said Rhea.

The young one looked at her.

He didn’t understand. She could see him not understand. Then he looked past her, at Salty, in the arch, and Salty said, very solemnly, in the kingdom’s tongue, “She means tomorrow. She always means tomorrow.”

The young one thought about that.

“Tomorrow,” he agreed.

And then, still holding her hand on the barrel, he said, in the bad kingdom tongue, slowly, so she’d get it:

“Oen.”

Rhea stopped laughing.

“Oen,” he said again. And nodded at the old woman. “Tavrin.” And at the third, who hadn’t moved, or spoken, or put back her hood, at the right of the road, with her stave. “Liraun.” He let go of Rhea’s hand. “For the loud one. Because she asked. At her gate.”

Rhea got up off her heels.

She looked at the three of them in the road, at the edge of the arch’s shadow, in the blue light. Oen, smiling at one corner. Tavrin, old as a hill, with her winter eyes. And Liraun, still hooded, at the right, with her face in shadow and her stave very still.

“Rhea,” said Rhea. “Of Ashby. Gleaming. Very loud.” She stepped back out of the middle of the arch. Not all the way. Half a pace. To the side. Leaving the road clear. “You can come in.”

They didn’t come in.

That was the next thing that was wrong. Rhea had stepped aside, and the road was clear, and the gate of Cinderhold was open in front of them, and they didn’t move. They stood at the edge of the shadow, the three of them, and Tavrin looked past Rhea into the arch.

At Group 3.

And said something, in the singing tongue, very softly.

“She says,” said Oen, “we have come for the elf and the tree.”

Rhea felt it go along the wall behind her. The old ones. The recruits. The elf and the tree. Every one of them had heard the song. The red-haired boy from Pellow’s Cross had sung it in the long hall every night for ten weeks, and the recruits had learned it, and the old ones had pretended not to and learned it too. The summoned brought an elf with ears like leaves, and a treant who wore the forest in his sleeves.

Rhea turned round.

Yan had stepped forward. Out of the arch. Past her. Into the blue light at the edge of the shadow, with the scythe on his shoulder and the white snake round his neck and his long silver hair down his back. Very tall. Not as tall as them. He stood in the road in front of three people who were taller than anyone Rhea had ever seen, with his calm half-lidded face doing nothing at all.

And his ears. Long, and pointed, going up through his hair like leaves.

Tavrin looked at them.

She looked at them for a long time. Then she came forward. One step. Over the line of the shadow, into the arch, for the first time. And put up one long thin hand, very slowly, the way you’d put your hand up to a deer, and touched the tip of Yan’s left ear with one finger.

Yan didn’t move.

Tavrin took her hand away.

She said something. Rhea didn’t understand it. But she saw Yan understand it, and she saw his face, which never did anything, do something very small at the corner of the mouth.

“What did she say?” said Rhea.

“She said a man,” said Yan. “She said the song was wrong. She said I’m a man made over in an elf’s shape. With a snake under it.” He was quiet a moment. “She said it’s a very good drawing.”

The tree came next.

Maigo had been behind Salty. Rhea hadn’t seen him come out. He was just there, suddenly, in the road at the edge of the arch, small and brown and hooded, with his bells ringing because he couldn’t keep still and half his collar leaves gone and his root foot bare in the snow. Looking up. All the way up. At three people more than twice his height.

Tavrin looked down at him.

And then she did something Rhea would remember for the rest of her life. She went down. Not kneeling. Crouching, the whole long length of her folding down in the road in her lichen coat like a heron folding its legs, until her face was level with Maigo’s. And the other two came forward, Oen and the one called Liraun, and crouched beside her. Three tall grey people, crouched in the snow in the west gate of Cinderhold, at eye level with a small tree person in a hood.

The third one put back her hood.

She was young. Younger than Oen. A girl, almost, with the same birch skin and the same long ears and the same winter eyes, and her hair not dark but pale, nearly white, and loose. She looked at Maigo for a long time. Then she put out her hand, very slowly, and laid it flat on one of his antlers. On the bare winter branch of it, where the leaves would come in the spring.

Maigo held perfectly still.

Liraun closed her eyes. She stayed like that for a long moment, with her hand on his antler, as if she were listening to something a very long way off. Then she opened them, and took her hand away, and said something. Soft. In the singing tongue. To Maigo. Not to anyone else.

Maigo started to cry.

“What did she say?” said Rhea. She didn’t mean to. It came out of her.

Maigo couldn’t answer. He was crying too hard. It was Wendy who said it, from the arch, in a voice Rhea had never heard her use.

“She said you’re not one of the old kind,” said Wendy. “She said the old kind are asleep. A long time. She said you’re something new.” Wendy swallowed. “She said a tree that walked out of a song. And she said we’re glad.”

The snake moved last.

Rhea saw it. Everybody saw it. Three tall grey people crouched in the snow at the edge of the arch, in front of a small weeping tree person, with the young one’s hand still lifted from his antler. And a pace beyond them, standing in the road, a tall young man with silver hair and a scythe on his shoulder and a white snake round his neck.

And the white snake lifted her head.

Slowly. Off Yan’s collarbone, where she’d lain since noon with her face turned south. Not south now. Down. At the three crouched in the road. Her two green eyes open, very bright in the blue light, like two small lamps.

Tavrin looked up.

Rhea saw her see the snake. She saw it go through her. Not her face; her face didn’t move. The whole of her, crouched in the road. The way the man with the rod had gone through the old ones on the wall in the autumn when he looked up. The way a bell goes through a tower.

Tavrin went still. All the way. Every part of her.

So did the other two.

And then, without a word to each other, without looking at each other, all three of them, at once, the way water goes down a slope, went from crouching to kneeling. In the snow. In the road. In the west gate of Cinderhold, in front of the whole west wall. And bowed their heads. All the way. Until their long dark and pale hair was on the snow and their foreheads were nearly on it too, and their staves lay flat in the road beside them, and their long hands were open on their knees.

To the snake.

Nobody on the wall said anything. Nobody in the arch moved. Rhea stood with her sword in her hand and felt every hair on her arms stand up under her mail.

Then Tavrin spoke. Very low. Into the snow. Without lifting her head.

One word.

Rhea didn’t know it. Nobody on the wall knew it. Nobody in the kingdom knew that tongue; it was older than the kingdom, older than the church. She heard it go out of the arch, soft, singing, the shape of it like a breath let go.

And behind her, at the back of the arch, Aldous dropped his book.

She heard it hit the stones. She turned round.

The old chaplain was standing at the back of the gatehouse with his hands open in front of him and his book on the flagstones at his feet, and his face. Rhea had known Aldous four years. She’d seen him on the red, burned out, on his knees, with his lips moving and no sound coming. She’d seen him at the grave, saying the old words over a girl and a stone in the moonlight. She’d seen him make his two-fingered sign at a white snake round a young man’s throat on the first day and every day after for a month.

She’d never seen his face do this.

“I know that word,” said Aldous. To nobody. In a voice that had gone very thin. “I know that word. I’ve said it over eleven hundred people.”

He looked at the three kneeling in the snow. At the white snake, lifting her head above them, very still, with her green eyes on them.

“It’s in the rite,” said Aldous. “The last line. The one nobody understands.” His hands were shaking. “We lay white over them, for the—” He stopped. He couldn’t say it. “That’s the word. The one I’ve always said as Soul Eater. Because that’s what my master said it meant, and his master.” He looked at the kneeling woman. “She just said it. To the snake. As if it were a name.”

In the road, Yan had gone very still.

Rhea saw him. Standing over the three of them, with the scythe on his shoulder and the white snake’s head lifted beside his face. Listening. The way he listened now, when the snake was talking. His face doing nothing.

Then something. Very small. At the corner of the mouth. Not a smile.

“Yan,” said Rhea. “What’s she saying?”

He didn’t answer for a long moment.

“She says,” said Yan, slowly, “tell them to get up.”

He looked down at the three kneeling in the snow.

“She says tell them,” said Yan, “that he’s mine.”
