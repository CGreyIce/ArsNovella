The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 9: The Other Front

Chapter 254

First Ice

4,677 words · 20 min read

Bram talked.

He’d always talked. He’d talked as a boy in a militia coat too big for him on the walls of Harrowby, and as a young man on the border with the Redmarch at his back, and as a sergeant, and as an old soldier sent to guard five girls from another world in a lake town where nothing ever happened. He talked in the yard and at table and on the road; he’d told the whole story of the Banner at the Lists to a hen, once, when there was nobody else, and Maren had been known to leave rooms. In the first month he’d told the five of them the legend of the Black Blade round the militia-hall fire, all of it, the ditch and the forty and the night, and got half of it wrong, and been very pleased with himself.

He’d got it wrong because he hadn’t been there. Nobody who told it had been there. He knew that now. He’d sat on a stool on the ice beside the man who had, every afternoon for nine weeks, and not said a word.

That had been the strangest winter of his life. He’d liked it more than any winter he could remember.

The column was on the fourth page of Higu’s book now.

She’d made it for him in the spring, after the ruler. BRAM · REED · INCHES AT THE TOP, in her small fierce letters, which he couldn’t read, so she’d read it to him; and under it, every morning, the number. How far over the reed went at the top, measured with the clerk’s ruler, before it came back. Two. Two and a half. One and a half, the morning he’d had a cold. Two. Three, in the first week of summer, and he’d sat down on the shingle and laughed out loud and frightened a heron.

Three and a half. Four.

And then four.

And four, and four, and four. All of the late summer, while Ars was east with the Kingsferry boys and the Duskhollow lot and the girl; while the stool at the end of the jetty sat empty, and his own beside it, because he didn’t fish alone; he’d tried, once, and it had felt like talking in an empty church. Every morning he went down to the reeds at the far end of the shore at first light and stood, and waited, and cut, a hand short of the nearest stem; and the sheen went off the edge, and the reed went over, and came back. Four inches. Higu wrote it. 4. A whole page of fours.

So he waited harder.

He didn’t know what else to do. It was what he’d always done. When a thing wouldn’t move, you leaned on it. When a line wouldn’t hold, you held it harder. Thirty-six years with a sword and that was the only thing he’d ever really known: hold. He’d been told it by sergeants and captains and once, on the border, by a general. He’d told it to recruits. He’d told it to five girls from another world in the yard in the first year until Maren took him aside and said, Bram, they’re not a door.

So he waited harder. He stood at the reeds every morning and waited, the way you’d squeeze a fist; with his jaw set and his shoulders up round his ears and his whole body leaning into the waiting as if the waiting were a wall he could shove over if he only put his back into it. And cut.

Nothing. Not four. Not three. The sheen sat on the steel like lamp-oil on water, the way it had sat for twenty-five years, and didn’t go off the edge at all; and the reed stood there in the dawn and didn’t so much as sway.

0, Higu wrote. And looked at him over the top of the book, and didn’t say anything.

0. 0. 0.

He lost his temper on the eleventh morning.

He’d known it was coming. He’d felt it in himself for a week, the way you feel weather in an old wound. He’d lain awake at night in the militia hall going over the stroke, the stand, the lean, the wait, over and over, the way he’d gone over the battle at Harrowby for a year after, and found nothing wrong with any of it. He was doing it right. He’d done it right two hundred times. It had worked. And now it didn’t, and he didn’t know why, and the man who’d know was two hundred miles east on a red plain, and wouldn’t have told him anyway.

On the eleventh morning he stood at the reeds and waited, and cut, and nothing happened; and he cut again, and nothing; and the third time he didn’t stop.

He didn’t remember deciding. He remembered the sword going across, and across, and across, not a hand short, not anything short, straight into them, the whole bed of reeds at the end of the shore, forty feet of it, brown and green and very still in the dawn; and the stems going down in armfuls in front of the steel with a sound like somebody tearing linen, and the water slopping round his boots, and his breath coming out of him in a roar he hadn’t heard himself make since the border. He cut the whole bed down. Every reed. He waded in to the knee to get the last of them. He was fifty years old and he stood in the lake at dawn hacking at reeds like a boy with a stick in a nettle-patch, and he couldn’t stop.

And on the last stroke, the big one, the one with all of it in it, something in his right shoulder went with a sound he heard inside his own head, like a wet rope parting.

He stood in the water with the sword hanging from his hand.

He couldn’t lift it. He tried. The arm came up to his chest, and stopped, as if somebody had tied it there; and something hot and white ran down from the top of his shoulder to the elbow; and he stood in the lake with forty feet of cut reeds floating round his knees, and his sword-arm dead, and the sun coming up.

On the shingle behind him, somebody cleared her throat.

He turned round. Slowly. It hurt.

Odette. The mayor’s wife. In her good blue shawl, with a basket of eggs on her arm, on her way along the shore to the cabin to leave them on the step, the way she did twice a week whether anybody was home or not. She stood on the stones and looked at him, and at the reeds, and at the forty feet of empty water where the reed-bed had been.

She looked for a long time.

“That’s not Reaching,” said Odette. “That’s thatching.”

And went on along the shore with her eggs.

Six weeks, Mother Ide said. At the herb-shop on the square, with her thumbs in the top of his shoulder and her mouth pursed up like a drawstring purse. Six weeks with the arm bound, and no lifting, and no sword, and if he so much as picked up a bucket with it before she said so she’d bind the other one to match.

He bound it. He hid it under his coat at the quay, the morning the Widow went down the river with the eight who were going; he stood on the quay steps with the arm strapped to his chest inside his militia coat and his ruler in his belt and his face perfectly straight, and nobody said anything. Maren knew. Maren always knew. She didn’t say anything either. She’d had her own hands in linen to the wrist that week; they’d sat at the end of the long table together, the two of them, in the evenings, the old guide and the old guard, one with no hands and one with no arm, and not mentioned it, the way you don’t mention the weather to somebody standing in the rain.

And then Ars came home.

Over the last hill, with the girl, on a grey afternoon in the autumn; and the hen in Odette’s window saw him first. Bram heard about it at the hall. He didn’t go up to the cabin. He didn’t know what he’d say. He’d been waiting the whole late summer for the man to come home so he could ask him what he’d done wrong; and now the man was home, and Bram knew exactly what he’d done wrong, because he’d done it with his own sword in the lake at dawn with the mayor’s wife watching.

Two days after, he went down the lake road anyway. With his stool under his good arm.

Ars was at the end of the jetty. On the last plank. With his rod across his knees and a red hen against his hip, not fishing, mostly. The other stool was there beside his, where Bram had left it in the spring. Ars hadn’t moved it. Bram didn’t know if that meant anything. He thought it probably did.

He walked down the jetty. He put his stool where it went. He sat on it, carefully, with his bound arm against his chest under his coat.

Ars didn’t look up.

After a while he reached down beside him without looking, and picked up a second rod that Bram hadn’t noticed lying on the planks, a short one, a boy’s rod, light, that could be held in one hand; and held it out sideways, still without looking, the way you’d pass a cup across a table.

Bram took it in his left hand.

They fished.

He couldn’t swing a sword. He couldn’t even hold one up. But he could sit on a stool with a short rod in his left hand and the line in the water, every afternoon, for five weeks, while his right shoulder knitted slowly back together under Mother Ide’s linen and the autumn went on round them.

And he found, about the third week, that he was learning something, and didn’t know what.

It was the rod. A one-handed rod, light, with a boy’s line. You couldn’t strike with it. Not the way he’d always struck, the way every fisherman in Wrenhaven struck, a big two-handed heave the moment you felt the fish, got you; you’d snap the rod, or the line, or your wrist. With one hand on a boy’s rod, when the fish took, all you could do was lean. Lean back. Not fast. Not hard. Just let your weight go back on your heels and the rod come up with it, slowly, and the line come tight, and the fish find out on its own that it was on.

He lost the first six. He struck from habit, one-handed, and the line went slack, and he swore, and beside him Ars didn’t look up.

The seventh, he leaned.

He didn’t think about it. He was tired, and his shoulder ached, and the light was going; and the line twitched and he didn’t strike, because he couldn’t be bothered, and he just let his weight go back on the stool a little, and the rod came up with him; and the fish was on. A perch. Small. Striped. Furious. He brought it in one-handed, slowly, leaning, and lifted it onto the plank, and sat there looking at it.

“Huh,” said Bram.

Ars, beside him, didn’t say anything. But his rod moved, very slightly, the way it moved when he was laughing and didn’t want anybody to know.

He thought about it for two weeks.

He’d learned something on the stool last winter. Wait. He’d been saying it to the girls ever since. Wait. Don’t push. Let it come. He’d thought that was the thing. He’d thought the waiting was the thing, and when the reed stopped going further he’d waited harder, because if waiting was the thing then more waiting would be more of the thing.

But it wasn’t the waiting. Not quite.

It was the lean.

He’d watched himself do it now, on the stool, a hundred times. You didn’t wait instead of moving. You didn’t strike after you’d waited. The waiting and the moving were the same thing; you let your weight go, and the rod came with it, and by the time you’d noticed you’d done anything the fish was already on. The lean got there before the strike did. There wasn’t a strike. There was only the lean, and then it was done.

He’d stood at the reeds all summer and waited, and then cut. Waited, then cut. Two things.

When he’d got four inches, it had been one thing. He hadn’t known that. He’d thought it was the waiting that did it. It had been the place where the waiting turned into the cut without a seam between them, like the place where the lake turned into the sky on a still morning, that you couldn’t point to.

And then he’d waited harder, and made it two things again. A wall of waiting, and a cut on the other side of it. No wonder nothing went over the top.

He didn’t tell anybody. He didn’t know if it was right. He didn’t know if it was anything. He fished, one-handed, every afternoon, leaning, and brought in perch and roach and once a pike the length of his forearm that took him twenty minutes and made Ars put his rod down and watch, which Ars never did.

In the sixth week Mother Ide took the linen off.

He could lift the arm to his shoulder. Not above it. Not yet. He couldn’t swing a sword; not a real stroke, not the long flat cut he’d made two hundred times at the reeds. Mother Ide said a month more for that, and if he tried it sooner she’d come down to the lake herself and push him in.

He’d worn the sword all six weeks, out of habit, on the wrong hip so his left hand could find it; he’d never once drawn it. He went down to the jetty that afternoon and took it off his hip for the first time and sat on the stool with it across his knees, in its scabbard, and the boy’s rod in his left hand, and fished.

That night it froze.

He knew in the morning, before he opened the shutters. The cold was a different cold. It came up through the floor of the militia hall instead of in at the windows. When he went out into the yard the water in the trough had a skin on it like the skin on boiled milk, and Gigichi was standing at the yard gate in her blanket, looking down at the lake with her face very still.

“It’s in,” said Gigichi. She always knew. She’d known the ice going out to the minute, last spring. “Just at the edges. Just the shallows. It’ll be gone by noon if the sun comes out.” She looked at the sky. “It won’t.”

The first ice.

He went down at noon. Not to the jetty. Ars was already on the shingle, at the far end of the shore, by the reeds, with the two stools. Bram didn’t know how he’d known; he hadn’t said anything about the reeds to Ars, not once, all autumn. But Ars was there, at the end of the shore where the stones gave out into the reed-bed, sitting on one stool with the other beside it, and a hole cut in the new ice in front of them with a hand-axe, just past the first of the reeds, where the ice was thick enough to stand a heron on and no thicker.

First ice, the fish come in under the reeds, Ars had said once, last winter, about the only thing he’d said all winter. They like the roof.

The reed-bed had grown back. Bram hadn’t noticed. He’d been on the jetty all autumn and hadn’t once come along the shore to look; he hadn’t wanted to see the forty feet of empty water. But it had grown back, the way reeds do, through the autumn, from the roots he hadn’t been able to reach; not so tall, not so many, green still at the bottom and brown at the top. And now the first ice had come in round them in the night, a skin over the shallows from the shingle out to the end of the bed, grey and clear and very thin, and the reeds stood up out of it, still, every one, like the masts of a hundred tiny ships frozen into a harbour.

He sat down on the other stool.

Ars handed him the boy’s rod. Without looking.

They fished.

He didn’t mean to do anything.

He’d think about that, afterwards. He’d tell it, of course; Bram told everything. He’d tell it at the militia hall that night and at the Bell the next and to Rowan on the mayor’s step and to a hen. And every time he told it he’d say, I didn’t mean to. Because he hadn’t.

It was the middle of the afternoon. The light was going grey and flat, the way it goes on the first afternoon of the ice, with no sun and no wind and the whole lake gone silent because the edges of it had stopped moving. He’d caught two roach. Ars had caught nothing and wasn’t trying. The red hen had come down the shore at some point and was sitting on Ars’s foot. Up the shingle, a little way back, Higu was sitting on a rock with her book on her knees and the ruler beside her, because Higu always seemed to know where to be, and had come down and stayed.

He had the rod in his left hand. His sword across his knees, in its scabbard, with his right hand resting on it. The way it had rested there every afternoon for a week.

The line twitched.

And he leaned.

Not with the rod. That was the thing. He’d been leaning with the rod for five weeks. He leaned the rod back, the way he’d learned, his weight going onto his heels on the stool, and the line came tight, and the fish was on, a roach, he felt it; and at the same moment, without deciding, without anything, his right wrist turned on the scabbard on his knee.

Just the wrist. Not the arm; he couldn’t lift the arm. A half-turn of the wrist, on the hilt, the way you’d turn a key. The sword came an inch out of the scabbard with it, the steel showing at the throat, catching the grey light. That was all. An inch. The lean in his whole body going back on the stool and the rod coming up and his wrist turning on the hilt, all one thing, with no seam in it anywhere.

And the sheen came off the inch of steel.

He felt it go. He’d never felt it go before; he’d only ever seen it, at the end of a stroke, a finger, less, a silver breath. He felt it this time. It went out of the steel and out of him, along the line of the lean, low, over the ice, the way his weight was going: not where the blade pointed, because the blade didn’t point anywhere, it was an inch out of a scabbard on his knee. Where he was going. Where the lean went.

Past the end of the steel. A finger. A hand. More.

And a reed fell over.

Not bent. Not leaned on and come back. Fell.

He saw it go. One reed, standing up through the new ice a little way along the edge of the bed, to his right, past the hole; tall and brown at the top. It didn’t bow. It didn’t sway. Its head just tipped, all at once, sideways, the whole long stem of it, and went over, and lay down on the ice with a small dry whisper like a page turning, and stayed there.

Where it had stood, a stub stood up out of the ice. Cut. Clean. A hand above the ice, the top of it pale and flat and new.

He sat on the stool with the roach on his line and his wrist on his sword and looked at it.

He didn’t say anything.

That was the thing they’d remember, afterwards, in the militia hall, when he told it. Not the reed. The reed was the thing he’d tell; but the thing they’d remember, Higu said, writing it down, was that Bram, who’d told the whole story of the Banner at the Lists to a hen, sat on a stool on the ice for as long as it takes a kettle to boil, looking at a cut reed, and didn’t say one single word.

The roach got off.

He didn’t notice.

Higu came down the shingle.

She didn’t hurry. She came down with the book under one arm and the ruler in her hand and walked out onto the shore ice, carefully, testing it, small and light; to the stub. She knelt down on the ice beside it. She looked at it for a long time, very close, the way she looked at everything, the way she’d looked at the frames on the hall step all summer. Then she laid the ruler on the ice. From the stub, back toward the stool. Toward where Bram’s sword lay an inch out of its scabbard on his knee.

She didn’t measure it in inches. She measured it the way the summoned did, the way Thea had told him in the spring that Ysolde did it at Valcrest, the way Petra’s went into a post on the gravel bar. She put her hand down flat on the ice beside the ruler, at the end of where his steel would have reached if it had been drawn and swung. And then the other hand after it. And looked.

“A hand and a half,” said Higu.

She wrote it in the book. On the fourth page, under the fours and the zeros. Not a number of inches. He watched her write it; he couldn’t read it, but he could see the shape of it, longer than a number, with a line under it. Two lines.

She came back up the ice and sat down on the shingle beside his stool, and didn’t say anything else.

Ars hadn’t looked up.

He was sitting on the other stool with the rod across his knees and the hen on his foot, looking at the hole in the ice. He hadn’t moved. He hadn’t turned his head when the reed fell or when Higu went out to it or when she said a hand and a half. He sat there in the grey afternoon with his line in the water and his face toward the lake, and didn’t look up.

Bram looked at him for a long time.

He knew what it meant. That was the thing. He’d sat beside the man for a winter and an autumn and he knew, now, what the not-looking meant. It meant: that’s yours. It meant: I’m not going to say a word about it, because if I say a word it’ll be mine. It meant, the girls had told him once, at the hall, laughing, here’s where it gets interesting; and then you found it.

He’d found it.

He looked at the stub, a hand above the new ice, pale and flat and clean.

A lean that gets there before the steel.

He’d been Gleaming since he was twenty-four. Everybody said you stopped. He’d stopped. Twenty-five years. And then a stool, and a winter, and a reed that bent, and a page of fours, and forty feet of thatching, and six weeks with a boy’s rod; and now this.

“Reaching,” said Bram.

Out loud. Very quietly. To the lake. The first word he’d said since the reed fell.

Nobody answered him. Higu didn’t. Ars didn’t. The hen didn’t.

But beside him, on the other stool, very slightly, the way it moved when he was laughing and didn’t want anybody to know, Ars’s rod moved.

He told it that night at the militia hall.

All of it. Twice. The first time to all of them at once, round the long table: Thea and Higu and Xola and Gigichi and PenguChi, and Maren at the end with her hands out of the linen at last and pink and new. He told the reeds and the fours and the zeros. He told the thatching, and did Odette’s voice, and Xola laughed so hard she had to go and lie down on the bench. He told the shoulder and the boy’s rod and the perch and the pike. He told the first ice and the stools and the reed falling over with a sound like a page turning, and he told it wrong, because he’d been there and still didn’t quite know what had happened. And at the end he told them he hadn’t said a word for as long as a kettle takes to boil, and they didn’t believe him, and Higu showed them the book.

The second time was just to Maren. Late. When the others had gone up.

She listened to all of it again. She didn’t leave the room.

When he’d finished, she sat for a while at the end of the table with her new pink hands flat on the wood, looking at them.

“You made it two things,” said Maren. “In the summer. When it stopped. The waiting and the cut. You made a wall of the waiting and then tried to cut over the top of it.”

“Yes.”

“And it only went when it was one thing again.”

“When I couldn’t lift my arm,” said Bram. “Yes. When all I could do was lean.”

Maren was quiet for a long time.

“I pushed at a whole room,” she said, at last. “In the summer. With my hands. Thea won’t tell me what I did wrong.” She turned her hands over on the table, palm up, and looked at the new skin. “I felt her cut, on the cord. Like a needle. The smallest thing I’ve ever felt, in exactly one place.” She closed her hands. “And you’re telling me it was a wrist. On a stool.”

“A half-turn,” said Bram. “Like a key.”

Maren nodded slowly.

“I’ll be at this a long time yet,” she said. “I’m not a fast woman, Bram.”

“I know,” said Bram. “I’ve been at mine twenty-five years.” He thought about it. “Takes as long as it takes. Like the hole.”

Maren almost smiled. She got up, slowly, and went to the door. And then stopped, with her hand on the frame, and said without turning round, in her small exact voice:

“Congratulations, Bram.”

And went up.

He sat at the table alone for a long time afterwards, with his sword across his knees and his right arm resting on it, aching, and the fire going down. He didn’t talk to anybody. There was nobody to talk to. He didn’t mind.

Tomorrow he’d go down to the reeds at first light, the way he had every morning since the ice went out in the spring. Not to cut. He couldn’t swing yet; Mother Ide would push him in. He’d sit on a stool at the edge of the bed with his sword across his knees and his wrist on the hilt, and lean, and see what fell over.

And in the afternoon he’d go down the jetty with his stool under his good arm, and sit beside the man on the last plank, and fish. And not say anything. All afternoon.

He was getting quite good at it.
