The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 7: Myths

Chapter 196

The Pass Opens

5,708 words · 25 min read

On Earth, Hyvred had seen the world through a hole.

A small one. About the size of a coin held at arm’s length. Everything else had been dark, or nearly, a grey smear round the edge of the hole where shapes moved and you guessed. She’d learned to guess very well. She’d learned to turn her head instead of her eyes, and to listen for doors, and to find a face in a crowd by the sound of it laughing. She’d made a clip once, for her stream, to show people what it was like: a black screen with a little circle of light in the middle, going round a room. It had done very well. A man in her chat had said it looked exactly like walking round with a flashlight, and laughed, and then found out it wasn’t a joke, and apologised eleven times. Then, the next stream, still sorry.

She’d laughed for a week. She’d never told him it was the nicest thing anyone had said about it.

Then she’d woken on the floor of a hall in another world and seen the ceiling. All of it. Every beam, at once, from one end to the other, with the light coming down through high windows and dust going round in it, gold. She’d lain there on her back on the cold stone and cried, and Bee had thought she was hurt.

She saw everything now. That was the joke. Everything, all the time, from the top of a tower eleven miles. Faces, and the light inside faces, and the threads between people, and the shapes of things a long way off that nobody else could see at all.

And the thing she most needed to see was the only thing in the world that wasn’t there.

She went up the north tower at the grey, the morning the snow went off the valley.

She went up every morning. She had since the autumn. Kei came with her, most days, with Hisui round his neck, because Hisui liked the air at the top and because, Kei said, somebody ought to. Bee came sometimes. Hild came sometimes and sat at the bottom of the last flight with her one eye shut, listening. Tiger never came. Tiger had never once come up the tower. Hyvred had stopped asking.

The tower was a hundred and twelve steps, and the top was a ring of stone with a wooden roof on posts and the wind going through it from every side, and from it you could see the whole of the valley north of Thornwall, all the way to the foot of the Greyspine. Eleven miles. Fenwick had measured it, the first winter, with chains and stakes, and written it down in a report to the capital that the capital had sent back asking why.

Hyvred put her glasses up into her hair. She didn’t need them. She never had, here. She wore them because they’d been on her face in the drawing, and because pushing them up into her hair was the thing she did before she looked at something properly, and she’d found she couldn’t look properly without doing it.

Then she let her sight go out.

The gold eye. The left one, with the red mark in it. She felt it lock, the way it always did, a little click behind her eye like a door shutting. And the valley came up toward her, all of it, eleven miles, sharp as the inside of a cut.

The snow was going.

It had been going for a week. She’d watched it. It went off the high ground first, the shoulders of the hills, in long dark stripes. Then the valley floor, slowly, from the edges in, so that every morning there was more brown and less white, and water everywhere, running. Little silver threads of it coming down off the hills. Pools in the hollows. The whole valley wet and dark and steaming faintly in the first sun, the way a horse steams when you take the blanket off.

And in the middle of it, the rings.

Thirteen of them. Round the circle burned into the valley floor, the size of a fort, black and glassy, that the snow had never once lain on all winter. People. Standing. Hundreds of them, in thirteen rings, one inside the other, all facing the middle. They’d stood in the snow to their knees in the first weeks and then to their waists, and they’d never moved, and now the snow was going off them and they were standing in water. Meltwater, ankle-deep, brown, running between their feet toward the low end of the valley. None of them looked down at it.

She found the green coat, in the outermost ring. The hat man. The old woman in the widow’s shawl with her stick. A boy in a cap.

She found the third ring. She found the freckled face in it, very young, turned up toward the pass.

Ossie. Still there. Still turned up. She checked every morning. She always would.

Then she made herself look at the middle.

The absence hadn’t moved.

It was still there, at the near end of the valley, at the foot of the pass, where it had been since the autumn. Facing out. Not looking; Hyvred had said that in the autumn and still didn’t know a better way to say it. Facing. The way a door faces a road. The rings at its back, the pass in front of it. Ready.

You couldn’t see it. That was the thing nobody understood, and she’d stopped trying to explain. It wasn’t dark. It wasn’t a shape. It was a place where her sight went, and found nothing, and came back. The size of a hill. Everything else in the valley had light in it, even the stones, even the water, faint and grey and ordinary, the light that everything had. The rings had light, wrong light, the light of a lamp with a hand inside it. And the absence had none. It was a hole. Not in the valley. In the seeing.

She’d spent her whole life on Earth looking through a hole. Now she spent her mornings looking at one.

The snow had fallen through it all winter. She’d told the channel. It falls through. She’d watched a flake go down into it and come out the bottom and land on the ground below as if nothing had been there. She’d stood on the tower afterwards with her hand over the gold eye for a long time.

This morning it was water.

The meltwater, running down the valley floor from the rings toward the pass, in a hundred little brown threads. Going into the absence. And coming out the other side. Not round it; through it. As if it were air. She watched one thread of it, a long glinting line of brown water, go in at the near edge of the place where her sight found nothing, and come out at the far edge, exactly where it would have come out if there’d been nothing there at all. Not slower. Not colder. Not changed.

“It tastes of nothing,” said Kei, beside her. Quietly. Not because he could see it. Because Hisui had said it, round his neck, the way Hisui said it every morning.

“I know,” said Hyvred.

Still, said Hisui. Kei said it for him. The only thing in the world that tastes of nothing. The water tastes of snow and iron and the hills. And then it goes through that, and comes out, and it still tastes of snow and iron and the hills. Kei paused. He says that’s the worst part. That it doesn’t even take anything.

Hyvred didn’t say anything.

She lifted the sight. Past the absence. Up. To the pass.

And stopped.

The Greyspine opened at midsummer.

Everybody knew that. Corvin had two years of maps that said so, in his small careful hand, with the dates. The pass road was the old one, paved, wide enough for armies, built by somebody nobody remembered for a reason nobody wanted to think about; and every year the snow lay on it from the first frost to midsummer, twenty feet deep in the high cut at the top, and nobody went up it and nothing came down. In the first summer they’d gone up it at midsummer with forty soldiers, and come back, and Hyvred had written in the channel all of us came back, and then, when somebody asked, not really. But I’m home.

Midsummer. Not before.

She looked at the pass road.

It was clear.

Not all of it. The peaks either side were still white, deep white, blinding in the low sun, and the high shoulders of the Greyspine were white all the way down to the tree line. But the road. The old paved road, going up the throat of the pass in its long slow switchbacks to the cut at the top. It was clear. Grey stone, wet, steaming a little. In a line. From the foot of the pass all the way up, as far as the scope would take her, to the cut. With the snow standing on either side of it in walls.

As if somebody had swept it.

“Kei,” said Hyvred.

Kei looked at her. He couldn’t see it. He couldn’t see anything past the first mile, not really. He looked at her face instead, the way he always did.

“The pass is open,” said Hyvred.

“It’s not midsummer.”

“I know.”

“It’s—“ Kei stopped. He was very good at stopping. “How open?”

“The road,” said Hyvred. She heard her own voice go thin and didn’t stop it. “Just the road. All the way up. The rest’s still white. It’s like somebody came down it with a broom.” She swallowed. “Weeks early. Months. It’s not even the end of the thaw.”

Round Kei’s neck, Hisui had gone very still.

Rain, said Hisui. Kei said it, slowly, after him. Like rain that hasn’t happened yet. Only it’s nearer. Kei paused. He says it’s like somebody’s said your name in the next room. And you’re not sure. And then they say it again.

The first one was that afternoon.

Corporal Dunn, on the north wall. Hyvred knew him. She knew all of them; she’d made a point of it. In the first spring, when the wrong ones started coming to the gate with borrowed faces, she’d gone round every soldier in Thornwall, all hundred and twenty of them, and looked at the light in each of them, and said you’re fine. Every one. It had taken four days. Corporal Dunn had been the forty-first, a square red-faced man from the south with a wife in Ashby he wrote to every week, and he’d laughed when she said it and said I know, miss, but it’s nice to hear.

He was standing on the north wall at the third hour with his spear grounded, looking at the pass. Not on watch. Just standing. And he’d stopped talking to the man beside him in the middle of a sentence and lifted his face.

Smiling.

Hyvred saw it from the yard. Not the face. The other thing. The frost. A thin cold shimmer of wrong light round his head, like breath on a window, like the first frost on a pane in the morning, spreading. She’d seen it on Drew in the autumn, at a supper table, and it had been the worst moment of her life in two worlds, and she’d seen it every night since in her sleep.

She was already running.

So was Kei. She didn’t know how he knew. He always knew. He came out of the kitchen door at a run with the satchel already on his shoulder, the one with the flasks in it, the green thread round each neck. He went up the wall stair two at a time. Hyvred got there first; she was faster; she’d always been faster. She got to the corporal and took his arm, and he turned his face to her, smiling, and his eyes were somewhere else.

“It’s all right,” said Corporal Dunn. Very gently. “Somebody’s asking. It’s all right, miss. I’m only going to see.”

“No,” said Hyvred. “No, you’re not.”

Kei got there. He had the flask out. He put it in the corporal’s hand and closed the man’s fingers round it and held them closed with his own, and said, very quietly, the way he said everything, “Drink this. Please. It’s for you.”

The corporal looked at the flask. At Kei.

He drank it.

Hyvred watched the frost go. Not all at once. From the edges in. The way the snow went off the valley. And the man’s eyes came back from wherever they’d been, slowly, and found her face, and his smile went, and something else came instead. Confusion. Then fright. Then, very slowly, a kind of grief, as if he’d been woken out of the best dream of his life.

“I was going home,” said Corporal Dunn. “Miss. I was— it was asking me in. Somebody was standing in the door, asking me in out of the cold. It was so kind.” He was holding the empty flask in both hands. “I was going home.”

“I know,” said Kei. “I know. I’m sorry.”

Hyvred stood on the wall with her hand on the man’s arm and looked past him, north, at the pass. At the clear grey road going up it in the sun, swept.

Twelve, she thought. Twelve left.

Kei had thirteen doses at the start of the thaw.

Twelve he’d made in the autumn, one every other day, after Drew. Enough, written into each one with his finger, in the air, in English, over the cup. And one more, at midwinter, the thirteenth, because Drew had sat on his stool by the door and asked him to. Thirteen flasks on the top shelf of the little room behind the kitchen, each with a green thread round its neck.

On the second day there were twelve.

On the third day, a washerwoman. Mother Ivey, from the laundry by the south gate, who’d come to the well at noon with her basket and put it down and walked away from it, north, through the yard, toward the postern, smiling, with her hands open at her sides. Bee’s chalk dog on the laundry wall barked. The one Bee had drawn there in the first winter, a running dog in one line, that had never done anything since. It came off the wall. It ran across the yard in front of Mother Ivey and sat down in her way and barked, and barked, and she stopped, and looked down at it, puzzled, the way you’d look at a dog in a dream. It gave Kei long enough. Eleven.

On the fourth day, a boy. Joss, the farrier’s apprentice, eleven years old, who held the horses for Tiger every morning and asked him about tigers. And a groom, at the same hour, in a different stable, on the other side of the fort. Two at once. Hyvred saw them both from the tower, two little frosts of wrong light, one at either end of Thornwall, and screamed down the stair, and Hild was there from nowhere, the way Hild was always from nowhere, and took the boy, and Drew took the groom. Drew didn’t hold him. Drew just stood in front of him, in the stable door, with his big bare hands open at his sides, and looked at him, and said something Hyvred didn’t hear. The groom stopped. Long enough. Nine.

Kei made one that night. It took him until dawn. He lay on his bed afterwards with his eyes shut and Hisui on his chest saying you look terrible and you look terrible and then, very quietly, please stop.

Ten.

On the fifth day, three.

She counted.

She couldn’t help it. She’d always counted. On Earth she’d counted steps, from the bus stop to the door, from the door to the stairs, so she’d know where she was in the dark at the edge of the hole. She counted here. It was what her sight was for. She stood on the tower at the grey and counted the rings, and counted the snow going off them, and counted the steps of the road up the pass that were clear. And then she went down into the yard and counted the people.

That was the new thing. That was the thing she hadn’t told anyone yet except Kei.

She could see it coming.

Not the frost. Before the frost. A day before, sometimes two. A very faint cold at the edge of a person’s light, like the first breath of wind before the rain. Like a window somebody had left open a crack in another room. You’d only see it if you were looking. You’d only know what it was if you’d seen it turn into the other thing, on Drew, at a supper table, and on Corporal Dunn, and on Mother Ivey, and on a boy called Joss who asked about tigers.

She’d seen it on Mother Ivey the morning before. At the well. She’d thought it was the cold.

She didn’t think that any more.

So every morning she came down off the tower and went round Thornwall with her glasses pushed up in her hair and her gold eye locked, the way she had in the first spring. Every soldier. Every washerwoman. Every groom and farrier and cook and child. Looking at the light in them. And at the edges of the light, for the open window.

And when she found it, she went to Kei, and told him the name. And Kei went to the top shelf.

She didn’t tell them you’re fine any more.

“You have to stop counting at night,” said Hild.

It was the sixth night. Hyvred was on the north wall. She’d been on the north wall since supper, which she hadn’t eaten, watching the pass in the dark. You could see it in the dark, if you were her. The road, swept, grey, going up. Faintly. The peaks white on either side. And the absence at the bottom of it, the hole in the seeing, facing it, ready.

She hadn’t heard Hild come up. Nobody ever did.

“I’m not counting,” said Hyvred.

“You’re counting,” said Hild. She sat down on the parapet beside her, with her back to the drop, the way nobody else did. She was old, and lean, and brown, with grey hair cropped close, and one eye, and a sword she’d had longer than Hyvred had been alive. She was the sixth tier. She’d told Hyvred once, in the first winter, that she fought with her ears and the eye was a courtesy. “I can hear you counting. You breathe in on the number.” She tilted her head. “Nine.”

Hyvred stared at her.

“Nine,” said Hild. “Since the thaw. Called. Including the boy.” She was quiet. “And Kei’s got six on the shelf. And he can make one a night, if it’ll come, and he doesn’t sleep, and it’s come twice.” She turned her one eye on Hyvred. “And you’ve got a list in your head of who’s next. And it’s longer than six.”

Hyvred didn’t say anything.

“How long?” said Hild.

“Nine names,” said Hyvred. Very quietly. “Tomorrow. Maybe the day after. Nine I can see. The window’s open on nine.” She swallowed. “There’ll be more by the morning. There always are.”

Hild didn’t say anything for a while.

Below them, in the yard, a light was on in the little room behind the kitchen. Kei’s. It had been on all night every night for a week.

“I used to count,” said Hild. “When I was your age. In the war. On the east line. Every night on the wall. How many of them, how many of us. I was very good at it.” She looked at the pass. “I stopped the night I lost my eye. Not because of the eye. Because the man who took it was the four hundred and twelfth, and I’d counted him, and it didn’t help.” She was quiet. “Counting tells you how bad it is. That’s all. It doesn’t tell you what to do.”

“Then what does?” said Hyvred.

It came out of her too loud. Too high. The way things came out of her when she’d been holding them a long time. She was hyper, everybody said; loud, upbeat, the one who made everybody else feel like they could do things. She’d been that for two years and a half. She’d been that for Bee, who couldn’t fly, and for Tiger, on a roof, and for Drew, at a supper table, and for a hundred and twenty soldiers in the first spring who’d laughed when she said you’re fine. She’d been that all week. She’d been that for Corporal Dunn and Mother Ivey and a boy called Joss.

“What does?” said Hyvred. “Because I can see it. I can see it coming, a day before, I can see whose window’s open, and I can’t do anything. I can tell Kei. That’s all. I can tell Kei a name and he goes to the shelf. And there’s six on the shelf, and nine names, and the road’s swept all the way to the top, and I don’t know who swept it, and I can’t see the thing at the bottom because it’s not there.” She was crying. She hadn’t noticed when she’d started. “I spent my whole life seeing through a hole. And now I can see everything. Everything. And the only thing that matters is the hole.”

Hild didn’t say anything.

She put her hand on Hyvred’s back. Between the shoulders. Flat. The way she’d done in the first summer, at the top of the pass, at midnight, when they’d looked down at the rings for the first time.

And left it there.

Corvin wrote to Hana on the seventh morning.

Hyvred watched him do it. In the map room, at the long table, with two years of maps under his hands and the cord that the cat girl had given him in Valcrest, a green one with a grey bead, round his wrist, and the strip of paper in front of him, and the blue pen. He was thirty-eight and the third tier and the most careful man Hyvred had ever met. He’d believed in the thing at the pass for two years before anyone else in the kingdom would. He’d drawn it, on every map, as a blank. He’d been right.

He wrote four lines. It took him an hour.

He showed her before he tied it, because she’d asked, though she couldn’t read his script and he couldn’t read hers. He read it to her.

“The pass is open,” said Corvin. “Weeks early. The road only. The call is stronger. Doses will not hold.” He looked at it. “That’s all it’ll carry. Four lines.”

“It’s enough,” said Hyvred.

“It’s not,” said Corvin. “But it’s true.”

He tied it on. He held the cord for a moment, with his eyes shut, the way they all did. Then he opened them.

The answer came at noon. He read it to her in the yard, by the well, where they’d stopped Mother Ivey. It was one line. In his script now; the cat girl’s hand, made into his.

“He’s two days out,” Corvin read. “He left at the thaw. He said spring. H.”

The king came up the valley road on the ninth evening, at dusk, with no banners.

Hyvred saw him first. She always saw things first; it was what she was for. She was on the tower, where she’d been every dusk that week, watching the pass with her glasses in her hair, and she felt something come up the south road behind her, from the downs, the way you feel somebody come into a room behind you. And she turned round, and let the sight go out, and there he was.

Eleven miles. Further. Coming up the long road from the south in the last of the light. A small party: twenty riders, maybe, and a cart, and no colours at all.

And in the middle of them, a light.

She’d seen it once before. In a hall, in the first week, on a dais, when she’d woken on a floor and seen a ceiling for the first time, and then, under the ceiling, a man in a plain coat with no crown, and the light in him. She’d thought it was the sun. She’d had to look away. Bee had said afterwards that the whole room had felt like being underwater.

It wasn’t like that now. It was held in. She could see him holding it in, the way you’d hold a door shut against a wind: a forge, with the door closed, and the glow coming out round the edges of the door. Enormous. Banked. Kept in, very carefully, so that the people riding beside him wouldn’t feel it, and the farms they passed wouldn’t feel it, and the children at the farm gates wouldn’t feel the air go thick and frightened. He was keeping it in for them. He’d been keeping it in, she thought, for days. For the whole road.

And beside him, very close, a second light. Smaller. Steady. Like a lamp somebody was reading by.

“Kestra,” said Hyvred, out loud, to nobody. And then, “Oh,” because she’d never once thought of the king’s guard as a person somebody could recognise by her light, and she just had.

She went down the hundred and twelve steps at a run.

They were at the south gate by the time she got there. All of them. Corvin, in his good coat, which he’d put on badly. Hild, from nowhere. Captain Brannoch, with his Redmarch scars and his hand on his sword hilt out of habit. Fenwick, with a report under his arm, because Fenwick always had a report under his arm. Bee, with chalk on her fingers. Drew, very calm, by the gatepost. Kei, grey in the face, who hadn’t slept in a week, with Hisui round his neck. And at the back, on the top of the gate, Tiger. Sitting on it. On his gate. The one he’d built at Valcrest for the Lists, two oak leaves, posts thick as a man, a fighting step; and then built again here, when they came home, because the old south gate had warped in the autumn rains. The only thing besides Ossie’s shed he’d ever built that hadn’t fallen down. He sat on the top of it with his legs over and his ears flat against the wind, and watched the road.

The riders came in under the arch. Plain coats. Mud to the knee. No banners.

The king got down off his horse. Slowly, the way big men get down off horses after nine days. And stood in the yard of Thornwall in the dusk with his gloves in one hand, and looked round at all of them.

Then at Corvin.

“I said spring,” said the king.

“You said spring, Majesty,” said Corvin.

“Is it?”

Corvin didn’t answer at once. He looked north. Over the wall. At the Greyspine, white on white in the last of the light, and the dark throat of the pass between the peaks, and the line going up it that you couldn’t see from here unless you were Hyvred.

“It’s spring here,” said Corvin. “It’s midsummer up there.” He looked back at the king. “On the road. Only on the road. Somebody’s opened it early.”

The king looked north for a long time.

Hyvred watched his light. She couldn’t help it. She watched it look at the pass, and she watched it not change, at all, by so much as a flicker, the way a forge doesn’t change when you shut the door on it. And she understood, standing there with her glasses in her hair and her tail going behind her because she couldn’t stop it, that he’d known. Not about the road. About this. That there’d be a road, one day, swept. That’s what you held it in for. So that when the road came, you were still standing.

“Show me,” said the king.

“From the tower,” said Hyvred. It came out of her before she could stop it. Everybody turned round. She felt her face go red to the horns. “Majesty. Sorry. I’m— I’ll tell you what I see. From the north tower. It’s a hundred and twelve steps. I can tell you all of it. The rings, and the road, and the—“ She stopped. “And the thing at the bottom. Which isn’t there.”

The king looked at her.

He looked at her for a long moment, the way he’d looked at all of them in the hall, on the first day, one face at a time, as if he were committing them to memory. At the golden horns. At the glasses in her hair. At the gold eye with the red mark in it, locked, because she’d forgotten to unlock it.

“You’re the one who sees,” said the king.

“Yes, Majesty.”

“In the morning,” said the king. “Show me in the morning. A hundred and twelve steps is too many in the dark for a man of my age who’s been on a horse for nine days.” He paused. “And you’ve not slept. I can see that from here, and I can’t see anything.”

Hyvred opened her mouth and shut it again.

“Tonight,” said the king. He turned. He looked past her, past Corvin, past Hild and Brannoch and Fenwick with his report. At the back of the yard, by the kitchen door. At a young man with a grey face and a green snake round his neck, who’d been standing there very quietly, trying not to be noticed, the way he always did.

“Tonight,” said the king, “I’d like to meet the one who makes the doses.”

Hyvred watched it through the door. She didn’t mean to. She was meant to be going to bed; the king had said so; Hild had said so, with a look. She’d got as far as the kitchen and stopped.

The little room behind the kitchen. The jars. The shelf, with five flasks on it now, each with a green thread round its neck. Kei, standing by the table in the middle of it, with his hands at his sides, not knowing what to do with them. And the king of Aurelan, in his muddy coat, in the doorway, filling it.

The king looked at the shelf for a long time.

Then he looked round. At the room. And at the stool by the door. The low three-legged one where people came and sat and told Kei things, and he listened, and didn’t fix anything, and was there the next day too.

The king sat down on it.

It was too small for him. Much too small. His knees came up nearly to his chest. He sat there anyway, on the stool by the door of a little room behind a kitchen at the edge of the kingdom, with his gloves in his hands, the ninth tier held in round him so carefully that the jars on the shelves didn’t even rattle.

“That’s Drew’s stool,” said Kei. Before he could stop himself. Then, “Majesty. Sorry. Majesty.”

“I’ll give it back,” said the king. “When I’ve done.” He looked up at Kei. “Tell me about the doses.”

So Kei told him.

Hyvred stood in the kitchen in the dark and listened. She didn’t need to see. She’d learned to listen, on Earth, at the edge of the hole. She listened to Kei tell the king of Aurelan about Enough. About a supper table in the autumn, and Drew’s face lifted to the shutters, and the frost. About the twelve, one every other day, and the thirteenth, at midwinter. About how each one took most of a day and most of what he had. About Corporal Dunn, and Mother Ivey, and Joss. About you don’t make medicine for a sickness. You make it for a person.

About five on the shelf. And nine names. And more by the morning.

The king listened. He didn’t interrupt. He didn’t say I see, or go on, or any of the things kings said. He sat on the stool with his knees up and his gloves in his hands and listened the way Kei listened, the way somebody had once listened to Kei on a call at two in the morning in another world, without fixing anything, until it was all said.

When it was all said, he didn’t say anything for a long time.

Then he got up off the stool. Slowly. And put it back exactly where it had been, by the door.

“I’ll be here tomorrow too,” said the king.

Kei made a sound. Hyvred heard it from the kitchen. A very small sound, like something coming loose.

The king went out past her in the dark, and didn’t look at her, and put his hand on her shoulder for a moment as he went. Very lightly. A big burned smith’s hand, with the ninth tier held in behind it so carefully it felt like any old man’s.

“Bed,” said the king. “Tower in the morning.”

“Yes, Majesty.”

She went to bed. She lay in the dark in the room she shared with Bee, with her glasses on the shelf and her eye unlocked at last, and listened to Bee breathing and the wind coming down off the Greyspine over the roofs. It tasted of nothing. She couldn’t taste it. Hisui could.

She didn’t count. She tried very hard not to. She lay there and thought about a stool by a door, and a man too big for it, and I’ll be here tomorrow too, and found that it was the first thing all week that had made the hole feel smaller.

She slept, a little.

In the morning, there were eleven names.
