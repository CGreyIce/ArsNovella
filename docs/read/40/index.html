The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 2: Letters

Chapter 40

Missing Letters

4,597 words · 20 min read

The first postcard Jared ever got from Ice wasn’t really a postcard, and it wasn’t really for him.

He’d posted a picture of himself eating chicken fingers at a Raising Cane’s in Texas, for no reason at all, the way you do. Within the hour Ice had turned it into a vintage holiday postcard: faded colours, a scalloped white border, GREETINGS FROM RAISING CANE’S in big looping fifties letters across the top, and Jared’s face in the middle, mid-bite, looking like a man who’d been caught. Ice hadn’t sent it to Jared. He’d sent it to Alice, with one line underneath.

Your friend is on holiday. He says he doesn’t miss you.

Alice had sent it straight to Jared, crying with laughter. Jared had sent back a picture of himself at a different Raising Cane’s, three weeks later, on purpose. Ice had made that into a postcard too. GREETINGS AGAIN. HE STILL DOESN’T MISS YOU.

It became a thing. It went on for years. By the end there were eleven of them, in a folder on Jared’s desktop called postcards that he never deleted and never opened, because opening it was too much.

He’d thought about them a lot, since he came here. More than he’d expected to. About a guy half the world away spending twenty minutes in an image editor, making a joke for two people he’d never met in person and never would, just because he could. Just because the distance was there and he wanted to reach across it.

That was what letters were, here. Postcards across the distance. The only thing they had.

Which was why, when Kestra came into the sitting room on a grey spring morning and said, “The tiger boy’s letter. What did it say?”, Jared went cold all the way down.

“What letter?” he said.

Kestra stopped in the doorway.

She didn’t say anything for a moment. She didn’t need to. Jared had been training with her every morning for ten months, and he’d learned to read her the way you learn to read weather: by small things. The set of her shoulders. The way she stood. Right now she was standing the way she stood in the yard just before she hit you.

“The Western Watch sends dispatches to the king,” she said. “Military riders. The court mage at Thornwall writes them. Fenwick.” She had a folded paper in her hand. “This one came in yesterday. Eight days from Thornwall.” She looked at the paper, then at Jared. “It says something walked up to their gate at dusk wearing the face of a soldier who went missing three weeks before. It says the dogs knew. It says the summoned girl with the bee wings drew a dog on the wall and the dog barked.” She paused. “And it says the boy with the tiger ears wrote it all down that night and sent it to all of you by the royal courier. Three days before this dispatch was written.”

Jared stood up.

“We didn’t get it,” he said.

“No,” said Kestra. “I thought not.”

On the other side of the room, Aleaka had put down her teacup very carefully. Brisk was on the back of her chair, hunched and furious the way he always was; at the sound of her breath catching he turned his head and glared at Kestra with one gold eye, as if it were her fault.

“We haven’t had anything from Thornwall in five weeks,” said Aleaka slowly. “I thought…” She swallowed. “I thought they’d stopped writing back. To me. I thought I’d asked too many questions. Who brought you to who. I thought they’d got tired of it.”

“They didn’t get tired,” said Jared.

He was already counting.

They laid it all out on the big table in the sitting room, the five of them and Oswin and Kestra. Every letter they’d received since the turn of the year, in date order, in seven piles. Cursiffe kept a list; of course he did. He’d been keeping it since the first week, in English, in a little book, every letter in and out, from who, to who, and the date.

It took an hour. When they were done, they sat and looked at it.

The piles weren’t empty. That was the strange thing. Letters were still arriving. Group 1 had written last week; Higu, a long happy rambling letter about the snow melting and Xola making a basin of water spin. Group 6 had written a fortnight ago. Group 4 a fortnight before that.

But there were holes.

Nothing from Thornwall in five weeks. Nothing from Valcrest in six. Nothing from Cinderhold in four. And the letters that had come, from Wrenhaven and Kingsferry and Duskhollow, were all late. Not very late. A few days. A week. Enough that Cursiffe’s dates didn’t line up: letters that should have taken four days on the road had taken nine, and letters that should have taken nine had taken fifteen.

“The roads,” said Oswin uncertainly. “The spring mud. The couriers—“

“The couriers carry the king’s post,” said Kestra flatly. “The king’s post isn’t late.”

“And Chris writes every week,” said Jared. “Every week, no matter what. I’ve had a letter from Chris every single week since the second month.” He tapped the empty space in the Valcrest pile. “Six weeks. Something’s happened in Valcrest and he hasn’t told us? Chris?”

Nobody said anything.

Then Milli, who’d been sitting with his arms folded and his tail very still, leaned forward and picked up one of the late letters. Deston’s, from Kingsferry. He turned it over and looked at the seal.

“Honey,” he said to Hanarisu. “Look at this.”

She leaned in. Jared leaned in with her.

They sealed their letters with wax. Ordinary red candle wax from the castle stores, and then, because Chris had suggested it in the second month and everyone thought it was funny, a thumbprint pressed into the wax while it was warm. Every group did it. You could tell whose letter it was by the thumb.

Deston’s thumbprint was there. Clear and neat.

And under it, at the very edge of the wax, if you held it up to the window and tilted it, there was a thin dark line. A seam. Where the wax had been lifted with something hot, very carefully, and laid back down again.

“Someone’s opening them,” said Milli quietly.

Hanarisu took the letter from him and looked at the seal for a long time. She didn’t say anything. She didn’t need to; Jared could see her face. Calm and pleasant and completely still, the way it went when she was angrier than she ever let anyone see.

“Not opening,” said Cursiffe, from the end of the table. He’d taken off his glasses and was rubbing his eyes. “Opening and reading. Or trying to.”

“Nobody can read them,” said Aleaka. “That’s the whole point. Nobody here can read English.”

“No,” said Cursiffe. “But they don’t know that until they try. And they don’t stop trying just because they can’t.” He put his glasses back on. “The castle scribes can’t copy my ink. Their eyes slide off it. I watched three of them try, in the winter. They tried for hours. They didn’t believe it.” He looked round at them all. “If somebody’s taking our letters to find someone who can read them, they’d keep them a while. A week. Two. Try every scholar they know. Then seal them back up and send them on, and hope nobody notices the dates.”

“And the ones that aren’t coming at all?” said Jared.

Cursiffe didn’t answer.

Kestra had been standing by the window the whole time, with her arms folded, saying nothing. Now she turned round.

“There was a report from Valcrest last week,” she said. “From Lucan. To the Advisor.” She paused. “A Vane household guard tried to kill the oni in the junior lists. A steel blade cased in a wooden one. The small loud girl shouted, and he dropped, and it missed.”

The room went completely silent.

“Ina,” said Jared. His voice came out wrong. “Ina?”

“He’s alive,” said Kestra. “Not a scratch. The guard will hang. Lord Vane’s son is said to be very upset.” Her mouth twisted, very slightly. “So you see why the oni’s friends might have written to you about it. And why somebody might have preferred that you didn’t hear.”

It took them two days to work out where.

Every royal courier route in the kingdom ran through the capital. That was how the post worked; it always had. The riders came in from the six roads with their satchels, and everything went to the Couriers’ Hall below the castle, a long stone building like a barn full of pigeonholes, where clerks sorted it by hand into the bags for the next six roads out. The summoned’s letters came in with everything else, in their own small bag with the Advisor’s seal on it, and went out again the same way.

Every letter between the groups passed through that one room. Even letters between Wrenhaven and Thornwall, which were nowhere near the capital, came here first and went out again.

“One room,” said Jared. “Twenty clerks. One of them’s been taking the bag.”

“Or more than one,” said Oswin.

“One,” said Kestra. “It’s always one. More than one, someone talks.”

The question was which.

Hanarisu could have found out in an afternoon. Jared knew it, and she knew it, and he could see her knowing it. Walk into the Couriers’ Hall with her pleasant face and her purple tail and her soft, smooth, reasonable voice, and stand in front of each clerk in turn, and let the thing in her chest come up, just a little, and ask. Any one of them would have told her anything. They’d have wanted to tell her. That was what it did.

She didn’t offer. Nobody asked her to. Jared had been there the night at the Council when she’d let it rise and pushed it back down again, and he’d been there in the corridor after the banquet when she’d told Kestra where her line was. I won’t use it on anyone who can’t tell it’s happening. He’d never once seen her step over it. He didn’t think he ever would.

So Cursiffe set a trap instead.

He did it at the table in the sitting room, in the evening, with the others watching.

It was a letter to nobody. Three pages of nothing, in English, about the weather and the food and a dream Cursiffe claimed to have had about a goose. He folded it in three, the way they always folded them, and addressed the outside in Oswin’s careful native hand: To the summoned at Duskhollow, by the Advisor’s leave. An ordinary letter to Group 6.

And then, instead of wax, he sealed it with ink.

“It doesn’t come off,” he said, as he worked. “My ink. On skin. I found that out in the third week. It lasts days. Soap doesn’t touch it. Scrubbing doesn’t touch it.” He dipped the pen in the little vial he wore round his neck, the dark blue one that had come through with him, and began to write across the fold of the letter, where the wax would usually go. Small, close, careful English, a single line, round and round in a tight spiral, like a coil of wire. “If anyone opens this, they’ll have to break the line. And when they break it…”

He finished the spiral. Sat back. Looked at it.

Then, very quietly, as if he were talking to himself, he said: “Stay.”

Jared felt it. He’d never felt magic before, not really; not like this. A little pressure in the air of the room, like a held breath. The ink on the fold of the letter seemed to sink, very slightly, into the paper. And for one second, just one, it shone: a dark gleam, blue-black, like the back of a beetle.

Then it was just ink.

Cursiffe sat looking at it. His hands were trembling.

“Did you just—“ said Milli.

“I think so.” Cursiffe’s voice was very calm, the way it always was. His hands weren’t. “I wrote it. Thea’s way. One line, in its language. Except my language is this.” He touched the edge of the spiral, very carefully, and took his finger away clean. “It knows who wrote it. It won’t stay on me.” He swallowed. “It’ll stay on anyone else.”

Nobody said anything for a moment.

“Well,” said Oswin faintly, “that’s going in my notes.”

The letter went into the bag the next morning with all the rest.

The day after that, Jared went down to the Couriers’ Hall with Kestra.

They had a reason. Kestra had made one up: a parcel for the king’s household that had gone astray in the winter, a pair of boots, very important, she wanted to speak to the senior clerk about it. The senior clerk was a fat anxious man called Prewitt, who went pale at the sight of the king’s colours and started apologising before she’d finished her first sentence. While he apologised, and found ledgers, and sent boys running for other ledgers, Jared stood behind Kestra with his hands loose at his sides, exactly where she’d taught him to stand. And looked at the room.

Twenty clerks. Twenty desks, under the long windows, with the pigeonholes rising up behind them to the roof. Bags coming in from the riders at one end. Bags going out at the other. Paper everywhere. Ink everywhere. Every clerk in the room had ink on their fingers.

Ordinary ink. Black and brown. Jared looked anyway. He looked at every pair of hands in the room, one after another, slowly, without seeming to. He’d always been good at that. In games, in lobbies, in a crowded room: noticing. Who was nervous. Who was lying. Who was standing a little too still.

Nineteen clerks, working.

One, at the third desk from the end, wearing gloves.

Indoors. In spring. Thin grey wool gloves, sorting letters with his fingers clumsy in them, dropping one, picking it up, dropping another. A young man, not much more than twenty, with a narrow face and a good coat that was much too good for a sorting clerk.

He’d seen the king’s colours come in. He hadn’t looked up since.

Jared touched Kestra’s elbow. Once. Lightly. The way you’d tap a teammate’s shoulder.

Kestra didn’t look round. She didn’t need to. She finished her sentence to Prewitt about the boots, and thanked him, and then she turned and walked down the long room between the desks, not hurrying, her boots very loud on the stone, until she was standing in front of the third desk from the end.

The young man in the gloves looked up at last. His face was the colour of paper.

“Take your gloves off,” said Kestra.

His name was Hollis. He was twenty-two. He had a mother in the lower town and a gambling debt in the upper one, and a little under a month ago a well-dressed man had come to his mother’s house in the evening and offered to make the debt go away.

He told them all of this in a small bare room at the back of the Couriers’ Hall, sitting on a stool with his hands flat on the table in front of him because Kestra had told him to put them there. They were blue-black to the second knuckle. Every finger. The ink had gone into the creases of his skin and the beds of his nails and up the side of one palm where he’d tried to wipe it off on his coat. He’d scrubbed them raw. It hadn’t made any difference.

Hanarisu was there. Jared hadn’t wanted her to come. Alice, you don’t have to, he’d said on the stairs, and she’d said I know, and come anyway, and now she stood by the door with her hands folded in front of her and her face perfectly pleasant. Cursiffe was beside her. Kestra sat across the table from Hollis. Jared stood behind Kestra.

Hollis wouldn’t stop crying.

He was lying, too. Jared could see it. Not about the debt, or his mother. About the man. He kept saying he didn’t know who the man was. A stranger. A gentleman. He’d never seen him before. He didn’t know where the letters went. He just put them in a satchel by the back door at night, and in the morning the satchel was gone, and a week later it came back with the letters in it, sealed up again, and he put them back in the bag.

“And the ones that didn’t come back?” said Kestra.

Hollis’s eyes went to the door. To Hanarisu. Then away.

Jared saw it happen. He was watching for it. He saw Hanarisu feel it: the boy on the stool, terrified, lying, so very easy to reach. He saw her hands, folded in front of her, go still. Her face didn’t change at all. But something in her lifted, very slightly, the way a cat’s ears lift at a sound. One step. One soft word. Please. The boy would have told her everything, and thanked her for it, and never known why.

And Jared saw her let it go.

It went back down. He couldn’t have said how he knew. Her hands unfolded. Her shoulders settled. She breathed out, long and slow, and when she spoke her voice was just her voice, calm and smooth and ordinary.

“Kestra,” she said. “Would you?”

Kestra didn’t look round. She was looking at Hollis’s hands.

“That ink,” she said. “Do you know what it is?”

Hollis shook his head.

“Neither do I.” Kestra leaned forward a little. “Nobody does. Nobody in this kingdom can read it. Nobody can copy it. Nobody can wash it off. It came out of another world, on a chain round that man’s neck.” She tilted her head at Cursiffe, without looking at him. “Every clerk in that hall saw your hands. By tonight, every courier on every road in the kingdom will know what blue fingers mean. You’ll never sort another letter as long as you live.” She paused. “Your mother will see them. Your debtors will see them. The gentleman who came to your mother’s house will see them. And he’ll know you were caught, and he’ll wonder what you said.”

Hollis had stopped crying. He was staring at her.

“So,” said Kestra, “you can tell me. And I’ll tell the king you told me. And the king’s guard will be standing outside your mother’s house tonight instead of someone else.” She sat back. “Or you can not. I’m not going to ask twice.”

She didn’t. She just waited.

It took about a minute.

“He said he was the steward,” Hollis whispered. “Of a household. A great one. He had a ring.” He swallowed. “With a heron on it.”

Jared didn’t know what that meant. But he saw Kestra’s face, and Hanarisu’s, and he didn’t need to.

The heron was on the gate of every house the Vanes owned.

They found the letters that afternoon.

Kestra went with six of the king’s guard and a magistrate’s warrant, to a narrow tall house in the Lantern Quarter that the steward kept for the household’s business. Jared went with her. He wasn’t supposed to. Kestra had looked at him on the steps of the castle and said nothing, and he’d taken that as a yes.

The steward wasn’t there. He was, it turned out, already on his way to the magistrate’s office, to report with deep distress that a junior member of the Vane household had been taking letters without his knowledge, and that Lord Vane had dismissed the man that very morning and was horrified, simply horrified, to learn of it.

“Of course he is,” said Kestra.

The house had been emptied in a hurry. But not well. In the back room on the second floor, under a loose board that one of the guards found by stamping, there was a long wooden box.

It was full of letters.

Dozens of them. More than Jared could count. Some of them opened and some still sealed, some scorched along one edge as if somebody had held them to a candle and changed their mind. Chris’s square careful handwriting. Yan’s big loose scrawl. Deston’s neat little letters. Wendy’s. Kei’s. Higu’s. Two of Aleaka’s, going out, to Thornwall; her questions. Who brought you to who?

And on top, the last one in, its seal broken and pressed down again badly: a letter in a hand Jared knew from the winter, all hurried capitals and crossings-out, with a smear of mud on the outside.

Tiger’s.

Jared stood in the back room of the narrow house in the Lantern Quarter, with the king’s guard tramping about downstairs, and read it.

It walked up to the gate at dusk. It had his face. It knew about the roof.

The dogs knew first.

It looked at Hyv and said they’d like to meet her. The people over the mountains. It said they were kind.

If anyone sees a soldier called Ossie, freckles, big feet, don’t open the door.

Jared read it twice. Then he folded it up, very carefully, and put it inside his coat, against his chest, the way you’d carry something that mattered.

On the table beside the box there was a sheaf of papers in the native script. A list of names. Scholars, merchants, a ship’s captain from the southern ports, an old woman in the river quarter who was said to know the tongues of the far islands. Each name had a line through it, and a note beside it, in a small neat hand.

Could not read.

Could not read.

Could not hold the page. Says the letters move.

Could not read.

At the bottom, a single line, underlined twice.

Keep looking.

Lord Vane was untouchable. Of course he was.

The steward was arrested. He said he’d acted alone, on his own initiative, out of concern for the kingdom’s safety and what the foreigners might be plotting in their secret script. Lord Vane wrote to the king, expressing his profound shame that a member of his household could have done such a thing. The king, Kestra said, read the letter twice, and then put it in the fire.

“It’s the same shape,” Jared said that night, in the sitting room, to nobody in particular. “Valcrest. Here. A servant did it. On his own. His lordship is horrified.” He shook his head. “Twice in a month.”

“Twice is a pattern,” said Kestra, from the window. “Patterns go to the king.”

“And then what?”

Kestra didn’t answer for a moment.

“And then he remembers,” she said. “He’s very good at remembering.”

The new system was Cursiffe’s idea, and it was very simple.

Every letter would have a number. Every group would keep a ledger, in English, of every letter it sent, to who, and when. Group 2 to Group 5, number one. Group 2 to Group 5, number two. If a number went missing, you’d know. If two arrived out of order, you’d know.

And every letter would be sealed in ink. His ink.

“Not wax,” said Cursiffe. “Strips.” He held one up: a narrow strip of paper, about the length of a finger, covered in a tight coil of small blue-black English. “You fold your letter, lay this across the fold, and fix it at both ends with a drop of wax. When it reaches you, you don’t break it. You slit the letter open along the other edge.” He turned it over in his fingers. “It can’t be lifted without breaking. It can’t be copied. Nobody in this kingdom can even look at it long enough to try. If it’s broken when it reaches you, someone’s been in it. And whoever broke it is walking round with blue hands.”

“How many of those can you make?” said Milli.

Cursiffe looked at the strip for a moment.

“I don’t know yet,” he said. “Let’s find out.”

He found out. He sat up that night at the table with a stack of paper and his little vial and wrote strips until his hand cramped, and then wrote more. Twenty. Forty. Sixty. Jared stayed up with him, cutting the paper into lengths, because he didn’t want to go to bed and he didn’t want Cursiffe to be alone. Around midnight Hanarisu came down in her nightgown and sat with them and cut paper too, without saying anything. A little after that Milli came and found her, and said “Honey, it’s one in the morning,” and she said “I know, honey,” and he sighed and sat down and started cutting paper as well.

At some point Aleaka came down with Brisk on her arm and made tea for everyone, and then fell asleep in the big chair by the fire with the hawk glaring over her like a guard.

By dawn there were two hundred strips. Cursiffe’s hand was black to the wrist, and shaking, and his window, when he finally opened it, said thirty-six. Up four, in a night.

They bundled the strips into seven packets, thirty for each group and the rest to spare, with a letter in each explaining how to use them. Numbered one. Kestra took them down to the Couriers’ Hall herself, and handed them not to the clerks but to a rider she knew from the king’s guard: a big quiet man called Aubrey, who would pick the riders for the summoned’s bags from now on, on every road, and see that no bag was ever out of the king’s guard’s sight.

There was one more letter.

Jared wrote it himself, in the grey light, after the others had gone to bed. To Xav, in Duskhollow, because of everyone outside the group, Xav was the one who’d understand without being told. And because Xav, in Group 6, had known Ice in person, had stood in the same room as him, had shaken his hand; and Jared never had, and never would, and sometimes, at four in the morning, that still felt like the unfairest thing in either world.

He wrote it all down. Hollis’s hands. The box under the floor. The list of names. Keep looking. He told Xav that Ina was alive, and that something had walked up to Thornwall’s gate wearing a dead man’s face. He folded Tiger’s letter inside his own, because Xav should have it, and he’d make sure the others got copies.

And then, at the end, because it was four in the morning and he was very tired and he missed his friend, he did something stupid.

He drew a postcard.

Badly. A rectangle with a scalloped edge, done in pen, and inside it a stick figure eating something that was supposed to be chicken fingers. Across the top, in big looping letters: GREETINGS FROM THE CAPITAL.

Underneath he wrote: He still doesn’t miss you.

Then he looked at it for a long time, and thought about crossing it out. And didn’t. Xav would know what it meant, or he wouldn’t. Either way it was true. It was a postcard across a distance, and it was the only thing they had.

He laid one of Cursiffe’s strips across the fold, and pressed it down, and felt it catch and hold.

At the top, very carefully, he wrote: No. 1.
