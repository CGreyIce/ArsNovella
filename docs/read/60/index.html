Chapter 60

Three Years

5,453 words · 24 min read

He read it the way he read everything in the channel: on the end of the jetty, at dusk, with the window open on his knees and a net he’d stopped mending lying beside him.

[ Althea: Rule three. Good news, loudly. I’ve been sitting on this for a week because I needed to be sure. Chris checked it for me. I’m sure.
Althea: Every window keeps two clocks. One counts days here. The other one is in English and it’s a date. It started on the day we left home.
Althea: It runs slow. About eleven of our days for every one of its.
Althea: It’s been nearly a year here.
Althea: At home, it’s been a month. ]

He read it once.

Then he read it again, very slowly, the way you read a letter you think you must have misunderstood.

At home, it’s been a month.

He sat very still on the end of the jetty. The lake was flat and gold in the last of the sun. A heron stood in the reeds on the far side, perfectly motionless, waiting for something to move.

Eleven of our days for every one of its.

He’d had a window for thirty-four years. He’d named its parts himself, as a boy, in a field behind the house he grew up in, in the only language he had. He’d taught it to a court mage at a garrison, and watched it spread through a kingdom like a seed on the wind. He knew it the way you know the house you grew up in.

He had never once looked underneath it.

He did now.

It took him a long time. He wasn’t Thea. He couldn’t Parse the world like code. But it was his window. The first one. The one everything else was copied from. And if the thing Thea had found was in all of theirs, it had come from somewhere.

He went down. Under the name and the tier and the long line he spent his life not looking at. Under the skills and the inventory. Under the channel, which sat at the bottom of his window like a pool at the foot of a waterfall. Down into the part he’d never touched, where the window was just window, lines and lines of it in his own language, written by a boy who hadn’t known he was writing.

And there, very faint, the way a watermark shows when you hold paper up to the light, at the tail of Thea’s last line, there were two numbers.

One was a count. He didn’t care about the count.

The other was a date.

A month. A day. A year. In English, in capitals, the way he’d have written it in a form, once, a long time ago. The day they’d left.

He looked at the year.

He knew what year he’d died in. He’d always known. It was one of the few things from the old world that he’d never let blur: the year, and the room, and the fan going round, and the AWP shot he’d missed, and love you guys except Newish. Thirty-four years he’d carried that year. Thirty-four years he’d assumed it was as far behind them as it was behind him.

The year at the end of Thea’s date was three years later.

Three.

The sun went down. He didn’t notice.

He sat on the end of the jetty with the window open on his knees and the year glowing faintly in the dark, and everything he’d thought for thirty-four years came apart in his hands, quietly, one piece at a time, like a net with the knots cut.

Thirty-four years, then, probably, back there too. He’d thought that. Last winter. Standing on this jetty in the snow. A world that had gone on without him. The fan in the empty room in Malacca long since thrown away. His father old by now, or gone.

Three years.

His father wasn’t old. Not old the way he’d imagined, all these years, lying awake: an old man, a very old man, or a grave somewhere in Singapore with a name on it that Ars would never see. His father was three years older than the last time Ice had seen him. That was all. Three years. Still working, probably. Still up early. Still himself.

Still with her, probably. The woman he’d found, the year Ice moved to Malacca. Ice had been awkward about it, at the time. Twenty-four and awkward and a bit hurt on his mother’s behalf, though his mother had been gone five years by then. He’d been polite. He’d never really talked to her. He’d thought there’d be time.

Three years. They’d probably married by now.

His father had probably remarried. He’d thought that too, once. In another life. It had been a guess, then, a vague sad thought about a world he’d never see again. It wasn’t a guess anymore. It was a man three years older, at a kitchen table somewhere, with someone across from him. A real man. Still there. On the other side of a floor.

The room in Malacca. Someone would have packed it up. Three years ago, not thirty-four. Someone would have folded his clothes and boxed his books and unplugged the fan and stood in the doorway of the empty room for a moment, the way you do.

He didn’t know who. His father, maybe. Gabe, maybe. Deston.

He found that his hands were shaking. He hadn’t known they could still do that.

He kept reading. He couldn’t stop.

He read them all find out about their mothers. Yan: she’s only been looking for a month. Rachael, in capitals. Newish: my nan won’t even have noticed. Milli: it’s a plan. He read their joy go through the channel like a wave, line after line, and some part of him, under everything, was glad for them, enormously glad, the way you’re glad for anyone who’s just been told the door isn’t shut after all.

Then he read Malcolm.

IF a month there is a year here, THEN three years there is about thirty-four here. Declan died three years ago.

He’d known it was coming. He’d known the second he saw the year. They were clever. Halvard had said so, in his letter. They are clever, Ars, and there are thirty-five of them. He’d written back let them, and meant it, and he still meant it.

He hadn’t known what it would be like to watch.

it’s him, wrote Gabe.

I knew his voice, wrote Aleaka. I told you. I knew his voice.

Xav, about Petra. About words under his breath before he moved, in a language nobody knew. Ars closed his eyes for a moment, reading that. He’d forgotten he did it. He’d done it on the Redmarch, apparently. The old phrases. The same ones he’d muttered before last rounds, in a computer lab, a lifetime ago, to make Xav laugh. He hadn’t known anyone had heard.

Yan, about the sword. Whoever killed that demon held that sword.

Jared. Trust.

And Milli, the one who’d said no all year, the one who asked for receipts. I’m sorry. It fits.

He read them decide he was alive.

He read them argue about where. North, maybe. North-west of the Redmarch. Somewhere a trader picked up a book. Hyvred, who could see further than anyone: I’ve never seen anyone with light like that. Of course she hadn’t. She’d never been close enough.

Thea didn’t write anything. Higu didn’t write anything.

He noticed that. He sat on the jetty in the dark and noticed it, the way he noticed a heron go still in the reeds. Thea, who’d built the channel and answered everything. Higu, who’d made the rules. Not a word. Not about the sum, or Gabe, or where.

He thought about a moth on the jetty rail, under a full moon, lifting off and drifting up the hill. He thought about Thea in the market in the snow, saying trust to his face. He thought about five girls in the militia yard a week ago, standing too still, and Alea coming home and putting the basket down by the door and saying, very carefully, Thea says to tell you thank you. From her.

They know, he thought. Wrenhaven knows.

And they hadn’t said. Not in the channel. Not to anyone.

He sat with that for a long time, too.

And then, very late, when the channel had gone quiet and the arguing had worn itself out, from Kingsferry:

[ Gabe: declan. if you can somehow read this.
Gabe: i don’t care where you are. i don’t care how.
Gabe: i’m coming to find you.
Deston: Me too. ]

Ars put his hand over his mouth.

He’d known Gabe since he was thirteen. In the back row of a choir, trying not to laugh in the quiet part. Gabe, who’d said sleep, Declan, seriously every night for eleven years. Gabe, who’d been the only one in the call that last night to use his real name.

If you can somehow read this.

He could. He’d read every word. He was reading it right now, at the end of a jetty on a lake in another world, with tears running down his face for the first time in seven years.

He could write back. He’d always known that. One line. I’m here. Or Trust. Or just Gabe. The channel would take it. They’d all see it. Gabe would see it, in Kingsferry, at three in the morning, a name with no window behind it, from nowhere.

He didn’t.

He didn’t know why, exactly. It wasn’t that he didn’t want to. It was that it wasn’t the way. Not a line in a chat at three in the morning. Not forty lines and then gone. If Gabe was coming, Gabe was coming. Ars knew him. He’d come. And when he did, Ars wanted to be standing in front of him, with his face, his real face, the one Gabe wouldn’t know. And say his name.

Not yet, he thought. But soon.

He closed the window.

Alea found him at midnight.

He heard her coming. He always did. The cabin door, and her bare feet on the path, and the creak of the third plank on the jetty that he’d been meaning to fix for seven years and never would, because it told him when she was coming. She didn’t bring tea this time. She just came, in her nightdress, with her shawl round her shoulders and her ears up and her tail very still.

She stopped at the end of the jetty, beside him, and looked at his face.

And he saw her see it.

He saw her ears go flat, slowly, against her head. He saw her face go still, the way a pond goes still when something big moves under it. He saw her look at his face, and then at the dark water, and then back at his face. And he saw that she knew exactly what had happened, before he said a word.

She sat down beside him on the end of the jetty, with her feet over the edge, and didn’t say anything.

They sat there for a long time.

“Three years,” he said at last.

His voice came out strange. He hadn’t used it in hours.

Alea didn’t look at him. She was looking at the water.

“I know,” she said. Very quietly. “I’ve known since the first week.”

He’d half expected it. He found it didn’t help.

“The first week,” he said.

“At the fire. In the yard. They told me about Ice.” Alea pulled her shawl tighter. “About you. Higu said you died in your sleep, three years ago. And I sat there and did the sum in my head, and I thought…” She stopped. Started again. “I thought, Dad’s been here thirty-four years. And for them it’s been three.” She swallowed. “And I thought about your dad. My grandad. That I’ve never met. That you used to talk about at bedtime. And I thought, he’s still alive. He’s probably still alive. And Dad doesn’t know.”

Ars didn’t say anything.

“I came home that night,” said Alea, “and I stood in the kitchen with the basket, and I had the words ready. I’d practised them on the lake road. Dad, they think you died three years ago. Dad, it’s only been three years for them. Dad, your father might still be alive.” Her voice cracked. “And I didn’t say it. I said the guild was fine.”

“Why?”

She turned her head and looked at him, at last. Her eyes were bright in the dark.

“Because you were happy,” she said. “Not happy. Quiet. You were quiet, for the first time since Mum. You were fishing, and growing the stupid pumpkins, and the number in your window was going up. I saw you look at it. You thought I didn’t, but I did.” She wiped her face with the corner of her shawl. “And I thought, if I tell him, it’ll break. Whatever it is that’s mending, it’ll break. He’ll start thinking about home again. About his dad. About the way back. And…”

She stopped.

“And?” said Ars gently.

“And you’ll go,” said Alea. It came out of her all at once, the way the truth does when it’s been held down for a year. “You’ll go home. Back to your dad, and your friends, and your world. One of them will get to Sun, and the door’ll open, and you’ll go. And I’ll…” Her face crumpled. “I’m a fox, Dad. I’m sixteen. I’ve never been anywhere but here. This is the only world I’ve got. Mum’s here. She’s on the Redmarch. I can’t…”

She couldn’t finish. She put her face in her hands.

He put his arm round her.

The way he had when she was nine, the first winter after, when she’d sat on the end of this jetty and cried until she was sick, and he’d sat beside her and held on, and not said anything at all, because there wasn’t anything to say. She was bigger now. Taller. Stronger. Her shoulders under his arm were a swordswoman’s shoulders. But she leaned into him the same way, all at once, as if her strings had been cut.

He held on.

“Alea,” he said. “Listen to me.”

She shook her head against his shoulder.

“Listen,” he said. “Your mother told you to. Remember?”

A choked sound came out of her that might have been a laugh. “Listen to your father.”

“Listen to your father.” He held her tighter. “I would never leave without you. Never. Not for my father. Not for my friends. Not for any world, this one or the other one. If a door opened tomorrow, I wouldn’t walk through it without you.” He took a breath. “And if you didn’t want to go, I wouldn’t either. I’d stay. Here. With you. On this jetty, with these stupid hens, for the rest of my life.”

Alea had gone very still against him.

“But your dad,” she said, muffled. “Grandad. He’s alive. He’s still there. He thinks you’re…”

“I know.”

“Doesn’t he deserve…”

“He’s a father,” said Ars.

She lifted her head and looked at him.

“He’s a father,” said Ars again. Quietly. “Same as me. And if he could see us now, if he could see you, sitting here, a fox girl in a nightdress with a sword arm and her mother’s eyes, and know what I know…” He shook his head. “He’d understand. He’d understand better than anyone. He’d tell me to stay. He’d do exactly the same.”

He was sure of it. He’d never been surer of anything. His father, at a kitchen table, three years older. His father, who’d held it together through the worst years of Ice’s life. His father, who’d found someone, after, because a man shouldn’t have to be alone. His father would look at Alea and understand in one second.

Alea stared at him. Then her face crumpled all over again, and she put her arms round his neck the way she had when she was small, and held on so hard it hurt.

“You’re not going anywhere,” she said into his shoulder.

“I’m not going anywhere.”

“Promise.”

He almost said it. The old word. He almost said it, out of thirty-four years of habit, at the end, the way he’d said it after every lie and every joke and every promise he’d never meant to keep.

He didn’t. Not tonight. Tonight it wasn’t a joke.

“I promise,” he said.

They sat on the jetty until the moon came up.

After a while she stopped crying, and sat with her head on his shoulder, and they talked. Quietly. About nothing much, at first. And then, slowly, about everything.

About his father. What he was like. What he’d do, if he could see Alea. (“He’d like you,” said Ars. “He’d like you very much. He’d pretend not to know what to say to you, and then he’d talk your ear off.”) About the room in Malacca, and the fan, and the kettle, which Alea had heard about in bedtime stories since she was four and had always secretly thought he’d made up. About Gabe and Deston, coming. I’m coming to find you.

“Will they?” said Alea. “Find you?”

“Probably.” He looked out at the water. “Deston always found people. Even at school. If anyone can, he will.”

“And Wrenhaven? Thea and the others?” Alea hesitated. “Dad. They looked at me so strangely last week. All of them. And Thea said to tell you thank you. And she wouldn’t look at me.”

Ars was quiet for a moment.

“I think they know,” he said.

Alea sat up. “Know?”

“I think they worked it out. Before the others. Before tonight.” He thought about the moth. The market. Trust. “And I think they decided not to say. To anyone. Not even to you.”

Alea stared at him. Then she looked up the hill, at the dark bulk of the town, at the militia hall at the top of it, where one window, very small and very far, still had a light in it.

“Why?” she said. “Why wouldn’t they say?”

“Because you asked them not to,” said Ars. “Didn’t you. In the summer. Not to put your name in their channel.”

Alea’s mouth opened. Then closed.

“How do you know that?”

He didn’t answer. He just looked at her, the way he’d looked at her on the ice in the winter, after three lessons she hadn’t needed. And after a moment, she started to laugh. Helplessly, the way she’d laughed on the rug by the fire, about there’s plenty of fish.

“You can see it,” she said. “Their channel. You’ve been able to see it all along.”

“It’s my window,” said Ars mildly. “Everything in theirs comes from it.”

“And you’ve been reading it. All summer.”

“Every word.”

“And you never said.”

“You never said either,” said Ars. “About the three years.”

Alea stopped laughing. She looked at him for a long moment, in the moonlight.

“We’re both terrible,” she said.

“We’re both terrible,” Ars agreed.

She put her head back on his shoulder. And they sat there, the two of them, at the end of the jetty, with nothing between them anymore that either of them was carrying alone, until the moon was high over the lake and Alea fell asleep against him, and he carried her up the path to the cabin the way he’d carried her when she was nine.

He didn’t open his window again that night. He didn’t need to look at the number.

He knew which way it was going.

Alea went up the hill at dawn with five fish in the basket.

Five. She’d counted them twice at the door. Her father had been at the stove with his back to her, the way he always was. She’d stood in the doorway with the basket and looked at him, and he hadn’t turned round, and she’d said, “Bye, Dad,” and he’d said, “Bye,” the way they always did.

But it meant something different now. Something lighter. She could feel it all the way up the lake road, in the early sun, like a weight she’d been carrying for a year that had simply gone.

He was leaving today. She knew that too. He left every year, a few days before the anniversary, with his pack and his rod, and walked east to the Redmarch, and sat with Mum. He’d be there in three days, the way he walked when nobody was watching, and gone a fortnight in all. She’d stay with Rowan and Odette, the way she had during the war. She always hated it.

She didn’t hate it this year. She didn’t know why. She just didn’t.

They were all in the yard when she came through the gate. All five of them. And Maren, by the door, and Bram on his bucket with his tea.

They looked at her.

She’d been ready for it. She’d been ready for it since the jetty, since I think they know. She stood in the gateway with the basket on her arm, and looked back at them. At Thea, who’d built the channel and said thank you to a hedge. At Higu, with her notebook closed on her knee. At Gigichi, and Xola with Squiddles, and PenguChi in her corner.

Nobody said anything.

Then Thea smiled at her. Small and crooked and real. And Alea, without quite meaning to, smiled back.

That was all. Nobody said a word about it. Not then. Not for a long time after.

But Alea knew they knew. And they knew she knew. And that, somehow, was enough.

“Five fish,” she said, and put the basket down on the bench. “He was feeling generous.”

She trained with PenguChi in the far corner, the way she did most mornings now.

PenguChi had been at thirty-nine for a week. Stuck, the way she got stuck: climbing a percent at a time, slowly, steadily, and then stopping, just short of something, and standing there in front of it with her eyes closed and her patient face, waiting. Waddles sat on the wall beside her like a small yellow coach.

“It’s close,” PenguChi said, as Alea came over. “I can feel it. It’s right there. It just won’t…” She opened her eyes. “It comes up. It stays now, the way you showed me. But it won’t come out. Into the sword.”

Alea looked at her.

She thought about three lessons on the ice in the winter. Don’t push it. The back foot. The out-breath. She thought about her father on the jetty last night, with his arm round her. Listen to your father.

“There’s a fourth thing,” she said.

PenguChi tilted her head.

“He told me once,” said Alea. “When I was thirteen. Before I’d ever felt it. I didn’t understand it then. I think I do now.” She took a breath. “He said: it doesn’t come out because you’re holding on to it. You’ve worked so hard to keep it that you won’t let it go. Stop keeping it. Give it away.”

PenguChi was quiet for a long moment.

Then she closed her eyes again. She stood in her wide stance, the way she’d stood every morning for a year. She breathed out, long and slow, until her chest was empty and still. And Alea, standing beside her, felt PenguChi’s aura come up from the very bottom of her, slow and smooth, like water filling a jar, and go down through her back foot into the flagstones, and stay.

And then, very gently, the way you’d hand someone a cup, PenguChi let it go.

It went out of her. Down her arms. Into the practice sword in her hands. Alea felt it go, the way you feel warmth leave a room when a door opens: the plain wooden blade, for one long moment, humming, faintly, with something that wasn’t wood.

PenguChi opened her eyes.

“Oh,” she said. Very softly. In that voice that steadied everyone. “Oh. It went.”

The yard erupted. Xola shrieking. Gigichi saying finally, FINALLY. Bram on his feet again, his bucket knocked over again, shouting Penguin! and PenguChi shouting PenguChi! back at him through her tears. Higu, on the wall, opening her notebook and writing something in it very fast, and then stopping, and just sitting there, smiling, which Higu almost never did.

PenguChi opened her window with shaking hands.

[ PenguChi · Tier 1: Drawn ]

“Ninth,” said Thea, reading it over her shoulder. Her voice was wobbling. “You’re the ninth. The first of us five on the hard path.”

PenguChi turned round and grabbed Alea’s hands, the way she had in the winter, and held on.

“Tell him,” she said. “Tell him thank you. Again. From me.”

“I will,” said Alea. She was laughing. She was crying a bit too. “I will. I’ll tell him.”

And it was then, standing in the militia yard with PenguChi’s hands in hers and the sun coming over the wall and everyone shouting, that Alea felt it.

It wasn’t like the first tier.

The first tier had come slowly. Three years of it, from thirteen to sixteen, on the jetty every morning with her father watching, your weight’s on your heels. A little more every day. A little more. And then one morning it had just been there, the way the lake was suddenly frozen one morning in winter, from the north shore.

This was different.

It came from inside her bones.

She felt it start somewhere deep, deeper than her aura had ever gone. Not rising. Settling. Going down into her, into the long bones of her legs and the bones of her arms and her ribs and her spine, the way water goes down into the ground after a long rain. Into everything. Filling her up from the inside, the way frost fills a pane of glass, edge to centre, one crystal at a time.

And as it went, she felt herself go hard.

Not her face. Not her heart. Her heart was wide open; it had been since midnight. Her body. Her bones, her muscles, her skin. Tightening, the way leather tightens when you soak it and dry it. As if somebody had taken her and tempered her, the way you temper steel: heat and cold and heat again, until it would bend without breaking.

Tempered.

She stood in the middle of the militia yard with PenguChi’s hands in hers and felt it finish. Felt the last of it settle into her, all the way down to the soles of her feet, and stay.

She knew, without looking, what it was. She’d known what it would feel like since she was thirteen. Her father had told her. You’ll know. When it comes. You’ll feel it go into your bones.

The second tier.

PenguChi felt it too. Alea saw her face change. PenguChi’s eyes went wide, and she let go of Alea’s hands, very slowly, and took a step back.

“Alea,” she said. “Alea. What…”

Alea didn’t answer. She was thinking about the first week. About standing in this same yard, with her copper guild token, and Maren reading her and asking can you see your window? and Alea saying no.

She’d lied. They’d all known she’d lied. They’d been kind enough, for a year, not to say so.

She opened her window. Right there, in the yard, in front of all of them. She’d never done it in front of anyone but her father.

[ Alea · Tier 2: Tempered ]

The yard went very quiet.

They looked at the window. They looked at her. Five girls from another world who’d known, for a year, exactly who she was and whose she was, and had never once said it.

“I lied,” said Alea. “In the first week. I’ve got one.” She swallowed. “I’ve always had one.”

There was a short silence.

“That’s not news,” said Gigichi. Not unkindly.

And then Thea laughed, and Xola laughed, and PenguChi threw her arms round Alea’s neck, and Higu came down off the wall with her notebook and stood in front of Alea and looked at her with her calm half-lidded eyes for a long moment.

“Second tier,” said Higu quietly. “That’s the one where you can carry a big sword, isn’t it.”

Alea looked at her.

“Yes,” she said.

Higu nodded slowly. She didn’t say anything else. She didn’t need to.

Alea ran all the way down the hill.

Down the lake road, in the morning sun, with the empty basket bouncing on her arm and her tail streaming out behind her and her new hard bones barely noticing the ground. Past the fields. Past the hedges. Down to the water.

He was on the step of the cabin, with his pack beside him and his rod over his shoulder, ready to go. Just the way he left every year. A plain man in a plain coat, with a fishing rod, looking out at the lake.

“Dad—” she said, skidding to a stop in front of him.

“I know,” said her father.

Of course he did. He’d known the moment it happened, probably, sitting on this step, the way he’d known when Thea’s first candle caught in the winter. He always knew.

She stood in front of him, breathing hard, with her ears up and her eyes stinging, and he looked at her. Not the way he usually looked. Not your weight’s on your heels. The way he’d looked at her on the jetty last night, when she’d told him the truth.

“Tempered,” he said.

“Tempered.”

He was quiet for a moment. Then something went across his face that she’d only seen a handful of times in her whole life. Not a smile. Something bigger than a smile, that didn’t know how to fit on his face anymore.

“You’ll need a sword,” he said.

Alea’s heart stopped.

She’d known. She’d always known. Since she was thirteen. When you reach the second tier. She’d known what sword he meant. The one that had stood over her mother’s grave for seven years. The one the Advisor had taken. The one that was in a chapel at Cinderhold right now, waiting to be carried back across the red.

“Your sword?” she said.

He looked east. Toward the Redmarch, a long way off, where her mother was.

“Half of it, maybe,” he said.

She didn’t know what that meant. She didn’t ask. She just put her arms round him, pack and rod and all, and held on.

He left at mid-morning, the way he always did.

Alea walked him to the edge of the town. She always did that too. Up the lake road, past the fields, onto the market road, where it ran through the top of Wrenhaven toward the east gate. Past the market square. Past the town hall, where Odette waved from a window.

Past the militia hall.

They were at the gate. All five of them. Standing there in the road, in the sun, the way they’d been standing in the road the very first week, a year ago, arguing, when he’d come down it with his basket and Maren and Bram had thrown themselves on the ground.

Nobody was arguing today. Nobody was blocking the road. They were just standing at the gate, the five of them, watching him come.

He didn’t stop walking. But he slowed.

And as he passed, he looked at them. Each of them. One after another. The way he’d looked at them on that first day, when nobody had understood why. Thea. Gigichi. Xola, with Squiddles on her head. Higu, with her notebook. PenguChi, with a practice sword in her hand that had hummed for the first time that morning.

He didn’t say anything.

Neither did they.

Thea nodded. Just once. Very slightly.

And he nodded back.

Then he walked on, up the market road, out through the east gate of Wrenhaven, with his pack on his back and his rod over his shoulder, toward the Redmarch and the anniversary and an old black sword in a chapel at Cinderhold, waiting to be carried home.

Alea stood at the gate with the five of them and watched him go, until the road went over the brow of the hill and he went with it.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then Xola, very softly, said: “He’s coming back, isn’t he?”

“He’s coming back,” said Alea.

She’d never been surer of anything in her life.

End of Volume Two.
