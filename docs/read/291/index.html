The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 10: The Sword's Country

Chapter 291

The Other End

5,522 words · 24 min read

Everything had a thread. That was the first thing Deston found out at the lake, and it took him twelve days, and he felt stupid about it for most of them.

He’d never looked. That was all. Nearly three years he’d been seeing them, the threads between people, since the first time, in Kingsferry, when he’d seen a line of faint light going from Gabe’s chest to his own across a dark room like somebody had strung a washing line between them. He’d looked at people ever since. People were what threads were for. Gabe to Rachael, thick and old and warm. Calvin to the river. Dennis to every coin in Kingsferry, thin and bright and very many. Every native with a window carrying a faint one running the same way, all of them, back to one man at a lake. And his own, the thickest he had, thirteen years of it, which he’d followed up a river once, to a jetty and a man with a different face.

He’d never once looked at a cup.

He did it by accident, on the third morning, at the cabin. Alea had put the cups out on the step, two, the way she always did, and then six more because Group 4 were there, and the kettle, and gone back in for the bread. And Deston had been sitting on the bottom step half asleep with the lake grey in front of him, letting his eyes go loose the way they went when he was tired, and the threads coming up all round him like they did: Gabe’s, Rachael’s, the faint long one going up the shingle to the cabin door where Declan was. And on the step beside him, very faint, so faint he thought it was the light on the glaze, a thread going from the kettle.

He sat up.

It was there. A thread, thinner than anything he’d ever seen, going from the dented black kettle on the step up the shingle toward the cabin. Toward the door. Toward Declan.

He looked at the cups. Plain brown glaze, the kind they sold by the dozen in the Wrenhaven market. Nothing. Nothing on six of them. And on the two at the end, Alea’s two, the ones she put out every morning, the faintest thing. A hair of light going up toward the cabin, the way the kettle’s went. Not to Declan. To somebody inside.

“Rae,” he said.

Rachael was at the top of the shingle, sitting on the upturned boat, with her coat round her knees. She looked down.

“Come here a minute,” said Deston. “I need you to tell me if I’m going mad.”

He wasn’t going mad. He was just late.

It took him the rest of the week, and Rachael, and Dennis, because Dennis was the only one who could tell him whether what he thought he was seeing was what he was seeing; and in the end he had it written on the back of a soup bill in Calvin’s pencil, the way they wrote everything. Rule four. Find out yourself.

Things people made had threads.

Not much. Nothing like a person’s. A person’s thread was a rope beside them, a line of light you could have hung a lamp on. A made thing’s was a hair. And not every made thing; the cups from the market had nothing, the planks of the jetty had nothing, his own coat, which a tailor in Kingsferry had made for money in a week, had nothing. It was the things somebody had made for somebody. Or made very carefully. Or made and kept and used every day for years, until the thing and the person had worn into each other like a step and a foot.

Alea’s two cups had a thread to her, because she’d bought them, at eleven, from her own first money, Declan said when Deston asked, and used them every morning since. The kettle had a thread to Declan, thin and tired and very old; he’d had it, he said, since the first winter at the cabin. The fishing rod, the old one, leaning on the cabin wall with the hook on it, had three: one to Declan, thick as a cord, one to old Garrow in Wrenhaven who’d whipped it twice, and one to Bram, who’d whipped it once. Gigi’s bench, by the lake road, where the roots had grown up into a seat and then a back, had a thread to Gigi that wasn’t a hair at all but a proper line, green, like the bench was nearly a person, which Deston thought it might be.

Yeva’s net, which hung on a peg inside the cabin door where Declan had put it the first night, had one so long and thin he could barely follow it. Out of the door. Down the shingle. Out across the lake, toward the mouth of the Wren, and down the river, a hundred miles, to a wharf at the south end of Kingsferry and an old woman with three shawls and a walnut face, mending.

And Gabe’s hook.

He found that one last, on the ninth day, because he’d been saving it, and because he was a little afraid of it. He went down to the jetty at dawn, where Declan had cast it every morning since they’d come, and stood on the last plank beside him and looked.

It was the brightest thing on the lake. A thread from the hook, down in the dark water at the end of the line, coming up through the lake, up the line, past Declan’s hands on the rod, and across the jetty to the shingle, to a big shape sitting on an upturned crate by the ash tree with a cup between his knees, watching. Gabe. A short thick thread like a struck bell, humming, with the same note Gabe’s own thread had. Gabe’s note. Low and steady and a bit too loud.

“It’s to Gabe,” said Deston.

Declan didn’t turn round. “I know.”

“How do you know?”

“I can feel him in it.” Declan reeled in a turn. The line hissed in the water. “Every time I cast. Like a hand on the rod.”

Deston looked at him. At the side of his face, which was a stranger’s face and wasn’t, in the grey light, looking out at the lake.

“It’s a star,” said Deston. “The iron. It’s older than anything.”

“I know.”

“And it’s only got a thread to Gabe.” He’d looked. He’d made himself look. “Nothing older. Nothing from before. Just him. From the cold working, and the R, and the M at two in the morning.” He stopped. “As if the star didn’t have one till he cut a word in it.”

Declan reeled in another turn.

“Thea said it wasn’t written in anything,” he said. “Before. When it was a lump in Harl’s tongs. She said it was the only thing she’d ever read that didn’t say anything.” The hook came up out of the water, bright, with the light on it, and swung in toward his hand. He caught it. Turned it so the word on the shank caught the light. RESURGAM. “It says something now.”

So that was the question. Deston had known it would be since the third morning, on the step, with a thread coming out of a kettle. He’d known it the way you know the last line of a song before it’s sung.

He asked Alea first.

He found her on the shingle on the eleventh day, in the late afternoon, sitting on the flat rock with the lamp beside her, unlit, the way it was every afternoon until dusk. She’d kept it since the autumn, when he went west. She lit it every night. She’d said in the winter she’d hand it back at the thaw, and the thaw had come, and the ice had gone off the lake, and she hadn’t yet. Nobody had said anything. Deston wasn’t going to be the first.

“Can I ask you something,” he said. “About Astra.”

She looked up. She had her father’s way of looking at you: all at once, with nothing held back, so that you felt for a moment like the only thing on the shingle.

“Yes,” said Alea.

He told her. About the threads on made things. About the kettle and the cups and the net and the hook. About Aldemar’s two, a man and a woman in a forge in the north yard of the old keep three hundred years ago, folding a star fifteen times with a word in it, and a striker called Tam Ashe who burned both his hands and said he’d do it again. About how a thing that somebody made very carefully, for somebody, carried a thread back to them. About how he wanted to look.

“You think there’s a thread,” said Alea. “On Astra. To them.”

“I don’t know,” said Deston. “That’s why I want to look.” He stopped. “They’d be dead. They must be. It’s three hundred years. I don’t know what a thread does when the person at the other end is dead. I’ve never looked at anything that old.”

Alea looked at the lamp.

“Mum’s things,” she said. “The box. On the shelf. Do they have one?”

Deston didn’t say anything for a moment.

He’d seen the box. Everybody had. A plain wooden box on the shelf over the bed, where Declan kept the things that had been Diwa’s: a comb, a pair of scissors, a little medicine bag Alea sometimes took down and unpacked and packed again. He’d looked at it on the fifth day, without meaning to, the way he looked at everything that week. He hadn’t said.

“No,” he said. “Not to her. To you. And to him.” He watched her face. “Every one of them. To both of you. Thick.”

Alea nodded slowly. She didn’t cry. She was nineteen, and she’d done most of her crying about her mother a long time ago, and the rest of it on a knoll on the Redmarch at the ninth anniversary with her father beside her; she’d told Rachael that. She looked at the lamp a long time.

“Then they’d go to whoever’s kept them,” she said. “If it’s like that. If the person’s gone.”

“Maybe,” said Deston. “I don’t know.”

Alea stood up.

“You can look,” she said. “Where he can see. That’s the rule. Thea read it at the cabin with him on his stool, and you’ll look on the jetty with him on the jetty.” She picked up the lamp. “Tonight. I’ll bring it down.”

She brought it down at dusk, wrapped in the old blanket.

Declan was on the jetty already. He was always on the jetty at dusk; he’d sit on the last plank with his legs over the edge and his cup going cold beside him and watch the light go off the water, and nobody went down to him unless he looked round. Gabe was with him tonight. He’d been with him most nights since the hook, sitting on the plank behind him with his back to the last post, not talking.

Alea came down the shingle with the blanket in her arms and the lamp in her other hand, lit now, and walked out along the jetty, and knelt, and put the blanket down on the planks between her father and Gabe, and unfolded it.

Astra.

Deston had seen it a hundred times. It was hard not to; Alea wore it on her back everywhere she went, a buster sword nearly as tall as she was, black as the bottom of a well. He’d seen her fight with it, at the Lists, in front of ten thousand people. He’d seen it lying on a folded blanket on the cabin floor on the night Thea read it, with Thea’s hand flat on the blade and Thea’s face going somewhere none of them could follow. He’d never really looked at it. Not the way he looked now.

It lay on the blanket in the last of the light. Black. Not shining. The grain of it showing down the flat like the pages of a shut book seen edge-on, the way Thea had said. Thousands of lines, so fine you could only see them when the light came across. Stay, said fifteen times over and over until it was thirty-two thousand times. Old leather on the grip, worn to the shape of a hand that wasn’t Alea’s. And by the grip, small, in letters he couldn’t read and didn’t need to, because Declan had told him once what they said: the Latin.

Alea put the lamp on the plank beside it and sat back on her heels.

“Where he can see,” she said.

“Where I can see,” said Declan. He didn’t move from the edge. He’d turned round, a little; he was sitting sideways on the last plank now, with his back half to the lake and his face to the sword, and his hands on his knees.

Deston knelt on the other side of the blanket. Gabe shifted behind him. He felt Gabe’s thread move, the way it did when Gabe was worried and pretending not to be: a hum, a bit louder.

“I’m just going to look,” said Deston. “That’s all. Not like Thea. I’m not going in. I’m just going to look at it the way I looked at the hook.”

“I know,” said Declan.

Deston let his eyes go loose.

It was easier at dusk. It always had been; the threads were faint in daylight, like stars, and came up as the light went. They came up now. All round him. Gabe’s, behind him, thick and bright and humming its low note. Alea’s, beside the lamp, gold, going in every direction: to her father, to the cabin, up the shingle to the bench where Gigi was sitting in the dusk with her cup, to a hundred people in Wrenhaven and a hundred more across the kingdom she’d met at the Lists and never forgotten. The lamp’s, thin and new and warm, to Alea. The faint long ones from the jetty to the shore and the shore to the town. And Declan’s.

Declan’s was always the same. He’d seen it first on the shingle three years ago and it hadn’t changed. Not bright. Not loud. Every thread from every person who had ever known him running into him from all over the kingdom, hundreds, thousands, from every native with a window and every one of the thirty-five and every soldier who’d stood on the Redmarch, and all of them, every single one, sounding the same note. His note. One low note, held. The way he’d been humming it with his mouth shut, Deston had said once, the whole time. Three years. Thirty-four years. Longer.

He looked at the sword.

Nothing.

He looked harder. He let his eyes go further, the way he’d learned to on the river, the way he’d followed his own thread a hundred miles upstream once to find out where it went. He looked at the black iron on the blanket with everything he had.

Nothing.

Not a hair. Not a frayed end. Not a cut. Not the faint tired line the kettle had, or the hair the cups had. Not the long thin one Yeva’s net had, going a hundred miles downriver to an old woman. Nothing went from the sword to anything. Not to Alea, who’d carried it on her back for three years. Not to Declan, who’d carried it for eight, and dragged it out of the black on a strap until his hands were in rags. Not to the forge in the north yard of the old keep, or the road east, or the two people who’d made it, or anybody who’d ever held it at all.

He looked at the grip. The leather. Somebody had wrapped that, once. Somebody’s hands had worn it to that shape.

Nothing.

He sat back on his heels.

“Deston,” said Gabe, behind him. Quietly.

“There isn’t one,” said Deston.

Nobody said anything.

“There isn’t one,” he said again. He heard his own voice, and it sounded strange, like somebody else’s down a long corridor. “Not to anybody. Not a hair.” He looked at it. “Everything has one. I’ve spent twelve days finding out everything has one. Your kettle has one, Declan. Alea’s cups. Gigi’s bench has one like a person’s. Yeva’s net’s got one that goes all the way to Kingsferry.” He stopped. “And this hasn’t got anything. It’s the most made thing I’ve ever seen. Two people folded it fifteen times with a word in it and walked it east and stood it in a hill to hold something down, and it’s not tied to anything.”

“Not to them,” said Alea.

“Not to anybody.”

“Because they’re dead,” said Gabe.

Deston thought about it. He made himself. He looked at the sword again, at the place where a thread would be, if there was one, and listened, the way he listened to threads: not with his ears.

There was a sound. There always was, with threads; every one of them had its note, and when he looked hard enough he could hear them all, a chord, like a choir warming up in another room. The jetty was full of it. Gabe’s low note, behind him. Alea’s bright one. The lamp’s, small. Declan’s, under everything, held.

And from the sword: a note.

Not a thread’s. Not a person’s. He’d never heard anything like it. A single note, very low, lower than Declan’s, coming up out of the black iron like the hum off a struck bell that’s been ringing so long you’ve stopped hearing it, and saying the same thing, over and over, without stopping, in a language that wasn’t one. Stay. Stay. Stay. Thirty-two thousand times. He’d heard it before. He’d been hearing it all along, he realised, every time Alea walked past him with it on her back; he’d thought it was part of her.

But that was the word. That was the sword itself, saying what it said. There was nobody saying it. There was nobody at the other end of it at all.

“It’s not that they’re dead,” said Deston slowly. “Diwa’s things have threads. To you. To Alea. When somebody dies, the thread goes to whoever kept them. Whoever’s still holding the other end.” He looked at the sword. “This hasn’t got anyone holding the other end. Not them. Not anybody they knew. Not anybody who knew anybody who knew them.” He swallowed. “Three hundred years. They went east and they didn’t come back, and Aldemar told the household they’d gone home, and there wasn’t anybody left who’d ever—“ He stopped. “Nobody kept them. That’s what it means. There’s nobody at the other end any more.”

The lamp hissed on the plank.

Declan was looking at the sword. He hadn’t moved. He was sitting sideways on the last plank with his hands on his knees and the lake going dark behind him, and his face was doing nothing at all, the way it did when there was a great deal going on underneath it.

“I carried it eight years,” he said.

“I know.”

“It hasn’t got one to me.”

“No,” said Deston. “It hasn’t got one to anybody. I don’t think it can. I think it’s—“ He tried to find it. “Everything you make, you put a bit of you in it. That’s what the thread is. That’s why the cups have one to her: she put eleven years of mornings in them. And they didn’t put any of themselves in it, Declan. They put a word in it. All of it. Fifteen folds. They put the whole of whatever they had into one word so it’d hold something down after they were gone, and there wasn’t any room left for them.” He looked at the iron. “It’s not tied to anybody because it was never meant to be. It was meant to stay. On its own. Forever. Without anybody.”

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Gabe, behind him, made a sound. Not a word. Deston felt his thread go, the low note gone suddenly very loud, and then quiet.

Alea had her hand on the grip. Deston hadn’t seen her move. She was kneeling beside the blanket with her hand round the old leather, not lifting it, just holding it, the way you’d hold somebody’s hand in a bed.

“That’s sad,” she said.

“Yeah,” said Deston.

“I didn’t think it would be sad.”

“No.”

“It’s a good word,” said Alea. She’d said it to Thea on the step. Deston had heard. It’s a good word, Dad. “It’s still a good word. It’s just—“ She looked at the black iron. “Nobody should have to say a good word on their own for three hundred years.”

They sat on the jetty a long time after that. Alea wrapped the sword back in its blanket, slowly, the way she’d unwrapped it, and took it back up the shingle in her arms, and left the lamp. On the plank between them, lit. Deston watched her go. He watched her stop at the top of the shingle by Gigi’s bench, and Gigi say something, and Alea shake her head, and sit down on the bench beside her in the dark, not going in.

Declan had turned round again. He was sitting on the edge with his legs over, the way he always did, looking out at the lake, which was black now with a thin line of grey along the far shore where the last of the light was. His cup was beside him. It had gone cold an hour ago. He hadn’t touched it.

Gabe sat behind him with his back to the post.

Deston sat on the plank between them with the lamp, and didn’t know what to say, so he didn’t.

“I never asked,” said Declan, after a while.

Deston looked at him.

“Diwa said it,” said Declan. “The day I came back with it. She was sitting in the dust in front of the infirmary and she asked me whose it was. I said mine. And she said—“ He stopped. His voice didn’t change. “She said it was somebody’s. And she never touched it. Eight years. She didn’t touch it once.” He looked at the lake. “And I never asked. Whose. Who’d made it, or why it was on a hill on its own in the middle of the black with the grass growing in rings round it, or what it had been doing there. I just took it. I was nineteen and I’d found a sword. That’s all I ever thought about it. I found a sword.”

“You were nineteen,” said Gabe.

“I was nineteen,” said Declan. “And then I was twenty, and twenty-five, and thirty, and I still never asked. I carried it into a war. I fought with it every day for years. I put it on her grave.” He was very quiet. “And I never once wondered who’d made it. And they’d been standing on that hill for three hundred years with nobody at the other end, and I pulled it out, and dragged it off, and never asked.”

Deston thought about rule five.

They’d written it on the back of the list in the hall, the first week. Gabe’s rule. Five rules, in English, so nobody could read them: Whoever brings you here wants something they haven’t said. The hero nobody talks about isn’t dead. Don’t show them your whole window. Be nice; find out yourself. If he turns out to be all right, we say sorry. They’d meant the Advisor, the last one. Gabe had. They’d said it to the Advisor, in the end, on the steps of the Broken Lance in Valcrest the morning after the king’s table, and the Advisor had stood there in the early light and said No. You were right. Keep asking.

They’d never thought it would be him.

“Declan,” said Deston.

Declan didn’t look round.

“We went digging,” said Deston. “All winter. Gabe at the forge. Cal on the river. Dennis with Yeva. Me at Emrys and in the threads. Cursiffe in the vault. Rule four. Find out yourself.” He stopped. “We didn’t ask you first. We said we wouldn’t ask you. Gabe said it on the shingle, the morning of the third move: we said we wouldn’t ask, we’re not. And we didn’t. We went and found out instead. About your sword. About your hill. About a thing you did when you were nineteen that you didn’t know you’d done.” He looked at the back of Declan’s head. “We went digging in your life without asking you. And it turned out to be the worst thing anybody could have found. And we brought it up a river and put it on your table.”

He swallowed.

“So, rule five,” he said. “We say sorry. That’s the rule. Gabe made it. I’m saying it.” He made himself say it. “Sorry, Declan.”

Behind him, Gabe said, very low, “Sorry.”

Declan didn’t move for a long time. The lake was black. The lamp hissed. Up the shingle, on Gigi’s bench, two shapes in the dark, very still.

“Don’t,” said Declan.

Deston waited.

“Don’t be sorry.” Declan turned his head, at last, and looked at them. At Deston, on the plank with the lamp, and past him at Gabe against the post. His face in the lamplight was a stranger’s face, and it wasn’t. It was doing the thing Deston had seen it do maybe three times in three years. Coming open, a little. At the corners. Like a door somebody had been leaning on from the inside and had stopped. “You didn’t dig in my life. You dug in theirs. The two of them. Somebody had to.” He looked back at the lake. “I never did.”

He was quiet.

“I didn’t ask Diwa why she wouldn’t touch it,” he said. “I didn’t ask Halvard why the gate took so long. I didn’t ask the girl at the well why she didn’t run. I didn’t ask the hill.” He looked at his hands on his knees. “I’ve spent my whole life here not asking. It’s how I’ve lived. You don’t ask, and then you don’t have to know, and then you can sit on a jetty.” He stopped. “And you came up a river with the hook and the song and the purse book, and put it on my table, and I’ve been sitting with it for two weeks. And I’m not sorry you did. I’m glad. I’m not okay. I’m glad.”

“That’s allowed,” said Gabe. “Both.”

“Yes,” said Declan. “Rachael said.”

Deston didn’t say anything. He didn’t think he could have.

“So don’t stop,” said Declan.

Deston looked at him.

“Don’t stop digging.” Declan’s voice was very low. Not quite steady; Deston had never heard it not quite steady. “Who they were. What their names were, if anybody wrote them down. Where they came from. Whether anybody back home ever—“ He stopped. “What they were holding. What it was, under the hill. Whether it’s the same as what’s under her grave. Whether it’s the same as what’s in the valley at Thornwall. Where it went, when I pulled it out.” He looked at the lake. “Whether the girl at the well had a name. I’d like to know her name.” He shut his eyes. “All of it. Everything. Rule four. Find out yourselves.”

“Declan—“

“Find out for me,” said Declan. “I never did.”

They didn’t say anything for a long time after that.

Deston sat on the plank with the lamp and listened to the threads. He couldn’t help it; he was a man who heard threads, the way some people couldn’t help hearing a song in the next room. They were all round him, on the dark jetty. Gabe’s, behind him, low and loud and a bit wet. Alea’s, from the bench up the shingle, gold, pointed down at the jetty like a hand on a shoulder. Gigi’s beside it, green. The lamp’s, small and warm. The long faint ones going off across the lake to the town and down the river and away across the kingdom, hundreds of them, to everybody.

And Declan’s. The low note, held. The same as it had been for three years, every time Deston listened.

Only it wasn’t.

He listened harder. He didn’t believe it at first. It was so small. He’d known that note for thirteen years; he’d followed it a hundred miles up a river. He’d have known it in his sleep. And it was still the same note, still low, still held, still running into him from everybody in the kingdom who’d ever known him. But there was something under it. A second note. Very faint. A hair below the first, so close to it you’d have to be listening for nothing else to hear it at all. Not a harmony. More like the sound a held note makes when the person holding it has been holding it so long they’ve forgotten they’re doing it, and for a moment, just a moment, remembers.

Deston didn’t say anything. He didn’t know what it meant. He didn’t know if it meant anything. He opened his window, sitting there on the plank in the dark, because he wanted something to look at that wasn’t Declan’s face.

Forty-four. It had been forty-one when they left Kingsferry.

He didn’t know what had moved it. A kettle, maybe. Twelve days of looking at cups. He shut the window.

“You okay?” he said. To Declan. He’d said it on the night they came, at the table, and Declan had said no, and Deston had said that’s the right answer.

Declan thought about it. He picked up his cup, and looked into it, and drank what was in it, cold, all of it, the way he never did. He put it down.

“Not yet,” he said.

Deston nodded.

“That’s a better answer,” he said.

“Is it?”

“Yeah,” said Deston. “No is a door. Not yet is a door with somebody’s hand on it.”

Gabe laughed. Behind him, against the post, wet and loud and too much, the way Gabe had always laughed, and stopped himself, and laughed again.

“That’s very Gigi,” he said.

“She started it,” said Deston. “On the bench. It’s not a door shut. It’s just a door. I’m only adding a bit.”

Declan turned his head and looked up the shingle, at the bench in the dark, where two shapes were sitting very close together, not going in.

“She’s going to give it back,” he said. “The lamp. She said at the thaw.”

“It’s the thaw,” said Gabe.

“I know.” Declan looked at the lamp on the plank beside Deston. Lit. Hissing a little, the way it always did, the wick a hair too high. “She’ll do it when she does it.”

“You’re not going to ask her?”

“No,” said Declan. And then, after a moment, very quietly, so that Deston almost didn’t hear it, and wasn’t sure afterwards that he was meant to: “Not yet.”

They stayed on the jetty till the lamp burned down.

Deston didn’t go to sleep on the Widow that night. He lay on the shingle by the ash tree, next to Gabe, wrapped in his coat, and looked at the stars and listened. The ash had six leaves now. Gabe counted them every morning. It had a thread, too, Deston had found out on the eighth day: a thin green one, up the shingle to the bench by the lake road. To Gigi, who’d found the place for it with her hands on the stones a year ago at ice-out, when it was a cutting off the ash on the knoll, and written here into the earth at the foot of it with one finger.

He’d write it all down in the morning. On the back of something, in Calvin’s pencil. Rule four. Threads on things. The kettle. The cups. The net. The hook. The bench. The ash. And at the bottom, on its own line, because Calvin would want it there:

Astra: nothing. Nobody at the other end.

And under that, because it was the next thing, and they’d need to know where to start:

Names?

Gabe was asleep. He slept the way he always had, flat on his back with his arms out, taking up as much shingle as two people. His thread hummed beside Deston in the dark, low and steady, Gabe’s note. And from the jetty, very faint, because Declan was still sitting there on the last plank in the dark with his legs over the edge, the low held note that ran into him from everybody in the kingdom.

And under it, a hair below, if you were listening for nothing else: the other one.

Deston lay on the shingle and listened to it till he fell asleep.
