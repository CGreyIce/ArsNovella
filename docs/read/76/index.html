The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 3: The Anniversary

Chapter 76

The Third Hunter

5,279 words · 23 min read

Dennis could hear a lie.

He always had. Long before the floor went soft, long before there was any such thing as a window, back when he was a boy in Singapore being told by an aunt that the injection wouldn’t hurt. He’d never been able to explain it. It wasn’t a voice going up, or eyes going sideways, or any of the things people wrote books about. It was just that a lie, when somebody said it, sounded wrong. Like a bell with a crack in it. You struck it, and it rang, and underneath the ringing there was a buzz, a flat little rattle, that told you something inside it was broken.

Here, it was louder. Here, it had a name, and a number that went up, and a path at the end of it with illusion written over the gate. Which Dennis thought was very funny, if you thought about it: the boy who could always hear lies, learning to tell them to the world.

Kingsferry was the worst place in the kingdom for it.

A river city. A trade city. A city of salt merchants and grain factors and moneylenders and barge captains, and every one of them, all day long, from the moment the market bell went at dawn to the moment the taverns closed at midnight, lying. Not wickedly. Just the ordinary lies of trade. Best price on the river. I’m losing money on this. Never seen salt this fine. Dennis walked down Rope Street in the morning and it was like walking under a bell tower in a storm.

He’d got used to it. He’d learned to listen through it, the way you learn to talk at a loud party. He’d made Group 4 a very great deal of money, listening through it.

He was the treasurer. Nobody had elected him. He’d just started doing it in the first week, when Dennis had heard the stipend clerk say that’s the full amount and rang like a cracked bell, and had smiled at the clerk until the clerk found the rest. After that, everyone just gave him the money.

Sabine had watched him do it. Sabine, who’d guarded merchant caravans for fifteen years and was here, she said, for the pay. She’d looked at him across the stipend office, and he’d looked back at her, and something had passed between them that made Gabe, standing beside them, visibly uneasy.

They understood each other perfectly. Everyone said so. Everyone seemed worried about it.

There’d been one person in Dennis’s whole life who never lied to him.

He thought about that sometimes, in Kingsferry, walking under the bell tower. He’d written it in the Ice book, in the channel, in the summer, when Hako asked: He was the only person who never lied to me. I’d have known.

It was true. He’d known Declan since he was seventeen, through Deston, and in all those years, every call, every game, every stupid argument about food, Declan had never once rung cracked. Not once. Not even with the trust thing. That was what had always fascinated Dennis. Declan would say something completely outrageous, I would never betray you in this game, and then say trust, and it was the biggest lie in the world, and it didn’t ring. Because it wasn’t a lie. Everybody knew. Declan knew they knew. It was a joke the size of a lie, wearing a lie’s coat, and underneath it, the bell rang perfectly true.

The last time Dennis had seen him was Malacca. The visit. A year before. Declan walking Calvin into the kitchen an hour after they’d met and pointing at the worst kettle Dennis had ever seen and saying this is the kettle like it was a trophy. Dennis had stood in the doorway and listened, and it had rung true. He really had been proud of it. He’d known it was terrible. That was why.

There’d been nobody since who didn’t ring. Nobody here. Not even Group 4, who loved him, and who he loved; they lied to him all the time, small kind lies, I’m fine, it doesn’t hurt, I’m not scared. He heard every one. He never said so.

Rachael lied the most. I’m fine. Every day, for three months, since she went up a tier and Sister Maud sat her down and told her what that meant. Every order will want you. And worse. Every day since, Rachael had walked to the infirmary between Gabe and Sabine and said I’m fine, and it had rung like a church bell with a split down the middle.

The autumn was the busiest season on the river.

Everything that had to go south before the winter went south now: salt and wool and timber and the last of the grain, on barges three deep along the wharves. The quays were so crowded you could cross the river on boats without getting your feet wet. Calvin had done it, once, for a bet, and Dennis had collected.

And in the crowds, Deston was seeing things again.

He’d gone quiet about it at first. Deston always did. He’d come back from the morning walk to the infirmary on the first day of the month and sat at the table above the chandler’s without taking his coat off and said, very calmly, “Three.”

“Three what?” said Calvin.

“Three people with no threads.” Deston looked at his hands. “On the quays. In a week. Not together. Not near her. Just… there.” He was quiet for a moment. “And the man from the barge. The red sails. The one from the summer. He’s back. Standing on the customs-house steps. Turning his hat.”

Nobody said anything.

“He looked at me,” said Deston. “He knows I can see him. He doesn’t seem to mind.”

Dennis heard one himself, two days later.

He was in the grain exchange, doing what he did, which was standing near people with money and listening to them. A factor from upriver, a big, pleasant, ruddy man with a good coat, was talking to a barge captain about the weather. Just the weather. Lovely weather for it, this time of year.

And it rang.

Not like a trade lie. Not cracked. Dennis had heard ten thousand cracked bells; he knew them the way a sailor knows rope. This was something else. It rang like a bell that had been made out of something that wasn’t metal. Like somebody had taken a memory of a bell and hung it up and hit it. There was nothing underneath. No buzz. No rattle. Nothing true inside it to be broken. Just the shape of a sound, and then silence where the rest of it should have been.

The factor turned his head, slowly, and looked at Dennis across the exchange floor.

And smiled.

Dennis left. He didn’t run. He’d learned not to run in markets; it made the price go up. He walked out of the grain exchange and down the steps and along the quay to the river-gate infirmary at a perfectly ordinary pace, and went in, and found Deston in the courtyard, and said, “The factor in the green coat. The big one. In the exchange.”

Deston went pale. “No threads.”

“No anything,” said Dennis. “He said lovely weather, and there was nobody inside it.”

Sabine doubled the watch that night.

She didn’t make a speech about it. She came up the stairs to the rooms above the chandler’s at supper, unbuckled her sword, sat down at the table, and said, “From tonight, we sleep at the infirmary. In shifts. Two awake at all times. Maud’s given us the corridor outside the long ward. I’ll take the first watch. Dennis, you’re with me.”

“Why me?” said Dennis.

Sabine looked at him.

“Because you’ll hear it before it gets here,” she said. “And because you won’t panic.” She paused. “And because you’ll lie to it, if you have to, and it won’t know.”

Gabe looked uneasy again. Dennis smiled at Sabine. Sabine didn’t smile back, but something moved at the corner of her mouth.

“And Rachael,” said Sabine, turning. “When I say go to the inner ward, you go to the inner ward. You don’t stay with a patient. You don’t finish a stitch. You go.”

“I’m not leaving someone halfway through—“

“Then you’ll be dead halfway through,” said Sabine. “And so will they.”

Rachael looked at her for a long time.

“Fine,” she said. “I’ll go.”

It rang like a cracked bell. Dennis heard it from across the table. So, he thought, did Sabine; she’d been listening to people lie for fifteen years too, in a different way. Neither of them said anything.

It came on the fourth night.

Dennis was on the second watch, with Sabine, in the long stone corridor outside the ward. Rain on the roof. The river loud beyond the outer wall, high with the autumn rains, running fast and black past the infirmary’s river-gate a storey below. A lamp in a niche at either end of the corridor. Brother Tamsin asleep on a bench in the hall. Gabe asleep against the ward door with his back to it and his blade across his knees, the way he’d slept every night since the first, so that anybody who wanted to open that door would have to move him first.

Rachael was still awake, in the long ward. A bargeman with a crushed hand had come in at dusk, and the bones wouldn’t set, and she’d been sitting with him for three hours with her hands round his, saying close under her breath, a finger’s width at a time.

Dennis was listening to the rain.

And then, under the rain, the river changed.

He didn’t hear a lie. That was the strange thing, afterwards. He’d been listening for one all night, the way you’d listen for a footstep, and it never came. What came was something else. A sound under the sound of the river. A wrongness. Like a bell that hasn’t been struck yet, but is about to be.

“Sabine,” said Dennis.

Sabine was already on her feet.

Out in the courtyard, through the rain, Calvin shouted. From the roof, where he’d been on watch all night, flat on the wet slates in a borrowed oilskin, feeling for the wind. One word, very high and very loud, in a voice Dennis had never heard Calvin use.

“WALL!”

And then the river wall came in.

Not the whole wall. A section of the old stone parapet at the far end of the courtyard, where the infirmary looked down on the river-gate. It burst inward in a spray of wet stone and mortar, as if a battering ram had hit it from the water side, and something came through the hole.

Dennis saw it from the corridor door, past Sabine’s shoulder, by the light of the courtyard lamp.

He’d read about them. In the channel, from Valcrest, at midsummer. Ina and Winter and Newish, the three of them, one after another, thirty feet, it jumped thirty feet, horns, it went straight for Winter. In Group 3’s report a month ago, three of the bigger ones. He’d read every word. He’d thought he knew.

It was taller than the corridor door. Heavier than Gabe. Grey, the grey of river mud, with horns curving back from where its ears should have been and arms that hung down past its knees and ended in hands that were mostly fingers. It had come up out of the river. It was streaming wet. It stood in the hole in the wall with the rain on it and turned its horned head, slowly, the way a hound turns when it’s casting for a scent.

It turned toward the long ward.

“Rachael!” Sabine’s voice cracked across the courtyard like a whip. “Inner ward! Now!”

From inside the ward, very faintly, over the rain: “I can’t, he’s—“

“NOW!”

The thing came across the courtyard.

Not running. It didn’t need to run. It came the way the ones at the Redmarch had come, Salty had said, in the channel: trotting, like a big dog, low, with its long arms swinging. Deston was in the courtyard. Dennis saw him, standing very still by the well in the rain with his face turned not toward the thing but toward the river. Toward the river-gate. Toward the quay beyond it, across the black water.

“They’re there,” said Deston. His voice was perfectly calm. “On the quay. Three of them. The green coat. The hat. They’re watching.”

Dennis looked.

Through the hole in the wall, across the river, on the far quay under a warehouse lamp, three figures stood in the rain. Not moving. Not hiding. Just standing there in a row, with the water running off their hats and coats, looking across the river at the infirmary. Watching the thing they’d sent.

Or that someone else had sent. And they’d come to watch it work.

Two sides, thought Dennis. Yan had written it, a year ago. They’re coming from two sides. He’d never thought about what it would look like when the two sides stood next to each other.

It looked like this. A Kharvos hunter coming across a courtyard in the rain, and the threadless people from the west, standing on a quay, watching it like men watching a dog work sheep.

Sabine met it in the middle of the courtyard.

Dennis had seen her fight before. In drills. Once at the night market, in the winter, when she’d cut the stranger’s coat with her edge reaching past her steel. He’d never seen her like this. She went at the thing the way a mercenary captain goes at anything that’s between her and her pay: without a word, without a wasted movement, low and fast, with her sword up and the light running along it and past it, Reaching, a hand’s width past the edge, two.

She went in under its reach and took it across the forearm, and the edge running past her steel went through grey hide and whatever was under it with a sound like a wet sail tearing. It screamed. Dennis had never heard anything like it: a high, rattling, furious shriek, like a fox in a trap with a man’s lungs. The black stuff came out in a spray, the stuff that wasn’t blood, and hissed where the rain hit it.

It hit her anyway. The other arm came round low and fast, and the back of its hand caught her in the ribs, and Sabine left the ground. She went across the courtyard sideways, six paces through the air, and hit the rim of the well with her hip, and folded over it, and went in head first; and Dennis heard her sword ring off the stones on the way down.

Gabe was there.

He’d come awake against the ward door with his blade already in his hands, the short heavy single-edged thing he’d made himself in the summer, a butcher’s cleaver married a gate, and he hadn’t come into the courtyard. He’d stayed exactly where he was. In front of the ward door. With his feet planted and his shoulders square and his aura coming up round him so hard Dennis could feel it from ten paces away, heavy and slow, the way it came up in Gabe: like a wall going up, stone by stone.

“Not past me,” said Gabe.

The thing came for the door.

Emrys sang.

He was on the gallery above the courtyard, in his nightshirt, with his hands on the rail. He’d been hoarse for ten months. Since the night market, when he’d thrown his whole Torch tier into one sung flash of light to blind the stranger, and his voice had never come back right. He sang now anyway. One note. High and clear and cracked down the middle, like a bell that had been dropped, and the light came with it: a flash, white, from the gallery, that lit the whole courtyard like noon for a heartbeat.

The thing flinched. It threw its long arm up over its eyes, half blind, and the blow it had meant for Gabe went wide by a hand’s breadth and hit the wall beside the door instead. The whole wall shook. A crack ran up the stone from where its fist had landed to the gallery above, fast as a thrown rope, and grit and old mortar came down on Gabe’s head. He didn’t move his feet.

And Calvin came off the roof.

He’d been up there all night, flat on the slates, feeling for the wind, the way he did on the masts. Now he came down the gutter pipe and off the gallery roof, a storey, half climbing and half falling, and as he came he shouted at the wind the way he’d been shouting at it all year. Turn. And for three heartbeats it did: a gust, a shove, out of nowhere on a still rainy night, that hit the thing broadside, and the thing staggered sideways three paces across the wet stones and nearly went over.

Calvin landed badly. Rolled. Came up on one knee, white-faced, holding his ankle.

“Turned it,” he said. “I turned it—“

The thing shook its head. Straightened. And looked past Gabe, past the door, at the long row of narrow windows that ran along the side of the long ward, a storey above the river. Looking for her. Casting. Its long fingers flexed.

Then it went for the corridor.

Not through the door. Gabe was in front of the door. It went round, along the courtyard wall, toward the corridor entrance where Dennis was standing. Because the corridor ran along the back of the ward. Because the inner ward was at the end of it. Because Rachael hadn’t gone to the inner ward. She’d said I can’t, he’s, and Dennis knew exactly what she’d meant: a bargeman with a crushed hand, three hours of close, a finger’s width at a time, and she wasn’t going to leave him halfway.

She was in the long ward still. Alone, with her patient. And the corridor was the way round.

Dennis stood in the corridor entrance.

He wasn’t a fighter. He’d never been a fighter. He’d been the treasurer, and the one who heard lies, and the one who stood near people with money. He had a knife. Gabe had made it for him, in the summer, after Rachael’s. He’d never used it for anything but cutting rope.

The thing was ten paces away. It was looking at him. Not at him. Through him. At the corridor behind him. At the inner ward at the end of it.

Where it thought she was.

Dennis thought: it doesn’t know.

It was very clear, suddenly. The way things went clear for him in the grain exchange, when somebody was lying and he could hear exactly where. The thing didn’t know where she was. It was casting, like a hound. It had come up out of the river and stood in the hole in the wall and turned its head, looking. It was looking for her the way you look for anything: with its eyes.

And eyes could be lied to.

He’d been trying for months. Illusion. That was what was written over his gate. He makes a demon scout see a wall where the road is. Emrys had said it in the first week, reading his window: a glamour mage, I think. Rare. Very rare. Dennis had been trying ever since, in the rooms above the chandler’s at night, to make a candle look like it was somewhere it wasn’t. To make his own hand look like it was a hand’s width to the left. He’d felt it, sometimes. Almost. A shimmer. Nothing more. He’d tried writing it, too, after Thea’s letter. Hide, over the candle, and nothing. Wall, across the doorway of his own room, and for half a heartbeat Sabine had walked straight into the doorframe, swearing; and for the whole of the next day he’d seen everything twice, two of every cup and two of every face, and walked into the doorframe himself. Both of them lies, he’d realised afterwards, lying on his bed with a wet cloth over his eyes. The world could hear a lie as well as he could.

Thea had written to all of them, last winter, about the edge: you have to write. One small line. In its language, not yours. And Chris had made a list, in the summer, of every line that had worked: they’re all asking something to do what it already wants to.

Eyes wanted to see. That was what eyes were for. They wanted to see the thing they were looking for.

The thing was eight paces away.

Dennis opened his window.

He didn’t look at the bar. He didn’t need to. He found the edge, the place Thea had written about, the gap at the bottom of everything where the window’s language ran under his own, and he could feel it there, the way you can feel a step in the dark with your foot. And he wrote one line in it. Not wall. Not hide. Not anything that wasn’t true, because that was the trick of it, the thing he’d learned from Declan without ever knowing he was learning it. A lie that rang true, because it was wearing the shape of something that was.

He wrote: There.

And he pointed.

Past the thing. Past the corridor entrance. Across the courtyard, up, at the long row of narrow windows along the river side of the ward. At the far end of them. The last window. The one that looked straight down over the river-gate onto the fast black water a storey below.

And Rachael was standing in it.

She was. Dennis could see her. In her infirmary apron, with her dark hair coming down out of its tie, and her hands glowing faintly gold the way they glowed when she was healing, standing at the far window with her back to the glass. Looking at the thing. As clear as the lamp.

She wasn’t there. She was forty paces away in the middle of the long ward, bent over a bargeman’s hand. Dennis knew that. He could hear the bell of it, ringing in him, the biggest lie he’d ever told, and it didn’t ring cracked at all. It rang perfectly true. Because it was the shape of something true. Because somewhere in that building, Rachael was standing at a window, holding her ground, refusing to leave. He’d just moved her forty paces.

The thing turned its head.

It saw her.

It went.

It went the way the one at Valcrest had gone, Ina had written. Not running. Gathering. Low, all at once, with its long arms out wide. Across the courtyard in two strides and up, off the wet stones, into the air, thirty feet, horns down, straight at the last window in the long row and the woman standing in it.

There was nobody standing in it.

It hit the window horns first. The glass went in a burst of glittering pieces, the leading tore like wet cloth, the whole oak frame went with it, splintering; and the thing went through the place where she wasn’t with its long arms closing on nothing. Out the other side, into the dark, over the river-gate, a storey up, still reaching, still twisting in the air to find her, and down.

Into the river.

The splash went up white in the warehouse lamps across the water, as high as the window it had gone through, and came down on the river-gate like a cartload of gravel. Then the river, loud and black and fast with the autumn rains, closed over it.

He sat down in the corridor entrance.

He didn’t mean to. His legs just went. He sat on the cold stone floor with his back against the doorframe and the rain coming in on him and his window still open in front of him, and he looked at it, because he couldn’t look at anything else.

[ Dennis · Tier 1: Spark · Skills: Glamour ]

The tenth. Of thirty-five. In a corridor, in the rain, in a nightshirt.

He started laughing. He couldn’t help it.

Across the courtyard, Sabine was climbing out of the well, which was dry, mercifully, and only waist-deep, with her sword still in her hand and blood running down her face from a cut over her eye. Gabe was still in front of the ward door with his blade up, staring at the broken window. Calvin was sitting on the stones holding his ankle. Emrys was on the gallery with both hands over his mouth. Deston was by the well.

Deston wasn’t looking at the window. He was looking at the river.

“Dennis,” he said.

Dennis got up. His legs held, more or less. He went across the courtyard to the hole in the wall, and stood beside Deston, and looked.

The thing came up out of the river on the far side, by the quay. Dennis saw it in the warehouse lamp: a long grey shape dragging itself up the slimy steps out of the black water, slowly, with one of its arms hanging wrong. The river watch was already running along the quay toward it, a dozen men with boathooks and lanterns, shouting. Sabine’s cut on its forearm was pouring black.

And the three figures on the quay, the ones who’d been watching, turned and looked at it. The green coat. The hat. The third one, a woman in a shawl.

They didn’t help it. They didn’t move toward it. They just looked at it for a moment, crawling up the steps with its arm hanging, the way you’d look at a tool that had broken. And then they turned away from it.

And walked into the river.

Exactly the way they had at the night market. All three, together, down the quay steps into the black water, without a splash, without a ripple, and gone. Leaving the river watch to the hunter on the steps, and the hunter to the river watch.

Except one of them looked back.

The one with the hat. On the last step, with the water to his knees, he turned his head and looked across the river at the infirmary, at the hole in the wall, at the broken window at the end of the long ward. And then at Dennis.

And smiled.

It didn’t make a sound. It was across a river in the rain. But Dennis heard it ring. For the first time in his life he heard a lie that didn’t have any words in it at all: a smile, made in the shape of a smile, with nothing inside it, and under that, something worse. Something that wasn’t a lie at all.

It had learned something. Dennis heard that ring perfectly true. It had watched a summoned boy make a hunter see a girl who wasn’t there, and it had learned that he could.

Then it went under, and was gone.

They killed the hunter on the quay steps. Dennis didn’t watch. He heard it. The river watch got their boathooks into it as it came up out of the water, a dozen of them, and held it there on the slimy steps, shouting, while it thrashed and shrieked and snapped two of the hooks like kindling. Then Sabine came over the river-gate bridge at a run with her face streaming blood and went down the steps at it, and there was one more shriek, cut off short in the middle, and a heavy sound like a sack of grain going off a cart into the water. Then only the rain.

He went into the long ward instead.

Rachael was still there. In the middle of the ward, on a stool, by the bargeman’s cot, with the bargeman’s crushed hand in both of hers and the faint gold light round her fingers. She hadn’t moved. Her face was white. She looked up when Dennis came in, and she didn’t say anything. Then she looked past him, at the far end of the ward, at the last window, which was now a hole full of rain.

“I saw myself,” she said. “In the window. Just for a second. I looked up and I was standing there.” She swallowed. “Was that you?”

“That was me.”

“It went through the window.”

“It did.”

“Into the river.”

“It was looking for you,” said Dennis. “I showed it where you were.” He sat down on the end of the next cot, because his legs were going again. “I lied.”

Rachael looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked down at the bargeman’s hand in hers, and her shoulders started shaking, and Dennis thought she was crying, and then realised she was laughing.

“That’s three times,” she said. “The hat man in the autumn. The night market. And this.” She wiped her eyes on her shoulder, without letting go of the hand. “Three times. And I’m still fine.”

It didn’t ring. For the first time in three months, fine rang perfectly true.

Sister Maud moved her to the inner ward that night, and wouldn’t hear a word about it. The bargeman’s hand had set. Rachael finished the last finger’s width before she went.

Dennis wrote it in the channel at two in the morning, sitting on the floor of the corridor with Sabine beside him holding a cloth to her eyebrow.

It went the way the channel always went now. He wrote it carefully, in five lines, the hunter and the threadless and the window and Rachael safe and Spark, Glamour, tenth, and the moment he finished, thirty people answered at once. Rachael’s I’m fine, really went up and off the top in a minute. Gabe’s she’s fine. i’m at the door. went after it. Xav, from Duskhollow, came in late, and asked what happened? where’s dennis’s post?, because it had already gone, all five lines, forty lines up and away while he was still waking up. Dennis wrote it again. It went again. Xav got the second half.

Dennis wrote it a third time. Then he gave up, and wrote:

[ Dennis: Thea we need more than forty lines.
Dennis: I’ve posted this three times and people still haven’t seen it. ]

And a few lines later, before it all went up and away again:

[ Althea: I know. I’m sorry. I’ve tried.
Althea: It’s the one part I can’t change. It’s written too deep. It won’t let me. ]

Dennis looked at that for a moment before it vanished.

Then he closed his window, and leaned his head back against the cold stone of the corridor wall, and listened to the rain, and the river, and Sabine breathing beside him.

“You lied to it,” said Sabine, after a while.

“I did.”

“Good.” She took the cloth away from her eyebrow and looked at it. “Best lie I ever saw.”

Dennis smiled at the ceiling.

“It wasn’t a lie,” he said. “Not really. She was standing at a window. Holding her ground. I just moved her.” He was quiet for a moment. “Someone taught me that. A long time ago. He didn’t know he was teaching me.”

Sabine looked at him sideways.

“Somebody who lied well?”

“Somebody who never lied at all,” said Dennis. “Even when he was lying.” He closed his eyes. “Especially then.”

Sabine didn’t ask what that meant. That was why they understood each other.
