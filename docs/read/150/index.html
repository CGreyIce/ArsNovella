The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 5: The Lists

Chapter 150

East

4,812 words · 21 min read

Yan had counted thirty-six from a grave, in the snow, in the winter.

He’d been sitting on the cairn on the Redmarch with Suzu round his neck and the black headstone beside him, half of a sword with four words on it in English, and the young tree Maigo had planted bare and asleep in its ring of stones. He’d been reaching. For the Earth grain. The thing he’d felt in all of them since the first day in the hall: the different grain their souls had, like wood from a different tree. He’d reached for it and his window had opened on its own and written Kin, and he’d felt them. All thirty-five. Across the kingdom. In seven places, like candles in seven windows of a house at night.

And one more. North-west. The biggest.

Thirty-six, he’d written in a letter, in Cursiffe’s blue ink. I counted.

He counted now.

He was sitting on the tailboard of Group 3’s cart on the first night out of Valcrest, at the edge of the downs, with the fire going and the stars coming out and Suzu warm round his neck. He reached, the way he’d reached on the cairn. And there they were.

Thirty-five. Across the kingdom. On four roads.

North, fifteen, a day’s walk up the long road toward Pellow’s Cross, camped round a brewer’s dray and a miller’s cart, close together. North-west, five, in the middle of four hundred people and two hundred horses, on the road to Solmere. West, five, half a day out of the west gate, going back toward a mountain pass. And five behind him, in a city, staying.

And here, at this fire, five more. Salty and Wendy, side by side. Maigo, talking. Mashi, chill. Himself.

And the thirty-sixth.

Sitting on the other side of the fire, on the ground, with a rod across his knees.

Close enough to touch.

He’d carried the water all day.

Aldous had told him he could. Yan had heard it, at the gate, at dawn: the man with the rod asking Aldous, very seriously, what should I carry? And Aldous, on the box of the cart with his book on his knee, had looked at him for a long time with his old grave chaplain’s face, the face of a man who’d buried the dead on the Redmarch the morning after and seen a black sword standing over a grave for eight years, and said, slowly: water.

So he’d carried the water. Two buckets on a yoke, all the way from the east gate of Valcrest over the shrine hill and down onto the plain and across it, twenty miles, at the back of the cart, beside his daughter. He hadn’t said much. He’d looked happy. Yan could feel it, the way you could feel weather: something very old and very large, carrying two buckets of water across a plain, and liking it.

Rhea had pushed the cart. The horse had objected all day. Maigo had talked to the horse. Wendy had shot two rabbits in the afternoon, from the back of the cart, without getting off, at sixty paces, while the cart was moving, and Salty had said show-off and she’d said yes.

And Alea had walked beside her father with her mother’s half of a black sword on her back and not said very much at all.

Yan had felt her all day too. He couldn’t help it. She was very bright. Not the way a healer was bright; the other way. Quick and hot and steady at the same time, like a sword that had just found out it fit. And something else under it, all day, that he’d been watching since the east gate and hadn’t yet put a name to.

Suzu needed feeding.

He felt it at dusk, the way he always did: the cord between them going a little cool, not cold, just cool, the way a stone cools when the sun goes off it. She’d eaten a hen two days ago, in Valcrest. A hen held her a day. He’d fed her a pigeon from the Lance’s roof yesterday, which Kei had caught for him without being asked, and she’d been fine. She was cool now.

Wendy was skinning the rabbits by the fire.

She looked up when he came over. She’d been in every call for years and in every fight for two, and she looked at him and at the snake round his neck and knew, without his saying anything.

“The second one’s still going,” said Wendy quietly. “I only winged it. It’s in the bag. It’s—“ She stopped. “I was going to finish it. Do you want—“

“Yes,” said Yan.

She gave him the bag. He took it a little way from the fire, to the edge of the dark, where the grass started. He knelt. He opened it.

Wendy came with him. She didn’t say anything. She knelt beside him in the grass and took the rabbit out, and did what she’d been going to do, quickly, the way an archer does, the way she’d done it a hundred times in the fort at Cinderhold for supper. And held it.

And the small pale light came out.

He didn’t look away.

He’d looked away every time for two years. In poulterers’ yards and behind barracks and in the snow on the red. He’d turned his head and let Suzu take it and felt the warmth come down the cord and hated it, every time, the way you hate a thing you have to do and can’t stop doing.

She says you don’t have to look away, Kei had said, in a poulterer’s yard in Valcrest. She says it’s not a thing to be ashamed of. It’s just eating.

So he watched.

He watched the little pale light lift out of the rabbit in Wendy’s hands, the size of a candle flame across a room. Wavering. Looking for something. He watched Suzu lift her head off his collarbone and turn it, very slowly, and look at the light with her two green eyes. He watched her tongue go out.

And the light went to her.

It was beautiful. That was the thing he’d never let himself see. Two years of looking away, and he’d never once seen it. The way it went. Not snatched. Not swallowed. It went to her the way a moth goes to a lamp, slowly, gladly, as if it had been looking for her all along and was relieved, at the end, to have found somewhere warm to go.

And was gone.

And down the cord, warmth.

Yan knelt in the grass at the edge of the firelight with Wendy beside him and a white snake warm round his neck and didn’t say anything for a long time.

“Okay?” said Wendy. Very quietly.

“Okay,” said Yan.

She didn’t ask anything else. She put her hand on his back, once, between the shoulder blades, the way you’d steady somebody going up a slope. Then she took the rabbit back to the fire.

He couldn’t hear Suzu any more.

He’d known he wouldn’t, after Valcrest. Hisui was going west, round Kei’s neck, twenty days to Thornwall. Nobody else in the kingdom could hear a snake. For a week he’d had somebody to tell him what she was saying, every word, all of it, and now he didn’t, and she was round his neck warm and fed and saying, he knew, a great many things, and he couldn’t hear one of them.

She says you’ll have to go back to guessing, Kei had said, on the roof, on the last night. She says guess better this time.

He wasn’t going to guess. He’d decided that on the roof. He’d guessed for six years, on stream and off, Suzu says hi chat, Suzu’s jealous, Suzu wants the window shut, and been wrong every time but once. He wasn’t going to guess any more.

He was just going to know.

That was the difference. He’d worked it out walking across the plain all day behind the cart. You guessed when you didn’t know. And he knew, now. He knew she’d liked the sheep. He knew she liked Aldous because he smelled of candles. He knew the hen had been fine and she was grateful and she’d rather never eat people. He knew she wasn’t jealous, she was correct. He knew she called him Yun, and that it had always been her, and never a bit.

He knew the feeling was mutual.

He didn’t need to hear her say it. She’d said it. Through a green snake on a roof, once. That was enough. That would be enough for a long time.

He put his hand up and rested it on her, very lightly, where she lay round his neck.

The cord was warm all the way down.

The fire was loud.

Group 3’s fires always were. They’d been loud at Cinderhold in the barracks for two years, and loud on the road south to Valcrest, and they were loud now, on the first night back, as if they’d been saving it up. Maigo was telling everybody about the tree. Rhea was trying to arm-wrestle Salty and Salty was eating rabbit and refusing. Aldous was reading by the light of the fire, or pretending to.

And Alea was listening to Maigo.

She was sitting on the ground beside her father, with Astra lying in the grass beside her and her knees pulled up and her chin on them. She hadn’t said anything all evening. But she was listening to Maigo with her whole body, the way you listen to somebody telling you about a place you’re going to and have never been.

“—and it’s an ash,” said Maigo. He was sitting on the cart’s tailboard with his hood down and his antlers full of summer leaves, and his white glowing eyes behind his pixel sunglasses fixed on Alea. “A little one. I grew it from a seed off the fort’s tree. In the spring. The first spring. I went out on my own and planted it inside the ring of flowers, so she wouldn’t be alone.” He was quiet, for Maigo. “It was this high.” He held his hand a foot off the tailboard. “Last summer it was taller than me. Now it’s taller than Salty.”

“Everything’s taller than Salty,” said Wendy.

“Everything’s taller than Salty,” agreed Maigo, delighted. “But it’s taller than Rhea. It’s taller than Aldous.” He looked at Alea. “It’s got leaves. It’ll have leaves when you get there. It’s the only tree on the whole of the red. You can see it from the fort wall.”

Alea didn’t say anything. She was looking at Maigo very hard.

“I talk to it,” said Maigo. “When we go out there. Every month. I tell it what’s happening. I told it about the lake. I told it about you.” He swallowed. “I told it you’d come one day. I didn’t know you would. I just told it.” He looked down at his hands. “Trees like being told things.”

“There are stones,” said Wendy.

She’d finished the rabbits. She was sitting by the fire with her headphones round her neck and her star-and-moon necklace catching the light, and she said it quietly, the way Wendy said things when they mattered and she didn’t want anyone to notice that they did.

Alea turned her head.

“On the cairn,” said Wendy. “Under the headstone. Children’s stones. From Wrenhaven.” She looked at the fire. “Your town. Every child in Wrenhaven brought a stone to the forge on the lake shore, last autumn, while the king made the headstone. A pile of them. Knee-high.” She was quiet. “We carried them out to the grave. In the winter. With the headstone. A handful each. The whole way across the red.”

Alea’s ears had gone very still.

“I carried eleven,” said Wendy. “One of them was white. From the lake shore. Somebody’d put it on the top of the pile.” She looked at her own hands. “I put that one at the head. Next to the stone. Where you’d see it first.”

Alea didn’t say anything for a long time.

“Who put it on the pile?” she said eventually. Very quietly. “The white one.”

Wendy shook her head. “I don’t know. It was just there.”

Beside Alea, on the ground, her father had gone very still. Yan felt it. The thing in him that was older than his body, from somewhere else, going very still the way a lake goes still when the wind drops.

He knew, Yan thought. He knows who put the white stone on the pile.

He didn’t say.

Salty carried the headstone.

Nobody said it. Everyone knew it. Salty didn’t say anything about it, because he never did; he just sat by the fire with a rabbit leg in his hand and the king’s father’s hammer in his belt and his crooked ribs, and when Maigo said and Salty carried the stone the whole way, Salty said it was fine and went on eating.

Alea looked at him.

“It’s half a sword,” said Alea. “The headstone. It took six soldiers and a winch to move the whole sword.”

“It was fine,” said Salty.

“Your ribs—“

“They match the sword now,” said Salty, bluntly. “They knitted crooked. I said they’d match.” He took another bite of rabbit. “It was fine. It wasn’t heavy. Not really.”

“It was heavy,” said Wendy. “He was sick twice.”

“I was fine.”

Alea looked at him for a long time. Then she got up. She went round the fire, past Maigo on the tailboard and Rhea and Aldous with his book, to where Salty was sitting with his rabbit, and stood in front of him. A fox girl of seventeen with her mother’s face, looking down at a short broad man who’d carried her mother’s headstone across a battlefield with broken ribs.

“Thank you,” said Alea.

Salty looked up at her. His blunt square face did something he didn’t seem to have meant it to do.

“It was fine,” he said. Very quietly. And then: “You’re welcome.”

Rhea arm-wrestled the man with the rod after supper.

She’d been working up to it since the shrine hill. She’d pushed the cart up it, and down it, and across twenty miles of plain, and she’d been asking every hour since noon, after supper, after supper, I’ve still got arms. So after supper she put her elbow on the tailboard of the cart and held out her hand and glared, and the man with the rod looked at her for a long moment, and put his elbow on the tailboard.

It took about as long as it took to blink.

Rhea’s arm went over and hit the tailboard. She stared at it. Then at him. Then she threw her head back and laughed so loudly the horse looked round from its picket.

“AGAIN,” said Rhea.

“Cinderhold,” said the man.

“AGAIN.”

“At the fort,” said the man. “In front of the garrison. If you’ve still got arms.”

“I’ll always have arms,” said Rhea, delighted, and went off round the fire to tell Aldous, who already knew, because everybody already knew, because Rhea had said it loudly enough for the fort to hear twenty days away.

Yan found him at the edge of the firelight, later, when the others had gone quiet.

He was sitting on the ground a little way off, where the grass started, with the rod across his knees, the way he’d sat on the bottom tier of the Lists and the end of a bench and, Yan supposed, the end of a jetty for eight years. Looking east. At nothing. At the dark, where the plain went on toward the downs and the river and, a long way off, the red.

Yan sat down beside him.

They didn’t say anything for a while. The fire crackled behind them. Somebody was snoring. Maigo, probably. Maigo snored like a tree in the wind.

“Your mother,” said the man, eventually.

Yan went still.

“You’ve never said,” said the man. “In the channel. Not once. You write okay at the bottom of everything and you keep everyone going and you never once put it on anyone.” He was quiet. “But I’ve read every line you’ve written for two years. You’ve written the word home four times. Every time, it was about her.” He paused. “She’s the reason. For the tenth tier. You’ve wanted to get to the top since the first winter. For her.”

Yan didn’t say anything for a long time.

“Two months,” he said eventually. “There. Since the hall.” His voice was very even. “She’ll have been waiting two months. She’ll still be waiting. She won’t have stopped.” He looked at the dark. “She’s the only one who would.”

The man didn’t say anything.

“I don’t talk about it,” said Yan. “Because it’s mine. Everyone’s got somebody. Everyone’s got a mother or a father or a sister or a boyfriend, waiting. Two months. I’m not the only one.” He breathed. “I’m just the one who has to get there.”

“You don’t have to.”

“I know.”

“Somebody does,” said the man. “Not you. Not on your own.”

“I know,” said Yan again. “Thea wrote TODO. In the blank half. Cursiffe told the channel.” He looked down at his hands. “I read it at the long table and I thought: that’s me. Somebody’s coming back to it. And it’s going to be me.”

“It might be.”

“Or Thea.”

“Or Thea,” the man agreed.

“I lost mine,” said the man. Very quietly. After a while.

Yan turned his head.

“There,” said the man. “Before all this. When I was nineteen.” He didn’t say anything else about it. He didn’t need to. “I know what it is to have somebody be the only one. And then not.” He looked at the dark. “I’d give a lot for you to get home to yours, Yan.”

Yan didn’t say anything. He couldn’t.

He sat on the grass at the edge of the firelight beside a man who’d died in his sleep in another world and lost his mother at nineteen before that, and whose father was a grey thread thirty years long going out of the world toward a car-park wall, and who’d never once, in thirty-four years, had a way back. And who’d just said I’d give a lot for you to get home to yours.

As if it were simple.

As if a man who’d never get his own door would naturally want everyone else to have theirs.

“Okay,” said Yan.

It was the only word he had. It always was.

“Which way does yours lean?” said the man.

Yan looked at him.

“Your soul,” said the man. “You told them, in the chapel. On the red. Maigo told me, at the long table; Maigo tells me everything. Souls lean toward what they love. Like a plant on a windowsill.” He was quiet. “You said Suzu’s leaned toward you. You said mine leaned toward each of them. Like a fire when you’ve come in from the cold.” He paused. “Nobody asked which way yours leaned.”

Yan didn’t answer for a long time.

Nobody ever had. That was true. Two years of seeing souls, of seeing which way every candle in a room leaned, of telling people about it when it mattered and not when it didn’t. And nobody had ever once turned round and asked him.

He didn’t need to look. He’d never been able to see his own; you couldn’t, any more than you could see the back of your own head. But he knew. The way you know which way you’re facing in a dark room.

He put his hand up and rested it on the white snake round his neck.

“You know which way,” said Yan.

The man looked at the hand on the snake for a long moment.

“I know,” he said. “I just thought somebody should ask.”

Round Yan’s neck, Suzu lifted her head. Just slightly. And put it down again, against his collarbone, closer than before.

And down the cord, warmth. Not fed warmth. The other kind. The kind he’d felt on the corner of a high street in Valcrest when she leaned out to touch the back of a man’s hand.

He didn’t guess what it meant. He knew.

The man took the first watch. Nobody argued. Then Mashi took the second, chill and wide awake, sitting on the tailboard with his window open in front of his face, looking at a grey line under two white ones.

Yan took the third.

He sat on the tailboard where Mashi had been, after Mashi had gone to sleep, with the scythe across his knees and Suzu round his neck and the fire burning low, and the stars very thick over the plain, the way they were out here, away from any city. The others were asleep round the fire. Salty and Wendy together, under one blanket. Maigo with his antlers against the cart wheel, snoring like a tree. Rhea flat on her back with her arms out, as if she’d been knocked down. Aldous sitting up against his pack with his book open on his chest.

And on the far side of the fire, a man asleep. For once. On his back, with his rod beside him and his coat over him. Gabe had made him promise. Every night. On the road. I’m going to ask Yan.

Yan would tell Gabe yes.

And beside him, curled on her side in a blanket, with her head on her father’s arm and her black sword in the grass beside her, his daughter.

Yan reached out.

He did it the way he’d done it in the Lists in the torchlight, and on the cairn in the snow. A candle at a time. Not reaching far. Just round the fire. The people asleep on the ground in the dark, each one a small warm flame of soul, leaning, the way candles always leaned, toward whatever they loved.

Salty’s leaned toward Wendy. It always had. Hers toward his. Two candles under one blanket, leaning so close they were nearly one.

Maigo’s leaned east. Toward the red. Toward a young ash tree inside a ring of flowers on a cairn a month away, that he talked to every month and had told about a girl who’d come one day.

Mashi’s was two candles. They always had been. Aura and mana. Side by side, close, almost touching, the way they’d been since the first spring. Closer tonight than Yan had ever seen them. As if something had shown them, a few days ago, in an empty arena at dawn, that the space between them didn’t need to be there.

Rhea’s leaned toward everything at once, the way a fire leans toward air.

Aldous’s leaned east too. Toward the red. Toward all the dead he’d buried there the morning after.

The man with the rod didn’t have a candle. He never had. He had the other thing, older than his body and from somewhere else, that Yan had felt on the red a year ago and never been able to name. It wasn’t a flame. It was more like the place a flame had been. Warm. Very large. Very quiet. Asleep, for once.

And it leaned toward the girl asleep on his arm.

All of it. The whole of it. The way the whole of a lake leans toward the low end, toward the place where the water goes out.

And Alea’s leaned east.

Yan had been watching it all day. Since the east gate. He’d felt it go out of the city with her, at the back of the cart, beside her father with the buckets, and he’d felt it all the way across the plain, and he hadn’t put a name to it until now.

It leaned east.

Not toward her father. Her father was right there, under her head. Not toward the fire, or Maigo, or the stars. East. Past the cart and the horse on its picket and the edge of the firelight and the plain going away in the dark. East toward the downs and the river and, a month away, a long red plain with grey grass on it and a fort on its edge. And an hour’s walk from the fort, a cairn of children’s stones under half of a black sword, with four words on it in English and a white stone at its head and a young ash tree beside it, in a ring of flowers.

A grave she’d never seen.

Her soul was leaning toward it the way Suzu’s leaned toward him. Like a plant on a windowsill. All night, in her sleep, on her father’s arm. Toward a woman with cold hands who’d stitched up a boy three times in a month at Cinderhold and asked him why he kept getting hurt, and stood on the end of a half-ruined jetty and said here, and sent her daughter, through the man who held her at the end, a mother’s last words. Stay healthy. Live well. Listen to your father. I’m sorry, and I love you.

Toward her mother.

Yan sat on the tailboard of the cart with the scythe across his knees and watched it lean, all night, the whole of the third watch, and didn’t look away once.

He thought, near dawn, about counting.

He’d counted thirty-six from a grave in the snow. Thirty-five across a kingdom and one more, north-west, the biggest. He’d written it in a letter. He’d been right.

He counted again now. Not with Kin. Just with his eyes, round the fire.

Five of Group 3. Aldous and Rhea. A man with a rod. A girl with a sword.

And one more.

Not here. A month east. Under a black stone with four words on it, in a ring of flowers, beside a tree. A woman nobody in Group 3 had ever met, whose name they’d learned from a soldier’s story on the red and a girl’s box on a roll in front of forty thousand people. Who Maigo talked to a tree about. Who Wendy had put a white stone at the head of. Who Salty had carried the headstone for with his ribs broken.

Who the girl asleep by the fire was leaning toward, all night, in her sleep, the way a plant leans toward a window.

Nine, thought Yan. We’re nine, going east. And one more waiting.

He’d tell the channel in the morning. Okay, he’d write, at the bottom, the way he always did. And nothing else. Because some things weren’t his to give.

The sky went grey over the plain to the east.

Yan sat on the tailboard and watched it. Over the downs, a long way off, the light coming up the way it came up on the red: slowly, and then all at once, grey and then pale and then gold. The direction they were going. The direction a girl’s soul had been leaning all night.

Round his neck, Suzu stirred.

He felt it down the cord before she moved. The way he felt everything with her. Warmth, and something under the warmth, the thing he’d felt since the first hour in the hall and never once put into words until a green snake on a roof made him.

She lifted her head off his collarbone. She looked at him, very close, in the grey light, with her two green eyes.

And he felt it.

Not heard. He couldn’t hear her. Nobody in this world could, now, except a green snake going west round a calm man’s neck, twenty days away. He didn’t hear it. He felt it, down the cord, into his chest, the way you feel your own name said by somebody in a crowded room before you’ve heard the word. One syllable. Warm. The half of Suzuyun that wasn’t her. The name only she had ever called him, in two worlds, in his own voice on stream and then, it turned out, in hers.

Yun.

He didn’t guess. He knew.

“Morning,” said Yan. Very quietly. To her. “I know.”

Suzu put her head back down on his collarbone, and closed her eyes, satisfied, as if she’d decided something and wasn’t going to discuss it.

On the far side of the fire, a man woke, and lay still for a moment on his back with his coat over him, looking at the sky. Then he turned his head and looked at his daughter, asleep on his arm, with her soul leaning east. And didn’t move his arm. Not for a long time. Not until the sun was all the way up.

Then the cart went on, east, toward a grave.
