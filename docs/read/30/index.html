The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 1: Thirty-Four Winters

Chapter 30

Thirty-Four Winters

2,706 words · 12 min read

Ars was on the jetty when it happened.

He’d told Alea he’d be mending something, and it was true. He’d brought the big net down with him after supper, the one with the long tear along the bottom where a pike had gone through it in the autumn, and he was sitting at the end of the boards in the dark with his coat buttoned to the throat and the net across his knees, working by feel. It was very cold. The lake had started to freeze at the edges; he could hear it, a faint ticking and creaking all along the shore, like a house settling. Up on the hill, Wrenhaven was a smear of gold against the black: lanterns, hundreds of them, and the great orange glow of the midwinter bonfire in the market square, throwing sparks into the sky.

He didn’t go to the festival. He never did. He’d told Alea it was because he didn’t like crowds, and that was true too. But mostly it was because Rowan would make a speech, and people would look at him during it, and he’d rather be here.

So he was on the jetty, tying knots, when the first candle caught.

He felt it the way you feel a change in the weather.

For six months he’d been able to sense them, up on the hill: five faint flickers of mana in the militia hall, five candles not yet lit. He hadn’t tried to. It was just there, the way the sound of the lake was there, or the hens. Five small presences, slowly brightening week by week, so slowly you’d never have noticed if you hadn’t spent thirty-four years learning to notice things.

And one of them, tonight, lit.

It was very small. A single bead of light, up there in the square, among the hundreds of lanterns and the roar of the bonfire. If he hadn’t known exactly where to look he’d have missed it.

But he knew exactly where to look. He’d known since the first week, on the market road, when he’d walked past five girls in clothes from another world and felt one of them watching the space under her own window as if it were a page of writing.

Thea.

He stopped with the net in his hands.

The first tier. Six months, more or less, since the summoning. He knew what it felt like. He’d done it himself a very long time ago, at fourteen, in a field behind his father’s house: the first time the aura came up through him and stayed. He’d stood in that field with his hands shaking and laughed out loud, and his father had come running out of the house thinking he’d hurt himself.

The first time the world answers back.

He sat on the end of the jetty in the cold and the dark, and up on the hill, very small and very far away, a girl he’d watched on a glowing screen in a room in Malacca held a flame on the end of her finger for the first time.

He found that he was smiling. He couldn’t have stopped if he’d tried.

Alea came home at a run.

He heard her long before he saw her: feet pounding down the lake road through the snow, much too fast for the ice, and her breath coming in great white gasps. He was back in the cabin by then, by the fire, with the net put away and a pot of something warming. She burst through the door in a gust of cold and snow with her red scarf half off and her ears straight up and her face blazing.

“Dad. Dad, Dad, Dad—“

And stopped.

He watched her stop. Watched it come up into her face and catch there, in the doorway, with the snow melting on her shoulders: the thing she’d run all the way down the lake road to tell him, and, standing in front of it, the thing she’d never told him at all.

“I know,” said Ars.

She went very still. “Know what?”

“That it was a good bonfire.” He took the pot off the fire. “I could see the sparks from the jetty.”

For a long moment she just looked at him: at his face, at his hands, at the two cups he’d already set out on the table. Then she let out a breath that went on for a long time, and shut the door, and came in, and sat down at the table, and told him about the bonfire.

All of it. Almost. The sparks going up, thousands of them, higher than the church. Rowan’s speech, which had been even longer than last year’s. The whole town in the square, and the fiddler, and somebody’s dog stealing a sausage off the brazier and being chased round the well by half the children in Wrenhaven. Everything.

Everything except the one thing.

Ars listened to all of it. He drank his tea and let her talk, and watched her face, which was lit up like the bonfire, and thought that he hadn’t seen her this happy since she was very small. And he didn’t say one word about the candle he’d felt catch on the hill.

“And then I said,” said Alea, and stopped.

“Said what?”

She looked at the table. “Somebody was talking about the first tier. At the fire. And I said what you said. That it’s the first time the world answers back.” She looked up at him, a little guiltily. “I said my dad always said. That’s all. Just… my dad.”

“That’s all right,” said Ars.

“Is it?”

“It’s true,” he said. “I did always say it.”

She looked at him a moment longer. The heaviness was still there, at the back of her eyes. It had been there since the end of summer, and it hadn’t gone. Whatever she was carrying, tonight hadn’t set it down. But tonight it was small, under all the joy. Tonight she was sixteen, and something wonderful had happened to someone she loved, and nothing else mattered very much.

“Go to bed,” he said. “It’s late.”

She went. At the foot of the stairs she stopped and turned round.

“Dad,” she said. “Are you happy?”

Ars thought about it, honestly, the way she deserved.

“Yes,” he said. “Very.”

She smiled, and went up.

He waited until he heard her breathing slow. Then he opened his window.

He hadn’t looked at it since the autumn. He’d decided not to, that night on the jetty with the quiet all around him, because he’d been afraid that if he watched the number he’d start wanting it, the way he’d wanted every tier before. And the wanting would ruin it.

But tonight he wanted to know.

Forty-four.

He sat very still in the firelight and looked at it.

Forty, for six years. Forty-one, in the autumn, the night of the horns, when the quiet had come and stayed till dawn. And now forty-four.

Three percent in a season. After six years of nothing.

He made himself think about it. Carefully, the way he’d once thought about a battle the night before, laying out every piece. When did it move? Not when he’d trained; he hadn’t trained, not properly, in years. Not when he’d fought; he hadn’t fought since the Redmarch. It had moved in the autumn and the winter, out here, by the lake, while he did nothing more remarkable than mend nets and dig pumpkins and feed hens that hated him.

No. That wasn’t quite true. That wasn’t all he’d done.

He’d put two fish in a basket every morning for five months, and never said a word.

He’d let his daughter walk up the lake road every day to sit around a fire with five girls from a life he’d lost, and never once asked her to stop, or to tell him about it.

He’d taken a pair of horns out from under the floor, horns he’d carried home across a battlefield seven years ago and never been able to look at, and sent them east to a fort full of people he’d never met, so that something counting the towers in the dark would think twice.

He’d put a third fish in the basket when the winter came.

And every time, every single time, the quiet had come afterwards. The world going still around him, listening. And the number had moved.

He sat by the fire for a long time, thinking about that.

For thirty-four years he’d carried it. He didn’t often admit that to himself, but it was true. He’d carried the life before. Not as an ache, after the first ten years or so, but as a weight: a stone in his chest that he’d stopped noticing because it was always there. A room in Malacca. A fan that clicked on every third turn. A call at two in the morning, and four voices saying goodnight, and his own voice saying trust, the last word he’d ever said in that world. Every friend he’d ever had there, gone in one night, as completely as if they’d never existed.

He’d made his peace with it. He’d told himself he had. He’d built a cabin, and a garden, and a life, and he’d raised a daughter, and he’d stopped thinking about the stone.

And then thirty-five of them had walked out of the light.

He’d thought, at first, that their coming would make the stone heavier. That’s what he’d braced for, that first night on the jetty, saying what did you do, Halvard to the black water. He’d thought it would open the room in Malacca back up, and the fan, and the call, and all of it would come pouring out, and he’d have to carry it all over again.

It hadn’t. That was the strange thing. It was the opposite.

Every fish in the basket had made it lighter. Every morning he’d let Alea go up the hill without a word. The horns, going east. The third fish. Not because any of it fixed anything. He couldn’t go back. He couldn’t stand in front of them and say it’s me, not yet, maybe not ever. They had their own lives here now, their own fights, their own ladders to climb. They didn’t need a sixty-year-old man walking up to them on a market road with a face they’d never seen, telling them a story they’d never believe.

But he could do this. He could be in the background. He could reach into their lives very quietly, from a long way off, and make them a little warmer, a little safer, a little better fed. He could help without being seen.

And every time he did, something in him came loose. Some knot he’d been carrying for thirty-four years untied itself, one strand at a time, and the world noticed, and went quiet, and listened.

Nobody climbs to the ninth tier by fighting, the old swordmaster had said. You have to find your own way up.

He’d thought, in the autumn, that his way was the slow life. The nets and the pumpkins and the hens. And it was. But it wasn’t only that.

It was this. Letting go of the life he’d lost by quietly taking care of the people who’d lost him. Not to get them back. Just because he could, and because it was the right thing to do, and because every time he did it, his heart got a little lighter and his mind got a little clearer, and the long weight of thirty-four years came down off his shoulders a little more.

He closed the window.

He didn’t need to look at the number again. He knew which way it was going. And he knew, now, what made it move.

He made a list, after that. Not on paper. In his head, the way he’d once planned a campaign.

Small things. Nothing anyone would notice. Nothing anyone could trace back to a fisherman on Lake Ilse.

There was a girl in the militia hall who’d chosen aura, the hard path, and was climbing it one percent a week with a patience he recognised. He knew three things about aura that would help her, things he’d learned the hard way and never written down. He’d teach them to Alea, in the mornings, on the jetty, as if they were simply the next lesson. Alea would take them up the hill without knowing why. That would be enough.

There was a man in Wrenhaven called Rowan, who owed him a great deal and would ask no questions. And there was a militia hall at the top of the town with a roof that had leaked in the autumn and walls that would be very cold by February. Five winter coats, perhaps. Firewood. Sent up from the town, from the mayor, as a gift to the summoned.

And there was the book. The one he’d been writing on and off since the summer, by the fire, in the evenings. Stories, and notes about fish, and the occasional joke he couldn’t resist. The trader would take it south in the spring, the way he always did, and sell it under no name at all. He’d add a chapter. Just one. About waiting. About how the first tier doesn’t come when you chase it, and how it’s like waiting for a fish: you breathe from the very bottom of you, and you stop trying to make it come, and one day it does.

Somebody, somewhere, might need that. He’d never know who. That was the point.

It started to snow again a little before midnight.

He went down to the jetty one last time before bed. He told himself it was to check the ice. Mostly it was because he wanted to be out there, in the dark, with the lake creaking and the snow coming down and the last of the bonfire glowing on the hill.

The snow fell straight and thick and silent. It settled on the boards, and on his shoulders, and on the black ice spreading out from the shore. Up in Wrenhaven, the lanterns were going out one by one. Somewhere up there, in the militia hall, a girl was probably lying awake with a little flame on her finger, not wanting to let it go out.

He counted, without meaning to.

Thirty-four winters, here. Thirty-four years of snow on this lake, or lakes like it. Thirty-four years since he’d opened his eyes as a baby in a commoner’s house and thought, in a language nobody around him spoke, where am I?

And for them?

He didn’t know. He’d never let himself work it out. They’d come through the floor looking exactly the way he remembered them, but that proved nothing: most of them wore faces they’d drawn for themselves, and drawn faces don’t age. Thirty-four years, then, probably, back there too. A world that had gone on without him. The fan in the empty room in Malacca long since thrown away. His father old by now, or gone. A group chat that had gone quiet one night and never started up again, and every one of them grown up and grown older and come here carrying someone they’d lost half a lifetime ago.

He’d thought, once, that all those years would be unbearable. That they would always stand between them like a wall.

Standing on the jetty in the snow, with his daughter asleep in the cabin behind him and a girl he’d once watched on a screen holding her first flame on the hill, he didn’t think it was a wall anymore.

It was just a distance. And distances could be crossed. Slowly. A fish at a time.

He stood there until the snow had covered his boots. Then he went back inside, and banked the fire, and went to bed.

In the morning there were two fish in the basket by the door. And a third. And, after a moment’s thought, a fourth.

It was going to be a long winter.
