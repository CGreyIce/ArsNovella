The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 2: Letters

Chapter 39

Ossie

4,262 words · 19 min read

Bee didn’t cry when Ice died.

She’d thought about that a lot, lately. More than she’d thought about it at the time. At the time she’d just been sorry: sorry for Hyvred, who’d cried for a week; sorry for the group chat, which had gone quiet in that horrible way chats go quiet; sorry in a general, distant, decent sort of way for a man she’d spoken to maybe four times in four months. He’d been funny in Hyvred’s streams. He’d called Hyvred’s clip the Flashlight mod and then apologised so many times that Hyvred had laughed until she wheezed. He’d once sent Bee a single heart emoji under a drawing of the Pollen Puffs. That was it. That was everything.

So she hadn’t cried. And then she’d been dragged through a floor into another world with thirty-four people who’d all loved him, and every one of them had a story, and every one of them went quiet sometimes in the middle of a sentence, and she’d sat there with her charcoal and her blunt mouth and the Puffs on her shoulders, and felt like a guest at somebody else’s funeral.

Nobody had ever said so. Hyvred would have hit anyone who said so. But Bee knew.

That was why, she thought afterwards, it was her who drew the dog.

Three weeks after Sergeant Dunmore’s ten walked over the ridge, Thornwall had settled into a new shape.

The patrols went out in twenties now, never tens, and never further than the burned hut. Brannoch had doubled the watch on the north wall and had the carpenter build a second gate inside the first, so that anything coming in would have to stand in a stone box between two doors while somebody looked at it. Fenwick had stopped sleeping, as far as Bee could tell; he lived in the long room with Corvin and the maps, and the two of them talked in low voices into the night, about passes and dates and the number of villages that had emptied since midwinter. Six, now. Seven, if you counted the shepherds’ huts above Coldwater.

The soldiers had stopped joking about the summoned. They’d started asking Hyvred, very politely, whether she’d mind looking at them when they came in from patrol. Just a glance. Just to be sure.

She never minded. She stood at the inner gate every evening in a borrowed cloak, with her glasses pushed up into her hair and her crosshair eye very bright, and looked at every single soldier who came through, one after another, and said, “You’re fine. You’re fine. You’re fine,” until it sounded like a blessing.

And every morning, at first light, Tiger ran the walls alone.

Bee watched him from the tower window sometimes. Round and round the battlements, the way he’d always done, except now there was nobody shouting up from the yard. Bet you can’t beat me to the gate. He ran faster than he used to. He’d gone from twenty-four to twenty-six since the night on the wall, and twice more, sparring with Drew, he’d done the thing with the gap. Go. Three paces. Four, the second time. He didn’t celebrate either time. He just stood there afterwards, breathing hard, looking north.

The shed was still there, against the inside of the north wall. Three crooked walls, no roof, a gap where the door should go. Nobody had touched it. The soldiers walked round it on their way to the latrines, carefully, like you’d walk round a grave.

Bee’s own window said twenty.

Twenty, after ten months, which was slow; slower than Hyvred, slower than Kei, about the same as Drew. She didn’t mind slow. She’d been drawing since she was four. She knew exactly how long it took to get good at something, and it was always longer than you wanted, and the only way through was to keep doing it every single day whether you felt like it or not.

So she drew every single day.

On the walls of the long room, until Corvin begged her to stop and gave her a whitewashed section of the old granary instead. On the backs of maps. On boards, on slates, on the flat stones of the courtyard with a lump of chalk the quartermaster had found for her. Birds, mostly, at first, because of the sparrow. Then cats, and horses, and the Puffs over and over, round and fat with their tiny blue wings. And she watched, every time, for the thread.

It came more often now. Not always. Maybe one drawing in ten. A thin line of light from the tip of her finger into the charcoal, a shiver, a lift, and then the thing would move. A wing would beat once. A cat would turn its head. For three heartbeats, sometimes four, and then it would go flat and still and be charcoal again.

Then Thea’s letter had come, in the winter. You have to write. One small line, in its language, not yours.

Everyone had read it differently. Tiger had read it and thought he couldn’t write. Kei had read it and started keeping a book of what Hisui told him about every herb, in English, very small. Hyvred had read it and cried, because she’d always been the one who saw things and never the one who made them.

Bee had read it once, and put it down, and said, “Well, obviously.”

They’d all looked at her.

“Drawing’s writing,” Bee had said. “It’s the first writing. Before letters there were pictures.” She’d shrugged. “Thea’s a coder. She thinks in lines of text. I think in lines. Same thing. Different language.”

And then she’d gone to the granary wall and drawn a sparrow, very slowly, with one long continuous line that never lifted from the plaster: the way you’d write a word in joined-up writing. And she’d felt it. For the first time, she’d felt it. A place where the line wanted to go, under her hand, that wasn’t quite the wall. A gap. And she’d drawn into the gap.

The sparrow had flown off the wall.

Not far. Not for long. It had lifted out of the plaster, flat and black and made of lines, and flapped twice in the air in front of her face with a sound like paper, and then come apart into charcoal dust and fallen on her boots.

Four heartbeats. Out of the wall. In the air.

She hadn’t told anyone. She’d just stood there with her black fingers, looking at the dust on her boots, while the Puffs went mad around her head. Then she’d gone back to work.

Since then she’d done it twice more. A cat that walked three steps along the top of the granary wall. A horse, small as her hand, that tossed its head. Twenty percent. Slow. Fine.

Her wings still didn’t work. She’d stopped mentioning it.

The dogs were the first to know.

It was dusk on the twenty-second day, and Bee was in the courtyard with her chalk, drawing the Puffs on the flagstones by the well, when every dog in Thornwall started barking at once.

Thornwall had a lot of dogs. The shepherds’ dogs from the town below, who came up with their masters on market days and slept in the gatehouse. The quartermaster’s old hound. The four big grey war-dogs the Western Watch had brought from the capital, who lived in a kennel by the stables and were mostly asleep. All of them, at once, were barking. Then the barking changed. It went up, and up, and turned into something else: a long, rising, terrible howl, like grief, coming from every corner of the fort.

Bee stood up.

On the north wall, a soldier was shouting. Not alarm. Something stranger. A name.

“It’s Ossie! It’s Ossie, it’s—”

Tiger came out of the barracks door so fast he took it off one hinge.

Bee had never seen him move like that. Not even in the yard, doing the thing with the gap. He crossed the courtyard in four strides and went up the steps to the gatehouse three at a time, and Bee dropped her chalk and ran after him. Her wings buzzed uselessly against her back, the way they always did when she ran, lifting nothing.

She got to the top of the gatehouse just behind him, out of breath, and looked over the wall.

There was a figure on the north road.

He was coming up the last stretch of the track towards the outer gate, walking out of the dusk with the dark hills behind him. A tall, lanky lad in a grey-and-gold tunic, torn at the shoulder, muddy to the knees. No pack. No sword. Freckles, she could see even from here, and big feet, and that walk; that slightly too-long, slightly loose-jointed walk she’d watched every morning for weeks, racing round and round the courtyard with Tiger.

He looked up, and saw Tiger on the wall, and waved.

“Tiger!” he shouted. “Oi! Tiger!” His voice came up clear and cheerful in the cold. “Bet you can’t beat me to the gate!”

Beside her, Tiger made a sound she’d never heard a person make.

And then he was moving. Toward the ladder. Toward the gate. And Bee grabbed his sleeve, without thinking, the way you’d grab someone stepping into a road, and he dragged her a whole pace before he stopped.

He didn’t stop because of her.

Hild was standing at the top of the ladder. Bee hadn’t heard her come up. Nobody ever did. She was standing there in the dusk with her blindfold pushed up and her one eye looking at Tiger, and her hand resting very lightly on the hilt of her sword.

“No,” said Hild.

“It’s Ossie—“

“I know who it looks like.” Hild didn’t move. “Nobody opens the gate.”

Hyvred got there a moment later, and Kei behind her, and Drew; and then Brannoch, puffing, buttoning his coat, and Fenwick with his spectacles crooked and a lamp in his hand. They lined the top of the gatehouse in the cold, a dozen of them, soldiers and summoned together, and looked down at the boy on the road.

He’d stopped at the outer gate. He stood in front of it with his hands in his belt, looking up at them, a little puzzled, a little hurt, the way you’d look at friends who hadn’t come down to let you in.

The dogs were still howling.

“Hyv,” said Bee quietly.

Hyvred didn’t answer. She was looking down at the boy with her left eye, the crosshair eye, and her face had gone the colour of the stones.

“Hyv.”

“It’s wrong,” Hyvred whispered. “The light. It’s the same as the man at the well. In Coldwater.” She was holding the edge of the wall so hard her knuckles were white. “It’s Ossie’s light. It’s the right colour. It’s the right shape. It’s just…” She swallowed. “It’s like a lamp with a hand inside it. Someone’s holding it up from inside.”

“Someone wearing someone,” said Kei, very softly.

Hisui was coiled tight around Kei’s wrist, his green head raised, tongue flickering. Kei listened to him for a moment. His face didn’t change, but his voice, when he spoke, was very flat.

“Hisui says he doesn’t smell of anything,” said Kei. “Not sweat. Not mud. Not blood. Nothing.” A pause. “He says he smells like a stone that’s been in a river.”

Bee looked down at the boy at the gate.

He’d heard none of this. He couldn’t have. But as Kei finished speaking he looked up, right at Kei, and smiled, a big easy freckled smile. And Bee felt her skin crawl from the base of her antennae all the way down to her heels.

“Lads,” said Ossie. “Come on. It’s freezing.”

Nobody answered him.

“Captain.” He looked along the wall to Brannoch, and straightened, and saluted, a proper salute, perfect, the way they did it in the yard. “Trooper Oswald Pell, reporting back, sir. Sorry it’s late.”

Brannoch’s face was like a closed door.

“Where’s your sergeant, trooper?” he said.

“Up the hill, sir.” Ossie gestured vaguely behind him, at the dark. “With the others. They’re waiting. They sent me down to say.” He smiled. “It’s all right, sir. It’s all right up there. It’s warm. Warmer than here.” He looked along the wall again, at all of them, and his eyes came to rest on Tiger. “Tiger. You built it back. The shed. I saw from the road.”

Tiger made that sound again.

“You did the walls,” said Ossie warmly. “I said I’d do the roof. Didn’t I? I said I’d show you a proper roof. My dad’s a carpenter.” He spread his hands. “So let me in, and I’ll do the roof. It’s waiting for the roof.”

It was exactly what Tiger had said. In the courtyard, in the dark, three weeks ago. It’s waiting for the roof. Bee had been the only other person there.

Drew was standing at Bee’s shoulder. He hadn’t said a word since he came up. He’d been watching the boy at the gate the way he watched people across a table, or a ring, or a bracket: quietly, carefully, all of him paying attention.

“He’s not blinking,” said Drew, very low. “Watch him. He hasn’t blinked since he got to the gate.” He paused. “And he only moves when he talks. When he stops talking, he stops. Everything. Like a puppet somebody’s put down.”

Bee watched. Drew was right. Ossie finished speaking and went still. Not standing-still still. Still. His chest didn’t move. His hands didn’t move. Not a hair of him moved, in the wind off the hills, until he opened his mouth again.

“He breathes,” said Hild, quietly, from the ladder, with her head tilted. “When he remembers to.”

“Tiger,” said Ossie, from the gate. “Come on, mate. Come down and let me in. Just you. I’ll do the roof.”

Tiger was shaking. Bee still had hold of his sleeve. She could feel it through the cloth: a deep, fine, terrible trembling, like a wire pulled too tight.

“Ossie,” he said. His voice cracked. “Ossie, what happened up there?”

“Nothing happened.” Ossie smiled. “We went for a walk. That’s all. Over the hill. And the people there were kind, Tiger. You’d like them. They’re very kind. They asked about everyone. They asked about you.” His eyes moved, slowly, along the wall, to Hyvred. And stopped. “And her. The one who looks.”

Hyvred took a step back from the wall.

“They’d like to meet her,” said Ossie pleasantly. “Very much.”

Hild’s sword came out of its scabbard with a sound like a breath.

The dogs, which had been howling, went suddenly, completely silent. All at once, all over the fort. As if somebody had shut a door on them. And in the silence Bee could hear the wind off the mountains, and Tiger breathing, and Fenwick’s lamp hissing, and nothing at all from the boy at the gate.

He wasn’t breathing. He’d forgotten.

Afterwards, Bee couldn’t have said why she did it.

She didn’t think about it. That was the truth. She was standing on the gatehouse with her hand on Tiger’s sleeve, and the dogs had gone silent, and the thing at the gate was looking at her best friend with Ossie’s freckled face and not breathing; and something in her went very cold and very clear, the way it went when she was drawing and the line was right.

The dogs knew. The dogs had known before anyone. Before the soldier on the wall, before Hyvred. And now they’d stopped. And somebody needed to keep telling it.

She let go of Tiger’s sleeve. She took the chalk out of her pocket: the lump the quartermaster had given her, the one she’d been drawing Puffs with in the courtyard. And she leaned out over the parapet, as far as she could reach, and drew on the outer face of the gatehouse wall, right above the gate, right above the thing’s head.

A dog.

Not a nice dog. Not a pretty one. A big rough shaggy shepherd’s dog, the kind that slept in the gatehouse on market days, in long, fast, joined-up lines that never left the stone. Head down. Hackles up. Mouth open. She drew it the way she’d drawn the sparrow off the granary wall: one line, all the way through, without stopping, feeling for the place under her hand where the line wanted to go that wasn’t quite the wall.

There. The gap.

She drew into it. And she thought, as hard as she’d ever thought anything, the only words she had, in no language at all.

Tell him.

The dog barked.

It was the loudest sound Bee had ever heard. It came out of the wall like a thunderclap, a huge, deep, furious bark, a real one, with breath behind it, the bark of a dog who knows exactly what’s standing in front of its door. The chalk lines blazed white. The whole drawing leaned out of the stone, hackles and teeth and lifted lip, and barked down at the thing in Ossie’s shape, once, twice, three times—

Three heartbeats.

Then it was chalk again. Just lines, white on grey stone, above the gate.

And the dogs of Thornwall, all of them, every dog in the fort, started barking again at once.

The thing at the gate looked up.

It looked up at the chalk dog on the wall above its head, for a long moment. Then it looked at Bee.

She’d thought it would be angry. She’d braced for it. Instead it did something much worse.

It smiled.

Not Ossie’s smile. Ossie’s smile had been big and loose and a little bit stupid, the smile of a boy who’d lost a race and didn’t care because he’d had fun. This was something else wearing that smile. Patient. Interested. The way you’d smile at a child who’d done something unexpectedly clever.

“Oh,” it said, in Ossie’s voice, softly. “Oh, that’s good.”

Then it turned round.

It didn’t say goodbye. It didn’t look back. It just turned, and walked away from the gate, back down the north road toward the dark hills. Not fast. Not slow. Very carefully. Putting each foot down exactly where the last one had been. The way Hyvred had said, that night three weeks ago on the wall.

Like they’re remembering how.

They watched it go. All of them, along the gatehouse, in the dusk, with the dogs barking. Watched it walk up the track toward the burned hut and the passes, smaller and smaller, until even Hyvred said quietly that she couldn’t see its light anymore.

Nobody went after it. Brannoch didn’t order it, and nobody asked.

Tiger didn’t say anything for a long time.

When the others had gone down, when the watch had been doubled again and Fenwick had gone to write it all up and Brannoch had gone to stand in the kennels with his dogs, Tiger was still standing at the parapet, looking north. Bee stood with him. She didn’t know what else to do. Hyvred had wanted to stay too, but Bee had looked at her, and Hyvred had understood, the way she always did, and gone.

Drew had stayed longest. He’d known Tiger for nine years, the kind of friend you don’t have to explain anything to. He’d stood at Tiger’s other shoulder for a long time without saying a word. Then he’d looked across at Bee, and something had passed between them, and he’d squeezed Tiger’s shoulder once and gone down the ladder. He’d be at the bottom when they came down. He always was.

The Puffs had come up from the long room and settled on Bee’s shoulders. They were very quiet.

“He knew about the roof,” Tiger said at last. His voice was raw. “Bee. He knew about the roof. I only said that to you. In the dark. Nobody else was there.”

“I know.”

“So how—“

“I don’t know,” said Bee. “Maybe it heard you. Maybe it was up on the hill that night, watching. Maybe it’s got his memories and knew what he’d say. Maybe it can read you like Hyv reads light.” She shook her head. “Doesn’t matter. Knowing what he’d say doesn’t make it him. A forgery’s still a forgery even when it’s a good one.”

Tiger was crying. Not loudly. Just steadily, with his face turned north, tears running down past the little XX mark under his eye, not wiping them.

Bee wasn’t good at this. She’d never been good at this. Hyvred was good at this, and Kei, and Drew, who could make anyone feel better just by sitting down next to them. Bee was the one who said the wrong thing at the wrong time and then went and drew something until it passed.

But she was the one standing here. So she said the only true thing she had.

“I didn’t cry when Ice died,” she said.

Tiger turned his head.

“I didn’t know him.” Bee looked out at the dark. “Four months. Hyv’s friend. He sent me a heart once, under a drawing. That’s it. And then he died, and everyone was wrecked, and I just… wasn’t. And then we got here, and it’s thirty-four people who loved him and me. And every time someone goes quiet in the middle of a sentence I think, I’m not allowed to be sad about this. I didn’t earn it.”

Tiger didn’t say anything.

“You knew Ossie a month,” said Bee. “You raced him in the mud. That’s it. That’s all.” She looked at him. “And you’re allowed. That’s what I’m saying. It doesn’t matter how long. You’re allowed to be this sad about someone you knew for a month. You don’t have to earn it.”

Tiger stared at her. Then his face crumpled, and he put his arms round her, very suddenly, the way Hyvred had once, charcoal and all; and Bee stood there being hugged, stiff as a board, with the Puffs buzzing in alarm on her shoulders.

“You’re terrible at this,” said Tiger into her hair.

“I know.”

“That was really good, though.”

“I know,” said Bee. And, after a moment, awkwardly, she put her arms round him too.

They went down together, eventually. Drew was sitting on the bottom rung of the gatehouse ladder, the way Bee had known he would be. He didn’t ask anything. He just got up and walked with them, across the courtyard in the dark, past the well and the chalk Puffs she’d been drawing when the dogs began, to the north wall where the shed stood waiting.

Tiger stopped in front of it.

Three crooked walls. No roof. A gap where the door should go.

“I’m going to finish it,” he said. “Not for him. It’s not him.” He wiped his face. “For Ossie. The real one. Wherever he is.”

“You’ll need a roof.”

“I don’t know how to do a roof.”

“So learn,” said Bee. “It’s like anything else. You do it every day until you can.”

She took out her chalk. And on the inside of the back wall of the shed, where the door would face, she drew a roof: a proper one, with rafters and a ridge and a little chimney, the way you’d draw it for a child. And under it, in small square letters, because she didn’t know what else to put, she wrote OSSIE’S.

It didn’t move. It wasn’t supposed to. It was just a drawing.

But Tiger stood looking at it for a long time, in the dark, with his hands in his pockets. And when he went inside, he didn’t go to bed. He went to the long room, and sat at the table under Corvin’s maps, and wrote to everyone, the way they’d promised. All of it. The dogs. The gate. The thing with Ossie’s face that didn’t blink and didn’t breathe and smelt of river stones. The people there were kind. They asked about everyone. They’d like to meet the one who looks.

He sealed it, and gave it to the courier at dawn.

It was the eleventh letter Group 5 had sent since the turn of the year. They hadn’t had an answer to any of them in five weeks.

Bee opened her window that night, last thing, sitting on the end of her cot with the Puffs asleep in her hair.

Six, in one go. She looked at it for a while. Then she closed it again, and lay down, and looked at the ceiling.

She thought about the chalk dog above the gate. The way it had leaned out of the stone. The sound of it. Three heartbeats of the loudest, truest thing she’d ever made.

And she thought about the thing in Ossie’s shape, looking up at it. Oh, that’s good.

Not angry. Not afraid. Interested.

Bee lay awake for a long time after that, with the dogs of Thornwall barking on and off into the small hours, at nothing, at the dark, at the hills. And she found that for the first time since she’d come through the floor, she wished, very much, that she’d known Ice better. Not for him. For herself. She’d have liked to ask somebody who’d fought things like this what you were supposed to do when one of them looked at you and smiled.
