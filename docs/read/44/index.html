The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 2: Letters

Chapter 44

Full Moon

6,292 words · 27 min read

Higu had been counting down to the full moon for a month.

She kept a page for it at the back of her notebook, behind the two pages of Things Alea says that aren’t answers and the one line she’d written in the winter and never shown anyone. A little column of dates, and beside each one the shape of the moon that night, drawn in pencil, and beside that her window. Every night since the snow.

The numbers made a pattern. Higu liked patterns. On the dark nights, when there was no moon at all, her window barely moved: a percent, if she was lucky, sometimes nothing. As the moon grew, so did the numbers. Two a night. Three. On the last full moon, sitting on the roof of the militia hall wrapped in a blanket, she’d gone up six in a single night and had to lie down afterwards because the sky wouldn’t stop tilting.

She’d done the sums. She’d done them three times, because she didn’t trust herself to hope.

Tonight the moon would be full again. And her window, this morning, said ninety-three.

She didn’t tell anyone. Not even Thea. She just closed the notebook, and put it in her pocket, and went down to breakfast, and was calm, the way she was always calm. Inside, she was counting the hours until dark.

The letters came at breakfast.

All at once, in a satchel carried by a rider in the king’s colours, which was new. Usually it was an ordinary courier, a cheerful boy on a pony who flirted with Odette at the town hall and brought the post up the hill whenever he remembered. This was a big silent man who wouldn’t let the bag go until Maren had signed for it. He’d ridden from the capital. He didn’t explain why.

Inside there were nine letters, some of them weeks late, with their seals broken and pressed back badly. A packet of narrow paper strips, covered in tiny coiled blue English. And on top, a letter in Cursiffe’s hand, numbered No. 1, sealed with one of the strips.

Thea read it aloud, at the table, with her porridge going cold. About the clerk in the capital with the blue hands. About the steward with the heron ring, and the box under the floor, and the list of scholars with lines through their names. Could not read. Could not read. Keep looking.

When she finished, nobody said anything for a while.

“Five weeks,” said Xola at last. She had Squiddles in her lap, and was stroking him very slowly, the way she did when she was upset. “That’s why nobody wrote back. I thought… I thought maybe we’d said something wrong.”

“We didn’t say anything wrong,” said Gigichi. “Somebody stole it.”

“And read it,” said PenguChi quietly.

“Tried to read it.” Thea put the letter down. “Nobody can. That’s the whole point.” But her voice was tight, and her hands on the table had curled up. Higu knew that look. She’d known it for years: Thea, on stream, in a game that wasn’t working the way it was supposed to, five seconds before she started taking it apart.

Then Thea read the rest of the letters, the late ones. Chris, about the junior lists, and a sword inside a sword. Tiger’s, copied out in Jared’s quick slanting hand, about a boy with a dead man’s face at the gate of Thornwall, who didn’t breathe unless he remembered to. Maigo’s, short and huge and all in capitals, which made everyone laugh even after Tiger’s: TELL THEA SECOND IS JUST FIRST BUT LATER.

And Yan’s, at the end, which said: It’s all one shape. East and west. I think we’re running out of time to find out what it is.

Thea put that one down very carefully on top of the others.

“Five weeks,” she said. “And even now they’re numbered, it’s still weeks. Thornwall to here is twelve days if the roads are good. Valcrest is longer.” She was staring at the pile. “Something walks up to Tiger’s gate, and we hear about it a month later. Someone tries to kill Ina, and we hear about it a month later. If something happened tonight, at any of them, any of them…”

She didn’t finish. She didn’t need to.

“Letters don’t send,” said Thea. “Not fast enough. Not safely enough. Not for this.”

And she got up from the table, porridge and all, and went upstairs. Higu heard her door shut.

Gigichi sighed. “She’s going to take something apart.”

“Yes,” said Higu.

“What, though?”

Higu looked at the ceiling, toward the room where Thea had gone. She thought about what Thea had been doing every night since the winter, since Spark: sitting on her bed with her window open, staring into it, for hours. Reading it, the way only Thea could. Line by line. Down and down, under the words everyone else saw, into whatever was underneath.

“I think,” said Higu slowly, “the windows.”

Thea came down at noon and asked for Higu.

Just Higu. Not the others. She stood in the doorway of the common room with her hair coming out of its ponytail and a smudge of ink on her cheek and a look in her bright blue eyes that Higu had last seen at two in the morning, years ago, when Thea had figured out how to get a stream overlay to do something it was absolutely not designed to do.

“I need a strategist,” said Thea. “And someone who’s run a chat.”

Higu put down her mending.

“What are you building?”

“Come and see.”

Thea’s room was small and bare and full of paper. Her whole wall was covered in it, the way Corvin’s was in Group 5’s letters: sheets of English, pinned up in rows, with arrows between them. Higu had seen the wall before. She’d never understood it.

“It’s my window,” said Thea, before she could ask. “What’s under it. As far as I’ve got.”

She sat down on the bed and opened her window, and Higu sat beside her and opened her own, out of habit. Two pale panels of light, hanging in the air of the little room. Higu’s said what it always said. Higu · Tier 0: Initiate · Progress to Spark: 93%.

“You see the top,” said Thea. “Name, tier, progress. Skills, inventory. That’s the bit everyone sees.” She touched the edge of her own window, very lightly. “I see more. I’ve told you that. Since Spark, I can Parse it. Go underneath. And underneath, it’s…”

She stopped. She looked at Higu.

“It’s written, Gu,” she said. “All of it. Not just the words on top. All of it, underneath, all the way down. It’s written. Lines and lines of it. It’s like code. It is code, almost. Somebody wrote it.” She took a breath. “And it’s written in English.”

Higu went very still.

“The Hero’s words,” she said.

“Not just the Hero’s words. Everything. Every line. All the way down.” Thea’s voice had gone quiet and fast. “That’s why I could read it from the first day, when nobody else could. That’s why I found the sealed value and the Medium: one blade line. I thought it was my gift. Reading magic like code.” She shook her head. “It is. But it’s also that it’s in my language. Whoever wrote the first window wrote it in English, and everyone’s window since has been copied from that one. Every native who can see theirs. Every one of us.”

“The first window,” said Higu.

“It’s still running.” Thea pointed at the wall. At the top of it, in the middle, there was a single sheet of paper with nothing on it but a circle, and an arrow from every other sheet on the wall pointing up into it. “All of them come from one. All of them still point to it. Like… like a shared file. Everyone’s window reads from it. If it changed, they’d all change.” She took a breath. “So I thought: what if I wrote something into my window, in English, in the right place, that every other window would read?”

Higu looked at the circle on the wall for a long time.

“A message,” she said.

“A channel.” Thea was practically vibrating. “Something we can all see. Something nobody can steal off a courier, because it never goes on a road. Something that’s there the second you write it, Wrenhaven to Thornwall, no twelve days.” She grabbed Higu’s hands. “Gu. I think I can build a chat. In the windows.”

They worked on it all afternoon.

Higu didn’t understand most of what Thea was doing. She didn’t need to. Thea didn’t need her for that. Thea needed her for the other part: the part where you stop and ask what it’s for, and who it’s for, and what happens when it goes wrong.

“How does it know who’s us?” was Higu’s first question.

“Every one of our windows has a line the natives’ don’t.” Thea pointed at her own. “Medium: one blade. Next to the sealed value. We all came through the same circle, so we all got the same line.” She frowned. “Maren’s doesn’t have it. I Parsed hers, in the winter, when she wasn’t looking. Neither will Bram’s, or the Advisor’s, or anyone born here. So I can tell it: only windows with that line.”

“Good,” said Higu. “Nobody from here can see it. Not Vane. Not anyone.” She thought. “Not Alea.”

Thea looked at her sideways. Higu kept her face calm.

“Next,” said Higu. “Names. When someone writes something, everyone has to see who said it. Otherwise it’s thirty-five people shouting in a dark room.”

“The window name,” said Thea. “Whatever’s at the top of theirs. Mine says Althea.”

“Fine. Everyone will know.” Higu tapped her chin. “How much can you write at once?”

Thea winced. “Not much. A line. A short one. It costs something. Not a lot. Like… breathing out hard.” She flexed her fingers. “And it won’t keep much. Maybe the last forty lines, and then the oldest ones fall off the top. It’s rudimentary. It’s really rudimentary, Gu. It’s the worst chat anyone’s ever built.”

“It’s the only chat anyone’s ever built,” said Higu. “Here.”

Thea laughed. Then she stopped laughing and bent over her window again, muttering.

Higu sat beside her and watched the light in the little room change, slowly, as the afternoon went on. She thought about chats. She’d run one for years: her stream, her little corner of the internet, a few hundred people every night, words scrolling up a screen. She knew what chats were like. She knew what they were like at their best, warm and silly and full of people who’d turned up just to be there with you. And she knew what they were like at their worst.

“Who’s going to mod it?” she said.

Thea looked up. “What?”

“The chat. Every chat needs a mod.”

“It’s thirty-five people, Gu. It’s us.”

“It’s thirty-five of our friends,” said Higu, “all frightened, a long way from each other, finding out bad news at the same time. It needs a mod.” She paused. “Or at least rules.”

Thea considered this. “You want to write the rules?”

“Yes,” said Higu.

So she did. Thea put them at the top of the channel, where everyone would see them first, the way the rules had always sat pinned at the top of Higu’s old chat. Higu wrote them in her careful calm hand on a scrap of paper, and Thea copied them in.

Be kind. Everyone here is scared.

Bad news: say it plainly. Don’t make anyone guess.

Good news: say it loudly.

Nobody is alone.

Thea read them over twice. Then she looked at Higu with an odd expression.

“That last one’s not a rule,” she said.

“It is now,” said Higu.

She’d thought about Ice all afternoon. She couldn’t help it.

He’d modded her chat. That was how she’d known him. A name in her chat with the little green sword beside it, always there, at every hour of the night. Entertaining the chat when she went quiet. Timing out the trolls before she even saw them. Making jokes she’d laugh at on stream without knowing which of the hundreds of names they’d come from, until she started recognising the rhythm.

He’d once banned a man for being horrible about her, and then, in the ban reason, which only the man and the mods would ever see, he’d written an apology. Not to Higu. To the man. A whole little poem, four lines, rhyming, very sincere, about how he was sorry that the man was clearly having such a bad day that he’d come into a stranger’s chat to be cruel, and that he hoped tomorrow would be better, and that he was still banned. Trust.

She’d only found out because another mod had screenshotted it and sent it to her, crying with laughter. She’d kept the screenshot for years.

Be kind, she thought, looking at the first rule. Everyone here is scared.

He’d have liked the rules, she thought. He’d have broken every one of them within the hour, and then apologised in a poem.

It was dusk when Thea finished.

She’d gone very quiet in the last hour. Not muttering anymore. Just sitting cross-legged on her bed with her window open in front of her, still, the way she’d been at the midwinter bonfire, when she’d held up her hand in the middle of the square with the sparks going up and written one line into the world and become the first of them to cross. Higu had been there. She remembered how still Thea had gone, right before.

She was that still now.

“Okay,” said Thea, at last, very softly. “I’m going to write it. The line. The one that connects it.” She didn’t look round. “If it doesn’t work, nothing happens. If it does…”

“If it does?”

“Then everyone’s window is going to do something new, all at once, all over the kingdom. And thirty-four people are going to look at it and wonder if they’re going mad.” She laughed shakily. “I should’ve warned them.”

“How?” said Higu. “By letter?”

Thea snorted. Then she closed her eyes, and lifted one hand, and laid her fingertip on the surface of her window, the way you’d lay your finger on a page to keep your place.

Higu didn’t see anything happen. She didn’t feel anything. She just watched Thea’s lips move, very slightly, around a word, a line, something in English that Higu couldn’t hear.

Then Thea opened her eyes.

And in front of Higu, without her touching it, her own window changed.

Below the name and the tier and the progress, below the skills and the inventory, where there had never been anything at all, a new panel had opened. Small and plain and pale, like the rest. At the top of it, Higu’s four rules, in Thea’s careful copy. And under them, empty, waiting, a single line with a small mark at the start of it that pulsed, very slowly, like a heartbeat.

Like a cursor.

Higu stared at it.

“Thea,” she said.

“I know.”

“Thea.”

“I know.” Thea had both hands over her mouth. “I know. Okay. Okay. First message. What do I say? What’s the first message? Gu, you’re the strategist, what do I—“

“Ask the only question that matters,” said Higu.

Thea looked at her. Then she laughed, a little wildly, and touched her window, and wrote.

[ Althea: Can everyone see this? ]

Downstairs, Higu could hear Xola shrieking. Upstairs, in her own little room, Thea had put her face in her hands and was crying and laughing at the same time.

Higu counted. She couldn’t help it. It was what she did.

Thirty-four. Thirty-four names, besides Thea’s. Every single one.

Everyone could see it.

They talked for hours.

At first it was just noise, the good kind: everyone saying hello to everyone, all at once, over and over, because they could. Maigo said I’M SECOND four more times. Newish started an argument about who had technically replied first and Chris settled it with a timestamp nobody else could see. Tiger asked whether you could send pictures, and Thea said no, and Bee said I’ll draw you one with words then, and did, a little bee made of brackets and lines, and everybody lost their minds.

Then, slowly, it turned.

It turned the way conversations do, late at night, when everyone’s together and someone finally says the thing they’ve been carrying.

It was Group 6. Of course it was. They’d been quieter than anyone, after their first hellos. And then John wrote, carefully, a line at a time, because that was all the channel could hold:

“Thea,” said Higu.

“What?” Thea yawned. “It’s true. It’s still running. It’s in the code.” She lay back on the bed with her arm over her eyes. “They’re probably dead. It’s probably just… running on its own, like an old server nobody switched off. But it felt rude not to say hi.”

Higu didn’t say anything.

She sat beside her on the bed in the dark, with the moon coming up outside the window, very large and very gold, and thought about the one line she’d written in her notebook in the winter. The one she’d never shown anyone.

The books come from the lake.

She went up on the roof at midnight, the way she had on every full moon since the snow.

The militia hall roof was flat and leaded, with a low wall round it, and from up here you could see the whole of Wrenhaven: the market square with the scorch mark from the autumn bonfire, the church, the dark huddle of roofs, and then the long slope of fields going down to the lake.

The moon was full. Higher now, and white instead of gold, so bright it threw shadows. Lake Ilse lay under it like a sheet of hammered silver, all the way to the far trees.

Higu sat down with her back against the chimney and her blanket round her shoulders, and opened her window.

Ninety-eight.

She’d gone up five since dusk. She could feel it pulling: the moon, on her, the way it pulled on the tide. Up through her chest and out into her fingertips and up into the white light. Like being a cup with the moon pouring into it.

One small line, she thought. In its language.

She’d been thinking all month about what the moon’s language was. Not words. Thea’s was code; Bee’s, so Maigo’s letter said, was drawing; Maigo’s was a tree’s. Higu had always thought hers would be something gentle. A lullaby. A poem, maybe, the way Ice had written a poem in a ban message.

But sitting on the roof with the moon pouring into her, she found it wasn’t any of those. It was simpler than that. The moon didn’t sing. The moon didn’t make poems. The moon only ever did one thing, every night, for everyone, whether they wanted it to or not.

It looked.

It looked at everything. Every roof, every road, every field, every face turned up to it. It went everywhere, and saw everything, and lit it, and never said a word about what it saw.

Higu lifted her hand, palm up, into the white light. She felt for the gap. It was right there. It had probably been there all along.

And she wrote into it, very quietly, the only line the moon had ever known.

Go and see.

It came up out of her palm like breath on cold glass.

Small. White. Soft. A moth: not a butterfly, the way she’d always half expected, but a moth, round and furred and pale, with broad soft wings dusted in light, and two feathery antennae, and no eyes that she could see. It sat on her palm, weightless, glowing faintly, opening and closing its wings in the moonlight as if it were breathing.

Higu stared at it.

She didn’t need to open her window. She could feel it, the way you feel your own hand. But she opened it anyway, because she was Higu, and she needed to see it written.

[ Higu · Tier 1: Spark · Skills: Moth ]

Third. After Thea, after Maigo. She thought about writing it in the channel. She thought about Maigo’s capitals, and Tiger shouting, and Xola downstairs shrieking all over again. She’d tell them in the morning. Loudly. Rule three.

For now she just sat there on the roof, in the white light, with the moth on her palm.

And the moth, very gently, lifted off.

She didn’t send it anywhere. That was what she would tell herself afterwards. She didn’t choose. The moon pulled it, the way it had pulled her; it rose off her palm and drifted up into the white air above the roof and hung there for a moment, turning slowly, and then the night wind took it, and it drifted away, down, over the roofs, over the fields, down the long slope toward the lake.

And Higu went with it.

Not her body. Her body stayed on the roof, against the chimney, wrapped in its blanket. But she found that if she closed her eyes, she could see what the moth saw. Faintly. Silver and black and soft-edged, like an old photograph. The fields going by underneath. The hedges. The lake road, pale in the moonlight. The water coming closer, huge and bright and still.

The cabin. One lamp in the window.

The jetty.

There was a man sitting at the end of it.

She’d seen him before. Everyone had. The fisherman, in the market, on the road, mending nets by the water. The man who’d stopped in the middle of the road in the autumn and made Maren and Bram bow to the ground. The man Thea had walked into with a basket of eggs and said trust to, and who’d said there’s plenty of fish. The man Alea brought the fish from, every single day.

He was sitting cross-legged at the end of the jetty in the moonlight, alone, with his rod laid down beside him and his hands in his lap.

And in front of him, in the air, there was a pale panel of light.

Higu knew what it was. She’d have known it anywhere. She’d been looking at one exactly like it all evening. A window. A status window, open, hanging in the air in front of his face; and he was reading it. The way they all read theirs. His eyes moving slowly, line by line. Once, he lifted one hand and moved a finger down, slowly, the way you scroll.

She couldn’t see what it said. The moth was too far away and too small, and the light was too pale. Just the shape of it. Just a man, alone, on a jetty, in the middle of the night, reading something in the air with an expression she couldn’t make out.

Then the cabin door opened, and someone came down the path.

Alea. In a shawl, with her ears up and her tail swinging, carrying two cups. Steam rising from them, silver in the moonlight. Tea. She walked out along the jetty in her bare feet, not hurrying, and sat down beside him, and handed him one of the cups, and he took it.

He didn’t close the window. Not straight away. He said something to her. Higu couldn’t hear what. And Alea leaned over, looking at the empty air in front of him, as if she were trying to see what he was seeing, and then laughed, and leaned against his shoulder.

He closed the window then. With a small flick of two fingers, sideways, the way they all did. The way Higu had done a thousand times.

And the two of them sat at the end of the jetty in the moonlight, drinking tea, looking at the lake. The girl with her head on the man’s shoulder. The man with his hand resting, very lightly, on the top of her head, between her ears.

Higu opened her eyes.

She was on the roof. The moon was still pouring down. Her face was wet. She didn’t know when that had happened.

“Sorry,” she whispered.

She didn’t know who to. Alea, maybe. The man. Both. She’d seen something that wasn’t hers to see, a girl and her father on a jetty at midnight, and she hadn’t meant to, and she was sorry.

She called the moth back. She felt it turn, far away, out over the silver water, and come drifting back up the long slope toward her on the wind. She didn’t look through it again. She kept her eyes open, on the roof, on the moon, until she saw it: a tiny pale speck, coming up over the edge of the roof. It settled on her palm, and folded its wings, and went out like a breath.

Then she took out her notebook, and her pencil, and turned to the page at the back.

Under the line she’d written in the winter, The books come from the lake, she wrote two more. Very small. Very neat. The way she’d once written mod notes, in another world, about people she was keeping an eye on.

Alea is his.

He has a window.

She looked at them for a long time.

Then she closed the notebook, and put it in her pocket, and went downstairs, and lay awake until dawn. She didn’t tell anyone. Not Thea. Not the channel. Not anyone.

She was calm. She was very calm. She just didn’t know, yet, what it meant. And until she did, she wasn’t going to make anyone guess.

The channel had opened in Ars’s window at dusk.

He’d been on the jetty, mending a net, when he felt it: a small, polite pressure behind his eyes, like someone knocking on a door in a house he’d lived in so long he’d forgotten it had doors. He hadn’t opened his window since the winter. He didn’t, much; looking at it made him want things, and wanting ruined them. But the knocking didn’t stop, so in the end he put the net down and opened it. Name. Tier. Progress. The long familiar lines.

And under them, something new.

A panel. Small and plain and pale. At the top of it, four rules, in a careful copied hand he’d have known anywhere.

Be kind. Everyone here is scared.

He’d read the rules three times, sitting very still on the end of the jetty with the net forgotten across his knees. Then he’d read the line beneath them.

[ Althea: Can everyone see this? ]

And then the flood.

He understood what she’d done straight away. He’d had thirty-four years to understand what windows were, and how they worked, and where they came from. He’d had the first one before anyone in this world had a word for it. He’d named its parts himself, as a boy, in the only language he had: status, inventory, skills. Then he’d shown a young court mage at a border garrison how to look, and the mage had shown seven others, and on and on. Every window since had been copied from his. Every one still pointed back to it.

Thea had hooked her line into the place they all pointed. So of course it had come here. Here was where everything came, in the end.

He looked at the list of names along the edge of the panel. Thirty-five. All of them. He wasn’t among them. His window wasn’t one of theirs; it was the one theirs were made from. He could see everything they wrote, all of it, every line, from every corner of the kingdom. And none of them could see him at all.

There’d been a word for that, in the old world. For the one person in a server who could see every channel and wasn’t in any of them.

He’d been that, for Higu, once. A name with a little green sword beside it, at three in the morning.

He supposed he still was.

He read every line.

All evening, as the sun went down and the moon came up, he sat on the end of the jetty and read. Maigo, shouting. Newish, arguing about being first. Tiger. Hyvred. Bee’s little bee, made of brackets. Xav, calm and exact, all five of us can see it. Gabe and Deston, side by side in Kingsferry, the way they’d always been side by side.

Then John’s lines, about the Hollow. He read those very slowly. Twice. He felt something in his chest go hard and cold and still, the way it hadn’t in a long time. A circle in a cave, and people standing in it, waiting. It pulled. He put that away, carefully, somewhere he could find it again. He’d think about it later. He’d think about it a great deal.

Then Higu.

He had to stop reading for a while, after that.

Alea brought him tea around midnight. She’d seen him from the window, she said, sitting out there for hours with his window open. She sat down beside him in her shawl and handed him a cup and peered at the empty air in front of him, the way she’d done since she was small, as if she might see it if she tried hard enough.

“What is it?” she said.

“Your friends,” he said.

She looked at him. He told her, a little. What Thea had built. That they could all talk to each other now, all thirty-five, all at once, from every corner of the kingdom. That nobody could steal it.

“Can they see you?” said Alea.

He noticed the moth a moment too late.

It had been sitting on the rail at the end of the jetty, in the moonlight, so pale against the silver water that even he hadn’t seen it. It lifted off as he looked. A small white furred thing with broad soft wings, glowing very faintly, and it rose up into the night air and turned once and drifted away up the long slope of the fields, toward the town.
