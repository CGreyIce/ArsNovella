The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 6: Nine

Chapter 153

Hands

6,291 words · 27 min read

Aldous said the office for the dead an hour before dawn, the way he had every morning for nine years.

He said it on the rise a mile west of Cinderhold, on his knees in the wet grey grass a little way from the fire, with his book open on the ground in front of him and his old chaplain’s coat buttoned to the neck. Quietly. In the church tongue nobody spoke any more except old men like him. The same words he’d said on the wall of that fort the first morning, nine years ago, and on the red every morning for two years after that with a shovel beside him, and in the chapel with no priest every morning since. The words didn’t change. That was what they were for.

Then he turned the page, and read the names.

That was what the book was. Nobody had ever asked him, in two years, and he’d never said. Group 3 thought it was prayers; the garrison thought it was scripture; Rhea thought, he suspected, that it was a very long and very boring novel. It was the names. Every one he’d been able to find, in two years on the red, from a tag or a ring or a letter in a pocket or a man on the wall who said that’s Corrie, that was, from Ashby, he owed me a shilling. Eleven hundred and four names, in his own small hand. And beside each name, a number: the row and the post. And after the names, three hundred and nineteen numbers with no names at all.

He’d told Group 3, on their first week, that he’d stopped counting. It was the only lie he’d ever told them.

He read one page every morning. Twenty-two names. It took fifty-odd days to go through the whole book, and then he started again.

This morning it was the page with the healers on it.

He hadn’t arranged it. He never arranged it. It was simply where the book had got to, the way it got to every page in turn, and this morning, on a rise a mile from Cinderhold, with the fort black against the grey to the east and the man asleep by the fire behind him for once in his life, it had got to them. Three healers, from the infirmary, whose bodies had never been found. Taken, he’d written beside each one, instead of a row and a post. There had been four taken. Three were never found. And six dead, found in the infirmary at dawn on the third day with the east wall of it opened like a door: five orderlies, and a boy from the ditch that nobody could name, on an orderly’s cot.

And one more, at the bottom of the page, on a line of her own.

A healer. Name not given. The rise beyond the east wall; then the grave on the knoll, under the sword.

He’d written that nine years ago. He’d never known her name. Nobody had told him, and the one man who could have had not been a man anyone could ask anything, that autumn.

He knew it now. A soldier’s story on the red, a year ago, under a broken ring of fire: her name was Diwa. She was a healer.

He took the pen out of his coat, and the little flask of ink, and on the line, very carefully, after A healer, he wrote it in.

Then he closed the book, and got up off his knees, which took longer than it used to, and went back to the fire to tell everyone what to carry.

“Rhea,” said Aldous. “The cart.”

“I know,” said Rhea. She had already put her hands on the tailboard. She hadn’t slept either; nobody had, much. She’d been watching the lamps on the walls of Cinderhold all night like everyone else, and her face in the grey light had a look on it he’d only seen on her once before: on her knees, on the red, a year ago, with her sword laid down in front of her.

“Salty. Your ribs. Wendy, your bow. Maigo, the horse. Niko, your breath. Yan, the front.”

They nodded. One after another, the way they had every morning for twenty days. They knew the shape of it now. They’d known it for two years.

Then the girl.

He hadn’t told her anything, on the road. Not once. She’d stood at the end of the line with the others every morning, with her mother’s sword on her back, and he’d got to her and looked at her and gone on to her father, every day, and she’d never asked why. He’d thought she might. She hadn’t. She’d only watched him with her mother’s eyes, he supposed, and waited.

He stopped in front of her now.

“Alea,” said Aldous. “Your mother’s sword.”

The girl’s ears went very still.

She already had it. She always had it. It was on her back now, in its harness of old saddle leather and sharkskin, the half of a slab of black iron that a king had drawn narrower with his own hands and given a new point. She didn’t need telling. That wasn’t why he’d said it.

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded. Once.

And then him.

He stood at the end of the line where he’d stood every morning, with the yoke and the two empty buckets, waiting to be told. Very seriously. The way he must have stood on a parade ground here when he was a boy. In a plain coat, with a rod, with a face that nobody could hold on to and that Aldous had stopped trying to, a year ago, on his knees.

Aldous looked at him for a long time.

Nine years ago this man had walked in through the east gate of that fort with a black sword on his shoulder that six men and a winch couldn’t move. And the whole of the wall above him had put their hands on their chests. And he hadn’t looked up.

Today his daughter would carry the sword.

“Water,” said Aldous.

He watched the man understand it. It didn’t take long. He saw it go through him: the shoulders, the breath, the hands on the yoke. He saw him look at his daughter, at the black iron on her back, and then down at the two empty buckets in his own hands. And then at Aldous.

“Right,” he said. Very quietly.

He went to find the stream.

They didn’t take the road.

The road went straight down the rise and across a mile of grey grass to the west gate of Cinderhold, where the dray had come in with the headstone last winter and where Group 3 had come in on their first evening, two years ago, tired and frightened and new. It was the way everybody came. It was the only sensible way.

Aldous turned the horse off it.

“Father?” said Rhea, behind the cart.

“We’re going round,” said Aldous.

“Round? Round where?”

“The south side. Across the old line. And out onto the red, and back to the east gate.”

Rhea stared at him over the tailboard. “That’s an hour. It’s a mile to the west gate. We’d be there before the sun’s up.”

“Yes,” said Aldous.

“Why are we going the long way round?”

Aldous didn’t answer at once. He clicked his tongue at the horse, and the horse, which had never once in two years done anything Aldous asked it without being asked twice, turned off the road onto the grey grass as if it had been waiting to be asked. Maigo said something to it, very softly, from the baggage. Aldous didn’t hear what.

“Because it’s the way he came,” he said.

Nobody asked anything else.

They crossed the old line in the half-light, where the red came round the south of the fort like an arm laid across the land, and the horse didn’t stop at the edge this time. It didn’t need to. The man with the buckets was already walking out onto the red ahead of it, and Aldous, on the box, watched the grey grass go still round his feet, in a ring, the way Wendy had told him it had yesterday, the way he’d seen it go still a year ago and not let himself think about.

He didn’t let himself think about it now, either. He had an idea what it meant. He’d had the idea for a year. It frightened him, the way the white snake had frightened him on the first day, the way the things in the old rites frightened him: not because it was evil, but because it was older than anything he had words for. He put it away. He’d been putting things away for nine years, under the names in his book. One more did no harm.

They went round the south-east tower in the grey, with the fort above them on its hill, dark, with its lamps still burning. And out. Onto the red proper, east of the walls, the whole long plain of it going away from them toward the mountains, rust and grey in the dawn. Past the first row of markers. Past the second. Aldous knew every post. He’d put every one of them in.

At the third row he stopped the cart, and turned it, slowly, until it faced back the way they’d come.

West. Toward the fort. Toward the east wall, and the east gate in the middle of it, with the horns of a dead general hanging over it, dark against the stone.

The sun came up behind them.

He’d come up the east road with the king’s column on the second evening of the invasion.

A chaplain of forty-five, with a horse and a book, attached to the relief because the king liked a chaplain with a column and Aldous had been the nearest one to hand. Two days’ hard riding from the river. Too late for all of it. They’d come up the last rise in the dusk, where Group 3 had camped last night, and seen the fort with fires all round it and the red not red yet, only green and trampled and dark. And they’d gone in through the west gate at a gallop with the host on the far side of the walls, and the king had gone straight up onto the east wall in his mail without a word to anyone, and Aldous had gone up after him, because that was where a chaplain went.

He’d stood on the wall that night. All night. With the king and the commander of the fort and Halvard, all of them, every man who could stand. And listened to the south line in the dark, where something was happening that he couldn’t see. And in the grey before dawn he’d gone down with the surgeons to the infirmary, because somebody had shouted, and seen what was there.

And that afternoon he’d gone back up onto the wall, and watched.

He’d never told Group 3 what he’d watched. He’d never told anyone. He’d told them a man going into the red alone, in the second night, and the general falling, and the sword left standing over a grave, and let them think it was a story he’d heard.

He’d watched it.

A ring of fires on a rise an hour beyond the wall, with something in the middle of it that he couldn’t see from that distance and was glad of. And a young man going out of the east gate, alone, on foot, toward it. Through the whole of the host. Not fast. It had taken him until dusk. Aldous had watched him go into the host the way you’d watch a man walk into the sea, and keep walking, and not come back up. And then, in the dark, the thing that had happened on the rise.

He didn’t think about that part. That was under the names too.

And in the morning, the man had come back.

He came back now.

Aldous watched it from the box of the cart, with the sun coming up behind him and lying long and gold across the red toward the wall, the way it had lain that morning. The cart rolled forward. Rhea pushing. Maigo talking to the horse, very low. Yan in front with the scythe on his shoulder and Suzu wound round its shaft. Salty and Wendy. Niko on the tailboard with his eyes open, for once, watching.

And at the back of the cart, a girl with a black sword on her back. And beside her, a man with two buckets of water.

The wall was full.

Aldous had known it would be. He’d seen the lamps go on all night. But he hadn’t known it would be this full. Every embrasure. Every gap between the merlons, all the way along the east wall from the south-east tower to the north-east, and on the towers too, and on the gatehouse over the east gate, under the horns. Soldiers. Cooks. Carters. Grooms. The fort’s commander, a careful young captain from Solmere who’d been sent out three years ago and had heard all the stories and never once, Aldous was fairly sure, believed a word. Old Oakes from the forge in his leather apron, with his arms folded. Orla from the laundry, very straight, with her hands together in front of her.

And the old ones.

He could pick them out. He’d been picking them out for nine years. Twenty-five of them, men and women, who’d been on that wall on that morning and were still at Cinderhold, nine years on, when everybody else had gone home or gone on or gone into the book. Aldous knew every one of them. He’d buried their friends. He’d said the office over their brothers. He’d sat with them in the chapel with no priest when they couldn’t sleep, in the first years, and listened to them not talk about it.

They weren’t together. They were all along the wall, scattered among the young ones, the way they always were. But he could see them. The way you can see the old trees in a wood.

Teague, over the gate, with his scarred arm. Jory Platt, who’d lost three fingers. Bennet and his brother. Old Wat, who was eighty now and still did the night watch on the north tower because nobody had the heart to tell him not to. Marguerite from the stables. Fenner. Ise. The others.

The cart came on across the red.

A hundred paces from the gate, Teague put his hand on his chest.

Aldous saw it start.

Teague, on the gatehouse, under the horns. He didn’t do anything else. He didn’t say anything. He just lifted his good hand and laid it flat on his chest, over his heart. And stood like that, very straight, looking down at the red.

And Jory Platt, three merlons along, saw him, and did the same.

And Bennet, and his brother. And Old Wat on the north tower, slowly, because his hands shook. And Marguerite. And all along the wall, one after another, the way it had gone nine years ago. Not all at once. Like candles being lit along a dark hall by somebody walking. The twenty-five of them. Hand flat. Over the heart.

And the young ones beside them looked. Aldous saw them look. A boy of seventeen on the wall beside Old Wat, who’d come up because the old men had, staring at the old man beside him, at the shaking hand on the old man’s chest. Not understanding. And then, slowly, as if he weren’t sure, as if he might be doing it wrong, putting his own hand on his own chest.

And the girl beside him.

And the man beside her.

It went along the wall the way it had gone nine years ago, only further. Out from the old ones into the young ones round them, like ripples out from stones dropped in a pond, until they met, and crossed, and there was no one left on the whole of the east wall of Cinderhold with their hands at their sides. Every soldier. Every cook. The young captain from Solmere, who’d never believed a word, standing on the gatehouse beside Teague with his face gone strange and his hand on his chest because Teague’s was. Orla from the laundry. Old Oakes, last of all, who’d never in his life done anything because anybody else was doing it, unfolding his arms and laying one big burnt hand flat on his leather apron.

Aldous, on the box of the cart, put his own hand on his chest.

He’d done it nine years ago. He’d been standing beside the king. He hadn’t known why he was doing it then. He knew now.

The cart went in under the gate.

Under the horns. Aldous felt the shadow of the gatehouse go over him, and the cool of the stone, and heard the horse’s hooves change on the cobbles. The courtyard opened out in front of him, empty, because everybody in Cinderhold was on the wall. Yan came in behind him, and the snake on the scythe lifted her head and looked up at the wall, all of it, with her green eyes. Then Salty and Wendy. Then Rhea, pushing, with her face wet.

Then the girl.

And then him.

Aldous turned on the box. He couldn’t help it.

The man with the buckets had stopped. Not under the gate. Just short of it, outside, on the red, with the sun behind him, in the open, where he could see the wall.

He set the two buckets down.

Very carefully, so they didn’t slop. One on each side of him. And slid the yoke off his shoulders and laid it on the red between them. And straightened up.

And looked up.

It was like being looked at by weather.

That was the only way Aldous could ever describe it afterwards, to himself, in the chapel, when he tried. He wasn’t on the wall. He was on the box of the cart in the courtyard, with the gatehouse between them, looking back through the arch. The man wasn’t looking at him. And still he felt it. The way you feel a storm coming before you can see it: something enormous turning its attention, slowly, toward where you are.

The man looked up at the wall.

Not at the gatehouse. Along it. Starting at the south-east tower, and going north. Slowly. Every embrasure. Every face. The way he looked at everything, the way his daughter looked at everything: all of it, the whole thing, at once, and then each part of it, one at a time.

And as he looked, he said their names.

Not loudly. He didn’t raise his voice. It was a quiet voice, a little rough, with the accent of somewhere north. Aldous didn’t know how it carried. It carried. It went up the wall in the still morning air the way smoke goes up on a day with no wind, and every one of them heard it.

“Jory,” said the man. “Bennet. Hob.” He looked along the wall. “Marguerite. Fenner. Wat.” He was looking at the north tower, at an old man of eighty with a shaking hand on his chest. “Wat. You’re still on nights.”

The old man on the north tower made a sound. Aldous heard it from the courtyard.

“Ise. Cobb. Lark.” The eyes went on, along the wall. “Orla.” The old woman from the laundry, very straight, with her hand on her chest and her mouth pressed shut. “Oakes.”

Oakes didn’t say anything. His big burnt hand pressed down on his apron.

“Teague,” said the man, last. Looking up at the gatehouse. At the old sergeant with his cap off and the white scar down his arm.

Twenty-five names. Aldous counted. He couldn’t help it. He’d been counting names for nine years. The man said every one of them, the twenty-five who’d been on that wall that morning, in order, from the south-east tower to the gatehouse, without missing one, without stopping, as if he’d been carrying the list for nine years in his head the way Aldous had been carrying it in a book.

As if he’d known, the whole time, every one of them who’d been up there. As if he’d seen them.

You didn’t look up, Petra had told him, in Valcrest. Aldous knew; the man had told Aldous on the road, one evening, very quietly. Not once. You were looking at the ground.

He hadn’t looked up. He hadn’t needed to. He’d known who was there by the sound of their breathing.

He was looking up now.

Nobody on the wall moved.

The young ones didn’t understand. Aldous could see it in their faces: something was happening and they didn’t know what, and they were frightened of doing it wrong, so they kept their hands where they were and didn’t breathe. The young captain from Solmere was standing beside Teague with his hand on his chest and his mouth slightly open.

And the old ones stood very still with their hands flat on their chests and their faces doing what faces do, and didn’t wipe them, because you’d have to take your hand off your chest to do that.

The man looked at them a while longer. Then he picked up the yoke, and settled it on his shoulders, and bent and hooked the buckets on, one and then the other, and straightened up with the water.

And his daughter, who’d stopped in the gateway beside him without anyone noticing, looked up too.

Aldous saw it happen. The old ones saw her. Up and down the wall, the twenty-five who’d been there that morning, looking down from the parapet at the girl standing in the east gate in the morning sun with her red-gold hair tied up and her ears up and the black sword on her back. At her face.

He heard it go along the wall. Not a word. A breath. The sound twenty-five people make, all at once, when they see somebody they buried.

Diwa, said somebody on the wall, very quietly. Aldous didn’t see who.

Orla from the laundry put her other hand over her mouth.

Alea looked up at all of them. She didn’t say anything. She didn’t know any of them. She didn’t know any of their names. She stood in the gateway with her mother’s sword on her back and her mother’s face, being looked at by twenty-five old soldiers who’d known her mother, and her ears went flat, and then came up again, slowly. And her hands came together in front of her, palm to palm, at the level of her chest.

And then she walked in under the gate. With her father beside her, carrying the water.

The commander came down off the gatehouse first, because that was a commander’s job, and found he had no idea what to say.

Aldous watched him try. A careful young man with a careful young moustache, who wrote very good reports to Solmere every month and had never seen a demon in his life closer than the far end of the red. He came across the courtyard to the cart with his hand still half raised toward his chest, as if he’d forgotten it was there, and looked at the man with the buckets. And his eyes went to the man’s face and slid off it, the way everybody’s did, and came back, and slid off again.

“Sergeant,” said the commander, over his shoulder, a little desperately. “Who—“

Teague had come down behind him. He stood at the bottom of the gatehouse stair with his cap in his hand.

“Sir,” said Teague. He looked at the man with the buckets for a long moment. “That’s the water, sir.”

The commander looked at him.

“Where do you want it?” said the man with the buckets, helpfully.

“I—“ The commander looked from him to Teague and back. “The kitchen?”

“Right,” said the man, very pleased, and went off across the courtyard toward the kitchen door with the two buckets swinging, as if he’d been going there his whole life. Which, Aldous supposed, for twelve years of it, he had.

The commander stood in the middle of his own courtyard and stared after him.

“Sergeant,” he said. “Who was that?”

Teague put his cap back on.

“Ask the Father, sir,” he said, and went off after the buckets.

He’d been right about the young ones not knowing.

Aldous found that out before noon. A boy called Pim, fifteen, a stable lad who’d come out from Ashby in the spring and been on the wall that morning with his hand on his chest because the old man beside him had, came across the courtyard at the trot an hour after they arrived with a bucket of his own and found a man at the well, drawing water, with a rod leaning against the well-head.

“Oi,” said Pim. “That’s the horse bucket.”

The man looked at the bucket in his hands.

“It is,” he agreed.

“You can’t use the horse bucket for the kitchen. Cook’ll have your ears.” Pim put his own bucket down and held his hand out, very stern, the way stable lads are stern with anyone who isn’t a horse. “Kitchen bucket’s on the hook. The blue one. Everybody knows that.”

“Sorry,” said the man, and gave him the horse bucket, and took the blue one off the hook, and began drawing water again.

Aldous was crossing the courtyard to the chapel. He stopped.

On the far side of the yard, coming out of the kitchen door, Teague had stopped too. He was standing very still with his good hand on the doorframe, watching a fifteen-year-old stable lad tell the Hero of the Redmarch which bucket to use, and his old lined face was going slowly purple.

“That’s better,” said Pim, approvingly, watching the blue bucket go down the well. “You new?”

“Sort of,” said the man.

“You’ll learn.” Pim picked up his horse bucket. “Here. Was you on the wall this morning? Did you see? When the old ‘uns did the thing? With their hands?” He did it, with his free hand, flat on his chest, very solemn. “Nobody’ll tell me what it’s for. I did it anyway. Seemed rude not to.”

The man stopped drawing water.

“It was kind of you,” he said.

“Was it?” Pim looked pleased. “What’s it for, then?”

The man thought about it.

“It’s so somebody’s looking,” he said.

Pim considered that. He didn’t seem to find it a strange answer. Stable lads, in Aldous’s experience, found very few things strange.

“Right,” said Pim, and went off to the horses with the horse bucket, whistling.

Across the yard, Teague took his hand off the doorframe and went back into the kitchen without a word, and Aldous heard something inside fall over, and Cook shout, and Teague not answer.

Old Oakes was at the forge door when they went past it, after.

He was standing in it the way he’d stood in it for thirty years, with his arms folded and his tongs in one fist and the heat coming out round him. A big old man, grey-bearded, with forearms like hams and a face like a burnt loaf, who’d never been on the red in his life and had made every horseshoe and hinge and spearhead that had gone out onto it since before the war. Salty was behind him, inside, at the second anvil. Salty had gone straight there from the gate. He hadn’t said anything; he’d just gone, the way you’d go to your own room.

The man with the rod stopped in front of the forge door.

Oakes looked at him. Up and down. Not at the face. At the rest. At the hands and the shoulders and the rod, the way a smith looks at a piece of iron to see what it’ll do.

“Still can’t put an edge on that thing, I suppose,” said Oakes.

“It doesn’t need one.”

“Never did.” Oakes sniffed. “Drove me mad. Three weeks I had it on my bench, the year you brought it. Three weeks. Couldn’t scratch it. Couldn’t heat it. Had it in my fire a whole day and it came out cold as a stone in a well.” He looked at the girl beside the man, at the black iron on her back. “Somebody’s put a point on it now, though.”

“The king,” said Alea.

“The king.” Oakes looked at it for a long time. Then at her. Then, for the first time, his face did something, under the beard. “Well. He always did have good hands.” He unfolded his arms. “You’ll want it looking at, while you’re here. That harness is coming loose on the left.”

“Is it?” said Alea, startled, reaching back.

“It is. I’ll see to it. Bring it down tomorrow.” He looked at the man again. “And you’ll be wanting the forge, for something, I suppose. You always wanted the forge for something.”

“Eleven dinners,” said the man.

Oakes stared at him.

“I’ll need a skillet,” said the man. “A big one. And the hearth, in the evenings, when you’re done. For eleven nights.” He looked past Oakes at Salty, who had stopped hammering at the second anvil and was standing very still with the hammer in his hand. “I owe him.”

Oakes looked at him for a long, long moment. Then at Salty. Then back.

“You owe the short lad dinner,” he said.

“Eleven.”

“And you want to cook it. In my forge.”

“Yes.”

Oakes made a noise. It might have been a laugh. It was hard to tell; Aldous had known him nine years and never been sure.

“Nine years,” said Oakes. “Nine years I’ve been waiting to see what you’d want next time you came through that door. I had a list.” He turned round and went back into the heat. “Skillet,” Aldous heard him say, inside, to nobody, in deep disgust. “Black Blade comes home after nine years and he wants a skillet.”

At the second anvil, Salty put the hammer down very carefully, and put his hand over his eyes, and stood there.

The man came to the chapel at dusk.

Aldous was waiting for him. He’d known he’d come. He’d lit the candles, all of them, the way he did on feast days, and the little room with no priest was full of their light, and warm. The bench at the back where Salty had sat all night with the sword on its trestles. The bare altar. The window over it, facing east, toward the red, going dark.

The man came in quietly and sat down on the bench at the back. He’d left the rod outside, leaning against the wall. Aldous had never seen him without it before.

Aldous sat down beside him. He had his book on his knee.

They didn’t say anything for a while. The candles burned.

“I came with the king’s column,” said Aldous, eventually. “On the second evening. I never knew her. I wanted you to know that. I told your friends, on the first day, a healer, that’s all I was ever told, and it was true.” He looked at the book on his knee. “I’ve prayed beside her for seven years. Every month. And I didn’t know her name until a year ago, or what she looked like until this morning.”

The man didn’t say anything.

“This morning,” said Aldous. “In the gate. When your girl looked up.” He was quiet. “I heard the old ones on the wall. All at once. I’ve buried a great many people, and I know that sound.” He turned the book over in his hands. “I didn’t know, until then, that she was loved. Here. In the fort. Not just by you. By all of them.”

“She stitched half of them up,” said the man. “Some of them twice.”

“Teague says three times.”

“Teague exaggerates.”

Aldous almost smiled.

He opened the book. He found the page. He’d marked it with a ribbon, that morning, on the rise. The page with the healers on it, and the six dead, and the line at the bottom on its own. He turned it so the man could see.

A healer. Diwa. The rise beyond the east wall; then the grave on the knoll, under the sword.

The ink on the name was new. You could see it in the candlelight: blacker than the rest, and a little uneven, because his hands weren’t what they’d been.

The man looked at it for a long time.

“I wrote it in this morning,” said Aldous. “Nine years, it said name not given. I’d have asked. But you weren’t a man anyone could ask anything, that autumn.” He was quiet. “And then you were gone, and the sword was there, and nobody else knew.”

“I’m sorry.”

“Don’t be. I’m only telling you.” Aldous closed the book, gently, on the ribbon. “She never had the rites. Did she.”

The man was very still.

“You buried her yourself,” said Aldous. “With your hands. I watched you go out of the gate with the spade, that morning. I was standing in the gateway with this book. You looked at me.” He paused. “You were the only man in the fort who looked at anyone, that day, and you looked at me, and I thought, he wants me to come, and then I thought, no. He wants me not to.” He looked at the altar. “So I didn’t. And nobody said the words over her. Not at the grave. Not ever.”

“No,” said the man.

“I’d like to,” said Aldous. “On the night. With the girl there. If you’ll let me.” He hesitated. “It’s not much. A few words, in an old tongue nobody speaks. And a white cloth, over the stones, for an hour. That’s the custom, here; it’s very old. It’s what we did for the dead on the Redmarch, the ones I could reach.” He was quiet. “It won’t do anything. I know that. I’ve never once in my life believed that the words did anything. I just think somebody should say them.”

The man didn’t answer for a long time.

The candles burned. Outside the window over the altar, the red had gone dark, and the first stars were out over Kharvos.

“She liked chaplains,” said the man, eventually.

Aldous looked at him.

“In the infirmary,” said the man. “She used to say chaplains were the only men in the fort who’d sit with somebody who was dying and not try to fix it. Everybody else wanted to fix it. Surgeons. Healers. Her. Me.” He was looking at the altar. “Chaplains just sat there. She said it was the bravest thing she’d ever seen anyone do, and she couldn’t do it, and it made her furious.” His mouth did something at the corner. “She used to throw things at the old one. Brother Amyas. When he sat with her patients. And then bring him tea.”

Aldous didn’t trust himself to say anything.

“Yes,” said the man. “Say them. With the girl there.” He was quiet. “She’d have liked that. She’d have been furious about it, and liked it, and brought you tea.”

Aldous sat in the chapel for a long time after he’d gone.

The candles burned down, one by one. He let them. He sat on the bench at the back with the book on his knee and thought about a wall, and twenty-five hands, and a man standing on the red in the sun saying their names. And a boy called Pim with a horse bucket. It’s so somebody’s looking.

He thought about a white cloth over a cairn of children’s stones, in a month, under a full moon. The words in the old tongue. A girl with her mother’s face, listening.

He found he was very tired, and very glad, and that his face was wet, and that he didn’t mind.

Before he went to bed, he took the cord out of the inside pocket of his coat.

Purple and gold, short, knotted, with half a bead on the end and a strip of thin blue paper wound round the knot. Hanarisu, the cat girl from the capital, had put it into his hand at the Broken Lance in Valcrest, on the last night, with the same in Maren’s and Oswin’s and Emrys’s and Lucan’s: One each. For the guides. Write on it and hold it, and I’ll hold mine. He’d never used it. He hadn’t been sure it would work. He’d never been sure of anything magic in his life, for all that he’d been a Bonfire-tier mage for twenty years. It was the chaplain in him. You said the words, and you didn’t expect them to do anything, and you said them anyway.

He took his pen out, and wrote on the strip of blue paper, very small, in his own careful hand, in the kingdom’s script. Not many words. It didn’t take many.

Arrived. He looked up.

Then he held it, the way she’d said, in his closed hand, in the dark, in the chapel with no priest, with the last candle going out.

And after a moment, very faintly, it went warm.

The way a hand goes warm when somebody, a long way off, in another city, has taken hold of the other end.
