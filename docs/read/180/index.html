The hens had gone in at dark.

He’d watched them go. All three, in a line, back along the jetty from the end where they’d sat with him for an hour in the snow, past him, up the shingle, across the yard to the hen-house, the big red one last. She’d stopped at the door of it and looked back at him over her shoulder, the way the guard had looked back from the top of the lake road. And then gone in.

He didn’t know what it meant. He’d stopped trying to know what the hens meant a long time ago. Nine years. They’d hated him every day of it, and he’d fed them every day of it, and today they’d sat on the end of his jetty in the first snow because a king had gone up the road and they’d missed him, and so had he, and for an hour there’d been nothing else to do about it but sit.

Tomorrow they’d go for him again. He was fairly sure of that.

He sat on the end of the jetty with the lamp beside him and his legs over the water, and the snow-light coming up all round him off the white shore, and the stars out over the lake, every one of them, doubled in the black.

The wrong stars. He’d called them that for thirty-four years. In his head, on this jetty, every night. The wrong stars. Not because they were wrong. Because they weren’t the ones over a room in Malacca with a fan that clicked on every third turn. He’d looked up at them every night and thought wrong, the way you’d touch a sore tooth with your tongue to see if it still hurt.

He looked up at them now.

He tried to remember when he’d last thought it. The word. He couldn’t. Some time last winter, perhaps. Before five girls came down a lake road with lanterns. He’d looked up at the stars over this lake every night for a year since and they’d just been the stars. Over the lake. Over the cabin, and the hall at the top of the road, and a fort on the edge of the red, and a city in the south with forty thousand paper rods in it, and a tower in Solmere with a drawer standing open and nothing in it.

He hadn’t noticed it go. That was the thing. You never did.

Nine.

He’d been thinking about it all autumn. He couldn’t help it; it was the shape of the year. Nine years since the rise, on the full moon, at the grave, with his daughter on the cairn beside him for the first time and Group 3 waiting on the fallen tree. Nine years of a drawer in a tower, locked every night. Nine years of is he well and he’s well. Nine days north for a thin grey man on a horse that wouldn’t do anything without being asked three times.

And two people, three hundred years ago, in the eleventh year of a dead king’s reign, asking every day for nine years to be sent home. And not being answered.

And line nine.

He thought about that one longest. Higu’s line. At the back of her book, under a copy of his window that she’d made on the ninth evening of the first winter, in pencil, with the ages she’d guessed beside each skill. Eight lines, filled. And two more under them, with a number in front of each and nothing after it. For when you find out, she’d written beside them, small.

He knew what went there. He’d always known. Not the word; nobody knew the word until it came. But the shape of it. It came when he went up. The ninth tier, and the ninth line, at once. The way the eighth had come, in one night, on a rise, with a black sword, and nothing burning under it at all.

It wouldn’t come like that this time. He knew that too. It was coming the other way. A percent at a time, on a jetty, over years, by putting things down.

Sixty-one. Since a letter, on a wall, at Cinderhold.

He hadn’t opened the window since the king left. He didn’t need to. He’d have felt it move. He always did; the quiet came first, before the number. It hadn’t come.

He opened the channel instead.

The small habitual movement, at the air in front of his face. The window, and under it, the other one, the one that wasn’t his and never had been. Thirty-five names in a column. All lit. And the lines going by.

It had snowed everywhere.

That was the first thing he saw, scrolling back. It had snowed everywhere at once, the way it does sometimes in the north at the start of winter, one great slow grey front coming down over the whole kingdom in a day. And thirty-five people in six towns had looked up at it, and every one of them had written it down.

Just that. No name after it. No where. Thirty-four people would read it in the morning and not know what it meant.

He knew what it meant.

He closed the channel.

He heard her come.

Not her feet. You couldn’t hear feet on new snow; it took them in. He heard the lantern. The small creak of the wire handle swinging, and the tick of the hot tin in the cold. Coming down the lake road. Past the gate, where the hens shifted in their sleep and didn’t come out. Down the shingle. And onto the jetty. Slowly. Very carefully, on the boards, the way you’d walk if you’d thought about every step before you took it.

He didn’t look round. He knew who it was. He’d known since the channel. He’d known, if he was honest, since the morning of the pike, a fortnight ago, when she’d sat on this jetty and asked him a question and he hadn’t answered, and she’d looked at him and said not yet as if it were a date she’d written in her book.

She stopped. A little way off. Not close. Not far. Where she’d sat a fortnight ago.

She put the lantern down on the boards. She sat down beside it, wrapped up in a coat too big for her, with a scarf round her face and her white bow crooked on top of it, and put her book on her knees. Closed. With her hands flat on it.

Neither of them said anything for a while.

“You wrote going down,” he said.

“I wrote going down,” Higu agreed.

“Thirty-four people are going to wake up and wonder where.”

“Thirty-three,” said Higu. “Thea knows. She was awake.” She looked at the lake. “And Gigichi. Gigichi’s always awake.” She thought about it. “Thirty-two.”

He nearly laughed.

“You put the king down,” said Higu.

He looked at her.

She wasn’t looking at him. She was looking at the water, with her hands flat on her closed book, and her half-lidded eyes very calm. She said it the way she’d said everything for two years: as if she were reading it off a list.

“Not the king,” she said. “The king’s fine. The thing about the king. Whatever it was. He went up the road this morning and the red hen cried and you stood at the gate a long time, and then you came down here with the hens, and you sat with them.” She was quiet. “You didn’t open your window. But you sat differently. I had a moth on the woodpile.”

“You had a moth on the woodpile.”

“I always have a moth on the woodpile,” said Higu. “It’s the best view.” She went on looking at the water. “And Halvard. You put Halvard down. The first night. On this jetty. I didn’t send a moth. I didn’t need to. Deston heard it from Kingsferry.” She paused. “And the knock. And the letter. And the knife. And the tree. And the cups at the mayor’s. You’ve put down more this autumn than in the whole of the two years I’ve been watching.”

He didn’t say anything.

“And it’s only moved once,” said Higu. “Sixty-one. For the letter.”

“How do you know?”

“Because you’d have looked,” said Higu. “If it had moved again. You’d have opened your window on the end of this jetty the way you always do, and looked at it for a long time, and closed it, and I’d have seen it through the moth. You haven’t. Not since Cinderhold.” She looked down at her book. “So it hasn’t moved. So all those things, the king and Halvard and the knock and the knife, they were real and they mattered and they weren’t it.”

The lantern ticked on the boards.

“There’s something else,” said Higu. “Under them. Something big. I asked you a fortnight ago what else you were carrying, and you didn’t answer, and it wasn’t because you didn’t know.” She was quiet. “It was because you did. And you didn’t want to say it to me. Specifically. To one of us.”

He looked at the water.

“So I worked it out,” said Higu.

She didn’t say it at once. She sat there with her hands on her book and let the lake be quiet for a while, the way he’d have done, the way he’d taught her without ever meaning to, in two years of not answering her questions.

Then she said it.

“It’s us,” said Higu. “Going.”

He didn’t move.

“Thea read the leaf,” said Higu. “It parses. He made a sound. He didn’t know what it was.

He didn’t say anything.

“That’s what you’re carrying,” said Higu. “Isn’t it.”

He looked out at the lake for a long time.

“Yes,” he said.

It was strange, saying it. He’d thought it would be like the letter, or the knock, or the gap in the parapet at Cinderhold: a thing he’d have to walk up to and stand at the edge of for an hour before he could step off it. It wasn’t. He just said it. Yes. On the end of a jetty, in the snow-light, to a girl with a white bow and a closed book. And it went out over the water and didn’t come back.

“The king saw,” he said. “At the forge. Over the leaf. He can see my face. He said I’d been doing it since the spring.” He looked at his hands. “Saying goodbye. A bit at a time. Every time one of you goes up a tier. Every time Thea says it parses. So it won’t all come at once.”

“Does it work?”

“No,” he said. “He said it never works. He said you just lose them twice.” He was quiet. “He said he did it once, all one winter, with his father. And lost the whole winter.”

Higu didn’t say anything.

“I’ve been doing it since the Lists,” he said. “On the east stand. Every round. Every time one of you won. I’d watch your faces and think good, and then I’d think that’s one more step, and I’d put a bit of you down. Somewhere. In advance. So it’d be lighter later.” He looked at the water. “I didn’t know I was doing it. Not till he said.”

“You knew,” said Higu.

He looked at her.

“You knew a fortnight ago,” said Higu. “On this jetty. When I asked. That’s why you didn’t answer.” She looked at him. Not at his face; she couldn’t hold it. Somewhere near it. The way they all did. You’d have said I don’t know, Higu, and fished. You only don’t answer when you know.”

He looked at her for a long time.

“You’d make a good strategist,” he said.

“I am a good strategist,” said Higu. “You keep saying that like it’s news.”

She opened her book.

Not at the front. Not at the back, where his window was, either. Somewhere in the middle. He watched her turn the pages in the lantern-light, very carefully, past moons and watches and lists of fish and lists of non-answers and a page he could just see the top of that said Alea is his and had been written two winters ago in a hand that shook a little.

She found what she wanted. She turned the book round on her knees so he could see.

A page. Near the beginning. Old pencil, gone soft and grey. In the careful hand of somebody who’d been in this world a few months and was writing on a scrap of paper in a militia hall at night, because thirty-five frightened people had just found out they could talk to each other and somebody had to keep the room.

Be kind. Everyone here is scared.

Bad news: say it plainly. Don’t make anyone guess.

Good news: say it loudly.

Nobody is alone.

And under them, the others, added later in other pencils. A number with nothing after it, that had never been written down at all. We wait for him, with a line through it, gently, in ink, the night of a supper. And When you need each other, go.

“The rules,” said Higu. “The first four. The night the channel started. I wrote them, and Thea copied them in.” She looked at the page. “I wrote the fourth one last. I nearly didn’t. I thought it was too much like the first.”

He looked at it.

4. Nobody is alone.

He’d read it a hundred times. More. In the channel, in two years, at the bottom of a line after somebody’s worst night, one name after another, all the way down. Nobody is alone. Thirty-four times. He’d read it on the end of this jetty and had to stop reading, more than once, and put his window down on his knees and sit there.

He’d never once thought it was for him.

“It’s for the channel,” he said.

“It’s for everyone,” said Higu.

“I’m not in the channel.”

“You’ve read every line of it for two years,” said Higu. “Every one. At night. On this jetty. We all know you do. You read Dennis at two in the morning and answered him by dawn at the bottom of the first window. You read Gigichi’s you okay and Tiger’s no, not really. You read Kei’s twenty-seven and twelve doses. You read going down tonight and knew what it meant before I’d got to the gate.” She put her finger on the page, under the fourth line. “You’re in it. You’ve always been in it. You’re the only person in it who never writes anything, that’s all.” She looked at him. “That doesn’t mean you’re not in it. That just means you’re Higu.”

He looked at her.

“I hardly write anything,” said Higu. “In the channel. Two years. The rules, and rule four when somebody needs it, and noted, and going down. Thea says I’m the quietest person in the kingdom.” She looked down at the book. “I’m still in it. I’m in it more than anyone. I read every line.”

The lantern ticked.

“Rule four doesn’t say nobody in the channel,” said Higu. “It doesn’t say nobody who came through the circle. It doesn’t say nobody until the door opens.” Her finger was still on the line. “It says nobody.”

He didn’t say anything for a long time.

The stars went round, a little. The snow-light lay on everything. Somewhere up the slope, in the hen-house, a hen said something in her sleep and was told off by another one.

“You don’t know what we’ll choose,” said Higu.

He looked at her.

“You keep saying it,” said Higu. “I want you to be able to choose. You said it to Thea over the circle. You said it to the mayor. You’ll say it at the end of it, probably, when it opens, standing there so we’re not on our own.” She closed the book on her finger. “And you’ve never once let yourself think about what we’ll actually choose. You’ve just decided. All thirty-five of us, back along the threads. Gone. And you’ve been saying goodbye to all thirty-five of us since midsummer.”

“Some of you have mothers waiting.”

“Some of us have mothers waiting,” Higu agreed. “Yan’s mother, every day. Others.” She was quiet. “Some of us have nobody. Some of us have a hall at the top of a lake road and a step and a basket and a mayor who knocks three times. Some of us have a guild in a ruin, and an infirmary on a wharf, and a forge on the edge of the red with a second anvil, and a tree.” She looked at the water. “Some of us are going to be forty by the time the door opens. Fifty, maybe. Thea says more than three tiers. You heard her.”

“I heard her.”

“I don’t know what I’ll choose,” said Higu. “That’s the point. Nobody does. Not one of us. We haven’t decided. We can’t decide yet. It’s years away, and we’ll be different people by then, and the door will be a different door.” She looked at him. Near his face. As near as she could get. “So stop saying goodbye to people who haven’t decided.”

He looked at her.

“Have us,” said Higu. “While we’re here. All of us. Every one. However long it is.” She opened her book again, to the page, and put her finger back on the fourth line. “And when the door opens, you’ll be at the end of it. And whoever goes, goes. And whoever stays, stays. And either way you won’t be on your own. Because—“

She tapped the line. Once.

“Rule four,” said Higu.

The quiet came.

He felt it come. The way it always came, before the number. The world going still round him on the end of the jetty, listening. The lake, and the snow, and the stars, and the hens asleep, and the hall at the top of the road, and a girl beside him with her finger on a line in a book. Listening. As if the whole of it had been waiting for him to put something down, for a long time, and had been very patient about it, and was very glad.

He didn’t put it down. Not exactly. He couldn’t have said what he did. He sat on the end of the jetty and stopped holding something. That was all. A thing he’d been holding since the Lists, since midsummer, since a girl’s box on a roll and forty thousand paper rods. Holding it the way you’d hold a stone in your fist so long you forgot your fist was closed. Holding it so it would be lighter later.

He opened his hand.

He didn’t decide to. He found it had opened. On his knee, on the end of the jetty, in the snow-light. His right hand, open, palm up, empty, with the cold on it.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he lifted it, and did the small habitual thing at the air in front of his face, and his window opened.

He turned it round. All the way. The way he’d turned it round once before, on the ninth evening of the first winter, for five girls at his table who’d waited sixteen months to see it.

Higu wrote that down.

Higu wrote that down.

Whatever the king’s going to make. He won’t tell me.” He looked at the water. “Walking east. With three of them. And four more asking.”

“In capitals,” he agreed. He was quiet a moment. “I’ll have to answer.”

“No,” he said. “I’ll think of something.”

Higu wrote that down too. Then she looked at the list. He couldn’t see it from where he sat. He didn’t need to.

“That’s next year,” said Higu.

“That’s next year.”

“You’ve never had a next year,” said Higu. “Not out loud. Not in two years. I’ve got every non-answer you’ve ever given me written down. I don’t know. Maybe. We’ll see. Not yet.” She looked at the page. “Nine of them, about next year. I counted. Every one of them a non-answer.”

“Nine,” said Higu. She tore the list out of the book.

He’d never seen her tear anything out of the book. Not once, in two years. She tore it very carefully, along the stitching, so it came out clean, and folded it in half, and held it out to him.

“Put it on the wall,” she said. “Over the table. Next to the drawing. Like the king’s dad.”

He took it.

He sat on the end of the jetty with a list of next year in his hand, in Higu’s small neat pencil, folded in half. Fish. A tree. Twelve dinners. Gabe. East. Answer them. And under it, in a different pencil, smaller, that she must have added when he wasn’t looking:

Ask again in the spring. (Gigichi.)

He put it in his coat. The inside pocket. Over his heart. With the stone.

The light came up behind the hills on the far shore a little after six.

Higu had gone to sleep at some point. Sitting up, against the last post of the jetty, wrapped in her coat with her book in her lap and her scarf over her nose and her white bow very crooked. He’d put his own coat over her about four. She hadn’t woken. She’d said, very clearly, in her sleep, noted, and gone on sleeping.

He fished.

He baited the line, and cast, the long slow curve going out over the black water in the grey, and the float settling, and sat with the rod across his knees in his shirtsleeves in the cold and waited. The way he’d waited thirty years. For the float to go down, and not pulling until it was time.

It went down.

He brought it in. A perch, striped, cold and bright. He put a string through its gill and hung it over the side.

He cast again.

By the time the sun came over the hills there were five on the string. Two perch, a bream, the long silver fish that had no name in any language he’d ever spoken, and a pike. Small. Very angry about it.

He looked at them for a moment.

Then he took the stone out of his coat. Flat, grey, a lake stone, the size of his palm. Higu had put it in the bottom of an empty basket on a step, two months ago, so the wind wouldn’t take it, when he went away. He’d taken it out a fortnight ago and put five fish in its place. He’d carried it every day since.

He held it in his open hand a moment, in the sunrise, on the end of the jetty.

And put it back in his coat. With the list. Over his heart. It wasn’t heavy. It had never been heavy. It was a stone you kept, not a stone you carried.

Beside him, against the post, Higu opened her eyes.

She looked at him. Near his face. At the five fish on the string, and the sunrise on the snow, and his coat over her. She didn’t say anything for a moment. She’d been asleep; it took her a while to come back, the way it took Higu a while to come back from anything, a moth, a page, a dream.

“Morning,” he said.

Higu looked at him a moment longer.

“Morning,” she said.

They went up together. The two of them, on the lake road, in the sun, with the snow going blue in the shadows of the field walls and gold where the light was on it. Higu carried her book. He carried the fish.

Past the cabin, where Alea was at the loft window with the crooked red curtain pulled back and her chin on her hands, watching them go, and didn’t wave, and didn’t need to. Past the gate. Past the field, white, with a ring of briar at the bottom of it, heaped with snow, and green inside. Past the mayor’s house, where a big grey-headed man was standing in an open doorway in his shirt with a cup of tea, and lifted it an inch. Across the corner of the square.

To the hall at the top of the lake road. With a step worn hollow in the middle, and a willow basket on it, empty, with straw in the bottom.

He knelt, and put the fish in. One at a time, on the straw, side by side. Five.

Higu stood on the step above him, with her book, and watched him do it. And when he’d finished, and straightened up, she didn’t go in. She stood on the step, with the sun on her, and looked at him. Near his face.

“Nine years,” she said. “At the end of that jetty.”

“Nine years.”

“And now there’s a list on the wall,” said Higu.

“There will be,” he said. “When I get back down.”

Higu nodded. As if that were settled. Then she turned, and opened the door of the militia hall, and went in. And he heard her, inside, going up the stairs, not quietly. And a door. And Gigichi’s voice, very loud, saying where have you BEEN, and Higu’s, quite calm, saying going down, and then Xola, and then all of them, and then the noise of five people in a hall at the top of a lake road waking up on the first morning after the first snow and all talking at once.

He stood in the road and listened to it for a while.

Then he went back down the lake road toward the water. Not hurrying. The way he walked everywhere.

He pinned it over the table that morning. Beside the drawing. On the same nail, a little to the right, so that the corner of it touched the corner of a blue crayon lake with forty-one planks on its jetty and three people on the end, holding hands.

Fish. A tree. Twelve dinners. Gabe. East. Answer them.

Ask again in the spring.

Alea came down the ladder while he was doing it. She stood at the bottom with her blanket round her shoulders and looked at it for a long time. She couldn’t read a word of it. She looked at it twice anyway. She didn’t say anything. She came and stood beside him at the table and put her hand into his, the way it had gone into his on the end of the jetty, and at the top of a hill, and at the head of a cairn, without deciding.

Her hand was cold. Her mother’s had always been cold.

His was warm.

They stood there and looked at the wall. A drawing that said COME HOME SOON, and a list that said next year, side by side, a hand’s width apart, the corners touching.

“Dad,” said Alea.

“Mm.”

“The hens are out.”

He looked at the window.

They were. All three. In the yard, in the snow, in the sun. In a line. The big red one in front. Looking at the cabin door with deep and patient hatred, the way they’d looked at it every morning for nine years.

He looked at them for a long moment.

“Some things,” he said, “are going to take longer.”

He went out anyway.

End of Volume Six.
