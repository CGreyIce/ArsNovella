The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 5: The Lists

Chapter 127

Sideways

5,950 words · 26 min read

The job wasn’t the bracket.

Drew had said that to himself so many times, over so many years, that it had worn smooth, like a stone you keep in your pocket. Anybody could draw a bracket. You needed a ruler and a list of names and a bit of patience. The job wasn’t the rules, either, or the prizes, or the stream, or the casters. The job was being the person who stood in the middle of it all when it went wrong and smiled and said okay, in a voice that made everybody else believe it was.

He’d been doing it for ten years. On Earth, from a bedroom, with a headset on and four tabs open and somebody’s internet dying in the deciding round. Here, from the top of a wall at Thornwall, with a hand on his oldest friend’s chest. It was the same job. It was always the same job.

But he’d be lying if he said he didn’t love the bracket.

He was standing in front of it now. On the sand of the Lists, at the foot of the east wall, an hour after noon on the fifty-ninth day, with the sun coming down hot into the great pale bowl and the stands all round him empty except for pigeons. Forty feet long. Lucan had wanted it painted in gold leaf. Drew had talked him down to black paint on whitewash, with gold only on the lines, and the painter had spent four days on a ladder with a plumb line and a pot and Drew standing at the bottom telling him where everything went.

It was the most beautiful bracket he’d ever made. Five squads, down the left. Three trials across the top. The Trios underneath, a proper single-elimination tree, sixteen slots, room to grow. The Singles under that, where Lucan had insisted. Every line ruled straight. Every box empty.

And in the top right-hand corner, a spider.

It had been there since the second day. A big brown garden spider with a cross on its back, sitting in the middle of a web it had built across the corner of the last box, in the space where the name of whoever won the whole thing was going to go. The painter had wanted to knock it down. Drew had said no.

“It’s seeded,” he’d said.

The painter had looked at him. “It’s a spider.”

“It’s the top seed,” Drew had said. “It got here first. That’s how seeding works.”

The painter had gone away shaking his head. And Drew had stood at the bottom of the ladder and looked at the spider in the top box, and thought, with the private, quiet, entirely serious part of himself that nobody but Tiger ever saw: that box is going to be the hardest one to fill.

He didn’t know yet what went in it.

They started coming in at the third hour.

He’d told everyone the fourth. They came at the third anyway, because they were who they were, and because nobody in the Broken Lance had slept properly in three nights, and because a draw was a draw. Tiger first, with his hair on end, straight from the dawn class, still talking about being early, earlier, not faster, Drew, earlier. Then Group 7, from the inn, in their loose line. Then Groups 1 and 4 and 6 in a crowd, with the squid, and the penguin, and Calvin’s kettle, which had become a sort of banner. Then Group 2 down the hill, Hana with her wrist held against her chest in a way she didn’t seem to know she was doing, and Oswin still carrying a book in both arms as if somebody might take it off him.

They spread out on the bottom tiers of the east stand, under the bracket, the way people spread out at the start of a lobby. Thirty of them. Guides and guards round the edges. Maren with a list. Kestra on the rail of the royal box, one boot up. Lucan, who had been in the Lists since dawn and had changed his cloak twice, in the middle of the sand, walking in small circles, weeping in anticipation.

And the water-carrier.

He came in last, with Alea, from the practice ground. He didn’t sit with any of the groups. He went up the stand to a step about halfway, off to one side, a little apart from everyone, and sat down on it with his rod across his knees and two Pollen Puffs on his head that Bee had given up trying to reclaim, and looked at the bracket.

Drew watched him do it.

He always ran in, he’d said to Tiger once, on a shed roof at Thornwall, in the autumn, in the dark. In every game. First through the door. And he’s been standing at the back for a year, by a lake, and I think he hates it.

He wasn’t standing at the back now. He wasn’t running in, either. He was sitting halfway up, off to the side, where you could see the whole thing at once. The caster’s seat. Drew knew it. He’d sat in it for ten years.

At the fourth hour he stood up on the sand in front of the bracket and turned round, and thirty people went quiet.

He didn’t have to raise his voice. He never did. That was the other half of the job. You learned, over enough years and enough lobbies, where to stand and how to breathe so that a room would listen without your having to ask it to. Lucan had never even seen him do it, the first time. He’d only read about it, in the autumn, in a letter Corvin had written for him. I’ve never once been in a room with all of them. Let me run it. Lucan had read that and wept and written back the same day.

“Okay,” said Drew.

That was all, for a moment. He let them look at him. Then at the bracket. Then back.

“Two years ago,” he said, “we were in a hall. All of us. In Solmere. Thirty-five people, in a room, at once. And somebody looked at us, and sorted us.” He let that sit. “In fives. By who knew who. By who’d brought who. Group 1 were the streamers Ice watched. Group 2 were Alice’s. Group 4 knew Deston. Group 6 were his class. Group 7 came through Yan.” He nodded up at the water-carrier on his step. “It was a good sort. It was the right sort. If you’re going to send people to the edges of a kingdom on their own, you send them with the people they know.”

He paused.

“We’ve been in those fives for two years,” he said. “We’ve stood next to the same four people every single day. We know exactly how they breathe. We know which way they’ll move before they do. We’d die for them. Some of us nearly have.” He didn’t look at Tiger. He didn’t need to. “And we’ve never once stood next to anyone else.”

He turned, and put his hand flat on the whitewash beside the bracket.

“So I’m turning the hall on its side,” said Drew.

He’d thought about it for a year. Since before he’d ever written to Lucan. Since a night in the first winter at Thornwall, lying awake, listening to Tiger breathe in the next bed and the wind come down off the Greyspine, thinking about thirty-five people in seven places who’d only ever known each other as names in a channel.

It wasn’t a tournament. Not really. He’d known that from the start. One Gleaming soldier from the king’s guard would beat any of them, any day, on any sand. You couldn’t measure the thirty-five against each other and call it the Lists; it’d be like a bracket of first-week players. It’d tell you nothing.

But you could do this.

“Five squads,” said Drew. “Seven to a squad. One from every posting.” He touched the bracket’s left-hand column: five empty boxes, each with seven empty lines. “One from Wrenhaven. One from Solmere. One from Cinderhold. One from Kingsferry. One from Thornwall. One from Duskhollow. One from here.” He looked round at them. “Nobody stands next to anyone from their own group. Not once. Not all week. Every squad’s a lobby of strangers who’ve been reading each other in the channel for two years and never shared a fire.”

The stand had gone very quiet.

“Three trials,” said Drew. “The Banner, on midsummer afternoon. You hold a banner on a mound against the guides and the guards for as long as you can. Sand-glasses. Longest hold wins.” He touched the next column. “The Gate, two days after. You hold a gate. Tiger built it. The champions of the Lists try to take it off you.”

“It’ll stand,” said Tiger, from the second tier. Loudly. Then, a lot more quietly: “Long enough.”

“It’ll stand long enough,” Drew agreed. He didn’t look at the water-carrier. He didn’t need to. He’d heard the story from Hyvred at dawn, on the walk back to the inn: a gate in the moonlight, and a shed, and a goat called Cabbage. He’d stood in the arch the whole time and heard most of it himself.

“And the third one,” he said, and stopped.

He hadn’t told anyone about the third one. Not even Tiger.

“A long time ago,” said Drew, “before any of this, I ran a tournament.”

He’d said it in the channel. Everybody knew. But he said it again now, out loud, on the sand of the Lists, because some things you have to say in the room.

“Apex. Trios. Online. Thirty-odd teams, a bracket in a spreadsheet, two casters who’d never cast before and a stream that crashed twice. I ran it from my bedroom. I didn’t sleep for three days.” He smiled. He couldn’t help it. “And in the final, there was this one team. And one of them died in every single round. Every round. First. Loudly. Narrating it. And he’s down. He’s gone. A tragic loss for the team. He had so much to live for.” A few people in the stand laughed. Most of them knew the voice he was doing. “The casters were laughing so hard they couldn’t cast. So I muted them. And I did the commentary myself. And his two teammates won it anyway. Carried him the whole way.”

He didn’t look up at the step. He felt it, though. The way you feel somebody in the crowd go still.

“So,” said Drew. “The third trial’s the Trios. Open entry. Any three of you. Any groups. Pick your own.” He touched the tree under the squad column: sixteen empty slots. “Single elimination. Day after the Gate.” He paused. “For the one I ran before.”

There was a silence.

Then, from halfway up the stand, off to the side, a voice. Quite quiet. In English.

“I did not get carried.”

Thirty people turned round.

“You died in every round,” said Drew, without turning round. “I’ve got it on video. On a hard drive. In a different universe.”

“I was baiting.”

“You were baiting for eleven rounds.”

“It’s a strategy.”

“It’s a crime,” said Drew, and the stand broke. Thirty people laughing at once, on the bottom tiers of the Lists, under a forty-foot bracket with a spider on it. Chris was laughing so hard he’d had to put his notebook down. Hako was lying on his back on the stone. And up on his step, the water-carrier had put his hand over his face, and his shoulders were going, and the two Pollen Puffs on his head were buzzing indignantly at being jostled.

Drew stood on the sand and let it go on for as long as it wanted. That was part of the job too. You learned when to let a room laugh.

Lucan came in on the Singles.

He’d been waiting for his moment. Drew had seen him circling. He came across the sand in his red cloak, which had gold on the hem today, with his hands clasped in front of him like a man about to make an announcement at a wedding, and stood beside Drew in front of the bracket, and looked up at it, and wept a little, and then turned to the stand.

“It’s the Lists,” said Lucan.

He said it with enormous feeling. He’d been master of the Lists for fifteen years before the Advisor made him a guide. He’d run every midsummer tournament in that time. He’d sold a horned girl and an oni to a crowd that wanted to spit on them, and made them love them. He knew more about this sand than anyone alive.

“It’s the Lists,” said Lucan again. “Squads are very well. Trios are very well. Banners and gates, very well, very modern, I’ve no objection.” He spread his hands. “But for three hundred years, at midsummer, on this sand, somebody has stood alone in front of the whole kingdom and been looked at. One person. On their own. With nobody beside them. That’s what the Lists are.” His voice wobbled. “Somebody has to stand alone in them.”

He looked at Drew.

Drew had known this was coming. Lucan had said it to him in the spring, in a letter, and then every single day this week at the Lance. And he’d thought about it every time, honestly, the way he thought about bracket seeds when it mattered. Whether it fit. Whether a tournament about people who’d stood next to each other had any room in it for someone standing alone.

And he’d decided, somewhere in the last week, watching the water-carrier sit on a step a little apart from everyone, that it did.

“The Singles,” said Drew. He touched the last tree on the bracket, the one Lucan had made the painter add. “Open entry. Anyone who wants. Blunted steel, any path. One bout at a time, on the side sand, in the gaps between the Fledgling rounds. Final on the last morning.” He looked at the stand. “For anyone who wants to stand on their own in front of everybody and find out what that’s like.”

Lucan burst into tears and hugged him.

Drew let him.

The draw was a helmet.

An old one, a cavalry helmet with a dent in it from somebody’s war, that Lucan had produced from somewhere with great ceremony. Inside it were seven bowls’ worth of folded slips, tipped together and then separated again on the sand into seven little heaps, one for each posting: five names in each heap, in Maren’s small hand, in English and in this world’s script both.

“Who draws?” said Lucan.

Drew had thought about this too. It mattered who drew. He’d watched draws go wrong because the wrong person put their hand in the hat. It had to be someone nobody could argue with. Someone outside it.

He looked up at the step.

The water-carrier looked back at him. Drew couldn’t see his face. Nobody could. But he’d spent a year reading people over the tops of brackets and he could read the set of a man’s shoulders, and he saw the answer before it came.

“No,” said the water-carrier quietly. “I’ve drawn enough of you.”

Drew nodded. He’d thought he’d say that.

“A child, then,” said Lucan. “It’s traditional. An innocent hand.” He turned and looked out at the empty stands, as if expecting an innocent hand to appear. “There’s always a child.”

There was.

He’d been sitting on the top tier of the west stand since the morning, on his own, very small. Drew had seen him come in and not thought anything of it. Children got into the Lists all the time; they climbed the walls. He was about eight, in a patched shirt, with a paper crown of horns on his head. Old horns, from last summer, battered and soft, with a little white mask stitched on the front.

Lucan saw him, and went very still.

“You,” said Lucan.

Ina stood up on the fourth tier.

He’d had the mask on, because the gates of the Lists were open and people kept looking in. Now he reached up and pushed it to the side of his head and stood there looking across the whole width of the Lists at a small boy on the top of the west stand in a paper crown of horns.

Drew didn’t know the story. Not then. He found out that night, at the long table, from Newish: a festival bout in the Lists last summer, and a thing that had come over the stands for Winter, and Ina with the mask on writing Catch. And afterwards, in the silence, high up in the east stand, a little boy of about seven in a paper crown of horns had stood up on his bench, and taken his horns off, and held them up over his head. The first one. And then ten thousand people had done the same.

“Come down,” called Ina. In his warm British garden-party voice, across the sand. “If you’d like. We need a hand.”

The boy came down. It took him a long time. The steps were very big for him. He came across the sand with his paper horns on and stopped in front of the helmet and looked up at all of them, thirty strangers in strange clothes, a squid, a penguin, a girl with ice in her hair and a man with an oni’s horn, and didn’t look frightened at all.

“What’s your name?” said Lucan.

“Wil,” said the boy.

“Wil,” said Lucan. “Would you draw some names out of a helmet for me?”

Wil considered.

“Can I keep the helmet after?”

“No,” said Lucan.

“Okay,” said Wil, and put his hand in.

It took a long time. Thirty-five names, one heap at a time, seven heaps, five draws. Wil took each slip out and held it up and Maren read it out, in her clear exact voice, and Drew wrote it in the box, and after every name the stand did something: cheered, or groaned, or laughed, or went ohhh.

Squad A came out first. Gigichi, from Wrenhaven. Jared, from Solmere. Wendy, from Cinderhold, drawn in her absence, three days out on the eastern road. Calvin, from Kingsferry, who lifted his kettle. Kei, from Thornwall. Azac, from Duskhollow. Hako, from here.

“It’ll be fine,” said Hako, delighted, to the whole squad.

“It’d better be,” said Gigichi.

Squad B. PenguChi. Milli. Salty, in absence. Gabe. Tiger. RX. Ina.

Tiger looked at the list. Then at Gabe, the biggest of the thirty-five, a man with forge-black in the creases of his hands who’d spent a year at an anvil in Kingsferry. Then at Salty’s name, a man who’d carried a black stone on his back across the Redmarch. Then at Ina.

“We’re holding my gate,” said Tiger, slowly. “Us. We’re holding my gate.”

“Squad B holds the Gate on day sixty-three,” said Drew. “Same as everyone else. Each squad gets a go.”

“We’re holding my gate,” said Tiger, and his tail went straight up, and he looked at Gabe like a man looking at a wall that had just volunteered to be a wall.

“I’m not leaning on it,” said Gabe. “I’ll tell you that now. If it falls on me I’m going home.”

“It won’t fall.”

“Mm,” said Gabe.

“If we’re holding a gate,” said Ina, from the fourth tier, in his garden-party voice, “we ought to have a name that sounds as if we mean it.”

“The House,” said Tiger at once. “Because it stands.”

“Absolutely not,” said RX, very fast. “No. Too sad. Everybody’ll know why. Next.”

They looked at each other, the five of them in the stand, with Salty’s name in the box for a sixth. Tiger with his ears back. Gabe like a wall. RX already thinking of something else. Ina with his mask pushed up. Milli, very still, with his tail round his ankles.

“Stronghold,” said PenguChi.

She said it calmly, the way she said everything, sitting on the bottom tier with her penguin leaning on her boot. Everybody looked at her.

“It’s a place you hold,” said PenguChi. “That’s what we’re doing. Holding.” She shrugged. “And it’s strong.”

Tiger stared at her. Then his tail went straight up again.

“Stronghold,” he said, reverently.

Drew wrote it at the top of the box. THE STRONGHOLD.

Squad C came out of the helmet one slip at a time, and with every slip the stand got quieter, and then louder, and then started laughing before Maren had even finished reading.

Thea. Hana. Yan, in absence. Deston. Hyvred. Xav. Chris.

Seven names in the box. Drew wrote the last one and stood back and looked at it and felt something inside him that was nearly despair and nearly joy, the exact feeling of a tournament organiser who has just watched a fair random draw produce the most unbalanced lobby in history.

“That’s,” said Calvin, from the stand, and stopped.

“That’s every one of the seven,” said Chris. He’d got his notebook open. He was looking at his list. “That’s every single person who ends up running their group. Every— every hub. Thea. Hana. Yan. Deston. Hyvred. Xavier. Me.” He looked up. “That’s seven people who have never once in two years not been the one making the call.”

“Oh no,” said Hyvred, with enormous delight.

“We’ll be fine,” said Thea.

“We’ll be fine if we listen to me,” said Xav.

“We’ll be fine if we listen to each other,” said Hana, smoothly.

“I’ll log who’s right,” said Chris.

“Yan’s going to be so annoyed,” said Deston. “He’s going to arrive and find out he’s in a squad with six people who all want to be him.”

Drew looked at the box. Seven names. All of them, he knew, already deciding, privately, that they’d be the one who called it.

He picked up the brush and wrote, at the top of the box, in neat black capitals:

THE COMMITTEE.

The stand screamed.

Squad D. Higu. Aleaka. Maigo, in absence. Rachael. Bee. Malcolm. Winter.

“We need a name,” said Bee immediately. “Everybody else is getting a name.”

“We didn’t get a name,” said Gigichi, from the stand.

“We’re the Kettle,” said Calvin. “Obviously.”

“We’re the Kettle?”

“I’m holding it,” said Calvin. “That makes it a decision.”

Squad D looked at each other. Higu, very calm, with a moth on her shoulder. Aleaka, with a hawk. Rachael, with her sleeves rolled and her face saying she’d already decided this was going to be annoying. Malcolm, with his notebook of contingencies, already writing IF we lose the mound. Winter, very still, with her face doing nothing. And Bee, antennae up, wings tense.

“Wolf,” said Bee.

There was a pause.

“It’s a dog,” said Rachael. “Everybody knows it’s a dog. You’ve drawn about four hundred of them. It’s a dog.”

“I know it’s a dog,” said Bee. “We’re called Wolf.”

“That makes no sense.”

“It doesn’t have to make sense. It has to be ours.” Bee folded her arms. “Anyone who wants to argue with me can draw a better one.”

Nobody wanted to draw a better one.

“Fine,” said Winter, after a moment. Very calmly. “Wolf.”

“Thank you,” said Bee.

Drew wrote it. WOLF. Under it, because he couldn’t help it, he added a very small dog.

That left one heap of slips in each pile and the last box empty, and Drew knew before Wil put his hand in the helmet that his own name was in it. He’d put it there himself. That morning. In Maren’s hand, because he’d asked her to write it.

“You’re in it?” Lucan had said, appalled, when he found out.

“The bracket’s fair,” Drew had said, “or it isn’t.”

Squad E came out. Xola. Cursiffe. Niko, in absence. Dennis. Drew. John. Newish.

Drew wrote his own name in the box with a perfectly steady hand.

“We’ll need a name,” said Dennis. He was already standing up. He had the look of a man who could smell an opportunity. “I’d like to propose that we sell the naming rights. There are merchants in this city who would pay a great deal of money to have their—“

“No,” said four people at once.

“I’m just saying there’s a market—“

“Except Newish,” said Newish.

Everyone looked at her.

She was sitting on the third tier with her knees up and her hands in her pockets, and her face perfectly straight, and her eyes very bright. She didn’t look up at the step. She was very carefully not looking up at the step.

“That’s the name,” said Newish. “Squad E. Except Newish.” She shrugged. “That way I’m in it, and I’m not. It’s very me. I’ve been in that one for three years.” She glanced sideways at Drew. “Write it.”

Up on the step, off to the side, Drew heard something that might have been a man trying very hard not to laugh out loud and failing.

Xola looked at John. John, with his three little motes of light going round his head, opened his eyes and looked at Cursiffe. Cursiffe tipped his head back, slowly, and smiled his sly half-smile.

“I’ll allow it,” said Cursiffe.

“I’m going to get it put on a banner,” said Dennis, who had clearly already worked out how to make money from it anyway.

Drew wrote it. EXCEPT NEWISH. In black capitals, at the top of the last box.

Lucan, who had understood none of it, wept anyway.

When it was done, the sun had gone round to the west and the bracket was full.

Not full. The squads were full. Five boxes of seven names, with five names in each that the stand had cheered and one in each that had been drawn for somebody three days away on an eastern road. The trials were blank. The Trios tree was blank. The Singles tree was blank. And the top box, the last one, in the right-hand corner, was still empty except for a spider.

Wil had sat down on the sand at the foot of the bracket with the helmet in his lap, which Lucan had pretended not to notice.

“Thank you, Wil,” said Drew.

“Can I come and watch?”

“You can come and watch,” said Drew. “Sit at the front. Bring the horns.”

Wil looked at the bracket for a long time. At all the names he couldn’t read. Then he looked up at Ina, who’d come down from the stand to stand beside him with his mask pushed to the side of his head.

“Are you going to do the fire again?” said Wil.

Ina crouched down so that he was level with him. His one purple horn, and his own face, with his own fanged grin, which was a lot less frightening than the mask’s.

“Would you like me to?”

Wil thought about it, with tremendous seriousness.

“Yes,” he said.

“Then I’ll do the fire again,” said Ina.

Lucan gave him the chair at sunset.

Drew hadn’t known he was going to. Nobody had. Lucan had been going in and out of the master’s box all afternoon, and Drew had assumed he was fussing with the cushions, the way he fussed with everything. Then at sunset, when the others had started going back to the inn in twos and threes and the bracket was a long black-and-gold shadow on the east wall, Lucan came down the steps of the master’s box with something in his arms, and set it on the sand in front of Drew, and stood back.

A chair. Old dark wood, carved at the back with the crossed lances of the Lists and a crown, worn pale on the arms where hands had rested on it for a very long time. A faded red cushion. One leg very slightly shorter than the others.

“The master’s chair,” said Lucan. His voice was quite steady, for once. “I sat in it for fifteen years. Midsummer, every year, on the platform, so the whole sand could see me. My master sat in it before me, for thirty. It’s older than the king’s grandfather.” He looked down at it. “It goes in the box, at midsummer. For whoever’s running the Lists.”

“Lucan,” said Drew.

“You’re running the Lists,” said Lucan. “Not me. I knew in the autumn, when I read your letter. I’ve never once been in a room with all of them. I read that and I thought: that’s a master. That’s the only thing a master ever wants. Everybody in one place.” He wiped his eyes, finally. “So. Sit in it.”

Drew looked at the chair.

He’d sat in a lot of chairs. A desk chair in a bedroom, with a broken wheel. A camp stool at Thornwall. The ridge of a shed roof. He’d never once sat in one that anybody had handed to him.

He sat in it.

It rocked very slightly on its short leg. It was the most comfortable chair he’d ever sat in in his life.

“It rocks,” said Drew.

“It’s always rocked,” said Lucan, with deep satisfaction. “You’ll get used to it. I did.” And he turned round and went off across the sand toward the gate, in his red cloak with the gold on the hem, not weeping at all, which Drew had never once seen him do, and would remember for a long time.

The water-carrier was still on his step when the sun went down.

He hadn’t moved all afternoon. He’d sat halfway up the east stand, off to one side, through the whole draw, with the rod across his knees and the two Pollen Puffs asleep on his head, watching. Laughing at the Committee. Very carefully not laughing at Except Newish. Going still, once, when Wil came down the stand in his paper horns, and staying still for a long time after.

Drew left the chair on the sand and went up.

He sat down on the step beside him. They didn’t say anything for a while. The bracket was a long dark shape on the wall in front of them now, the gold lines catching the last of the light. The spider was a small black knot in the top corner. The sand was empty and raked and going blue.

“Eleven rounds,” said the water-carrier, eventually.

“It was eleven rounds.”

“I was baiting.”

“You were baiting,” agreed Drew, “for eleven rounds, and Yan and your third carried you to a win, and I muted the casters, and then you got on the stream afterwards and gave a winner’s speech about teamwork and trusting your squad.”

“I meant every word.”

“I know you did.” Drew smiled. “That’s what made it so funny.”

The water-carrier laughed, quietly. One of the Puffs woke up and buzzed and went back to sleep.

They sat there. Drew looked at the bracket. He’d been looking at it all day; he could have drawn it with his eyes shut. Five squads. Three trials. Two trees. One empty box in the corner.

“You did that last night,” said Drew. “With Tiger.”

The water-carrier didn’t say anything.

“I’ve been holding him for a year and a half,” said Drew. “Since the pass. Hands on his chest. Not here. Not on your own. Not ever. I’m good at it. It’s the job. You stand there and you hold the thing that’s going wrong and you smile.” He was quiet. “And then you walked into the yard and stood next to him in front of a gate, and asked what the goat was called. And he put some of it down. I watched him. For the first time since the pass.” He breathed out. “So I put some of it down too. Just for a bit. In the arch. I didn’t have to hold it for about an hour.”

“You always held it,” said the water-carrier. “Back home. Every tournament. Every crash.”

“Somebody has to.”

“I know.” A pause. “Doesn’t mean you have to do it on your own.”

Drew looked at him. At a face he couldn’t see.

“You came and sat in the caster’s seat,” he said. “Today. Halfway up, to the side. Where you can see the whole thing. I clocked it the second you sat down.”

“It’s a good seat.”

“It’s my seat,” said Drew. “It’s been my seat for ten years.” He paused. “You used to always run in. In every game. First through the door. Tiger asked me once, on a shed roof, if I thought you’d come. And I said I thought you’d been standing at the back for a year, and you hated it.” He looked at the bracket. “You’re not standing at the back. You’re not running in, either.”

“No.”

“What are you doing?”

The water-carrier thought about it for a long time.

“Watching,” he said. “The whole thing. All of you. From a step.” He turned the rod in his hands. “I’ve watched you for two years. One at a time. In a channel. From the end of a jetty. I’ve never seen you all at once.” He was quiet. “I wanted to see what it looked like when someone turned it on its side.”

Drew didn’t say anything.

“It looks good,” said the water-carrier. “Thank you.”

They sat there until it was dark. Then a bit longer.

“There’s one box left,” said the water-carrier, at some point. He nodded at the corner of the bracket, where the spider was.

“There is.”

“Who goes in it?”

Drew looked at the box. At the spider, sitting in the middle of its web in the dark, top seed, where it had been since the second day, waiting. He’d thought about that box for a week. He’d thought about it on the ladder, and in the master’s chair, and lying awake in the Broken Lance listening to Tiger breathe.

“I don’t know yet,” he said honestly. “I’ll know at the end.”

“That’s the right answer.”

“It’s the only answer,” said Drew. “That’s how a bracket works. You don’t know who goes in the last box until everyone’s played.” He reached up, absently, and touched the nameplate at his throat. A cheap thing, from a shop on Earth, that had come through the hall with him because he’d been wearing it. Five characters, in metal, on a chain.

The water-carrier looked at it.

“88EXP,” he read.

“Funniest piece of jewellery in the kingdom,” said Drew. “Everybody here’s got an actual EXP bar. I’ve been wearing a joke about it round my neck for two years and nobody’s laughed but Tiger.”

The water-carrier laughed.

Not loudly. Just once, a short warm sound, on a step halfway up the Lists in the dark, the laugh of a man who’s been told a good joke at the end of a long day. And Drew, who’d spent ten years being the one still standing there smiling when it all went wrong, sat beside him on the step and found that for once nothing was going wrong at all, and there was nothing to hold, and he could just sit there.

So he did.

Far below them on the sand, in the dark, the master’s chair stood where Lucan had left it, rocking very slightly on its short leg in the evening wind. And at the top of the bracket, in the corner, the spider sat in its web over the last empty box, and waited, the way it had been waiting all week, to see who would come and fill it.
