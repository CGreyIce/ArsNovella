The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 5: The Lists

Chapter 130

Yun

6,515 words · 28 min read

Hisui never stopped talking.

That was the first thing anyone would have needed to know about him, and nobody ever had, because nobody but Kei could hear him. To everyone else, Hisui was a green tree snake about as long as Kei’s arm, the colour of new jade, who sat round Kei’s neck or in his sleeve or in the hood of his haori and looked at people with small bright eyes and occasionally flicked his tongue. A quiet snake. A calm snake. The calm mystic’s calm companion.

To Kei, Hisui was a voice in his head that had not shut up for two years.

It’s too hot, Hisui was saying now. It’s too loud. Why is everyone shouting? Why is the tiger one standing on the table? Somebody should tell him to get off the table. You should tell him. You won’t. You never tell anyone anything. You just sit there being trustworthy. It’s exhausting to watch.

“Mm,” said Kei.

Don’t mm me.

On Earth, Hisui had been a drawing. A line of jade on Kei’s model, coiled round his shoulders, that did a little animation when Kei laughed. Sometimes, on stream, late, Kei had given him a voice: a bit, a grumpy little voice that complained about chat and the heat and the music. People had liked it. It had been a good bit.

He’d come through the hall in Solmere two years ago with the drawing round his neck, and the drawing had opened its eyes and said, very clearly, where are we, I hate it. And Kei had looked round at everybody else in the hall, panicking, screaming, crying at ceilings, and realised that nobody else had heard it.

Nobody else ever had.

He’d got used to it. You got used to most things. He’d learned to answer under his breath, or in his head, or not at all. He’d learned which of Hisui’s opinions to pass on (the mushroom by the north gate would kill a horse) and which to keep to himself (the captain’s wife is having an affair with the farrier). He’d learned that Hisui could taste the air and tell you what was in it, and that the air in the passes above Thornwall tasted like the bottom of a well, and that when Hisui went quiet, which happened about once a season, you should be very frightened.

And he’d learned, somewhere in the first year, that Hisui was lonely.

He’d never say it. He’d die first. But Kei had sat on a lot of steps at Thornwall in the dark with soldiers who couldn’t sleep, and he knew what lonely sounded like, even in a snake. Two years of talking to the one person who could hear you. Two years of saying everything you thought to a man who mostly said mm. Two years of being the only one.

And then, in the spring, a line in the channel. Yan, from Cinderhold, writing about his snake. A white one. Who couldn’t talk. Who Yan answered for, the way he’d answered for her on stream, and who hissed at his answers.

There’s another one, Hisui had said. Very quietly. The quietest Kei had ever heard him. There’s another snake.

He hadn’t shut up about her since.

The other snake was at the far end of the long table, wound round the shaft of a scythe.

Group 3 had come in at dark, from the bottom of the Street of Armourers, with a horseshoe and a cart and a king’s father’s hammer, and the Broken Lance had gone up like a bonfire. Kei had been with them all the way up the street and hadn’t said a word. He hadn’t been able to. Hisui had been talking so fast and so loud in his head, the whole way, that Kei hadn’t been able to hear himself think.

That’s her. That’s her. She’s white. She’s on the stick. Why is she on the stick? She’s big. She’s bigger than me. Is she bigger than me? She’s bigger than me. Kei. Kei. She looked at me. She looked at me on the street. Kei, she LOOKED at me.

“I know,” Kei had said, under his breath, on the street.

Don’t say I know. Say something useful.

Now Yan was at the end of the long table with the scythe leaning against the wall behind him and Suzu wound three times round the top of its shaft, near the blade, with her head resting on the curve of the iron and her eyes half closed. Salty was beside him, eating the first of eleven pies, slowly, while the man with the fishing rod sat across from him and watched him do it. Maigo was standing on the bench telling the whole table about a tree. Wendy was trying to get him to sit down. Niko had his chin in his hand.

And Yan was looking at Kei.

He’d been looking at him since the forge. Not staring. Yan didn’t stare. Just looking, every so often, down the length of the table, with his calm half-lidded eyes. At Kei. And then at Kei’s neck.

Kei had known Yan for six years. Since before Ice. It was Yan who’d brought him to Ice in the first place: a voice in a call, this is Kei, he’s chill, he plays snake too, and Ice had laughed and said a snake guy, another one, how many of you are there. Kei had been twenty-three. They’d played together for a year and then two, the three of them and whoever else turned up. And then there’d been a night, about a year before the end, when Kei had needed somebody, and Yan had been asleep, and Ice hadn’t been.

He hadn’t seen Yan in person in two years. Not since the hall. Not since Yan had been sent east to the red and Kei west to the mountains, with a kingdom between them and a channel to talk in.

And neither of them had ever, once, in six years, been in the same room with both their snakes awake.

They went up on the roof.

Kei didn’t plan it. He just got up from the long table at some point after the third pie and walked to the stairs, and Yan got up from the other end of the table at the same moment and walked to the stairs too, and they met at the bottom, and looked at each other, and went up. Yan had the scythe. Suzu was still on it. Nobody at the table noticed, except the man with the fishing rod, who watched them go and didn’t say anything, and Hyvred, who noticed everything, and smiled.

The Broken Lance had a flat bit of roof above the back stairs, where the landlord dried washing. There was no washing on it now. There was a chimney, and a low parapet, and the whole of Valcrest lit up below, and the dark bowl of the Lists against the stars, and a warm wind coming in off the plain smelling of hay and bread and too many people.

They sat down on the leads with their backs against the chimney.

Neither of them said anything for a while.

Say something, said Hisui. Say something. Kei. Kei. Say something to him. Ask him if she’s all right. Ask him what she eats. Ask him—no. Don’t ask him that. Ask him if she—

“He wants to know if she’s all right,” said Kei.

Yan turned his head.

“She’s fine,” said Yan. His deep slow voice. “She ate yesterday. A hen. From a farm on the downs. She’s—“

And then he stopped.

He stopped completely. His whole long body went very still against the chimney, the way it went still in a fight, Wendy had written once, when he felt something coming. He turned his head and looked, not at Kei, but at Kei’s neck. At Hisui, coiled there in the dark, jade-green, small bright eyes.

—if she ever talks about me, which she won’t, because she doesn’t know I exist, because nobody knows I exist, because I’m a voice in the head of a man who says mm, and she’s a big beautiful white snake on a stick who belongs to a man with very good hair, and I’m just—

“I can hear him,” said Yan.

Hisui stopped talking.

It was the first time in two years. Kei felt it like a door slamming in a quiet house. One moment the voice in his head, going on and on, the way it had gone on every waking minute since a hall in Solmere; and the next moment, nothing. Silence. Hisui had frozen round his neck like a piece of carved jade.

“You can,” said Kei.

“I can hear him,” said Yan again. Very slowly. “He said I’ve got very good hair.”

Hisui made a sound. Kei had never heard him make it before. It was the sound a snake might make if a snake could die of embarrassment.

“He’s never,” said Kei, and had to stop. His voice had gone. He tried again. “Nobody’s ever heard him. Two years. Nobody. Not Hyvred. Not Drew. Not anyone. Only me.” He swallowed. “He talks all the time. He hasn’t stopped since the hall. And nobody’s ever heard a word.”

“I can hear him,” said Yan. “I could hear him at the forge. I thought it was—I don’t know what I thought. I thought I was imagining it. Somebody saying she looked at me, she looked at me.” He turned his head slowly toward Kei. “That was him.”

Kill me, said Hisui, very small, in Kei’s head. Kill me now. Bite me. Throw me off the roof.

And Yan, beside him, laughed.

It was a small laugh. Low and deep and slow, like everything Yan did. Kei had heard it on calls. He hadn’t heard it in person in two years. And Yan turned toward Kei’s neck, toward the small frozen jade snake there, and said, quite gently, the way you’d talk to a nervous horse:

“Hello, Hisui.”

Hisui didn’t move.

“I’m not going to throw you off the roof,” said Yan.

He heard that too, said Hisui. He heard everything. He’s heard everything I’ve said since the forge. He heard the hair.

“I did hear the hair,” said Yan. “Thank you. It’s a lot of work.”

And Kei, sitting on the roof of the Broken Lance with his back against a chimney and his oldest friend beside him, put his face in his hands and laughed until he had to hold on to the parapet.

It took Hisui a while to recover. A long while. Long enough for the noise from the common room below them to go up and down twice, and for Maigo’s voice to come up the back stairs shouting about a tree, and for someone, probably Tiger, to fall off a bench.

Then, very slowly, Hisui uncoiled from round Kei’s neck.

He’d never done that either. Not in company. He went down Kei’s arm, and onto the leads of the roof, and across the warm lead in the dark, a small green line, toward the scythe leaning against the chimney on Yan’s other side. Toward the top of its shaft, where a long white snake lay wound three times round the pale wood, with her head on the curve of the blade, watching him come.

Suzu had been watching him come since the street. Kei understood that now.

Hisui stopped at the bottom of the shaft. He looked up. All the way up. She was a great deal bigger than him, even shrunk. She was white as bone in the dark, with a pale green pattern on her belly and eyes like two small green lamps. She looked down at him for a long time.

Neither of them moved.

She’s looking at me, said Hisui. In Kei’s head. Not loud. Kei. She’s looking at me.

“I know,” said Kei, under his breath.

Don’t say I know.

And then Hisui went very still. Stiller than before. Kei felt it in his own chest, through whatever it was that tied him to the small green snake on the leads: a jolt, like a fish taking a line.

Oh, said Hisui. Oh. Kei. She talks.

Beside Kei, Yan had stopped breathing.

“What did she say,” said Yan.

His voice didn’t sound like his voice. It sounded like it was coming from the bottom of a well.

Kei didn’t know. He couldn’t hear her. He could only hear Hisui, and Hisui had gone quiet again, frozen at the bottom of the scythe-shaft, with his head tipped up toward the white snake above him and his tongue going in and out very fast, the way it did when he was tasting something enormous.

“Hisui,” said Kei. “What did she say?”

She said, said Hisui slowly, that I’m very small.

Yan made a sound.

And she said I talk too much. She says she’s been listening to me since the street and I haven’t stopped once. A pause. She says that’s fine. She says she’s used to men who talk too much. She says hers does it all the time.

“I don’t,” said Yan, faintly. “I don’t talk too much. I hardly talk at all.”

She says, said Hisui, not out loud.

Yan didn’t say anything.

She says, said Hisui, and Kei could hear him working it out, the way you work out a sentence in a language you’ve only just started to understand, she says he talks all the time. Inside. Down the cord. She says she hears all of it. Every day. Since before the hall. A pause. She says he never shuts up either. She says it’s the only thing she likes about the noise.

Kei looked at Yan.

Yan was sitting against the chimney with his long hair down his back and his hands in his lap, absolutely still, looking up at the top of the scythe. At Suzu. His face was doing nothing at all. It always did nothing. It was the thing everyone in Group 3 said about him: Yan, who carried everything, and showed nothing, and kept everybody else going.

His face was wet.

“Tell her,” said Yan. He stopped. Started again. “Tell her I didn’t know. I didn’t know she could hear it.”

Hisui looked up at Suzu. There was a silence that Kei couldn’t hear the inside of.

She says, said Hisui, of course you didn’t. You never ask her anything. You just answer for her.

That was where it started.

Kei would think about it for the rest of his life, he suspected. A flat roof above a back stair at the Broken Lance in Valcrest, on midsummer eve, with the warm wind off the plain and the noise of thirty-odd people coming up the stairs below. Two men sitting against a chimney. Two snakes on a scythe. And Hisui, who’d talked every single minute of the last two years and never once been heard, being heard, at last, by the only person who could hear him and the only creature who’d listen. Turning round, very carefully, at the bottom of the scythe-shaft, and passing on, one sentence at a time, in his grumpy small voice, what the white snake at the top of it had been saying for years to a man who’d never once been able to hear her.

She’d been listening to his streams.

That was the first thing. She’d been there. All of it, Hisui said, slowly, as if he could barely believe it himself; Suzu said she’d been there, round his shoulders, the whole time, every stream, for years. A line of white on a model. A drawing. She didn’t know how. She didn’t know what she’d been, then. She knew she’d been there. She knew she’d heard every word.

She says, said Hisui, you used to say she said hello to people.

“I did,” said Yan. “Every stream. Suzu says hi, chat.”

She says she was not saying hi.

Yan stared up at the scythe.

“What was she saying?”

She won’t repeat it, said Hisui. She says it was rude. She says they deserved it. She says most of them were looking at you.

Kei put his hand over his mouth.

She says, Hisui went on, warming up now, the way he did, you used to tell everyone she was jealous. When people in chat said nice things about you. You’d say Suzu’s jealous, Suzu doesn’t like that, and do a little hiss. A pause. She says she was not jealous.

“She hissed at them,” said Yan. “On the red. At Rhea. At everyone who said my name wrong.”

She says she was not jealous, said Hisui. She says she was correct.

It went on.

Every translation. Every single one, it seemed to Kei, that Yan had ever done for her: on stream, on Earth, and here, on the edge of the red, for two years, when she was real and he was still answering for her because he didn’t know what else to do. Suzu had a verdict on all of them. Hisui passed them on, one at a time, in his grumpy small voice, and Kei listened, and Yan sat against the chimney with his wet face and listened too.

She says the time you said she wanted the window shut, she wanted it open.

She says the time you told Aldous she didn’t like him, she likes him. He smells of candles. She says tell him that.

She says the time you told Maigo she was sleeping, she was listening to him talk to a sheep. For an hour. She says she liked the sheep.

She says when you told Rhea she was angry, she wasn’t angry. She was hungry. She says you should know the difference by now.

“I do know the difference,” said Yan.

She says you don’t.

“I do. She goes cold. Down the cord. When she’s hungry, she goes cold, and I—“ He stopped. “I always feed her. The same day. I’ve never once let her go a whole day.”

There was a silence at the top of the scythe.

She says, said Hisui, more slowly, she knows. She says you never have. She says not once. Not on the red, not in the snow, not on the road. She says the hen yesterday was fine. She says thank you for the hen. A pause. She says she knows what it costs you. Every time. Watching. She says she knows you hate it. Another pause. She says never people. She says she agrees. She says she’d rather.

Yan put his hand over his eyes.

Kei didn’t say anything. He’d learned that, on steps at Thornwall. When to say mm, and when to say nothing at all. He sat beside his oldest friend on a roof in the dark, close enough that their shoulders touched, and let him sit there with his hand over his eyes, and didn’t look.

He’d done this for two years. For everyone. Tiger, every night, for months, about a freckled boy in a third ring. Bee, once, about her wings, so angry she couldn’t finish a sentence. Hyvred about the absence. Soldiers he didn’t know the names of. People came to him the way they came to a fire, and he sat with them, and they put down whatever they were carrying for a while.

He’d never told anyone why. Hyvred knew. He’d told her once, late. Somebody did it for me. Stayed on a call till four in the morning. I never said thank you properly. So I’m just doing it forward.

He was doing it forward now. For the man who’d brought him to Ice in the first place.

It occurred to him, sitting there, that this was probably what Ice had felt like, at four in the morning, on a call, with a voice on the other end that couldn’t stop crying. Not like doing anything. Just like being there.

“There’s a thing,” said Yan, eventually. His hand was still over his eyes. “There’s a thing I’ve never translated.”

Hisui looked up at the top of the scythe. Suzu looked down at him.

“On stream,” said Yan. “People used to ask. Does Suzu love you? It was the bit. Every stream. And I’d say—I’d say what the bit was. I’d say the feeling is mutual.” He breathed. “And chat would go mad. Hearts, all the way up. It was the best part of every stream. Everyone loved it.” He took his hand away from his eyes. “I said it like it was a joke. Every time. For years. Because it was a bit. Because she was a drawing. Because you can’t—“ He stopped. “And then we came through the hall and she was real, and I felt it down the cord, the first day, the first hour, and it wasn’t a bit. It had never been a bit. And I’ve never once said it to her. Not here. Not when she could hear it. Not when it was real.”

He looked up at the white snake on the scythe. Directly. Not through Hisui. Not through anyone.

“I was too shy,” said Yan. “I’m sorry. I was too shy to translate the only true thing.”

There was a very long silence on the roof of the Broken Lance.

Then Suzu moved.

She came down the scythe-shaft. Slowly. Unwinding from the top of the pale wood one coil at a time, down past Wendy’s lavender grips, down past Hisui at the bottom, who pressed himself flat against the lead to let her by. Onto the roof. Across the warm lead in the dark, a long white line, to the man sitting against the chimney. Up his arm. Round his neck, the way she always sat. Once, twice. Her head on his collarbone. Her eyes, two small green lamps, looking up at his face.

And at the bottom of the scythe-shaft, Hisui made a small choked sound in Kei’s head.

She says, said Hisui. His voice had gone strange. She says the only translation you ever got right was that one.

Yan didn’t say anything.

She says, said Hisui, the feeling is mutual. A pause. She says she’s been saying it every day. Since before the hall. Since before she had a voice. She says she said it every time you said it on stream, at the same time, and nobody heard her, and she didn’t mind, because you said it too. Another pause, longer. She says you can stop being shy now. She says it’s very boring.

And Yan, who had carried everything, for everyone, for two years on the edge of the red, without once putting any of it down where anyone could see, put his face down against the white snake round his neck and cried.

Not much. Not loudly. Just enough.

Kei looked out at the city, so that nobody would be looking at him.

It was Hisui who asked.

Kei found out afterwards that Yan had always known someone would. He’d known since the spring; since he wrote about Suzu in the channel and a man at Thornwall wrote back my snake wants to know about your snake. He’d known that if it ever happened, if anybody ever heard her, that would be the question.

Hisui asked it the way he asked everything. Bluntly. Out of nowhere. In his small grumpy voice, from the bottom of the scythe-shaft, where he’d been sitting by himself since Suzu went down to the chimney.

Why does she call you Yun?

Yan went very still against the chimney.

She keeps saying it, said Hisui. All the time. When she talks about you. Not Yan. Not Suzuyun. Not anything else everyone says. Yun. She says Yun every time. He sounded genuinely puzzled. Everyone calls you Yan. Kei calls you Yan. The tiger one calls you Yan. The one with the rod calls you Yan. Why does she say Yun?

Kei looked at Yan.

Yan didn’t answer for a long time. He sat with his back against the chimney and the white snake round his neck and his hand resting on her, very lightly, the way you’d rest your hand on someone sleeping. The noise from the common room came up the stairs. Somewhere below, in the street, somebody was singing.

“My name,” said Yan, eventually. “On stream. Suzuyun.” He said it slowly. “Suzu. And yun. Her name, and then mine. Joined together. It was the whole idea. Her, and me, and you couldn’t take either one out without breaking it.” He was quiet. “Everyone took the first bit for her and called me Yan. It was easier. It stuck. Everyone. For years.”

So what’s Yun?

“The half that isn’t her,” said Yan.

He looked down at the snake round his neck.

“When I did her voice,” he said. “On stream. When I answered for her. She used to call me Yun. In the bit. Never Yan. Never anything else. Just Yun. I made it up. I did it in her voice, every time, for years, and nobody else ever called me that. Not chat. Not Salty. Not my mom. Nobody in the world.” He breathed. “It was the only name only she used. Because I only ever used it for her.”

He stopped.

“And I thought it was me,” he said. “All those years. I thought I was making it up. I thought it was a bit. I thought it was my voice, doing hers.” He was very quiet. “And she just told Hisui she calls me Yun. Here. In this world. Where I can’t do her voice any more. Where I’ve never once said that name out loud in two years.” His voice went. “So it was never me. It was never a bit. It was always her.”

Suzu, round his neck, lifted her head and touched the side of his jaw with the end of her nose, once, very lightly. And put her head back down.

She says yes, said Hisui quietly. She says it was always her, Yun.

They sat on the roof a long time after that.

Hisui talked. Of course he did. Now that he’d started, now that there was somebody who could hear him, and somebody who’d answer, he talked more than Kei had ever heard him talk in his life, which was saying something. He talked to Suzu, and passed on what she said to Yan, and argued with her about the passes above Thornwall, which he said tasted like the bottom of a well and she said tasted of nothing because she’d never been there, and asked her what the red tasted of, and what a soul was like to eat, and whether she’d ever eaten a snake, and was very relieved when she said no.

And Suzu, apparently, answered all of it. Calmly. Patiently. Coldly, Hisui said, to everything except anything about Yun, about whom she was warm. She says she doesn’t like anyone, Hisui reported, at one point. She says she likes him. She says she’s prepared to tolerate me. A pause. She says I’m the first one she’s ever tolerated.

“That’s high praise,” said Yan, faintly.

I know, said Hisui, with enormous satisfaction. I know it is.

Kei laughed. He’d been laughing on and off for an hour, the helpless sort, the sort that came from somewhere he’d forgotten about. Two years of being the only one who could hear a snake. And now there were two of them, two men on a roof with two snakes who were talking to each other, and the whole of Valcrest below them lit up for midsummer, and nobody in the city had any idea.

“Thank you,” said Yan, at some point. To Kei. Quietly.

“For what?”

“For bringing him.” Yan nodded down at Hisui, still at the bottom of the scythe-shaft, still talking. “For being the one he talks to. For two years.” He was quiet. “I brought you to Ice, once. On a call. This is Kei, he plays snake too.” He looked at Kei. “You did this for me.”

“It wasn’t me. It was him.”

“He wouldn’t have come down your arm,” said Yan, “if you hadn’t been sitting there.”

Kei didn’t have an answer to that. He sat on the roof and looked at the city and found he didn’t need one.

Drew found them a little after midnight.

He came up the back stairs, unhurried, with his hands in his pockets and his 88EXP nameplate catching the light from the stairwell, and stood at the top looking at them. Two men against a chimney. A white snake round one of their necks. A green snake at the bottom of a scythe-shaft, talking.

“I’m not interrupting,” said Drew.

“You’re interrupting,” said Kei.

“I know.” Drew smiled. The one that was the whole of his job. “But the lobby’s full.”

Kei looked at him.

“Group 3’s in,” said Drew. “That’s everyone. Thirty-five.” He took a key out of his pocket, a big old iron one, and held it up so that it caught the light. “Lucan’s key. To the Lists. The side gate.” He looked out at the dark bowl of it, against the stars, over the roofs. “We’ve never once all been in one place. Not since the hall.” He paused. “And he’s never been in one place with all of us. Ever.”

Yan stood up.

He picked up the scythe. Hisui went up the shaft to the top, where Suzu had been, and wound himself round it, small and green, and looked very pleased with himself. Suzu stayed round Yan’s neck. She’d decided something. She wasn’t going to discuss it.

“Okay,” said Yan.

They went through the city in the dark. All of them.

Kei would remember it in pieces. The common room emptying all at once, benches going over, Lucan waking up in his chair by the kitchen and saying what and nobody answering. The street outside, dark and warm, with the lamps lit and the paper fishing rods in every window going up toward the Lists like a line of reeds. Thirty-five people walking up the high street of Valcrest at midnight in a long loose crowd: a squid on somebody’s shoulder, a penguin at somebody’s heel, three moths going round somebody’s head, a hawk, a white snake, a green one, two round yellow bees asleep on top of a man’s head, an oni in a mask, a horned girl, a tree with a crown of new leaves, a man with a kettle. Nobody stopped them. The few people still out at that hour stood back against the walls to let them pass and stared, because they were the summoned, and they were famous, and there were thirty-five of them in one street for the first time in the history of the kingdom.

Nobody looked at the man with the rod.

He walked in the middle of them. Not at the front. Not at the back. In the middle, where you could see him from everywhere, with his daughter on one side and Maigo on the other, and the leaves on Maigo’s antlers leaning toward him the whole way like a plant on a windowsill.

Drew opened the side gate. Down the stone passage that smelled of sand and old oil. Out.

The Lists were empty and silver.

The moon was up, nearly full, the night before midsummer. The sand was raked smooth, ready for the morning, and the stands went up all round them into the dark, tier on tier, pale as the inside of a shell. The bracket on the east wall was a long dark shape with gold lines catching the moonlight, five boxes full of names, the trees empty, and the spider in the top corner over the last box. Lucan had tied paper fishing rods all along the rail of the royal box, and along the rail of the master’s platform, and along the top of the wall where the stands met the sand, all the way round, hundreds of them, swaying in the warm wind.

They walked out into the middle of it.

Nobody said anything. Nobody needed to. They spread out on the sand without anyone telling them to, the way water spreads out, until they were standing in a rough wide ring in the middle of the Lists, all thirty-five of them, facing in. Kei stood between Yan and Hyvred. Hisui was on the scythe and Suzu was on Yan’s neck, and both of them, for once, had gone quiet.

And in the middle of the ring, on his own, the man with the fishing rod.

He hadn’t meant to end up there. Kei saw that. He’d walked out with the others and stopped when they stopped, and the ring had formed round him without anyone deciding it, the way a lobby forms round a spawn. And now he was standing in the middle of the sand on his own with thirty-five people round him in a circle, all of them looking at him, and Kei, who’d sat on a hundred steps with a hundred people, saw him not know what to do with his hands.

He’s frightened, said Hisui. Very quietly. From the scythe. Kei. He’s frightened.

“I know,” said Kei, under his breath.

She says, said Hisui, he’s frightened that we’re happy.

Kei looked round the ring. At thirty-five faces in the moonlight. Group 1, who’d waited at the top of a road for twenty months. Group 2, who’d never once been in the same room as him. Group 3, who’d watched him carry a sword home across the red. Group 4 and Group 6, his brothers, his class, who’d followed a thread upriver. Group 7, his squads, one down for three years. Group 5. Them. The newest friends, who he’d known shortest, who’d come anyway.

Everybody was happy. Everybody, on the sand, in the moonlight, was so happy it was almost frightening.

Chris stepped forward.

He had his notebook under his arm and his pen behind his ear. He stepped out of the ring, two paces onto the sand, and opened the notebook, and turned to the page, and held it up in the moonlight where he could read it. His hand was steady. His voice, when it came, was perfectly steady too, the voice of a man logging a session.

“Logging,” said Chris.

Nobody laughed. Everybody smiled.

“Day sixty. Midsummer eve. The Lists, Valcrest. Midnight, roughly.” He looked at the page. “Present.”

And he read them.

All of them. One at a time. Every name on the page, in the order he’d first written them two years ago, the week after the hall, group by group, the way they’d been sorted, when nobody knew half of anybody’s names yet and he’d gone round the letters asking.

“Althea.”

“Here,” said Thea.

“Higu.”

“Here.”

“Gigichi.”

“Here,” said Gigichi, gruffly.

“Xola. PenguChi. Hanarisu. Milli. Aleaka. Cursiffe. Jared.”

Here, here, here. One after another, round the ring, in the moonlight. Not loud. Just here, the way you say it at the start of a lesson, the way you say it when somebody calls your name and you want them to know you’re still there.

“Suzuyun.”

“Here,” said Yan. And then, very quietly, so that only Kei and two snakes heard it: “Yun.”

“Maigo. Salty. Wendy. Nikorasu.”

“HERE,” said Maigo, at the top of his lungs, and the leaves on his antlers shook.

“Deston. Gabe. Rachael. Dennis. Calvin. Hyvred. Bee. Tiger. Drew. Kei.”

“Here,” said Kei. His voice wasn’t steady. He didn’t mind.

“Xavier. Malcolm. RX. Azac. John. Winter. Ina. Hako. Newish.”

“Here,” said Newish. Lightly. With her eyebrows up.

“Chris,” said Chris, to himself, and wrote something down. “Here.”

He looked at the page.

“Thirty-five,” said Chris. “Of thirty-five.” He paused. “First time since the hall.”

The ring was very quiet.

Then Chris turned the notebook round, and held it up, so that the man in the middle of the ring could see it. The page. Thirty-five lines, in Chris’s small square hand, with Lucan’s script beside them, horns and masks and antlers and squids. And at the bottom, a thirty-sixth. With paper fishing rods written small at the end of it, months ago. And at the start of it, written three days ago at a north gate, a name.

“Line thirty-six,” said Chris.

He didn’t read it out. He didn’t need to. Everybody on the sand knew what it said.

He just held the notebook up in the moonlight, and looked at the man in the middle of the ring over the top of it, and waited. The way you wait, at the start of a lesson, for the last name on the list.

The man in the middle of the ring didn’t say anything for a long time.

Kei watched him. They all did. Thirty-five people on the sand of the Lists at midnight, under hundreds of paper fishing rods, watching a man whose face none of them could see stand very still in the middle of them, with his hands at his sides and a rod over his shoulder and two bees asleep on his head.

He’s going to say it, said Hisui, from the scythe. Very quietly. She says he’s going to say it. She says watch.

And the man in the middle of the ring took a breath. Kei saw his shoulders go. The way a man’s shoulders go when he’s put something down that he’s been carrying a very long way, and is about to pick up something else instead, something lighter, that he’s wanted to carry for years and hasn’t been allowed.

“Here,” he said.

In English. Quietly. To all of them.

And then, because he was who he was, and he’d always been who he was, on every call, in every lobby, at the end of every long night when everyone was tired and nobody wanted to be the one to hang up, he said the other thing too. The thing he’d said into a headset in a room in Malacca at two in the morning, with a fan going, more times than any of them could count.

“Hi, everyone.”

Nobody moved.

Then Tiger made a sound like a kettle and went, and after Tiger everybody went. All at once. Thirty-five people on the sand of the Lists in the moonlight, collapsing in on the middle of a ring like a wave going in, and Kei went with them, with Yan beside him and a white snake round Yan’s neck and a green one on a scythe, and somewhere in the middle of it all a man with a fishing rod disappeared completely under thirty-five of his friends and didn’t come out for a long, long time.

Somewhere in the middle of it, in the moonlight, Chris’s pen scratched once more, quickly, on a page, and stopped.

Thirty-five of thirty-five, it said, in a small square hand. And one.

End of Part Two.
