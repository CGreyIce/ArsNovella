Drew’s job, as far as he’d ever been able to tell, was to know where everybody was going.

He’d done it on Earth for ten years, from his bedroom: brackets, and the spider in the top box. He’d never been the best at anything. He’d been the one who knew who was meeting whom, and when, and where the winner went next, and he’d liked it more than winning; he’d tried to explain that to people for years and mostly given up.

He’d done it here for four years. The bracket on the backs of Corvin’s maps. The bracket on the forty-foot wall at Valcrest, with the spider in the top box. And since the autumn, at Thornwall, the list: who was on the wall, who was on the arch, who was asleep and who should be, who was in the long room with Kei’s knitwort and who’d been called and come back. Drew said where everybody was going. It was what he was for.

And every morning, since the thaw, he’d stood in the middle of a bridge and not gone anywhere at all.

The third ring had come down four times since the morning Tiger turned.

Nobody had known it would. On the first evening, at dusk, the forty of them had walked back up the swept road the way they’d come, eleven miles in the dark to the valley, with Ossie last and his head turned over his shoulder the whole way; and Hyvred had watched them from the tower until they were back in their place in the rings, standing, and said, that’s it, then, in a voice that didn’t believe it.

It wasn’t. Five mornings later Hild heard the feet again before the first grey. Like people coming home from market, she said, the same as the first time, and the whole town was on the wall before the light came, and there they were. Across the road, a pace off the far end of the millstream bridge, facing the town. Forty of them, in the clothes they’d been wearing when the valley called them: aprons and work coats and a miller’s widow’s shawl and Sergeant Dunmore’s ten in their grey watch coats with the collars up. Standing. Ossie nearest, with his freckles, and his hands open at his sides.

They didn’t come onto the bridge. They didn’t do anything. They stood all day at the far end of Tiger’s race, and at dusk they walked home.

They’d done it again after six days, and after four, and after five. Always at dawn. Always, Drew worked out by the third time, on a morning Tiger was going to run, though nobody could say how they knew; Tiger ran every morning, and they didn’t come every morning. It knows where you turn round, Xav had said, the first time, and nobody had liked that. And every time they came, Drew walked out through the postern onto the swept road, a quarter of a mile at a walk, and onto the bridge, to the middle, with the millstream roaring white under him and forty people a pace off the far end, and turned round with his back to them to face the town. And waited for Tiger to come and go back.

His bar had moved every time.

Ninety-three, the morning Tiger turned. Ninety-four the next time they came. Ninety-five. Ninety-six, five days ago, in the rain.

It didn’t move when he stood on the bridge alone. He’d tried that. He’d gone out on the mornings they didn’t come and stood in the middle with the millstream going under, an hour, two, and the number had sat there like a stone. It moved when they were there, at his back, and Tiger came down the road at him full tilt with the look on his face that meant Bolt, and turned three paces off, and went back to the town. And Drew didn’t have to touch him.

That was the thing. He’d said it to Tiger on the moss, the first night, without knowing he was going to. Not whether I can catch you. Whether I can stand on a bridge and not have to.

He was a brawler. Hild’s path, Hild’s school: bladed knuckles, wrapped, and your hands up, and your feet under you, and Counter, which was the first thing aura had ever given him. Last spring, on the valley road, the night of the calling, with Annet from the bakery leaning on his chest with flour on her hands and eleven more behind her, and the call coming down the road through all of them; and he’d put his hands up and felt the whole weight of it come into him and go straight back out the other way. That was Counter. Whatever came into him, he could send back. Through his hands. Through anybody he had his hands on. He’d broken three of his own ribs on the field last autumn doing it, and he’d do it again.

And it was the whole of what he knew how to be. You come at me, I send it back. You fall, I catch you, and the fall goes somewhere else. He’d held doors open for ten years and caught people for four, and his hands went up on their own when anything moved toward him fast, and he’d never once in his life stood still in front of a thing and let it arrive without sending it somewhere.

The number knew that. That was why it was stuck.

“Hit me,” he said to Hild.

It was the morning after the rain, in the yard behind the gatehouse, at the first grey, before the town was up. Hild looked at him. Hild was fifty-two and Shaped and quiet, with a face like the side of a hill, and she’d taught him everything he knew about hands, and she did not, as a rule, ask why.

She asked now. “Why?”

“I want to stand there and not send it back.”

Hild considered this.

“You can’t,” she said. “It’s in your hands. It’s been in your hands since the field. You don’t decide to counter, lad. You’ve never once decided it.”

“I know. That’s why I want you to hit me.”

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she picked up the practice stave from the rack, ash, as long as she was, with a leather pad bound over the end, and turned it in her hands, and said, “Hands down.”

He put his hands down.

“Down,” said Hild. “Not by your belt where you can get them up. Down.”

He put them down at his sides. Flat. He made himself open them. He stood in the middle of the yard with his feet under him and his hands open at his sides and his chin up, and felt every bit of him want to get his hands up, the way your eyes want to shut when something comes at your face.

“I’m going to put it in your chest,” said Hild. “Not hard. Firm. Like somebody walking into you.” She set her feet. “Don’t send it back.”

She came. Not fast; Hild never needed to be fast. The padded end of the stave came at his chest at a walking pace, firm, the whole of her quiet weight behind it, and he watched it come and told his hands no.

They came up anyway.

He didn’t feel them do it. He felt the stave land, and the weight come into him, and something in him take hold of the weight the way it always did and turn it and send. His left hand was on the stave. He didn’t remember putting it there. Counter went out through his hand into the ash like a kick, all of it, everything she’d put into him and some of his own; and Hild, who’d known it would, had a wall up before it got to her.

Not a real wall. A Shaped one. A grey plane of aura in the air in front of her chest, the size of a door, there and gone, that she made the way other people made a fist. Counter went down the stave and hit it. And the stave stopped, and Drew’s hand didn’t.

He heard the knuckles go. Two of them, the third and the fourth on his left hand, with a sound like somebody stepping on a dry stick. Then the pain came, white, and he was sitting on the cobbles of the yard with his hand in his lap and his mouth open and no sound coming out of it.

Hild put the stave back on the rack. She came and crouched in front of him and took his wrist, very gently, and looked at the hand.

“Two,” she said. “Clean. You’ll keep them.” She looked at his face. “That’s what I meant, lad. It’s in your hands.”

Kei splinted it in the long room, with Hisui watching from round his neck with his tongue going.

“Hisui says you’re an idiot,” said Kei, binding the two fingers together onto a slip of ash with a strip of linen, his long careful hands not hurrying. “He says it with love.”

“Tell him thanks.”

“He heard you.” Kei tied it off. “Six weeks. No fists. Don’t counter anything with it or you’ll have me to deal with, and I’m worse than Hild.” He looked up. “What were you doing?”

Drew told him.

Kei listened the way Kei listened to everything, with his whole calm face, while Hisui’s tongue went in and out tasting the air round Drew’s splint. When Drew had finished, Kei was quiet for a while.

“There’s a thing,” he said at last, “with doses. You learn it in the first month. If you want to know what something does to a body, you can’t test it on a body that’s fighting it. You have to wait until it stops fighting. Then you can see what the thing actually does.” He wiped his hands on a cloth. “Otherwise all you’re measuring is the fight.”

Drew looked at his hand.

“I’m not saying anything,” said Kei. “I’m talking about doses.”

Tiger came to find him on the gate step that evening. He sat down on Drew’s left, carefully, the side with the splint, and looked at it.

“Hild?”

“Hild.”

“What did you do?”

“Asked her to hit me.”

Tiger thought about that. Tiger was the fastest thing in the north and he’d run eleven miles of road every morning since the thaw, there and back, and he had the kind of face that showed you every single thing he was thinking about a heartbeat before he’d finished thinking it. It showed Drew now that he’d decided not to say anything stupid, and then changed his mind.

“That’s stupid,” said Tiger.

“Yeah.”

“Can you still do the bridge?”

Drew looked at the splint. Six weeks. No fists. He couldn’t get the left hand up properly if he wanted to; the two fingers stuck out stiff in their ash and linen, and the whole hand throbbed if he lifted it above his belt.

“I don’t know,” he said.

He did know. He’d been thinking about it all afternoon, on the cot in the long room, with the hand on his chest and Kei’s doses in his head. He’d stood on that bridge four times with his hands up and open, the bladed knuckles, ready. Ready to catch. Ready to put his hands on Tiger and send the whole run somewhere else if Tiger couldn’t turn. I’ll be a pace from you. I’ll put my hands on you. He’d said that to Tiger, the first morning. It was the bravest thing he’d ever said, and he’d meant it, and he’d been standing there every time since with his hands up, meaning it.

And the number had gone up a step every time. And stopped a step short.

All you’re measuring is the fight.

“I’ll do the bridge,” he said.

They came on the seventh morning after the yard.

He heard them before Hild did, for once. He was awake; he hadn’t slept much, with the hand. He was lying on his cot in the long room in the dark with the splint on his chest, listening to the millstream, and under the millstream he heard it. Feet. A great many feet, on the swept road, a long way off, not marching, not hurrying. Like people coming home from market. He got up.

By the first grey the whole town was on the wall again. The Watch on the parapet with their lamps out. Old Pruett at the postern with his hand on the bar. Bee on the gatehouse roof with her chalk and her dog, drawing the dog on the slates over and over the way she did when she was frightened, so there’d be something on the roof that wasn’t. Azac on the steps narrating under his breath to nobody, forty, I make it forty, same as last time, Ossie at the front. Malcolm with his notebook. Rui Xiang beside Tiger, a hand off, not touching. John on the wall with his fingers moving, putting the picture up into everybody’s windows the way he did now every morning they came, so that far off in Solmere and Valcrest and at a lake in the north thirty people sat up in their beds and saw it. Hyvred on the tower, at the very top, a small shape against the grey with the red mark in her eye. Kei on the arch with Hisui. Petra in the square below, with the children.

And Xav, on the wall walk, with his arms folded and the driftwood stick under one of them, looking at the bridge. He’d been here since the autumn. He’d stayed, with the rest of Group 6, when he didn’t have to, because Petra stayed, and because, he’d said once to Drew, very quietly, somebody should watch the ones who are watching. He looked at Drew’s splint as Drew came along the wall walk, and then at Drew’s face.

“Hands down?” said Xav.

“Hands down.”

Xav nodded. He didn’t say anything else. That was Xav.

And across the road, a pace off the far end of the millstream bridge, in the grey, facing the town: forty of them. Ossie at the front. Hands open.

Drew went down to the postern.

The swept road was cold under his boots and hard and wrong, the way it always felt, the way everything felt that led toward the valley. A quarter of a mile. He walked it. He didn’t hurry. He’d learned that on the first morning: you didn’t hurry toward them, and you didn’t hurry away, you walked, the way you’d walk to the end of a garden.

The bridge came up out of the grey. The milestone, the parapet, the white noise of the millstream under it, the spray coming up cold onto his face. Old stone, two carts wide, thirty paces long. He walked onto it.

And at the far end of it, a pace off the last stone, they stood.

He’d never got used to it. He’d stood here four times and he’d never once got used to it, and he thought he’d be a worse person if he did. Forty faces he knew. Not knew; he hadn’t been in Thornwall when the valley called them. But he’d learned them. He’d learned them from the town, the way you learn a bracket: that was the miller’s widow, Agnes; that was the boy soldier; that was Sergeant Dunmore, square and grey with his coat buttoned wrong, and his ten in a row beside him. That was Ossie. Who’d raced Tiger every morning in the first spring from the well to the oak post and back, and gone up the north road with Dunmore’s ten to look at a burned hut, and not come down. Freckles. A grey watch coat with the collar turned up. Hands open at his sides.

Their faces were their own. That was the worst of it. Drew had read Hyvred’s notes, all of them, and he knew what she saw when she looked: something else, behind, wearing them. But you couldn’t see that. You saw the miller’s widow, and her shawl, and her face, patient and a little puzzled, the face of a woman who’s walked out to the end of the lane and can’t remember why.

He walked to the middle of the bridge and stopped.

Then he turned round, with his back to them, to face the town.

That was always the worst moment, and he made himself do it slowly. Forty people a pace behind him who’d walked eleven miles in the dark to stand there, and his back to them, and the millstream so loud under him that he’d never hear them if they moved. He stood with his feet under him and looked up the swept road at the postern, a quarter of a mile off, and the wall above it, and the town on it, and the tiny figure at the top of the tower.

He put his hands down.

Not by his belt. Down. The right one open at his side. The left one in its splint, two fingers stuck out stiff, open as far as it would go.

Every bit of him wanted them up. He let it want.

Tiger came out of the postern.

Drew saw him come: a small fast shape in the grey, at the well first, a flat hand on the rim, and then through the north arch and down to the post and the flat hand on the oak, flat hand on the wood or it doesn’t count, Ossie’s rule, and then out of the postern onto the swept road, and Bolt.

He came down the road at full.

He was the fastest thing Drew had ever seen. He’d been that before he was Tempered, and since he was Tempered he was something else, something that didn’t have a word yet: a boy with the whole of his life in his legs, coming down a quarter of a mile of road toward a bridge like a stone off a cliff. You could hear him over the millstream. His boots on the road, so fast they made one sound.

Drew stood with his hands down.

Three paces off, he thought. He turns three paces off. He always did. He’d done it four times. Back: the whole of the run going into him and ringing, and him going home. Drew didn’t have to do anything. He’d never had to do anything, any of the four times; he’d stood there with his hands up, ready, and Tiger had turned every time, and the hands had never been needed.

And the number had gone up a step. And stopped short.

Tiger was halfway.

And behind Drew, over the roar of the millstream, he heard a sound he’d never heard on that bridge.

Boots. On the stone.

On the bridge.

He knew who it was without turning round. He’d have known it anywhere. He’d heard it on the moss, the first night, from Tiger, in the dark, a hundred times: he ran like a dog that’s been let off the lead, all elbows, and he always, always looked back to see if you were coming.

Ossie was running.

From the far end. Off the last stone onto the bridge, the first of the forty ever to set foot on it; and onto the bridge, toward the town, toward the middle. Toward Drew’s back. Running.

He heard the wall go quiet. A quarter of a mile off, a whole town stopping breathing.

Every bit of Drew turned round. Every bit of him except the bit that was standing there. His hands came up. He felt them come, the right one first, the left one with the splint dragging after it, and the pain went up his arm like a hot wire and stopped it at his belt, and the right one got as far as his chest before he felt the thing in him that had been ready for four years take hold of the coming weight, the way it always did, ready to turn it and send.

All you’re measuring is the fight.

He put the right hand back down.

It was the hardest thing he’d ever done. Harder than the field. Harder than three ribs. He stood in the middle of the bridge with his back to a turned boy running at him and his face to his friend running at him, and his hands open at his sides, one of them in a splint, and he didn’t turn round, and he didn’t put them up, and he didn’t catch anything.

He just stood there.

Tiger was three paces off. He didn’t turn. Drew saw it in his face, the heartbeat before it happened: Tiger had heard the boots too, and seen past Drew, and seen who it was, and his face did the thing Tiger’s face did, all of it at once, every single thing; and he didn’t turn three paces off. He came on.

Behind Drew, Ossie’s boots, three paces off.

They both arrived.

A hand hit him in the chest. Flat. From the front, Tiger’s, the whole of Tiger’s run in it, a flat hand on the breastbone the way you put a flat hand on an oak post at the end of a race, or it doesn’t count.

And a hand hit him in the back. Flat. Between the shoulder blades. Cold through his coat, and small, and open, and with the whole of a run in it too.

Two runs. From both ends. Into him at once.

It should have gone back. All of it, out through his hands, the way it always had; it should have gone through him and out the other side and put both of them on their backs on the stone. He felt Counter reach for it. He felt it take hold.

And he let it not.

He didn’t decide to. He couldn’t have; there wasn’t time. He’d spent seven days with his hand on his chest and a man’s voice in his head saying wait until it stops fighting, and somewhere in those seven days the part of him that decided things had stopped standing between him and the rest of him. Counter reached for the weight and found the hands weren’t there to send it through, they were down, they were open, they were out of the way; and the weight came into him and didn’t go anywhere.

It went into him. Into his chest, and his back, and down. Down through his ribs and his hips and his legs, the whole of two runs, Tiger’s and Ossie’s, and into his boots and the stone of the bridge, and the stone took it. And he took it. He felt his whole body go hard, not the way his hands went hard, not to hit; hard the way a post is hard, a post you put your hand on and push off from and turn. Something set in him. All through him, every part of him at once, like iron going grey in a quench.

He didn’t move. Not a hair.

And both hands pushed off.

He felt them do it, the one on his chest and the one on his back, both at once, the way you push off a post. And both of them turned.

Tiger went back up the swept road toward the town. Not at Bolt. At a run, his ordinary run, the one he’d had in the first spring, all elbows; and Drew saw over his shoulder, as he went, that he was crying.

And behind Drew, small boots on the stone, going the other way. Back across the bridge. Off the last stone. Back to the far end, to the forty.

Drew stood in the middle of the bridge with the millstream roaring under him and his hands open at his sides and didn’t turn round.

He didn’t have to.

He felt it go up.

Not a candle; he wasn’t a mage; that was what Kei and Bee and Hyvred said it was like for them, a flame going up a step. For him it was the thing in his body that had set, setting all the way. Like a post going into the ground. And in front of his face, in the grey air over the bridge, small and clear, where only he could see it:

He looked at it for a long time.

Post. The thing at the end of the race. The thing you put your hand on so it counts. The thing that stands there so somebody else can turn round.

He’d held doors open for ten years. He’d never once thought about what it was like to be the doorpost.

On the wall a quarter of a mile off, something was happening. He could hear it, faintly, under the millstream. A sound a town makes. He didn’t turn round to look. He was looking up the swept road at Tiger, still running, nearly at the postern now, and at the tiny figure on top of the tower, which had both hands up over its face.

Then he turned round.

They were standing where they always stood. Forty of them, a pace off the far end of the bridge, facing the town. Agnes the miller’s widow. Sergeant Dunmore with his buttons wrong. The boy soldier.

And Ossie, at the front, with his hands open at his sides. His right hand was still a little out from his body, the fingers spread, the way your hand stays after you’ve put it flat on something.

He was looking at Drew.

Not at the town. Not at the gate. Not past him up the road, the way they always looked. At Drew. With his own face. Drew saw it. He’d never seen it before, any of the four times, and he’d have known it now if he’d never been told a thing about it: a freckled boy of nineteen in a watch coat, out of breath, with the look on his face of somebody who’s just touched the post first and isn’t sure whether that counts if the other one touched it at the same time.

It lasted a heartbeat. Then it went, the way a face goes under water. And the patient puzzled look came back, and Ossie’s eyes went past Drew, up the road, to the gate.

Drew stood there.

“It counts,” he said. To Ossie. Over the millstream, which meant nobody heard it but him. “You both touched it. It counts.”

He walked back at a walk. A quarter of a mile. He didn’t hurry.

Tiger was sitting on the ground just inside the postern with his back against the wall and his knees up and his face in his arms. Rui Xiang was crouched beside him, a hand off, not touching. Old Pruett had the bar back across the postern and was standing with his hand flat on it and his face doing nothing at all, very hard.

Tiger looked up when Drew came through.

“He touched the post,” he said.

“Yeah.”

“He touched it. He ran. Hisui says nobody called him. Kei came down and said. Hisui says there was nothing on him, not the call, not the hand, nothing, he just—“ Tiger stopped. “He ran. By himself. He raced me.”

“I know,” said Drew. “I was the post.”

Tiger stared at him. Then he started to laugh, still crying, both at once, the way Tiger did everything, and put his face back in his arms.

Drew sat down beside him. On the cobbles, his back to the wall, carefully, with the splinted hand in his lap. It throbbed. He’d done something to it, pushing it down; he’d hear about it from Kei. He found he didn’t mind.

He put it in the channel.

They stood at the far end all day, the forty, the way they always did. Drew went back out at noon with a cup of tea for nobody and stood in the middle of the bridge for an hour with his back to them and his hands down. Nothing happened. He hadn’t thought it would. He went back in.

At dusk they turned and walked home up the swept road. Eleven miles in the dark to the valley, to their places in the rings. Ossie last, with his head turned over his shoulder.

Hyvred came down off the tower at midnight and found Drew on the gate step, where he’d been since dusk, and sat down on his right, the side without the splint.

“He looked back,” she said. “The whole way. Eleven miles. He’s never not looked back. But tonight he wasn’t looking at the gate.” She was quiet. “He was looking at the bridge.”

Drew didn’t say anything.

“I don’t know what that means,” said Hyvred.

“No,” said Drew. “Me neither.” He looked at his hand in its splint, in his lap. “I’ll be on it, though. Whatever it means. In the middle.” He thought about it. “Where I always am.”

She leaned her head on his shoulder for a moment, the right one, the way she did sometimes when she’d been up the tower too long and the world was too far away and too close at the same time. Then she sat up.

“You’re very hard,” she said. “Did you know? Like leaning on a wall.”

“Post,” said Drew.

“Post,” Hyvred agreed, and went in to sleep, because somebody else was on.
