The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 9: The Other Front

Chapter 253

Tomorrow

4,047 words · 18 min read

From the well to the north gate. Touch it; you had to touch it, flat hand on the wood, or it didn’t count. And back.

Tiger had run it every morning for three years. In rain. In snow to the knee. With a fever in the first winter so bad Kei had followed him the whole way with a flask. On the morning of the first call, when Drew was in the long room and the town was holding its breath, and on the morning after the second, when it wasn’t. He’d run it the morning after Elin. He’d run it with Joss on his back once, when Joss asked, and Joss had shouted Ossie, he’s beating you, RUN the whole way, from habit, at nobody.

Every morning. Well, gate, well. And at the gate, with his hand flat on the post, the one word.

He didn’t know why he said it any more. He’d stopped knowing in the first summer. He said it the way you’d touch a doorframe going out of a house you’d grown up in. Tomorrow.

And every morning, eleven miles north, in the third ring, a freckled face turned a little further toward the town.

“It’s not the running,” said Hyvred.

She was on the tower when he came up. She was always on the tower now; she’d stopped coming down for meals and Bee brought them up, and Corvin had moved his maps up there, and the king came up at dusk and sat against the north parapet for an hour with his back to the mountains and his big hands in his lap. She was facing south, toward the town, with her back to the valley and her eyes shut and her glasses pushed up in her hair, the way she stood now since One Pour. Feeling it, not looking.

He’d never been up here until the spring. He came up every morning now. He didn’t like it. He came anyway. He’d asked her to find out, and you didn’t ask somebody to do a thing like that and then not climb the stairs to hear it.

“It’s not the running,” she said again. “I thought it was. All summer. You run, he turns. I thought it was the speed, the light in you going fast, like a lantern going past a window. I thought something in him just liked the movement.” She opened her eyes and looked at him. Gold, with the red mark in them. “It’s not the speed. I tested it.”

“How?”

“You didn’t run on Tuesday,” said Hyvred. “You had the shoulder. Bee told you to stop, and you did, for once. You walked. Well to gate, gate to well.” She looked at her hands. “He turned anyway. The same. A hair further.”

Tiger didn’t say anything.

“So it’s not the speed,” said Hyvred. “And then on Thursday you ran, the way you do, flat out, and at the gate Joss shouted something and you looked round and didn’t touch the post. You went straight back.” She looked up. “And he didn’t turn. Not a hair. Nothing. I had my eyes shut and my back to him and I felt for him the whole way, and he stood there in the third ring with his face exactly where it had been the day before.”

Tiger was very still.

“It’s the gate,” said Hyvred. “It’s your hand on the gate. That’s when he turns. Every morning. The moment you touch it. I can feel it, now I know to. You slap the post and a heartbeat later, eleven miles off, he turns.”

“Flat hand on the wood,” said Tiger. Very quietly. “Or it doesn’t count.”

“I didn’t know that was a rule.”

“It’s his rule,” said Tiger. “He made it up. The first morning. Lying in the mud.” He put his hand over his face. “Hyv.”

“I know.”

“The thing that’s wearing him,” said Tiger, through his hand. “Whatever it is. It wouldn’t know that. It wouldn’t care about that. Why would it care whether I touched a gate?”

“It wouldn’t,” said Hyvred.

“So it’s him.”

She looked at him for a long time. She’d stopped saying you’re fine in the spring. She’d stopped saying anything she didn’t know.

“There’s something of him in there,” said Hyvred, “that still knows the rules of a race he made up in the mud three years ago. And every morning, when you touch the gate, it looks to see if you’ve won.” She swallowed. “That’s all I know. I don’t know how much of him. I don’t know if it’s enough. But it’s his.”

He ran it again the next morning to be sure.

He didn’t need to. He knew. But Hyvred had taught him that you checked; she’d learned it from Corvin and Corvin from Fenwick and Fenwick from whoever had taught Fenwick to chain eleven miles of valley with a rope and a boy. So he ran it. Well to gate. And at the gate he stopped, with his hand an inch from the post, and didn’t touch it, and stood there with his breath going and his tail lashing, for a count of ten.

Then he slapped it. Flat hand on the wood.

He stood at the north gate of Thornwall in the grey light with his hand flat on the post and his forehead against the wood, and the soldiers on the wall-walk above him looked very carefully the other way.

Then he ran back to the well, because you had to go back or it didn’t count either, and slapped the well-stone, and lay down in the mud the way Ossie used to, on his back, with his feet in the air, wheezing.

Joss was at the farrier’s door with his red scarf on. He didn’t shout anything this morning. He came across the yard, slowly, and stood over Tiger and looked down at him.

“You won,” said Joss.

“I always win.”

“Ossie’d be cross,” said Joss. It was what he always said. Then, after a moment, in a smaller voice: “Is he still looking?”

Tiger looked up at him from the mud. Eleven, now, with his gap tooth and his red scarf that his sister had knitted, and the place behind him in the farrier’s door where a girl with blue hands used to stand half a pace back with her hand not quite on his shoulder. Nobody stood there now. Joss stood there by himself, every morning, very straight.

“He’s looking,” said Tiger.

Joss nodded.

“Is Elin?”

Tiger didn’t know. He didn’t know if Elin could look. He didn’t know if Elin had ever raced anybody to a gate. He thought about saying I don’t know, and then he thought about Hyvred, who’d stopped saying anything she didn’t know.

“I’ll ask Hyv,” said Tiger.

“All right,” said Joss, and went back to the forge.

He opened his window on the shed roof that night.

[ Tiger · Tier 1: Drawn · Skills: Bolt · Progress to Tempered: 74% ]

Seventy-four. It had been seventy-four since the summer. He’d been Drawn since the first spring and climbing ever since, slow and steady, a point a fortnight, the way you’d climb a hill you’d decided to walk up instead of run. He’d never minded. He’d never been in a hurry for the tiers. He’d been in a hurry for everything else.

He closed it.

He sat on the ridge of the roof with his knees up and his tail hanging over the eaves and looked north. He couldn’t see the valley. He’d never been able to see it from here; the north wall was in the way, and beyond it the dark, and beyond that the eleven miles, and the rings, and a freckled boy in the third ring who still looked up every morning when a tiger slapped a gate.

It’s him.

He’d built this shed three times. The first one he’d built for the Pollen Puffs in the first spring, and a horse had kicked it down on the third day. The second had come down in a gale. The third had been standing two years now, with nobody under it; with Ossie’s shirt on a nail inside, mended, and OSSIE’S in Bee’s chalk on the back wall. He’d built it for Ossie to come home to. He’d known all along that was stupid. He’d built it anyway.

Everything he built fell down. It always had. On Earth and here.

This one was still standing.

He sat on it and thought about the rings, and the third one, and the gaps between the rings that he’d seen in the spring when he went out after Joss, lined up like spokes, a corridor of them, straight to Ossie. He’d seen down it. He’d been a hundred and fifty paces from the first ring and he’d seen all the way down the corridor of gaps to a freckled face, and the face had been looking at him; and something had said his name, gently, there you are, and he’d turned round and run.

He’d turned round and run.

There and back. That was Bolt. That was all it had ever been. He got there first and he got back first. He’d never once in his life got there and stayed.

He sat on the roof until the lamps went out in the town, one by one, and the guard changed on the north wall, and the moon came up late over the east wall, lopsided, a few days past full. And then he stood up on the ridge of the roof with his tail out for balance and looked at the north wall, a pace away from the end of the shed, where the shed leaned on it; and at the wall-walk, where the guard had just gone past with his lantern, the other way, toward the tower.

He didn’t decide. He just knew. The way he knew where the gate was in the dark.

He could be at the third ring in a quarter of an hour. Less. Eleven miles. He’d never run eleven miles flat out in his life, but he’d never needed to; he’d never had anything at the end of eleven miles. Down the valley road, along the corridor of gaps, the spokes, straight in, to the third ring. To a freckled face. He’d put his hand flat on Ossie’s chest, the way you put it on a gate, or it doesn’t count; and say his name; and say I won; and Ossie would sit up in the mud of his face and say tomorrow, and Tiger would take him by the arm, and they’d go.

There and back.

He’d been asked in by name. He knew that. The king had said so. You get there, you’re in the next one. Drew had said it on the floor of the long room with blood coming out of his nose. He knew.

He’d be fast. He’d be so fast it wouldn’t have time.

He went up onto the wall.

Nobody saw him. That was what being fast was. The guard was forty paces off with his back turned and his lantern swinging, and Tiger went up the shed’s end onto the parapet and over it in one movement, and down the outside of the north wall the way he’d come down it in the spring after Joss, a hand and a foot in the old mortar gaps and then a drop, the last fifteen feet, into the frosted grass of the ditch below.

He landed on all fours. He didn’t make a sound.

Then he stood up, and the valley road was in front of him, pale in the thin moon, going north between the fields toward the dark; and he put the aura into his legs, the way you’d open a door, and went.

He’d gone a hundred paces before he saw Drew.

Drew was standing in the road.

Right in the middle of it. At the first milestone, a quarter mile out from the north gate, where the road went over the little stone bridge across the millstream; in his dark track jacket with the red stripes down the sleeves, and his knuckles on, both hands, the short blades catching the moonlight. He wasn’t doing anything. He was just standing there, with his weight even and his hands open in front of him, the way he’d stood in the long room with thirty-three people leaning on him.

He’d been there a long time. Tiger could see it on him; the frost was on his shoulders.

Tiger didn’t stop. He couldn’t have stopped. At a hundred paces he was already going faster than he could stop, and the road was narrow there, at the bridge, between the parapets; and Drew was in the middle of it.

Go round, thought Tiger.

He went round. Left, onto the parapet of the bridge, the low stone edge, the way he’d gone round the end of the line at the Gate trial in Valcrest in front of forty thousand people, so fast they went oh.

Drew stepped left.

Not fast. Drew was never fast. He’d just been watching. He’d been watching Tiger’s feet for twelve years; he knew which way Tiger went round things before Tiger did. He stepped left one pace, onto the end of the parapet, and put his open hands up in front of his chest, and Tiger, at full Bolt, with nowhere left to go, went into them shoulder first.

It was like running into himself.

That was what he’d think, afterwards. He’d hit things before. He’d hit a wall at Valcrest in the Gate trial once, coming round a corner, and broken two fingers. He’d hit the ground from fifteen feet. He’d hit Ossie in the mud, the first morning, going for the well-stone at the same time, and they’d both gone down laughing.

This wasn’t like any of it. He hit Drew’s hands at full speed, the whole weight of him, everything Bolt had, and for a heartbeat there was nothing; no give at all; like hitting the face of a cliff. And then it came back.

All of it. Exactly as hard as he’d gone in. Exactly along the line he’d come in on. Counter. It went into him through his own shoulder, his own speed, his own weight, thrown straight back at him, and picked him up off the parapet and threw him backwards down the road the way he’d come, ten paces, through the air, and into the frozen ditch beside the millstream with a sound like a sack of grain thrown off a cart.

He lay in the ditch.

He couldn’t breathe. He lay on his back in the frosted grass with the stars going round and his shoulder gone numb from the neck to the elbow and his tail under him at an angle that was going to hurt in a minute; and he couldn’t get any air in, none, his chest had stopped, the way it stops when you fall flat on your back as a child; and he lay there with his mouth open, trying, and the stars going round.

Then it came. A great whooping gasp, and the cold air going in like water, and he rolled onto his side and was sick in the grass.

On the bridge, Drew was sitting down.

He hadn’t fallen. He’d sat. On the parapet of the bridge, quite suddenly, with one hand pressed flat to the left side of his chest, low, where his ribs were, and his face gone grey in the moonlight. He was breathing very carefully. In through his nose, out through his mouth, short. The way you breathe when something in your chest has just cracked again along an old line.

They looked at each other. Across ten paces of frozen road.

“Ow,” said Drew, after a while.

Tiger got up. It took him a long time. His shoulder came back, slowly, in pins and needles, from the neck down. He walked back up the road to the bridge, not fast, and stood in front of Drew.

“You knew,” said Tiger.

“I’ve known you twelve years,” said Drew. “You’ve had that face since Hyvred told you it was him. You’ve had it for three days.” He breathed, carefully. “You always go at night. You always go round left. You always go off the end of the shed because it’s the only bit of the north wall you can get up without the guard seeing.” He looked up at Tiger. “And you always forget I’m the one who stays.”

Tiger didn’t say anything.

“I’ve been sitting on this bridge since the guard changed,” said Drew. “Three nights. I was starting to think you’d grown up.”

“I could have gone round you.”

“No,” said Drew. “You couldn’t. Not here. The bridge is too narrow.” He moved his hand on his ribs, and winced. “That’s why I picked it.”

Tiger sat down beside him on the parapet. The stone was very cold. The millstream went under them, black, talking to itself. North, up the road, the valley went on into the dark.

“He’s in there,” said Tiger. “Drew. He’s in there. He looks to see if I’ve touched the gate.”

“I know. I read it.”

“He’s been looking every morning for two and a half years and I didn’t know. I just ran. I ran past him every morning and I thought I was just running.” His voice went. “I could have gone. In the spring. When I went out for Joss. I was a hundred and fifty paces from the first ring and I could see him down the gaps, and he was looking at me, and I turned round and ran.”

“You were asked in.”

“I know I was asked in.”

“By name,” said Drew. “At the third ring. His ring.” He looked up the road. “Tiger. You go up there now, tonight, at full Bolt, along the gaps, and you put your hand on his chest. And you’re in the third ring. Next to him. With your face turned the wrong way. And every morning I’ll be at that gate, and you’ll be eleven miles off, and I’ll slap the post for you, and you’ll turn your head a hair.” He breathed. “And I’ll have to run the race on my own. And I’m very slow.”

Tiger put his face in his hands.

“Not here,” said Drew. Quietly. “That’s all. Not up there. Not where it’s strongest and he’s in the middle of it and it’s already got your name. Not here.”

“Where, then?”

Drew was quiet for a long time.

“It’s coming down,” he said at last. “Hyvred says. Corvin says. The fourteenth’s going up a word a day now. One day it comes down the pass, and they all come with it. All the rings. Walking behind it, down the road. To the town.” He nodded at the north gate, a quarter mile behind them, dark, with the lamp over it. “To the gate.”

Tiger lifted his head.

“Where he always comes,” said Drew. “Every morning. To see if you’ve won.” He looked at Tiger. “He’ll come to the gate. It’s his race. He made the rules. Touch it, or it doesn’t count.” He took his hand off his ribs and looked at it, as if there might be something on it, and put it back. “And you’ll be there. At the gate. With your back to it, and the whole town behind you, and me. Not eleven miles up a valley on your own in the middle of the night with a cracked friend on a bridge.”

“Two cracked,” said Tiger.

“Two,” Drew agreed. “Again.”

Tiger looked at the gate. At the lamp over it.

“When?” he said.

“Not yet,” said Drew.

They walked back.

Slowly. Drew couldn’t walk fast, and Tiger, for once in his life, didn’t want to. Along the road, a quarter mile, in the thin moon, with the frost crunching and the millstream beside them and the dark valley at their backs. To the north gate, where the guard on the wall had seen them at last, and was shouting, and somebody was running for Hild.

At the gate, Tiger stopped.

He put his hand out. Flat. On the post. The way he did every morning.

He didn’t say anything. He just held it there, on the wood, with Drew beside him breathing short and careful, and the guard on the wall gone quiet above them; and eleven miles behind him in the dark, he knew, though he couldn’t see it, though he’d never be able to see it, a freckled face in the third ring turned a hair and a bit further toward the town, to see if he’d won.

Not yet, thought Tiger. To the face. But I’m coming. When you come down. I’ll be at the gate. You’ll have to touch it, or it doesn’t count.

Hild opened the postern. She looked at them both, with her one eye, for a long moment, in the lantern light. She looked at Drew’s hand on his ribs, and Tiger’s shoulder, and the frost on Drew’s jacket, and the grass in Tiger’s hair.

“Bridge,” said Hild. It wasn’t a question.

“Bridge,” said Drew.

Hild nodded, as if she’d known for three nights, which she probably had, and stood aside to let them in.

He opened his window in the bunk room, at the end of the night, with the grey coming in at the shutters and Drew asleep in the next bunk on his right side, with Kei’s bandage round his ribs again and Kei’s opinion of him written on a slip of paper and pinned to the blanket.

Seventy-seven.

Three points. In a night. It hadn’t moved like that since the first spring.

He looked at it for a long time. He didn’t know what had moved it. He hadn’t got anywhere. He’d been thrown into a ditch by his best friend and been sick in the grass and walked home. He hadn’t got there. He hadn’t got back first. He’d just turned round.

No. That wasn’t it. He’d turned round before. He’d turned round in the spring at a hundred and fifty paces and run, and it hadn’t moved anything.

He’d turned round tonight and walked. Back to the gate. Not running away from something. Going back to the place he’d be when it mattered. Choosing the back before the there.

There and back. He’d always thought the back was the easy part. The bit after.

He closed the window.

Across the room, without opening his eyes, Drew said: “What is it?”

“Seventy-seven.”

“Huh,” said Drew. “It moved for that?”

“Apparently.”

“Being thrown in a ditch.”

“Walking back,” said Tiger.

Drew was quiet for a while.

“Good,” he said. And went back to sleep.

In the morning Tiger ran it. Well to gate. He touched the post, flat hand on the wood, and stood there for a count of three. And then he said the word.

“Tomorrow.”

Up on the tower, Hyvred wrote one line in the channel. He read it at the gate.

“Hyv says,” said Tiger.

And told him.
