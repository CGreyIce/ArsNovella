The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 7: Myths

Chapter 208

Struck

8,961 words · 39 min read

He held the gap all night, and he didn’t open his window once.

He’d thought about that afterwards and decided it was the most important thing he did. Not the scouts. The window. Every dawn for two and a half years he’d opened it first thing, before he did anything, the way you check your phone before you’re properly awake. And every dawn it had said the same thing in the same plain white letters, Tier 0: Initiate, with two bars under it and a grey third line, and every dawn he’d looked at it and thought one left.

He didn’t look at it once on the knoll.

He didn’t have time. That was all it was, at first. There was a gap on the east side of the ring, by a rock, where the edge of the green dipped and the slope went down to the riverbeds, and Rhea had put him in it and said hold it, and he’d said holding, and then he’d held it. That was all. A gap the width of a man between two old soldiers with spears. Every so often something came up the slope at it in bursts, step-step-step and stop, grey and thin and bent forward, and came through it exactly where he knew it would, because there was only one place it could come through.

The first came an hour after dark. Close to, it was two heads taller than he was and no wider than a ladder, with a smooth grey nothing where a face should be, and a cold coming off it like the air from an open cellar door. It stepped into the gap and he put the short sword in under its arm, where the long bones met, and felt the point go through something like wet leather and grate on something like gristle. He pulled it out. The thing folded down onto the green in front of him, all its joints at once, and lay there with its long fingers opening and closing on the grass.

He stepped back into the gap. After that he stopped counting them.

He’d done it before. He’d done it on the red two summers ago, in a broken ring round a black sword. He’d done it on Earth ten thousand times, at three in the morning, with his eyes on a doorway, waiting. You didn’t look at the thing coming. You looked at the gap it had to come through. You didn’t go out to it. You let it come to you.

He’d learned that from somebody.

The old man on his left, whose name was Jessop and who’d lost an eye on this plain nine years ago, said to him at about two in the morning, between bursts, very quietly, “Where’d you learn that, lad?”

“Off a man,” said Niko. “Who never moved first.”

Jessop thought about that for a while, leaning on his spear.

“Aye,” he said. “I’ve seen him do it.”

His nose had bled at about three. Not from anything inside. One of them had come through the gap with its long arm swinging and the back of its wrist had caught him across the face, and he’d sat down on the green very suddenly and then got up again with his mouth full of blood and stepped back into the gap. Hesper had come and looked at it and pinched it and said you’ll live and gone back to the ones who mightn’t.

It had stopped. It was dried now, all down his chin and the front of his shirt, stiff.

He didn’t mind. He’d bled all over the east wall at dawn for two and a half years. It was the first time it hadn’t been his own fault.

He heard everything. You do, in a gap. You can’t see much, because you’re not looking; you’re looking at the gap. But you hear all of it, the way you hear a whole house from one room at night.

He heard Rhea’s arm go. He heard Teague shouting ring. He heard the first big one sit down and not get up, and Jory Platt say that’s one. He heard Suzu hit the cairn, and the sound Yan made, which he’d never heard Yan make and never wanted to again. He heard the stone start to come out of the ground and Maigo scream, and Salty’s sword hit the grass, and then, a long moment later, a sound from the middle of the knoll that he’d never in his life expected to hear on a battlefield.

Salty, laughing.

He heard Wendy shout don’t you dare. He’d never heard Wendy shout. He heard Garth hit the ground. He heard Wendy’s last three arrows and Rhea’s sword and Jory Platt saying three.

And after that he heard the long cold part. Aldous’s song going down to a thread, and stopping, and starting again. Hesper’s bags clinking. Somebody crying very quietly on the south side and somebody else telling them to shut up, kindly. Liraun, out past the north edge, singing to the grass, low, the same few notes, all night, like somebody humming to a horse.

And under all of it, the whole time, in front of him, at the bottom of the slope where the riverbeds met, the one that hadn’t come.

He’d been watching it all night. Between bursts. You watch the one that isn’t moving. That’s the one that knows something.

It had sat down at the very start, at the meeting of the riverbeds, on the green threads, behind the line of scouts. Like a dog. And it hadn’t moved. Not once. Not when the others went in. Not when they went down. It had sat there through the whole night, very still, horned head level, facing up the slope.

Not at the stone.

Niko had worked that out at about four, and he’d felt it go down his back like cold water. It wasn’t looking at the stone. The others had been, all night; you could see it in the way they leaned. This one wasn’t leaning.

It was looking at the gap.

The sky went grey over Kharvos.

Then a thin edge of gold. Then a thinner thing above the gold, white, lying on its back over the black line of the mountains: the very last of the old moon, the day before it went. He’d never seen it like that before. He’d been on the wall every dawn for two and a half years and he’d never once looked up from his window long enough to see the moon come up over Kharvos the day before it went.

And at the bottom of the slope, all together, the scouts leaned.

He felt it. The whole line of them, tipping forward at once toward the knoll like grass under wind. Toward the stone behind him. He heard Maigo say something, high and thin, from the tree: it’s listening. And Salty: I know. Don’t let go. I won’t.

And the fourth one stood up.

It was bigger than the others.

Of course it was. That was why it had waited. You keep your best one back. You let the others go in and find out where the walls are, and then you send the one that matters at the place where the walls aren’t. He’d done it himself, a thousand times, at home, at three in the morning, on calls. You sent three to make noise in one place and you sat in the other and waited.

He’d played against somebody who did that better than anyone. For years.

It came up the slope.

It didn’t trot. It walked. One heavy step and then another, with its long arms hanging down past its knees and its horns going back from where its face should have been in a long curve like a ram’s, level, steady. The scouts opened in front of it without a sound and closed behind it. It came up out of the line at the bottom of the slope into the first gold light and walked up the east side of the knoll toward the cairn, toward the stone, toward Salty kneeling with his back to it and his arms round the iron, unable to turn round.

Straight at the gap.

Because the gap was the way to the stone. There wasn’t another. It had sat there all night and watched where everybody went and where the fire was and where the walls were, and the gap was the way.

Niko was in it.

He looked round once. He couldn’t help it. He knew you weren’t supposed to; you never took your eyes off the gap. He looked anyway. One look, quick, over his shoulder.

Everyone was down.

Not dead. Down. Rhea on the grass on the south side by the third one, with her left arm strapped across her chest and her sword across her knees, trying to get up and her legs not doing it. Teague on one knee with his head down and his shield split. Aldous on his knees at the edge of the green where the fire had been, swaying, his lips moving, no fire. The old men, too few, sitting where they’d been standing. Jessop, on his left, leaning on his spear with both hands because he couldn’t stand without it. Maigo against the tree, grey, holding everything. Yan against the cairn with his eyes shut and his face like ash and Suzu round his neck and the scythe in the grass beside him. Wendy beside Salty with her bow, drawing her one last arrow.

She let it go. It went into the big one’s chest. It didn’t stop.

She lowered the bow.

And Salty, on his knees, with his arms round the black stone and his back to all of it, holding it down. The one thing on the knoll that couldn’t move at all.

Niko looked back at the slope.

It was a long way down, still. Eighty paces. Seventy. It wasn’t hurrying. It was walking up the slope the way a man walks up a path to a door he already knows is going to open.

He was the last one standing in its way.

He was the last one.

He’d been the last one for a long time.

Then be last, the man had said.

On the east wall. The summer before. With the sun coming up over Kharvos, while Niko sat against the parapet with blood on his chin and said he didn’t mind being last. That if he was going to be last, he wanted to be last at this. Not Drawn. Not Spark. Both, at once, the first time, anywhere.

Then be last, the man had said.

He’d thought it was a kind thing to say. It was. He’d thought it was all it was.

He understood now, at seventy paces, what it actually meant. It meant: the last one is the one who’s still standing when everyone else is down. It’s not the worst seat. It’s the one that matters. It’s the one at the end when it’s one against one and everybody’s watching from the ground and it’s on you.

He’d been last in a lot of rounds.

He’d won some of them.

He lowered the sword.

He didn’t decide to. That was the strange thing. He was standing in the gap with the short sword up, the way he’d stood all night, weight on his back foot, ready, and he looked at the thing coming up the slope at sixty paces, and his arm just went down. The point of the sword going down to the grass. His shoulders dropping. His weight coming off his back foot onto both, even, easy.

Because he’d been holding his breath.

He noticed it. The way he’d noticed it at the forge, with the skillet out over the fire and the whole fort watching in silence. He’d been holding his breath since the fourth one stood up. Since before. Since about three in the morning, maybe. He’d been holding it in, at the top, like a smith who’s hit the iron too hard and is afraid to hit it again.

You are thinking with your breath, Tavrin had said.

He let it out. Through his nose. Slowly.

It was the loudest thing he’d ever heard.

And beside the stone, behind him, very quietly, the way she’d said it every dawn on the wall for two and a half years, Wendy said, “One.”

He began.

Aura first. It always was. Down in his chest, the warm thing, the swordsman’s thing, the one that had taken him a year to find and that Rhea had said was a waste. He let it come up.

And mana, on the other side, the cold bright one, the one that came in from outside like breath. Aldous’s thing. Yan’s. Thea’s. The one he’d sat up nights over in the chapel with Aldous’s old books, learning the long chants, and never once been able to cast, because you couldn’t cast at Initiate. Two years of knowing every word and never once getting to say one.

Two candles, Yan called them. Two candles that keep almost touching.

He let them touch.

The circle closed. Out like a spell, in like breath, round, and round. He’d first seen it not far from this knoll. Two summers ago, on the red half an hour from here, a man walking across the plain with a rod over his shoulder and his aura going out of him the way a spell goes out, and coming back in the way breath does. Not two lines. A circle. Niko had stood in a gap that day too, and watched it, and gone back to the wall and sat there all night trying to work out what he’d seen.

He knew what he’d seen now.

“Six,” said Wendy. “Seven.”

Fifty paces.

It came up the slope.

He didn’t look at it. He looked at the gap. At the place it had to come through, the dip in the edge of the green by the rock, where the soft grass met the red. That was all. You don’t look at the thing coming. You look at where it has to be.

“Eleven. Twelve.”

Forty paces. He could hear it now. Not footsteps; they didn’t make footsteps. The green. The grass under it tearing, very softly, a long way down the slope, the young grass on the riverbeds and the slope going grey under every step.

“Fifteen.”

He didn’t push.

It was the hardest thing he’d ever done. Everything in him wanted to push. There was a thing the size of a cart walking up a hill at him and his friend behind him on his knees who couldn’t turn round, and every part of him that had ever been frightened wanted to hold on. Grab both candles and squeeze them together and keep them there by force, the way he had every morning since the thaw, holding his breath at eighteen.

He didn’t.

He just let them go round. Like a pan over a fire. Like food in a pan, going round, catching on the iron.

“Seventeen.”

Thirty paces.

“Eighteen.”

The wall came.

It always came. Eighteen. Nineteen. The moment the two candles stopped wanting to go round and wanted, with everything in them, to come apart. To be two things again. It came up through him like a tide. Every morning for two and a half years. Everything in him saying hold it. Hold it together. Don’t let go. You’ll lose it.

He’d lost it a thousand times.

He’d got past it once. Twice. On the wall, after the skillet. Twenty-nine, both times. Because he’d stopped holding on and let it go up, and been there when it came down.

He let it go up.

A flick. Nothing else. A little flick from somewhere in the middle of his chest, forward and up, and a drop, a give, the hand going down a fraction so the pan got out of the way. Both candles. Up and out. Out past the edge of him into the cold gold air of the dawn over the knoll, the aura and the mana, apart, gone, and for half a heartbeat there was nothing in his chest at all.

Still the most frightening thing in the world.

He was there when they came down.

“Nineteen,” said Wendy. “Twenty.”

They came down changed. Already round each other. Already going round, smoky and dark and sweet, the way the food came down into the iron. And under the two lines in his window, which he wasn’t looking at, which he didn’t need to look at, he felt the third line light, grey to white, the way a lamp comes on in a window in a street you know.

“Twenty-two.”

Twenty-five paces.

The frost-letters came up on the third line.

He felt them. He didn’t look. He let his eyes stay on the gap, soft, and felt them come up at the edge of things the way you feel somebody’s hands moving across a table. Guessing. Trying things on.

BATTLEMAGE. He felt that one go past. It didn’t stay this time. It didn’t need to; he knew it. And then others, faster. Words he’d half-seen a hundred dawns and never been able to read. He could nearly read them now. Spellsword. Magic knight. Hybrid. And words that weren’t English at all, in the kingdom’s tongue, in the window’s own odd careful lettering. And words that weren’t any tongue he knew. Guessing. All of them. Every one of them with one thing in front of the other, and every one of them knowing it was wrong, and going.

Rude, he thought. Something very far down in him that might have been laughing. Suzu says it’s rude.

“Twenty-five,” said Wendy. “Twenty-six.”

Twenty paces. He could hear it breathing now. It didn’t breathe like a thing that was alive. It breathed like wind in a crack.

He tossed it again. Up. Out. Gone. There.

“Twenty-seven.”

And the little upright mark at the start of the third line, under all the guessing, went on, and off, and on.

Not guessing. It had never once guessed anything. It just sat at the start of the box and waited, the way a cursor waits at the start of an empty page, for whoever was going to write.

Enter name.

He’d been twelve.

That was the thing he thought of. Of all the things. With a thing the size of a cart at fifteen paces and his heart going like a hammer on a bench and the whole of him spun up into a circle on the edge of breaking. He thought of being twelve.

A bedroom in a flat that was too hot, with a fan that screamed. A book he’d saved up for, a big one, with a soft cover gone white at the corners. The first page of it. Who are you? And a row of little pictures: a man with a sword, a man with a staff, a man with a bow. And one at the end, a man with a sword in one hand and fire in the other, and under it, in gold letters, a word he’d never seen before.

He’d picked it. Of course he had. He’d been that one in every story he’d told himself since. He’d been told it was a waste by everybody he ever told. Pick one, mate. You’re half as good at both. Rhea, at supper, in the first year: it’s a waste, lad, pick one. And he’d never picked one. He didn’t know why. He’d just always wanted to be the man at the end of the row with the fire in one hand and the blade in the other, and he’d never once in his life wanted to be anything else.

He’d written his name on the line under it, in pencil, at twelve, in a hot bedroom, with a fan screaming.

Nobody had guessed that word for him. Nobody had needed to. He’d read it in gold letters on the first page of a book and picked it, and it had been his ever since, and he’d forgotten it was a word at all.

The frost-letters had never guessed it. They never would have. It wasn’t anywhere in this world to guess. It was in his.

Names aren’t guessed. They’re given.

It didn’t have to be right. That was what he understood, at fifteen paces, with Wendy saying twenty-eight behind him. Battlemage might even have been true. True enough. Suzu had said it: the old ones’ name for her was a good name, a true one even, and it wasn’t her name. It didn’t matter if the word had one thing in front of the other. It didn’t matter if it was perfect. It mattered that it was his. That he gave it. That nobody else could have.

“Twenty-nine,” said Wendy.

Ten paces.

The big one stopped walking. And gathered itself. Low. Its long arms going out wide on either side of it, its horns coming down, every line of it bending toward him, toward the gap, toward the stone behind the gap and the man on his knees holding it. The way the one two summers ago had gathered itself at Wendy. The way a big dog gathers itself before it jumps.

He gave it.

Spellblade.

He didn’t type it. There was nothing to type with. He just gave it, the way you give your name to somebody at a gate, and the frost on the third line went still.

All of it. Every guessing letter. Stopped, all at once, the way the scouts stopped, as if somebody had cut the string. And faded. And under them, in the place where the cursor had blinked for nine months, in plain white letters exactly like the letters on the line above, where it said his own name:

Spellblade.

And the line moved.

He felt it, without looking. The third line didn’t stay at the bottom, under the other two. It went up. Between them. Into the place where the tier word went, on everyone else’s window; where Wendy’s said Drawn and Yan’s said Candle. And the other two lines, aura and mana, the two bars that had sat at ninety-six for six months like two clocks set by each other, came in toward it from either side, and met it, and went into it.

And there was a space. Where a word should be. Tier 1: and then nothing. And a cursor, at the start of the nothing, going on, and off.

Waiting.

Not for a name this time. For a word. The tier word. The one that said what you were, at this step. Drawn. Spark. A swordsman’s word for what aura did, a mage’s word for how bright the fire burned. And there wasn’t one. Nobody had ever needed one. Nobody had ever stood here.

He had half a heartbeat.

It jumped.

It came off the slope into the air, all of it at once, and the ground it left jumped under Niko’s feet. For a moment it was only a shape against the sun coming up over Kharvos: horns, and long arms flung wide, and the black line of the mountains under it, and the thin white moon. Its shadow went over him, cold. It wasn’t jumping at him. It was jumping over him: over the gap, over his head, at the stone and Salty’s back, and coming down.

You let it go up, he thought. And you’re there when it comes down.

He tossed it.

Both candles. Up out of his chest and down his arm, the aura and the mana together, the way he’d never once done it on the wall. On the wall he’d tossed them into the air. This time he tossed them into his hand, and through his hand into the short sword, into the iron: the warm one and the cold one going round each other inside the steel, and catching, like a flint on a striker.

His wrist came up. His arms hardly moved. A flick, forward and up, small, the way the man’s had at the fire on the eleventh night; and the short sword came up off the grass in a short bright arc into the place where the thing was going to be when it came down.

It came down.

He was there.

It rang.

That came first, before anything else: before he felt the sword go in, before he knew what he’d done. One note, clear and pure, going out from the short sword the way a note goes out from a bell when the clapper hits it. It wasn’t loud. It was everywhere: out across the knoll and down the slope and over the red, up into the gold air, and down through the soles of his feet into the green.

Then the weight arrived.

The long arm came down onto his edge just below the elbow, the arm that was reaching past him for Salty’s back, and the whole falling weight of the thing came down with it. He felt it in his wrist and his elbow and his shoulder and all the way down into his heels, like catching a sack of grain thrown off a cart. His knees bent. His boots slid back a hand’s width on the grass. And the edge went in.

It went in hot. A line of fire ran along the steel, gold-white, exactly the width of the edge; where it touched the grey hide, the hide hissed and blackened and parted in front of it, and the smell came up into his face, the cold snuffed-candle smell of them with something scorched in it now, like hair caught in a lamp. He felt the edge meet bone. It checked there for half a heartbeat, the way an axe checks on a knot. Then the fire went into the bone too, and the bone gave with a sound like a log cracking in a grate, and the edge came out the other side into the open air.

Struck.

The word came to him at the exact moment the arm came off. Not before. It wasn’t a word he’d thought of. It was the word for what had just happened. A match is struck. A blow is struck. A bell.

He gave it.

And the arm, the long reaching arm with its grey fingers spread for Salty’s back, came off below the elbow and went past his ear, turning over, burning at the cut like a torch, and hit the green behind him with a heavy wet slap. The fingers closed on the grass once, and opened, and lay still.

The big one came down.

It missed Salty by an arm’s length. Its whole weight had been thrown at him out of the jump, and the arm it had been reaching with was gone, and its balance went with the arm. It landed on its side on the green beside the cairn, past Salty, past the stone, with a thud that bounced Niko’s heels off the ground and threw a spray of torn turf and white flowers over Salty’s back. It rolled. It came up onto its knees with its one arm out and the stump of the other burning gold-white where he’d cut it, and the fire running up inside it, under the grey hide, a glow climbing toward the shoulder like a coal going up a flue.

It screamed: high and thin and enormous, like wind in a crack.

Salty didn’t let go. Niko saw that. Turf all over his back, and the thing on its knees an arm’s length from him, screaming, and he hadn’t moved.

And the note was still ringing.

He didn’t know how. It should have stopped. A bell stops. You hit it and it rings and the ring goes down and down until it’s gone. This didn’t. It went down, but not out. Down into the ground. He could feel it, through his feet, through the green. Going down from the short sword in his hand through the soft grass with the white and blue flowers in it, through the dark red earth, into the roots.

Into the roots, and along them.

And into the stone.

He saw Salty’s face. He couldn’t not see it; Salty was right there, ten paces from him, on his knees with his arms round the black iron and his cheek on it, and his eyes went wide.

The stone was ringing.

Niko could hear it. Everyone could. Under Salty’s arms, under his cheek, the black stone was ringing with the same note, low, much lower, the way a big bell answers a small one when you hit the small one near it. The same note exactly. Clear and deep and pure, coming up out of the black iron that had been a sword, through the cairn, through the children’s stones round its foot and the white stone at its head, into the gold morning air.

“It’s the same,” said Maigo. From the tree. He’d sat up. His sunglasses had fallen off. His white eyes were huge. “It’s the same note, it’s—Alea’s sword, at the full moon, it’s the same—”

And under the stone, under the spike, a long way down, something that had been lifting its head put it down.

Niko felt it. He didn’t know how. Through the note, maybe. Through his feet. He felt it the way you feel a dog in another room lie back down when the shouting stops. Slowly. With a sort of sigh. Down. And down. Further than it had been. Further than it had been all night, all spring, further than it had been since the autumn when the swords rang. Down, and the roots of the ash going down with it and closing round it, like fingers.

“It’s going down,” said Maigo. “It’s lying down. It’s—oh. Oh. Like a dog. Like a dog lying down—”

And at the bottom of the slope, all the scouts stopped dead.

Every one of them. Together. Not the stop between bursts; the other stop. The one they’d done the first winter at the edge of the red when the horns went off on the wall. All of them perfectly still, all at once, with their faceless heads up. Listening.

To the note. Still going. Out across the red, very faint now, over the riverbeds, toward the mountains.

They weren’t leaning.

He saw that. They’d leaned all night toward the stone, like grass in a wind, like the ash’s leaves, like a field of compass needles all pointed at the one thing. And they weren’t, now. They were standing straight. As straight as those things ever stood. And swaying, very slightly, this way and that, the way a compass needle swings when there’s nothing for it to point at any more.

Then they turned.

All of them. Together. The way the seven in Kingsferry had turned in the spring, Rachael said: all in the same moment, without a sound, as if somebody a long way off had said a word only they could hear. They turned round on the green threads at the bottom of the knoll and went east. In bursts. Step-step-step, and stop, and step. Down the riverbeds, on the grey stripes they’d made coming in. Away. Toward the mountains and the sun.

The big one went after them.

It got up off its knees by the cairn, with the stump of its arm still burning gold and the fire gone into it, and it didn’t look at Niko. It didn’t look at the stone. It went down the east slope of the knoll past him, past the gap, a pace from him, close enough to touch, stumbling, its one arm swinging, and he didn’t move. He stood in the gap with the short sword in his hand and the note still in it and let it go past.

At the bottom of the slope it fell, and got up, and went on. East. Burning.

“Let it go,” said Yan.

Rhea had got up. Niko didn’t know how. She was on her feet on the south side with her sword in her one good hand and her face white as paper, starting down the slope after it.

“Rhea,” said Yan. He hadn’t opened his eyes. He was still sitting against the cairn with Suzu round his neck and his face grey. “Let it go.”

“It’s running—“

“I know. Let it.” Yan’s voice was very quiet and very calm. “He let them go. The first time. On the red. Fifty of them. So they’d go home and say one thing.” He opened his eyes. “Let this one go home and say one thing.”

Rhea stopped.

“What thing?” she said.

Yan looked at the stone. At Salty, still on his knees with his arms round it, with his eyes shut now and his cheek on the iron and the low note still coming up out of it, fading, very slowly, the way a bell fades.

“That it’s held,” said Yan. “Without him.”

Then the note stopped, and it was very quiet.

Niko stood in his gap.

The sun was all the way up now over Kharvos. Gold, enormous, the way it only was out here, with nothing between you and it. The red going from grey to red to gold all the way to the mountains. The riverbeds with their green threads, and the grey stripes down the middle of them going away east, and on the grey stripes, very small, getting smaller, a long broken line of things moving in bursts, and at the back of them, a long way behind, one bigger one, stumbling, with something burning on it like a lamp.

He watched them until he couldn’t.

Then he looked at his window.

It was open in front of him. He didn’t remember opening it. Maybe he hadn’t. Maybe it had opened itself, the way they did the first time, in the hall, two and a half years ago, when thirty-five people woke up on a stone floor and found a white box in the air in front of their faces with their names in it.

Plain. White. The same letters it had always had.

[ Nikorasu · Tier 1: Struck · Spellblade ]

He looked at it for a long time.

There weren’t any bars. That was the first thing he noticed. No Aura 96%, no Mana 96%. They’d gone. They’d gone into the middle line, both of them, when it came up; he’d felt them go. There was just the one line. His name. A word for what he was at this step, that nobody had ever needed before. And a word for what he was, that he’d read in gold letters at twelve in a hot bedroom with a fan screaming and never once in his life thought anybody else would need.

Struck.

Spellblade.

He’d given them both. Nobody had guessed either.

“Thirty-five,” said Niko. To nobody. Out loud.

His voice came out very strange.

He sat down in the gap.

His nose wasn’t bleeding. Not new blood. He checked, because it was habit, and there was the old dried blood from three in the morning down his chin, and nothing else. His hands were shaking, a bit. Not more than a bit. He put the short sword down on the green beside him, very carefully, the way you’d put down something that might still be hot.

It wasn’t. He touched it. The edge was cold, and clean, and there was nothing on it at all. Not even the black. As if the fire had taken it.

Jessop, on his left, the old man with one eye, was looking at him.

“Where’d you learn that, lad?” said Jessop.

Niko thought about it.

“Off a skillet,” he said.

Aldous came. On his knees, across the green, because he couldn’t get up, with his book under his arm. He got to Niko and sat back on his heels and took Niko’s wrist in his fingers without asking and felt for the beat there.

Niko let him.

Aldous counted. Ten. His lips moving. Then again, ten more.

“Fast,” said Aldous.

“Still there?”

“Still there.” Aldous didn’t let go of his wrist. He sat there on the green beside the gap holding it, in the sun, with his face grey and his eyes red and his spectacles crooked. “Nobody had done it,” he said. “I said. Every week. Two paths. Half the speed of either. Nobody’s done it.” He looked at Niko. “I didn’t say it to stop you.”

“I know.”

“I said it so you’d know you’d be the first.” Aldous let go of his wrist, finally, and wiped his eyes with the back of his hand, and put his crooked spectacles straight. “In two worlds, I think. Not just this one. I think yours didn’t either.”

“It didn’t,” said Niko. “It just had a word for it.”

Aldous looked at him.

“I’ve got a word for it now,” said Aldous, and opened his book, and Niko saw that he’d turned to the very back, to the blank endpapers, where there was nothing written, where in nine years the register of the dead had never gone. And Aldous took the stub of pencil out of the spine, and wrote, very small, in the kingdom’s script, in the top corner of the empty page, a single line.

Niko couldn’t read the kingdom’s script. Not with his eyes. His window read it to him, the way it read everything.

The knoll. The last morning of the old moon. Nobody.

Wendy was on her knees beside Salty.

Niko watched them from the gap. He didn’t get up. He didn’t think he could, yet. He sat there in the sun with his back against the rock and watched Wendy kneel down on the green beside the black stone, with her empty bow on the grass beside her, and put her hand on Salty’s arm.

Salty hadn’t let go.

He was still kneeling there with his arms round the stone and his cheek on the iron and his eyes shut. The note was gone. The scouts were gone. And he hadn’t moved. Niko could see why. His arms had been round the stone since midnight, nearly. Seven hours. They’d locked. He’d been holding on so long that he didn’t know how to stop.

“Salty,” said Wendy.

He didn’t open his eyes.

“It’s done,” said Wendy. “They’ve gone. It’s down. Maigo says it’s lying down.”

“Can’t,” said Salty. Thickly. Against the iron.

“I know.”

She didn’t pull his arms. Niko thought she might; it was what you’d do. She didn’t. She put her hand on his, on the black iron, on the west face, where his fingers were locked together over the letters he couldn’t see. And left it there.

And said, very quietly, the thing a man had said to Salty on the red two summers ago, with his hand on the grip of a black sword above Salty’s, when Salty couldn’t let go of that either.

“It’s all right,” said Wendy. “You can put it down now. You’ve carried it far enough.”

Salty opened his eyes.

His hands came apart.

Slowly. One finger at a time. The way a fist comes open in sleep. They came off the black iron and his arms came off it and he sat back on his heels on the green, and the stone stayed exactly where it was, and didn’t move, and didn’t need him to hold it any more. And Salty looked at it for a moment. And then he went over sideways, the way the first big one had, the way Rhea had, like a very tired dog, onto the grass, with his head in Wendy’s lap.

Wendy put her hand in his pocket and took out the other heel of bread.

“After,” she said.

Salty laughed. It came out of him like air out of a bellows.

The ash had turned.

Niko saw it when he looked up from Salty. He’d been looking at that tree from the east wall every dawn for two years, a small green flame on a knoll a long way out. He’d seen it turn south for the elves in the winter. He’d seen it turn east in the spring and not known why, and gone and asked Maigo on a Thursday.

It wasn’t pointing east any more.

Every leaf on it was turned in. Not one way. All ways. Toward the middle of the knoll. Toward the cairn, and the stone, and the people round it. Toward Salty on the grass with his head in Wendy’s lap. Toward Yan against the cairn with Suzu round his neck. Toward Maigo, sitting at its own foot with his sunglasses in his hand and his face wet. Toward the old men sitting on the green, and Hesper with his bags, and Garth on his cloak, and Rhea, who’d sat back down on the south side where she’d been and put her face in her one good hand.

Toward him. In the gap.

Soft. All of it. The whole crown leaning, gently, the way a face turns.

Maigo was looking up at it.

“She’s not on watch any more,” said Maigo. His voice had gone completely. It was just a whisper with a crack in it. “Look. She’s come off watch.” He put his bark-brown hand flat on the trunk behind him. “Thank you,” he told it. “Thank you, you clever, clever—“

He couldn’t finish. He leaned his head back against the tree and cried, with his eyes open, looking up at the leaves, and the bells on his antlers, which had been muffled in a scarf all night, came loose, and rang, very softly, all on their own, in the little wind that had come up off the red with the sun.

Liraun was at the edge of the green.

She’d stood up. She’d been kneeling there all night, singing to the grass, with her long hands flat on the ground just outside the green. Now she was standing, looking at Niko, with the sun on her pale hair and her face doing something he didn’t have a word for. Like somebody who’s been told a secret, and has just watched it come true.

She touched her ear. Once. The way she had on the wall, at twenty-nine heartbeats.

“Like a bell,” said Liraun. In the singing tongue. He heard it in English. “I said.”

“You did,” said Niko.

“I heard it, on the wall. In you. A bell that had not been struck yet.” She smiled at him, shy. “Now it has.”

Tavrin came to the edge of the green beside her.

The old elf stood there with her stave in her hand, the stave she’d put across Oen’s chest, and looked at the knoll. At all of it. At the three great grey bodies on the green, and the burned place on the red outside where the second one lay, and the long grey stripes down the riverbeds going east. At the old men and the wounded and the chaplain on his knees. At the stone.

Then at Niko.

“You gave it a name,” said Tavrin.

“Yes.”

“Two.”

“Yes.”

“Good,” said Tavrin. “Nobody else could have.” She looked at him a while longer, the way she looked at hammer marks. “That was a well-made thing. I will remember it.” She nodded, once. “I said I would remember.”

“You watched,” said Salty.

He’d sat up. Niko didn’t know how. He was sitting on the green with his back against the cairn and the black stone behind his head and his arms hanging useless at his sides, and Wendy’s hand on his shoulder, holding him up. And he was looking past Niko at Tavrin, at the edge of the green, and his face was the colour it went when he lost to Rhea.

Tavrin looked back at him.

“Yes,” she said.

“All night. You stood there. He went for her,” said Salty, “and it went past you, it went right past you, close enough to touch, and Oen went to go and you stopped him.” He breathed. “Garth could’ve died.”

“Yes,” said Tavrin.

Nobody on the knoll said anything.

Oen was standing a little way behind her. He wasn’t looking at anybody. His ears were flat. He’d been crying, Niko thought, or something elves did instead.

“If we had held it,” said Tavrin, “it would be ours.”

Salty stared at her.

“If we had fought for it tonight,” said Tavrin. Slowly, in the kingdom’s tongue, so that there would be no mistake. “It would be held by us. And then, every spring, it would wait for us. And so would you. You would look south every spring, and wait.” She looked at the stone. “And we go home before your summer. To a forest past the end of your maps. And it would not be held.”

She was quiet a moment.

“It is held now,” said Tavrin, “by the ones who live beside it. Who will be here next spring. And the one after. And who know now that they can.” She looked at Salty. “That is the only holding that lasts. We have watched a great many things be held by the wrong hands, for a short time, very well. And then let go.”

“That’s a stupid rule,” said Salty.

Something happened at the corner of Tavrin’s mouth.

“Yes,” she said. “It is very old.” She looked at Garth, on his cloak by the tree, with Collum holding his hand. “If the boy had died, I would have sung him across myself. And walked home. And never forgiven myself. And still been right.” She bowed her head, a little, to Salty. To all of them. “It is not a good rule. It is only a true one. I am sorry it is so heavy.”

Salty looked at her for a long time.

“Everything’s heavy,” he said at last. “That’s fine. You just carry it.”

“Yes,” said Tavrin. “I have seen you.”

Oen carried Garth home.

Nobody asked him. When the commander came out from the fort an hour after sunrise, at a canter, with the rest of the garrison behind him and the cart and Pim with every horse in the stable on long strings, and pulled up at the foot of the knoll, and looked up at it, and couldn’t say anything at all; when the old men got up off the green and started lifting the wounded onto the cart, one by one, carefully, the way they’d lifted the wounded onto carts on this plain nine years ago and never thought they’d do again; Oen came through them, without a word, and went down on one knee by Garth’s cloak, and picked him up.

Like a cup off a table. Gently. The way he’d put Salty’s hand down on the anvil.

Garth woke up. He looked at the long grey face above him with its flat ears.

“I can walk,” said Garth.

“No,” said Oen. In the kingdom’s tongue. Badly. “I carry. It is allowed.”

And carried him. All the way. An hour, back along Aldous’s track between the markers, four hundred and eighty-one of them, Wendy said, with the recruit in his arms like a child, and his long legs going at Salty’s pace so that nobody had to hurry, and never once put him down.

The commander stood at the foot of the knoll for a long time.

Niko watched him from the gap. A careful young captain from Solmere, with a neat beard and a brass glass in a leather case at his belt, who’d stood on his wall all night with a hundred and ninety men waiting for a feint that never came, and watched a fire on a knoll an hour east go down, and come up, and go down. And at sunrise, Pim said later, he’d had the glass on the knoll when the note went out, and he’d heard it, all the way back at the fort, faint, like a bell from another village. And he’d seen the scouts turn. All of them at once. And he’d put the glass down and said saddle every horse we’ve got in a voice nobody had ever heard him use.

He looked at the three dead ones on the green. At the stone, standing. At the forty soldiers, every one of them, sitting and lying and standing on the knoll in the sun.

Then he put his hand on his chest.

He didn’t know you were supposed to. Nobody had told him. He’d seen the old men do it going out of his gate last night, and the old men do it on his wall every time a man with a rod went past it, and he’d never once done it, because he’d never believed a word.

He did it now. Flat. On his chest. And kept it there.

Teague, on the south side, on one knee, looked up and saw him do it. And didn’t say anything. And looked away, so as not to embarrass him.

Rhea came over last.

She came across the green on her own two feet, slowly, with her broken arm strapped across her chest and her sword in her other hand, dragging a little, and stood over Niko in his gap.

He looked up at her.

She’d said it at supper. The first year. In front of everyone. It’s a waste, lad. Both paths. Pick one. She’d said it more than once. She’d never said she was sorry. Rhea didn’t say sorry.

She looked at him for a long time.

“Again,” said Rhea.

Niko laughed. He couldn’t help it. It hurt his chest.

“Not today,” he said.

“No,” said Rhea. “Not today.” She sat down on the rock beside him, heavily, the way she’d sat down beside the third one. “Some day, though.”

“Some day.”

“I want to see it in the yard,” said Rhea. “Where I can watch it properly. I didn’t see a thing. I was lying on the grass.” She was quiet. “I heard it, though. All the way down.”

He wrote it at seven in the morning, sitting in the gap, with his back against the rock and the sun on his face.

He didn’t think about what to put for very long. He’d had two and a half years to think about it.

He closed the window.

He didn’t read what came after. He knew there’d be a lot. He could feel it, almost, the way you feel a phone buzzing in another room. Thirty-four people. Chris, logging. Maigo in capitals again, from the foot of the tree, ten feet away, with his face still wet. Tiger. Drew. Gigichi. Hana, somewhere in Solmere, sending it everywhere at once. He’d read it later. He’d read every line.

Not now.

He sat in the gap in the sun and shut his eyes.

He thought about the man on the jetty.

He couldn’t help it. A long way west. A month. On the end of a jetty, with a lamp gone out at his elbow and three hens and a rod across his knees, sitting up, probably, all night, the way all of them had. Reading. A number at a quarter to eight last night: 161. 4. And then nothing from the knoll for eleven hours, because nobody on it had had a hand free to write. Everybody else’s lines going past, all night. And nothing from the knoll. And him reading the nothing. Unable to do a single thing about it. Knowing, the whole time, that he couldn’t.

And then, at seven in the morning: 35.

He wouldn’t write. He never did. Niko knew that. That was all right.
