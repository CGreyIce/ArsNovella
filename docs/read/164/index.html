The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 6: Nine

Chapter 164

Seven Halves

6,791 words · 30 min read

Hana had always been the middle.

She’d been the middle on Earth. In a choir, in a friend group, in a Discord server with four hundred people in it and a channel called general that she’d kept alive for six years by being in it every night. Ice had met Jared through her, and Milli, and Aleaka. Cursiffe had come through Jared, through her. She’d been the door that people walked through to get to each other, and she’d loved it, the way you love a thing you’re good at without ever having had to try.

And then a hall in Solmere, and a window, and a word on its path line that she didn’t like the look of.

Charm.

The dangerous half. She’d known it the first week. She could make people like her. Not a little. A lot. She could walk into a room and put a hand on somebody’s arm and feel the thing in her go out of her fingers into them, warm and sweet, and watch their face soften toward her the way a flower turns toward a window. She could have had anything. She’d known that by the end of the first month, and it had frightened her so badly that she’d spent two years pushing it down. Under her ribs. Under knots and beads and ribbons, under charm-craft that kept her hands busy so the other kind of charm stayed quiet.

Two years of being the one in the room who didn’t reach for people.

And then, in Valcrest, at the long table in the Broken Lance, with Tiger watching and Oswin crying twice, she’d made seven pairs of knotted cords and given half of each to a guide, and put the other seven halves on a purple cord on her wrist, beside the half of a gold heart.

And she’d been the middle again.

Not the dangerous half. The other one. The one that didn’t make anyone do anything. The one that just let them talk.

She hadn’t known, until the halves started to warm, how much she’d missed it.

The first night home she didn’t sleep.

She lay in the narrow bed in the rooms Group 2 had in the castle’s east wing, with Milli asleep beside her with his tail over her ankle the way he always slept, and her wrist on the pillow in front of her face, and looked at the halves. Seven of them. Purple and gold, knotted, each with half a bead and a strip of thin blue paper wound round it. In a row on the cord, like charms on a bracelet. And beside them, on its own cord, half a gold heart.

None of them were warm.

She’d expected that. They’d been warm on and off on the road home, faintly, the way they were when the other half was being held. But nobody had written anything. The guides were all still on the road, or just home, or asleep. And the heart had been cool since Valcrest. Since a man by a horse trough had held his half of it in his fist for a minute at the end of the last night, and looked at her across the yard, and put it away in his coat beside a knife.

She couldn’t sleep anyway. She’d been in a vault. She’d watched a king put his hand on a seal and say please. She’d heard a dead king’s letter read out by lamplight, and watched her husband’s tail go so still it might have been carved, and then watched it move, once, at the tip, when Jared put a hand on his shoulder and said trust.

So she got up, and went to the window, and sat on the sill with her knees up and her tail round her feet, and looked out over the castle.

And there was a light at the top of the west tower.

She knew whose. Everybody did. A single lamp in a high narrow window, very small and very steady. The Advisor’s study. She’d been in it, once, in the first autumn, with Aleaka and Milli, and asked him plainly whether the Hero was their friend, without any charm at all. She remembered his desk. And his hand, moving toward a drawer on the left, and stopping before it got there.

I have a drawer, he’d said tonight, at the top of the vault stair. To the king. By his name.

She sat on the sill and watched the light in the west tower and didn’t think about the drawer. It wasn’t hers. She’d learned that, the first autumn: there were things you could ask for, plainly, with no charm, and there were things that weren’t yours, and the second kind you left alone, even when you could feel the shape of them from across a room.

The light stayed on.

All night. She watched it through every hour. Milli turned over in his sleep once and said Honey into the pillow and went back under. The castle bells rang the hours. The lamp in the west tower burned. At some point it rained, hard, for an hour, on the slates, and stopped.

And at dawn, in the grey, she heard the gate.

She leaned out of the window. Below, in the castle yard, the gate was open, and a rider was going out of it on a tall bay horse. One rider. Small, in a riding coat, bent low over the horse’s neck. Out of the gate at a canter, and then a gallop, down the long hill into the waking city.

East.

Somebody was standing in the yard watching the rider go. Hana saw her from above: tall and straight, in a guard’s coat, with a book under her arm. Kestra. Standing very still in the wet grey yard, looking east, the way she’d looked north for two years.

Kestra looked up. At Hana’s window. As if she’d known Hana would be there.

Hana didn’t call down. You didn’t call down to Kestra. She just raised one hand, a little.

Kestra looked at her for a long moment. Then she raised one hand back. And then she turned and went in, and the yard was empty, and the light at the top of the west tower went out.

Aldous’s half went warm on the second night.

She was at supper. In the long hall, at the end of the near table, with Group 2 round her and the castle household all down the tables, and Oswin at the far end with ink on his face and his eyes red from the vault, where he’d been since dawn with forty grey leaves and a stack of paper and a lamp. And her wrist went warm.

Not all of it. One half. The third along. She knew them by place: Maren first, then Oswin, then Aldous, then Emrys, Lucan, Corvin, and Garrick’s at the end, that Xav was carrying down a river. The third one. Warm. The warmth of a hand, as if somebody very far away had reached across a table and laid two fingers on her wrist.

She held it.

The strip of blue paper on the knot. The tiny letters on it went faint, and came back, and they weren’t the same letters. She held her breath. She’d tested the halves a hundred times, at the Lance, across a table, across a yard. Never this far. Never from the edge of the world.

Four words. In English. In Cursiffe’s ink, coming up out of nothing on the paper on her wrist, in the hand she’d taught the cords to write in, which was her own.

Arrived. He looked up.

She read it twice. She didn’t understand it.

Arrived: yes. Cinderhold. Group 3 had been due, by Aldous’s last letter from the road, about now. Twenty days from Valcrest. Arrived. Group 3, and Aldous, and Rhea, and a man with two buckets, and his daughter.

He looked up.

She didn’t know what it meant. She sat at the end of the long table with her wrist in her lap and read it a third time. He looked up. At what? Looked up from what? It was Aldous. Aldous didn’t waste words; he was the most careful man Hana had ever met. He’d had four words and he’d used two of them on this.

“What is it?” said Milli, beside her.

She showed him.

Milli read it. He couldn’t read the knot’s English any better than she could read the guides’ script; but he could read her face, and the tiny English on the strip, and he looked at the four words for a long time with his calm cat’s eyes.

“I don’t know,” he said. “But it’s good.”

“How do you know?”

“Because Aldous wrote it,” said Milli. “And Aldous wouldn’t waste a word on anything that wasn’t.” He gave the wrist back. “Somebody here will know.”

She asked Kestra, after supper. Kestra hadn’t been at the Redmarch; Kestra had been at the other end of the kingdom when it happened. She read the four words and shook her head and said I don’t know, but I’ll put it in the book, and wrote it in the honest book under the day’s date, arrived, he looked up, as if that would make it make sense.

It didn’t, yet.

Maren’s half went warm on the fourth night. One word.

Home.

Hana sat on the sill of her window in the dark with her wrist in her lap and laughed out loud, and Milli woke up and said what and she said Maren and he said oh and went back to sleep, smiling. Group 1 was home. Wrenhaven. The militia hall at the top of the lake road. Five girls who’d waited at the top of a road for twenty months, home.

She wrote back. Welcome home. And held it. And after a moment, Maren’s answer came up on the strip, in Hana’s own English, from Maren’s small exact hand a month’s ride away:

The basket is on the step. Empty. Nobody touches it.

Hana didn’t know what that meant either. She thought she might. She didn’t ask.

She broke the channel on the fifth night.

She’d never forget it. She’d tried, afterwards, for weeks, the way you try to forget the sound of a glass breaking in a quiet room. She’d been so pleased. That was the worst part. She’d been so pleased with herself.

The king had said she could tell them. At his table, after supper, with his sleeves rolled up and soot on his forearms from the castle forge, where he’d been every night since the vault: tell the seven. About the door. They should know. And Hana had gone up to her room, glowing, and sat on the bed, and looked at the seven halves on her wrist, and thought: all at once. I’ll tell them all at once. The same moment. All seven. Because she could. Because the middle could do that: speak once, and have it go out everywhere at the same time, to everyone who needed it.

She’d written it on all seven strips. Carefully. In English. Four words, seven times.

The door is open.

And held all seven at once.

And somewhere very far away, in a channel she’d been in every night for two years, thirty-five names went grey.

She hadn’t known. She’d felt the halves go hot, all seven, much hotter than they’d ever gone, too hot, and then she’d felt something on the other end of them that wasn’t a guide. Something enormous. Something slow and old and turning, that reached for the seven little cords on her wrist the way a river reaches for a twig. And she’d been frightened, and hadn’t known what she was frightened of, and hadn’t let go, because she didn’t know how.

And then Milli had come in from the corridor with his window open in his hand, white, saying Hana, the channel, the channel’s going, and she’d opened hers and seen it.

Lines going off the top. Ten at a time. Names flickering. And at the bottom, over and over, seven lines with no names at all.

: The door is open.

: The door is open.

: The door is open.

Her words. With nobody’s name in front of them. Breaking the thing that held all thirty-five of them together.

She’d sat on the bed with her wrist burning and her window open and watched it break, and couldn’t stop it, and couldn’t let go, and thought: I did this. I did this. I’m the middle and I broke it—

And then, very far away, on the other end of everything, somebody had held it.

She’d felt that too. Through the cords. Through all seven, at once. A pair of hands, very steady, taking hold of the enormous slow turning thing and the seven little cords at the same moment, and holding them both still. Like somebody steadying a ladder.

And a word.

It came up on all seven strips at once. On her wrist. On every half. In the middle of all the heat and the turning and the breaking, very small, very plain, in lowercase, in English.

hello

And the heat went out of the halves. And the turning thing let go. And in the channel, the names came back.

Fixed, Thea wrote, a few minutes later. The cords and the channel were talking over each other. They’ve been introduced now. Hana, write as much as you like.

And then: Lantern.

Hana had sat on the bed and read it and put her face in her hands.

She wrote, eventually, when she could. thank you. I’m sorry. Thank you. And then she sat there for a long time with Milli’s arm round her and her wrist cool again in her lap, and looked at the seven halves, and the word on every one of them, under the door is open. hello. In the hand of a girl with pink and violet hair, a month’s ride away, on the end of a jetty, who’d held the whole thing still with her bare hands and said hello to it.

“I nearly broke everything,” said Hana, into her hands.

“You didn’t,” said Milli.

“I nearly—“

“You made something nobody’s ever made,” said Milli. “And it touched something nobody’s ever touched. And Thea introduced them.” His voice was very calm. It always was. “That’s not breaking things, Honey. That’s what happens when there are two things in the world that didn’t know about each other, and then there aren’t.” He was quiet. “It’s a plan. It’s a real plan.”

She laughed, wet, into her hands. He’d been saying that since the first day. It was the stupidest thing she’d ever loved anybody for.

After that, they worked.

Perfectly. Thea had been right. Hana wrote on one strip, and held it, and the words went, and nothing broke anywhere, and the channel ran smooth and steady with its twenty-five lines and then forty and then two hundred again, as if nothing had ever happened. And the halves went warm, one at a time, from every corner of the kingdom, at every hour, and Hana sat at the end of the long table or on the sill of her window or in the archive with Oswin and read them.

Garrick: Back. All six. Counted. The Hollow is very quiet.

Lucan: NEXT MIDSUMMER. ALL THIRTY-FIVE. I HAVE ALREADY ORDERED THE PAPER. Ysolde says I may not order the paper. I have ordered the paper.

Aldous: Rhea held him one count. The fort is in good order.

She didn’t know what that meant either. She laughed anyway.

Oswin, from three floors down, from the vault, on the second day of trying it, because he’d been dying to: Leaf one. Both sides. I made four mistakes and started again. Thirty-nine to go. And Hana wrote back you’re in the same castle and Oswin wrote I know. I wanted to see if it worked from underground. And it did.

Maren: Thea is well. She has slept for a day and a half. Gigichi stood at her door the whole time with a lamp.

Emrys: An apple on Maud’s step this morning. Placed. Rachael threw it in the river. She is well. We are watching.

Hana read that one at midnight, and her whole tail went stiff. And then she wrote back, before she could think about it, the only thing that seemed right: Thank you. Tell her it was a terrible apple.

And she passed it all on.

That was the part nobody had asked her to do. Nobody had written it on a list. The Advisor had said, when he gave the guides their halves, tell Hanarisu anything that bears on the summoned, and she’ll know where it needs to go. He hadn’t said what that meant. Hana found out by doing it.

Every evening, she went to the king. At the castle forge, mostly, where he stood at the anvil in his shirtsleeves with soot to the elbow, making things that didn’t need making so his hands had something to do while he thought. She’d sit on the step of the forge and tell him. Everything. The basket on the step. Rhea’s one count. The apple. And the king would listen with the hammer in his hand and not strike, and nod, and say good, or hm, or once, about the apple, a word Hana didn’t know and Kestra afterwards declined to translate.

And every evening, the parts that were fit, the channel. Not the guides’ words; those weren’t hers. The shape of them. Aldous says Cinderhold’s well. Maren says the basket’s on the step. Emrys says Rachael’s all right.

And the guides to each other.

She hadn’t expected that. She’d thought the halves would be seven lines, from her to them and back, like spokes on a wheel. But on the third day Maren wrote how is Aldous? I have not had a letter from him since Valcrest, and Hana wrote he says the fort is in good order and somebody called Rhea held somebody for one count, and Maren wrote back ah, as if that explained everything. And the next day Lucan asked after Emrys, and Emrys asked after Garrick, and Garrick asked after Corvin, because Garrick had been a raid leader for twenty years and couldn’t stop counting his people, even when they weren’t his.

Seven people, all over the kingdom, who’d been writing letters by rider for two years. Who’d trained together under the Advisor, once, a long time ago, and been sent to seven corners of the map and never once all been in a room since. Asking after each other.

Through her.

She sat on the sill of her window on the tenth night with her wrist in her lap and seven warm halves on it and understood what she was. Not a door any more. A middle. The thing in the middle of a wheel that all the spokes go into, that doesn’t move, that holds the whole thing together by staying still.

The safe half.

She’d been afraid, for two years, that she only had the other one.

Corvin’s half went warm on the hundred-and-fifth day, at dusk.

The rings are full.

Hana went cold all down her back.

She wrote back at once. Thank you. I’m here. Tell me everything. And he did. A few words at a time, from the top of a tower in the west, eleven miles from a pass. The green coat. The hat man. The woman from the fountain. Thirty-nine, one at a time, over eleven days. The last gap filled. The absence has turned. It faces the pass. Hyvred says: it is ready.

She took it to the king that night. She sat on the step of the forge and read it to him, all of it, and he didn’t strike once. He stood at the anvil with the hammer hanging from his hand and looked at the fire until she’d finished.

“Ready,” he said.

“That’s what she said.”

The king was quiet for a long time.

“Tell Corvin,” he said eventually, “that the Western Watch will be doubled again by the first snow. And that I’ll come myself in the spring.” He put the hammer down on the anvil. “And tell him he’s not on his own out there any more.”

She told him. Corvin’s half went warm again almost at once, as if he’d been holding it, waiting, on top of a tower in the dark. He didn’t write anything back for a while. And then just: thank you.

Six nights later, Corvin wrote again.

At midnight. Hana was asleep, for once. The half went so hot on her wrist that it woke her, and she sat up in the dark with her heart going, and read it by the light of her own window.

Drew stood at the window and turned his face to the pass.

She read all of it. Sitting up in bed in the dark, with Milli awake now beside her, reading over her shoulder. The wind that was calling. The sentries. The dogs. Drew. The wrong light round him, faint, like frost. Kei’s cup that wasn’t enough.

Then he wrote a word into it and it was.

Kei has reached Spark. The window says Dose.

Drew is well. He does not remember. Tiger has not let go of him.

And the last part, very small. And we have a dose. One. For one man. For now.

Hana read it three times.

Then she got up, in her nightgown, and put Milli’s coat on over it because it was nearest, and went down through the sleeping castle to the forge, where there was a light, because there was always a light in the forge now. And the king was there, at midnight, sitting on an upturned bucket by the dead fire with a cup of something in his hands, not sleeping either. She read it to him.

He listened. He didn’t say anything until she’d finished. Then he put the cup down.

“Calling,” he said.

“That’s what Hisui said.”

“The one who keeps everyone going,” said the king. “That’s who it called first.” He looked at the dead fire for a long time. “Of course it was.”

He stood up.

“Tell Corvin,” he said. “Tell Corvin I’m sending every healer in Solmere who can be spared, and every apothecary, and every book on poisons in the archive, west, tomorrow, to the boy with the snake. Whatever he needs. Whatever he asks for.” He was quiet. “And tell the boy with the snake—“ He stopped.

“What?”

“Tell him well done,” said the king. “From a smith.” He looked at Hana. “And tell Corvin he’s not on his own out there for one more day. Not one.”

Hana went back up through the castle and sat on the sill of her window and wrote it all, and held it, and Corvin’s answer came up on the strip, and she sat there reading it with her eyes wet and Milli’s coat round her.

And at some point, sitting there, she realised that her wrist was warm in a different place.

The heart.

The half of a gold heart, on its own purple cord, beside the seven. It had been cool since Valcrest. Since the horse trough.

It was warm now.

Not the quick warmth of a half being picked up and put down. A long, steady, patient warmth. The warmth of something being held, in somebody’s closed hand, and not let go of.

Hana sat very still on the sill with her other hand over it.

She knew whose hand. There was only one. She’d made the heart in the winter and cut it in two and given one half to Kestra, if you ever go north again, give him this half. And Kestra had carried it in the honest book for ten months and given it to him at the north gate of Valcrest, in the mist, perhaps anyway. He’d held it for a minute at the end of the last night, by the horse trough. And put it in his coat beside the good knife.

He was holding it now.

At Cinderhold. On the edge of the red. In the middle of the night.

She sat on the sill and held her own half and felt his warm under her fingers, steady, not letting go, and didn’t know why. He’d never held it this long. Not even for a minute, since Valcrest. She sat there and felt it, and thought about a man on the edge of the red with something in his closed fist in the middle of the night, holding on.

It was still warm at dawn.

She took Corvin’s report to the Advisor in the morning.

She took it every morning, the reports from the halves, written out fair on a sheet so the Advisor could keep them. Usually she gave it to the clerk at the bottom of the west tower and went away. This morning she went up. She didn’t know why. The heart on her wrist had gone cool at last, about an hour after dawn, the way a hand goes cool when somebody finally puts down the thing they’ve been holding, and she’d sat and looked at it and then got up and gone to the west tower.

Aleaka came with her.

Aleaka always came, these days, when Hana went anywhere she thought she might need somebody. She came with Brisk on her bare arm, the way Brisk went everywhere: a furious bark-coloured hawk with a bent primary feather and a yellow eye that hated everyone in the world except the woman whose arm he was on. Aleaka had found him in the castle mews in the first autumn, in a cage, half starved and trying to kill anyone who came near, and opened the cage door. And he’d flown out. And come back. And never once since then sat on anything but her bare arm.

He’d never once done anything she asked him, either. She didn’t ask. That was the whole thing, Aleaka said. You don’t ask a hawk. You wait for him to decide.

They went up a hundred and four stairs.

The Advisor’s door was open.

He was at his desk. Hana had never seen him look the way he looked. He was always grey and thin and very straight, buttoned to the neck, a man who looked as if he’d been put away in a drawer at night and taken out in the morning exactly as he’d been left. He wasn’t buttoned. He was in his shirtsleeves, at nine in the morning, at his desk, with his hands lying open on the blotter in front of him, palms up, as if he’d put something down and forgotten to close them.

And the bottom drawer on the left was standing open.

Hana saw it the moment she came in. She didn’t mean to look. She looked away at once. But she’d seen it: open, all the way, the way a drawer is open when someone has stopped bothering to shut it. Full of paper. Old letters, unsealed, laid in it in order. A strip of black ribbon. A small folded square on top, in a different hand.

The Advisor looked up.

“Hanarisu,” he said. His voice was quiet. It was always quiet. It wasn’t always tired. “And Lady Aleaka. Come in.”

Hana came in and put the fair copy of Corvin’s report on the corner of the desk. He looked at it. He didn’t pick it up.

“I’ve read it,” he said. “The king came up at dawn and told me. All of it.” He looked at the sheet. “Thank you.”

She should have gone. She didn’t. She stood by the desk with Aleaka beside her and Brisk on Aleaka’s arm, glaring at the Advisor with his yellow eye, and found that there was a thing she wanted to ask, and that it might be hers to ask after all.

“My lord,” she said. “Can I ask you something? Plainly.”

The Advisor looked at her. Something in his thin face moved, very slightly. He remembered, she thought. The first autumn. I’m asking you plainly.

“Ask,” he said.

She held out her wrist. The third half. And turned the strip of blue paper on it so he could see, though he couldn’t read the English; she said it for him.

“Aldous wrote to me,” she said. “Three weeks ago. The night they reached Cinderhold. Four words. Arrived. He looked up.” She looked at him. “Nobody here knows what it means. Kestra doesn’t. Milli says it’s good. I think it’s good.” She paused. “I think you know.”

The Advisor didn’t say anything for a long time.

“The morning after,” he said, eventually.

Hana waited.

“The Redmarch. The morning after.” He was looking at his open hands on the blotter. “He came back to the fort from the rise on foot, alone, with the black sword on his shoulder. Across the red. And the whole garrison was on the wall, every man still standing. And somebody put his hand on his chest. Like this.” He did it. Flat. Over his heart. “And then everyone. All the way along the wall. Hand on chest. While he walked to the gate.” He was quiet. “I was on the wall. I did it too. So did the king.”

He took his hand away.

“He didn’t look up,” said the Advisor. “Not once. He walked in under the gate looking at the ground. And an hour later he walked out again with a spade, and none of us—“ He stopped. “None of us could make him look up. Nine years.”

Hana didn’t say anything.

“He looked up,” said the Advisor. Very quietly. He said it as if he were reading it off the strip, though he couldn’t read it. “Aldous doesn’t waste words. If Aldous wrote that, then three weeks ago he walked in under that gate again. And this time—“ He stopped again. He put his hand over his eyes.

Hana stood very still.

She’d never seen the Advisor do anything. Not once, in two years. He was the thin grey man who’d pulled thirty-five people out of the sky. He didn’t put his hand over his eyes.

“Thank you,” he said, behind his hand. “For asking. Plainly.”

Aleaka stepped forward.

Hana felt her do it. Aleaka was slow and careful, always; she stumbled over words and took a long time to decide things and then decided them all at once. She’d been standing beside Hana with Brisk on her arm, very still, looking at the Advisor with her round gold glasses and her fox ears up. Doing the thing she did. Hana knew the look. Aleaka felt what people carried. She’d felt it in the hall, the first day, in every one of the thirty-five. She’d felt it in the Advisor, the first autumn, in this room: a sealed thing, heavy, in a drawer.

“You’re not carrying it,” said Aleaka. Slowly.

The Advisor took his hand away from his eyes.

“The thing in the drawer,” said Aleaka. She was looking at him, not at the drawer. “I felt it. The first autumn. In here. You had something you’d been carrying for years. It was so heavy.” She stumbled on the next word and said it again. “So heavy.” She was quiet. “It’s gone. You put it down.”

“Yes,” said the Advisor.

“And now you don’t know what to do with your hands,” said Aleaka.

The Advisor looked at his hands. Lying open on the blotter, palms up. He looked at them for a long time, as if he’d never seen them before.

“No,” he said. “I don’t.”

Hana saw Aleaka look at Brisk.

Brisk was on her bare forearm, gripping, the way he always gripped, hard enough to mark. His yellow eye was on the Advisor. Glaring. The way he glared at everyone in the world except the woman whose arm he was on.

Aleaka had never asked him for anything. Two years. You don’t ask a hawk. She’d waited for him to decide, every time, and he’d decided, every time, to stay exactly where he was and hate everyone.

She lifted her other hand.

Not to Brisk. To the air. The way they all did, the way Hana had written remember on a knot in the winter and felt the halves go warm. Aleaka put one finger to the air beside the hawk, slowly, carefully, and wrote, in her round British hand, in English, one word.

Please.

Brisk turned his head.

He looked at Aleaka. Very close. With his yellow eye, an inch from her face. Hana watched them look at each other: a furious bark-coloured hawk with a bent primary and a fox girl with round glasses, who’d opened a cage door once and waited two years.

And something went between them.

Hana saw it. She’d never seen anything go between people before, not like this. Not the charm; the charm went one way, out of you, into someone, and they bent to you whether they wanted to or not. This went both ways. A warmth, faint and gold, going out from Aleaka’s raised finger to the hawk, and coming back from the hawk to her. Not a command. Not a charm. An agreement. The way two people agree to something across a table without saying anything, just by looking.

Brisk let go of Aleaka’s arm.

He went up off it with a crack of wings that filled the study, across the room, over the desk, over the open drawer full of letters. And the Advisor, who had been a court mage at a border garrison once and had spent a year in the mews there in his youth before anybody knew he was any good at anything, lifted his right forearm off the blotter without thinking, the way a falconer lifts his arm, palm down, fist closed.

And Brisk landed on it.

Nobody moved.

The hawk sat on the Advisor’s forearm. Gripping. Hard enough to mark through his shirtsleeve. His wings half spread for balance, and then folding, slowly, and settling. And his yellow eye, an inch from the Advisor’s thin grey face, looking at him.

Not glaring.

Hana had never once seen Brisk not glare at anyone but Aleaka. He wasn’t glaring. He was looking at the Advisor the way he looked at Aleaka, in the mornings, on her arm, on the castle wall: fierce, and watchful, and entirely at home.

The Advisor sat at his desk with a hawk on his arm and looked at it.

His face did something Hana had never seen it do. Not the hand over the eyes. Something else. Something that started at the corners of his mouth and went all the way up into his tired eyes and stayed there.

“Oh,” said the Advisor. Very quietly. To the hawk. “Oh, look at you.”

He laughed.

It wasn’t much. A short sound. Startled. The sound of a man who’d forgotten he could make it. But Hana heard it, and Aleaka heard it, and the hawk heard it and didn’t fly away, and somewhere at the bottom of the west tower, Hana would find out later, the clerk heard it, and stopped writing, and looked up the stairs, and didn’t know what it was, because he’d worked for the Advisor for eleven years and never once heard it before.

Aleaka’s window had opened.

Hana saw it beside her. Plain and white, in front of Aleaka’s round gold glasses, while Aleaka stood with her finger still raised and her ears straight up and her face doing what faces do when they’ve waited a long time for something and stopped expecting it.

[ Aleaka · Tier 1: Spark · Skills: Accord ]

Aleaka looked at it.

“Oh,” she said. Slowly. “Oh. That’s—“ She stumbled. “That’s the word. That’s the right word. That’s what it is.” She looked at Brisk on the Advisor’s arm. “I didn’t tell him to. I asked. And he—“ She stopped again. “He agreed.”

“Twenty-eight,” said Hana. She didn’t know why she said it out loud. It just came out. Chris would want to know. “Aleaka. That’s twenty-eight.”

Aleaka turned and looked at her, with her window still open and her eyes wet behind her glasses.

“Hana,” she said. “I was first. Remember. You said I’d be first.”

“You were first,” said Hana. “When I was sure. You were the first one I told.” She was laughing. She didn’t know when she’d started. “This isn’t that. You can’t be first at this. Twenty-seven people beat you.”

“I know,” said Aleaka. “I don’t care.” And she laughed too, the low slow British laugh she’d had on every call for a year, the one Ice used to tease her about, the one that made him call her cute until she told him off. And she put her hand over her mouth, and the window hung in front of her face, and across the room a furious hawk sat on the arm of the Advisor of the kingdom and looked at her, and didn’t come back. Not yet.

He’d come back. Hana knew he would. He’d decided.

But not yet.

They left him there.

The Advisor, at his desk, in his shirtsleeves, with the drawer open beside him and a hawk on his arm. He didn’t ask them to take Brisk back. He didn’t seem to have thought of it. He was sitting very still, with his forearm up, looking at the hawk, and the hawk was looking at him, and Hana thought, going down the hundred and four stairs with Aleaka beside her not able to stop laughing, that she had never seen anybody in her life look less like they’d been put away in a drawer.

At the bottom of the tower, the clerk was standing in the doorway of his little room with his pen still in his hand.

“Was that,” he said. “Was that the Advisor? Laughing?”

“No,” said Aleaka. “That was a hawk.”

She wrote it to everyone that night.

All seven. One at a time, this time; she’d learned. Maren first. Then Oswin, three floors down. Then Aldous. Then Emrys, Lucan, Corvin, Garrick.

Aleaka reached Spark this morning. Twenty-eight. The window says Accord. She asked Brisk to sit on the Advisor’s arm and he agreed.

And they came back, one at a time, all evening, from every corner of the kingdom. Maren: Good. Oswin: Oh, she would. Lucan, in capitals. Garrick: Twenty-eight. Counted. And Corvin, from a tower in the west, eleven miles from a pass: Tell her well done. Tell her a boy with a snake says well done too.

And Aldous, last, from the edge of the red, very late, a few words:

Tell the Advisor: he answered.

Hana read it.

She sat on the sill of her window with her wrist in her lap and read it four times. He answered. She didn’t know what it meant. She thought, this time, that she did. A light at the top of a west tower all night. A rider going out of the gate at dawn, east, three weeks ago. And last night, at Cinderhold, a man holding half a gold heart in his closed fist until morning.

She got up, and went along the corridor, and down the stairs, and across the dark yard, and up a hundred and four more. The Advisor’s door was open. His lamp was lit. He was at his desk, and the hawk was gone, back to Aleaka’s window at dusk, the way she’d known he’d go. And the drawer was still open.

He looked up.

“Aldous says to tell you,” said Hana, “he answered.”

The Advisor didn’t say anything.

He sat at his desk under the lamp with his hands open on the blotter and looked at her, and his face didn’t do anything, for a long time, and then it did. And Hana, who’d been the middle on Earth and the middle here and had carried a great many words for a great many people across a great many rooms, said nothing else, because there was nothing else to carry. She’d brought it. It was his.

She went back down the hundred and four stairs.

Behind her, at the top of the west tower, the light stayed on. Not all night, this time. Only for an hour or so. And then it went out, quite early, the way a light goes out when somebody finally goes to sleep.
