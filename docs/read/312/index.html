The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 11

Chapter 312

Well

4,857 words · 21 min read

Xolalotl had given Gigi frog hands, and she was never, ever going to be allowed to forget it.

It had been an accident. It had been a kind accident; that was the worst part. Gigi had come home from Odile’s yard on the second night of the grey with her hands scratched to the wrists from tearing her own thorny chairs out of the ground, all those long red lines going up her forearms like somebody had drawn on her with a pen, and sat on the edge of her cot in the militia hall loft not letting anyone look at them. And Xola, who couldn’t stand it when people didn’t let you look at them, had sat down beside her and said give me your hands in her best voice, the one that nobody had ever said no to except Rachael, and Gigi had given her them.

And Xola had done what she always did.

Tide. Water, the thing she’d had since the winter before last, since the night the militia hall’s well froze and she’d put her hand on the ice and felt the water underneath it lean toward her, the way a cat leans into your hand. It had been all she could do for the first year of it: lean water about. Lift it. Push it. She could fill a bucket from the lake without carrying it, and make a wave in a washtub that soaked Higu to the knees, and once, memorably, make the whole of the militia hall’s rain barrel stand up out of itself in a column and fall over on Bram.

And she healed. Not the way Rachael healed, or Odile. Her own body did it; it always had, here, since the hall: she’d cut her thumb to the bone on a fish knife in the first spring and watched it close and pink and be a thumb again by morning, and Maren had made her do it twice more to be sure, and gone very quiet, and written it down. Axolotls grew things back. She’d made jokes about it for three years. And sometimes, if she pushed, she could push a little of it out of herself, down her own water and into somebody else’s: a cut on a child’s hand on the hill above the town, closed in a breath. She’d done it a dozen times. She’d been very proud of it.

So she’d taken Gigi’s scratched hands in hers, and pushed.

And Gigi’s hands had swelled up like gloves.

Not a little. Like gloves somebody had blown into. Purple. Shiny. Every scratch closed, all at once, beautifully, and every finger the size of a sausage, and the backs of both hands puffed up so tight that Gigi couldn’t bend them, and she’d sat on the cot holding them out in front of her in the lamplight and stared at them with a look of the purest horror that Xola had ever seen on a human face.

“I look,” Gigi had said, very slowly, “like a frog.”

“You look like a toad,” Higu had said, from the other cot. “Frogs are thinner.”

“I’m an axolotl,” Xola had said, mortified, with her nose bleeding onto her own lap, which it did when she pushed too hard. “I’m allowed to say frog. You’re not allowed to say frog. It’s practically a slur—“

It had taken two days to go down. Gigi had worn mittens. Gigi had said frog hands to her at breakfast every morning for a week, and then, worse, stopped saying it and just looked at her, which was so much worse. Xola had cried about it once, on the jetty, where nobody could see her except the man on the last plank, who’d been very carefully looking at the water.

She’d pushed too much. She’d pushed her own regrowing into somebody whose hands didn’t want regrowing, they only wanted the scratches closed; and all the extra had gone into the hands and had nowhere to go, and swelled.

She’d thought that was the lesson. Push less.

It wasn’t.

Maren’s hands were worse.

Everybody knew. Nobody said. It had been nineteen days since the night in Odile’s back room when Maren burned a word out of a little girl’s arm, and Maren had walked up the hill afterwards with both hands in a bucket of cold water and slept fourteen hours and then got up and gone back to work as if nothing had happened, because that was Maren. She wrote her reports. She read with Thea at the long table in the evenings. She held her pen.

But she held it badly. She held it between the first two fingers of her right hand, stiffly, the way you’d hold it if the palm didn’t bend; and she held her cup in both hands, carefully, by the very ends of her fingers; and she wore linen to the wrists, under her cuffs, and changed it every morning in the back room with the door shut, and came out with her face set very hard.

Eleven times. Xola had heard Thea say it in the channel the night it happened, and hadn’t been able to stop thinking about it since. Maren had burned her own name off the backs of her own hands eleven times in an afternoon, on purpose, learning to aim. And then she’d held a flame steady over a child’s arm for a quarter of an hour with the blisters breaking under her bandages. And then she’d said thank you to Thea, and gone to bed.

Odile mended them. Every second day. And Odile, who’d mended everything in Wrenhaven for thirty years, came out of the back room after the fourth time with her soft old face troubled and said to Thea, where Xola could hear, it’s slow. Her own fire. It doesn’t want to come back from its own fire. I don’t know why.

And Maren said nothing, and held her pen between two fingers, and wrote.

The second hook came on the nineteenth day.

A boy at the weir below the town, where the lake went out into the Wren between the mill-stones; the miller’s youngest, Davy Marsh, nine, who fished the pool under the sluice every evening with a line on a stick. A man on the far bank had thrown him a hook across the water. A good one, bright, for nothing. Davy had caught it in his hand, because he was nine and somebody had thrown him something, and the barb had gone in.

Bram brought him up the hill himself, at a run, with the boy over his shoulder and his face like a shut door.

And this time everybody knew what to do. Nobody closed it. Odile put the boy in the back room and sent for Thea and Maren and didn’t put the chair against the door, because they knew, now. They knew what it was and what it needed. Thea read it in an hour: the same word, an emptying word, very small, ten thousand times, in a line round the cut like a stitch, a hair wide. Only at the edges yet. Only a day.

“It’s early,” said Thea. “It’s only the edges. It’ll come out easily. A few heartbeats.”

Maren held her hands out over the boy’s palm, the way she had over Lissa’s arm, and read her flame.

Xola was in the doorway. She saw it happen. She saw Maren say the first five words under her breath, wake, shape, here, the two Lantern words that held; and the heat came, a small flame, a seed, on the pad of her right finger. And then Maren’s hand shook.

Not much. A tremor. The burned palm under the linen, cramping, the way a hand cramps when you’ve held a pen too long. And the flame on the end of her finger shook with it, and the seventh word, the one that said what, the one Maren had spent eleven burns finding, went. Xola saw it go. She saw Maren’s face as she lost it: not frightened. Furious. The flame wavered between meaning that and meaning there and couldn’t decide, and Maren put it out between her finger and thumb before it could touch anything at all, and stood there.

“I can’t hold it,” she said. Very quietly. Very formally. As if she were reporting the price of grain. “The hand won’t hold. I’ll burn him.”

Nobody said anything.

“Tomorrow,” said Maren. “When it’s better. It’s only the edges. Thea, it’ll keep till tomorrow.”

“It’ll keep till tomorrow,” Thea agreed, in her flat frightened voice.

But Maren’s hands wouldn’t be better tomorrow. Xola could see that. Everybody could see that. They’d been nineteen days not getting better.

She thought about it all night.

She lay on her cot in the loft with Squiddles asleep on her head, which he did when he was worried about her, and Gigi asleep on the next cot with her un-frog hands folded on her chest, and thought about water.

She’d been thinking about water since the winter before last and she’d always thought about it the same way: as something you moved. That was what Tide was. The water leaned toward her and she pushed it about. Bucket, washtub, rain barrel, Bram. And her own body’s water, when she healed: she’d always thought of that the same way, too. Something she had, and pushed. Out of her. Into somebody else.

And it had gone into Gigi’s hands and had nowhere to go.

She thought about Gigi’s chairs. The thorny ones, nine mornings of them, that had come up because Gigi was trying to make a chair for nobody. And then the seat by Odile’s door, that had come up out of the ground the shape of where Ada Fowler wanted to be. It’s not me deciding, Gigi had written. It comes up the shape of where they want to be. I just have to be sitting nearby and not trying.

She thought about Maren’s seventh word. Not where. What.

She thought about Rachael, in Kingsferry, writing in the channel the night Lissa’s arm came clean: that’s what Maud says. You don’t make anything. You help what’s already there do what it’s already trying to do.

Xola lay in the dark and thought: Gigi’s hands were already trying to do something. They were trying to close the scratches. That’s all they wanted. And I pushed my whole regrowing into them like they wanted to grow new hands.

She hadn’t listened to them. She’d never once listened to anybody’s water. She’d only ever listened to her own, and pushed.

She got up very quietly, so as not to wake Gigi, with Squiddles clinging to her hair and making a small disgruntled noise, and went down the ladder and out of the militia hall into the warm dark, and down the lake road to the shingle by the cabin, and took her boots off, and walked into the lake up to her knees.

The lake at night was the best thing she knew.

She’d never told anybody that. It sounded stupid. But since the first winter, since the frozen well, water had leaned toward her wherever she was, and the lake was the most water there was, and it leaned toward her all at once, the whole of it, miles of it, cold and dark and patient, the way a very big dog leans against your legs.

She stood in it up to her knees and didn’t push.

She’d never done that. Not once. She’d always come here to do something: to lift a wave, to make the shallows stand up, to show off to Gigi on the shingle. She stood still in the lake in the dark with her hands at her sides and her gills open in the night air, and let the water lean, and didn’t do anything about it at all.

It took a long time. She wasn’t good at not doing things. She stood there till her feet went numb and Squiddles had climbed down her hair onto her shoulder to look at the water, and she nearly gave up three times.

And then she felt it.

Not the leaning. The other thing. Under the leaning, the lake was going. Not toward her. Its own way. In, along the shingle, a hair, and out again; in, and out. A tide. Not the big tides of a sea; a tiny one, a lake’s, a breath. The whole lake breathing, in and out, slowly, along every shore it had, the way it had been breathing every night for a thousand years without anybody once noticing. And it didn’t need her. It wasn’t leaning toward her to be pushed. It was just doing what it did. It had always been doing it. She’d been standing in it for a year and a half shouting.

She stood very still and listened to the lake breathe.

She went to Maren at first light.

Maren was in the yard of the militia hall, at the well, changing the linen on her hands, alone, with her back to the door so nobody would see. She heard Xola’s bare wet feet on the cobbles and didn’t turn round.

“Not now, Xolalotl.”

“Yes now,” said Xola. “Please. Come to the lake.”

Maren did turn round, then. She stood by the well with the old linen in one hand and the other held against her chest, and Xola saw the palm.

It was raw. Not blistered; past blistered. The skin had come off and grown back and come off again, eleven times and a twelfth, and what was there now was red and shiny and tight, cracked in the creases, weeping a little at the base of the thumb. It didn’t look like a hand that was healing. It looked like a hand that had decided to stay exactly as it was.

“Odile says it doesn’t want to come back from its own fire,” said Xola.

“Odile is correct,” said Maren.

“Can I try?”

Maren looked at her for a long time. Xola knew what she was seeing: the girl with the gills and the squid who’d given Gigi frog hands and cried about it on the jetty. Maren knew everything that happened in Wrenhaven. Maren had probably written it down.

“You pushed Gigichi’s,” said Maren.

“I know.”

“Her hands swelled for two days.”

“I know. I pushed. I’m not going to push.” Xola held out her own hands, open. “I’m going to listen. I didn’t know how till last night. I stood in the lake for an hour and didn’t do anything, and it was the hardest thing I’ve ever done, and I think I know how now.” She swallowed. “Please. Davy’s grey is at the edges. It’s only a day. And you need your hand to hold the seventh word. And Odile says it won’t come back, and I think it will if I stop telling it what to do.”

Maren was quiet a long moment.

“The lake,” she said.

“The lake’s the most water there is. And it’s mine. Sort of. It leans.” Xola went red. “I can hear it better there.”

Maren put the old linen down on the well’s edge. She didn’t put the new on.

“Very well,” she said.

They went down the lake road in the early light, the two of them, past Gigi’s bench, which was empty, and down onto the shingle by the cabin, where the man on the jetty was already out on the last plank with the old rod and the hook on it.

He looked round when they came down the shingle. He saw Maren’s hands, uncovered. He saw Xola’s bare feet and her face. He didn’t say anything; he never did. He turned back to the water.

And on the step of the cabin, with Astra across her knees and a pack beside her half-filled for a long road south, Alea looked up.

Xola didn’t look at her. She couldn’t, quite. She walked down to the water’s edge with Maren beside her and knelt in the shallows, in the cold, on the stones, with the lake round her knees, and held up her hands.

“In the water,” she said. “Both of them. Under. It’s cold. Sorry.”

Maren knelt beside her, stiffly, in her good grey skirt, which was going to be ruined, and put both raw hands down into the lake.

She didn’t make a sound. Xola heard her not make it.

Xola put her own hands in, either side of Maren’s, not touching, a finger’s width off, the way she’d seen Rachael do it once, at the lake, in the spring, with Gabe’s rib: near enough to work and no nearer. And she shut her eyes.

And didn’t push.

The lake leaned.

It always did. It leaned toward her the moment her hands went in, the whole cold weight of it, glad, the big dog against her legs. She let it lean. She didn’t do anything with it. She waited, the way she’d waited last night, until her knees were numb on the stones and Squiddles had climbed down onto her shoulder to look, until under the leaning she could hear it again.

The lake breathing. In, along the shingle, and out.

And then, smaller, closer, right there between her hands:

Maren.

She’d never felt anybody’s water from the inside before. She’d only ever pushed into it, the way you’d shout into a room without listening for an answer. It was strange. It was like hearing a voice you’d only ever talked over. Maren’s hands, in the lake: the blood in them, the water in the skin, the long slow tide of a body going in and out with every heartbeat, warm against the cold. And in the palms, where the fire had been—

It was stuck.

That was what Odile had felt, and not had a word for. The tide in Maren’s palms wasn’t going. Everywhere else in her hands it went in and out, in and out, the way it was supposed to, the way the lake did; and in the palms it had stopped. Held. The way you hold your breath against a pain. Twelve burns, one on top of another, and the skin had learned to hold its breath against fire, and had been holding it for nineteen days, and didn’t know how to let it go.

Xola didn’t push.

She’d have pushed, a week ago. She’d have poured her own regrowing into those palms and made them swell like Gigi’s. She knelt in the lake with her eyes shut and didn’t, and it took everything she had.

She just listened. To the stuck place. To the tide round it, going on everywhere else. And to the lake, the whole of it, breathing in and out along the shingle the way it had for a thousand years. And she let the lake be near Maren’s hands. That was all. She didn’t move it. She let it be there, all of it, very close, breathing.

And Maren’s palms heard it.

She felt it happen. A hair. The stuck place in the left palm, at the base of the thumb, where it wept: it went. In. And out. The smallest tide there’s ever been, one breath, the skin remembering. And then again. And then the right palm, at the crease, cracked: in, and out. As if a hand that had been holding its breath for nineteen days had been standing next to something that breathed so slowly and so surely that it couldn’t help, in the end, breathing with it.

She didn’t do anything. She knelt there with her hands either side of Maren’s in the cold and listened, and Maren’s palms breathed with the lake, in and out, and every breath they took, something went out of them that had been held, and something came in.

She felt the skin knit.

From underneath. Not her doing it. Not pushed. The hand doing it, on its own, the thing it had been trying to do for nineteen days and couldn’t, because it was too busy holding on. The red going down. The tight shiny skin going soft at the edges. The cracks in the creases closing from the bottom up, the way frost-cracks in a puddle close when the sun comes, without anybody touching them.

Squiddles made a small surprised sound on her shoulder.

And something went up in her.

It went up the way the lake breathed. In. And it didn’t go out again. It stayed, a step higher than it had been, quietly, the way the water stays a hair higher on the shingle when the rain’s come in the night.

She opened her eyes.

In front of her, over the water, in the early light, where only she could see it:

She looked at it for a long time.

Well. Like the one in the militia hall yard that had frozen in the first winter, with the water under the ice leaning up toward her. A well wasn’t something you pushed water out of. A well was a place where water came up by itself, from underneath, from somewhere a long way down, because that was where it was going anyway; and all you did was be there with a bucket.

And the other well. The one you said to somebody. Get well.

She laughed out loud, kneeling in the lake, with her eyes full. She couldn’t help it. It was so stupid. It was such a stupid joke. It was exactly the kind of stupid joke somebody would make, and she knew exactly who.

Maren took her hands out of the water.

She held them up in the early light and looked at them. The palms were pink. Not raw; pink, soft, new, the colour of the inside of a shell, with the creases back in them and the cracks gone. She closed the right one, slowly. It closed. She opened it. She closed it again, and held it shut, and looked at her own fist as if it belonged to somebody she’d been introduced to once, a long time ago, and liked.

“Xolalotl,” she said.

“I didn’t push,” said Xola. Her voice had gone wobbly. “I didn’t. I just let the lake be near them. They did it themselves. They’d forgotten how to breathe, that’s all, they were holding their breath, and the lake reminded them.”

Maren looked at her hands for a long time.

“Thank you,” she said.

Then she got up out of the lake, in her ruined grey skirt, dripping, and stood on the shingle, and said in a voice Xola had never heard from her, not formal at all, “I am going up the hill to burn a hook out of a boy’s hand,” and went.

Xola stayed kneeling in the shallows. She couldn’t get up yet. Her legs had stopped working somewhere round the second breath and hadn’t started again.

Somebody’s boots came down the jetty.

She knew whose. Everybody on the lake knew whose; he walked like nobody else, not hurrying, as if he were going to the end of a garden. He came down off the last board onto the shingle and stood at the water’s edge a few paces from her, with the old rod in one hand and the hook on the end of it catching the sun.

She looked up at him.

He wasn’t looking at her. He was looking at the water where Maren’s hands had been. His face was doing the thing it did, which was nothing, very hard; and there was something in it Xola had seen once before, two summers ago, on a fox girl’s face, on the hill above Wrenhaven, the day Xola healed a cut hand and Alea went very quiet and walked outside.

“You listened first,” he said.

“I—yes. I didn’t know how. Before. I only ever pushed.” She wiped her face with a wet hand, which didn’t help. “Gigi’s hands went like a frog’s.”

“I heard.”

“Everybody heard.”

He nearly smiled. Not quite.

“She used to do that,” he said. “Diwa. Before she did anything. She’d put her head down by it, close, like it was telling her something, and wait. You’d think she wasn’t doing anything at all. She’d kneel by a man on a cot with her head down by his leg for so long the surgeons would start shouting at her.” He was quiet. “And then she’d put her hands on it, and it would already be halfway there. Because she’d waited till it told her where it was going.”

Xola couldn’t say anything.

“She called it listening,” he said. “I never heard anybody else call it that.” He looked at her, then, for the first time, kneeling in the lake with her gills open and her eyes streaming and a squid on her shoulder. “Till now.”

Then he turned and went back up the jetty, at a walk, to the last plank, and sat down on it, and cast.

Alea came down off the step.

She came down the shingle with Astra on her back and her ears up, and waded straight into the lake in her boots without stopping, which Xola had never once seen her do, and knelt down in the shallows in front of her, in the cold.

“I saw,” said Alea.

“I know. I’m sorry. I should’ve—I didn’t think—“

“Don’t be sorry.” Alea’s voice was very steady. Her eyes weren’t. “I watched you do it on the hill, two summers ago. A cut hand. And I had to go outside. I never told you why.”

“I know why,” said Xola. “Everyone knows why now.”

“Yes.” Alea looked at the water. “I’m going south in three days. To the Lists. I promised a boy I’d be in the stands.” She looked back at Xola. “I didn’t want to go with my mother’s healer in the lake and not say anything.”

Xola put her arms round her.

She didn’t ask. She never asked; she’d hugged everybody in Wrenhaven, and half of Valcrest, and one of Odette’s geese. She put her wet arms round the fox girl kneeling in the lake in her boots and held on, and Alea let her, and held on back, with Astra’s hilt digging into Xola’s collarbone and Squiddles squashed between them making a noise of great complaint.

On the last plank of the jetty, the man with the rod didn’t look round.

Xola thought he was probably crying. She thought it would be very rude to check.
