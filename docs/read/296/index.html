The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 10: The Sword's Country

Chapter 296

The Spring Probe

4,774 words · 21 min read

Kharvos came every spring. That was the first thing anybody at Cinderhold told you, and the last.

It was in the fort’s bones. The old soldiers said it the way you’d say the river floods: not a threat, not a prophecy, just a thing the year did. Hold till the frost, they said in the autumn, because Kharvos never came in winter. And in the spring, when the red went soft and the stink came up off it and the first green showed in the cracks of the old wall, they said here they come, and sharpened things, and waited.

They’d come in winter this year. Once. In the fog, with the threadless walking beside them, and Trooper Ennis was in the ground behind the chapel because of it, and Rhea had cut a hunter’s arm off from three paces through a wall. The old soldiers didn’t say hold till the frost any more. They didn’t say much.

But it was spring. And Yan felt them coming on the fourth night after the thaw-winds, the way he always did: from the east, very far, a cold at the edge of everything like a draught under a door.

There, said Suzu. Round his throat, in the dark of the chapel, her head lifting. Lots.

“How many?”

More than the fog. She tasted the air. Her tongue went in and out against his jaw. And the other ones. The holes. Lots of them too.

Yan sat up on the pew.

He told Aldous first. He always did. Then the commander. Then the channel, because Higu’s rules said so and because it was easier than not.

Salty came over from his pallet in the dark without being asked and sat down on the end of the pew, enormous, smelling of the forge, and didn’t say anything.

“Big one,” said Yan.

“Okay,” said Salty.

“Bigger than the fog.”

“Okay,” said Salty again. He rubbed his face. “I’ll tell Oakes we’ll want the big shields. Then I’ll eat something.” He looked at Yan in the dark. “Then you’ll eat something.”

“I’m not—“

“You’ll eat something,” said Salty. “That’s my thing. Malcolm’s got the bowls at Thornwall. I’ve got them here.” He got up. “And then you can sit on the knoll.”

He sat on the knoll.

He did it every dusk. He’d done it since the snow, since the night Thea had posted Stay and he’d gone out to the grave and put his hand on the black stone and felt the note he’d felt for two winters turn into a word. Stay. A hand flat on a lid. He sat with his back to the headstone and his hand flat on the earth, the way Aldous had told him once, in the chapel, with the snow coming down: somebody sits with you and says yes.

He did it for the iron. The old point, the spike, driven down through Diwa’s grave into the dark, saying the same word it had said for ten years into a cold that was small and dense and lying very still. Suzu said it was tired. Suzu said it had been saying it so long it had forgotten how to stop. So Yan sat with it, every dusk, and didn’t say anything, and that was the yes.

He did it for the other thing too. The warm thing behind the iron, behind the headstone, that wasn’t his and never would be, that he let be. He didn’t reach for it. He never had. He just sat near it, the way you’d sit near a fire somebody else had built.

Tonight the cold was awake.

Not up. It had stood up once, in the autumn, on the night of the battle at Thornwall, and faced west all night, and lain down again at dawn when the iron rang. This wasn’t that. It was lying where it always lay, small and dense under the spike, with the ash’s roots round it. But it had lifted its head.

He felt it do it. The way you feel a dog in a dark room lift its head off its paws.

It was listening east.

It hears them, said Suzu.

“Kharvos?”

Not them. She went very still round his throat. Past them. Further. The way they’re coming from. She was quiet a long time. I don’t know what it’s listening to, Yun. It’s further than I can taste.

Yan sat on the knoll with his hand on the earth and his back against the stone and the red going dark round him, mile after mile of it, and the cold under him listening to something past the edge of the world.

He didn’t know what it was. He didn’t ask.

He sat with it till full dark, and said nothing, which was yes.

Niko was on the wall when he came back. The east wall, the old one, the one Rhea had stood on in the fog: the bottom courses older than the kingdom, cracked and patched all the way down like an old man’s hands. Niko was sitting on the walk with his back to a merlon and his sword across his knees and a candle stub burning on the stone beside him, and he was looking at the sword the way you’d look at somebody who owed you money.

“Flash,” he said, when Yan sat down.

Yan waited.

“It’s the next one. I’ve known since the autumn.” Niko didn’t look up. “Struck was easy. Not easy. You know what I mean. A match is struck. A blade is struck on the anvil. A bell. It’s the same word for both. I found it.” He turned the sword over. “And the next one has to be the same. A word that’s true of fire and of steel at once. And I know what it is. I’ve known for a year.”

“Flash,” said Yan.

“Flash.” Niko put his finger on the flat of the blade, near the candle. “A flash of fire. A flash of steel. The same word. It’s perfect.” He sounded disgusted. “And I can’t do it.”

“What happens?”

Niko didn’t answer. He picked up the sword, and stood, and held it out over the parapet into the dark, and Yan felt him do the thing he did. Both candles. The two in him that didn’t mix, that Yan had felt since the first day on the road east: one hot and one steady, side by side, the aura and the mana, the sword and the spell. He felt Niko pull them both up at once, the way he’d pulled them up for Struck, and the bell-note came, faint, the ring of a struck blade in the dark—

And then he pushed. Flash.

The fire came first.

It went down the blade like oil catching, orange, bright, all at once from the guard to the point, so bright Yan’s eyes shut on their own. And Niko swung. Fast. Faster than Yan had ever seen him swing, a flat cut out over the parapet into nothing.

And the steel was late.

That was the only way Yan could ever say it. He felt it. The fire went, and the sword went after it, a heartbeat behind, the way thunder comes after lightning; and the fire was gone off the end of the blade into the dark by the time the steel got there, and the steel cut nothing, and the fire burned nothing, and they’d missed each other by a hair.

Niko stood at the parapet with the sword held out and smoke coming off the blade and his right hand red to the wrist.

“Like that,” he said.

“Does it hurt?”

“Every time.” He put the sword down. He looked at his hand. There was a blister coming up across the palm, the shape of the grip. “Forty-one times since the autumn. Every time the fire’s first. Or the steel’s first; that’s worse, I burn my sleeve. Never at once.” He sat down again with his back to the merlon. “Struck was a bell. A bell’s one sound. You hit it and it rings and the hit and the ring are the same thing. I could do that. A flash is two things that happen so fast they look like one.” He shut his eyes. “And I can’t make them the same speed.”

Yan sat beside him and felt his candles. Both of them. Hot and steady, side by side, not mixing. And in the middle of the hot one, a hard small knot of something: not pain. Being in a hurry.

He didn’t say it. It wasn’t his to say. Niko would find it or he wouldn’t.

“Tomorrow, then,” said Niko. “When they come. I’ll try it on one of them.”

“Niko.”

“I know,” said Niko. “I know. Not on purpose. But if it’s there.” He opened his eyes and looked east, into the dark. “It’ll be there.”

They came the next night, at moonrise.

Wendy saw them first. She always did; she was on the gate with her bow and her Count, and her voice came along the wall in the dark, very clear and very calm, the way it had in the fog. “Hunters. Eleven. Twelve. Fourteen. Coming in on the east side, in a line, low. Scouts behind. Forty. Sixty. Stop counting scouts. More behind them.” A pause. “And the others.”

Yan felt them. The cold of the hunters, like iron in rain, fourteen bright cold points coming across the red at a lope with their long arms swinging, hush, hush. The scouts, smaller, colder, a scatter of them like sparks off a wet fire. And behind them all, so many he couldn’t count them, a field of nothing.

Holes. Where people should be. Walking.

“How many of them,” said Aldous, beside him on the wall. He couldn’t feel it. He could see Yan’s face.

“A lot,” said Yan. “More than the fog. More than the ring round the knoll.” He tried. He couldn’t. “A hundred. More.”

Aldous didn’t say anything for a moment.

“Then we hold the wall,” he said, “and we see what they want.”

They wanted the wall. For an hour.

Yan had seen probes before. Every spring for three years, from the chapel, from the wall, from behind Salty: hunters coming at the east side in the dark, testing, finding where the old courses were weakest, pulling back. This was a probe the way a fist is a tap.

The first hunter came up the wall at the south stretch, where Rhea was.

Yan felt it go up. A cold bright point going up stone like a spider, fast, the long arms finding the cracks, and Rhea on the walk above it with her sword out and the autumn draft round her, eleven of them, Tobin Ferris in the middle with his collarbone mended and his father’s sword. And Rhea didn’t wait for it.

She’d never have done that, a year ago. Yan felt her light go down. Not ungrounded; not held up in the sword the way she’d held it all winter, like somebody standing on ice. Down. Into the walk, into the wall, the old courses older than the kingdom, already broken over and over and still there. And it came back up out of the stone like a spring coming up through a floor, and out of her, and out past the end of her sword, a hand and a finger, and she cut.

From the walk. Down the face of the wall. At a thing that was still three paces below her.

The hunter’s arm came off at the shoulder. Yan felt it: the cold point split, half of it falling, the long arm going down the wall on its own, hush, and the rest of the hunter hanging by one hand and its jointed fingers, and then Tobin Ferris leaning out over the merlon with his father’s sword and putting it, two-handed, with a shout like a boy jumping off a roof, straight down into the place where its face would be.

It fell. Both halves.

“One,” shouted Wendy, from the gate. And an arrow went up, blue, Aloft, and hung in the dark over the second hunter, the one coming at the gate tower. “Under the light. Two. Look under the light.”

Salty was in the gate tower.

Yan felt him the way you’d feel a furnace across a yard. Huge and unsteady and all at once, the biggest candle at Cinderhold, the one that had made him slow for two years and made every one of his tiers come big when it came. He was in the gate tower door with the big shield Oakes had made him, and Bear up, and the hunter came over the parapet at him with both arms, and Salty didn’t move.

The hunter hit him. Yan heard it from forty paces off over all the noise: a sound like a cart going into a wall. Both arms, a man’s length each, jointed twice, all the weight of the thing, into the shield and Salty behind it.

Salty went back a pace.

One. On the stone. And stopped. And then, with a noise Yan had never heard him make, a roar that came up out of the bottom of the furnace, he went forward. Into it. The shield first, all of him behind it, the way a bear goes into a thing it’s decided about; and the hunter went back over the parapet the way it had come, and down, forty feet, on to the red, and didn’t get up.

“Two,” said Wendy.

“I’m fine,” said Salty, to nobody, in the gate tower door, with his shield arm hanging. “I’m fine. That’s two.”

Niko tried it on the fourth.

Yan was on the walk behind him. He’d come along the wall from the chapel stair because Suzu had said Niko, very sharp, the way she said things she was frightened of; and he got there in time to see it.

The fourth hunter came up the wall at the north stretch, where Niko was, with three soldiers and a lantern. It came up fast, faster than the others, and over the merlon, and it was big. The biggest Yan had felt. Grey as a wet stone and horned and faceless, with beetle-plate down its back, and the smell came off it, hot iron in rain, so strong the soldier beside Niko gagged.

And Niko pulled both his candles up at once.

Yan felt them come. Hot and steady, side by side. The bell-note, faint, the ring of a struck blade. And then the push, Flash, and the fire went down the blade orange from the guard to the point, and Niko swung—

And the fire was first.

It went off the end of the blade into the hunter’s face, the place where its face would be, a sheet of orange; and the hunter flinched, all of it, the way they did from fire, the long arms coming up. And the steel came after. A heartbeat late. Into the space where the hunter had been a heartbeat before, when it had flinched; and it wasn’t there any more.

The sword went past it. Into nothing.

And the hunter’s arm came round from the side, where Niko wasn’t looking because he was looking at the place where his sword should have been, and hit him across the chest.

Niko went along the walk like a thrown sack. Into the next merlon. Yan heard something go in him, a crack, a rib or two, and the sword went out of his hand and over the parapet into the dark, and the hunter came after him along the walk with both arms up—

“Niko,” said Yan.

He didn’t know what he did. He found out after, from Suzu. He didn’t do anything; he couldn’t, he was a man who felt souls, not a man who stopped hunters. But Suzu went off his throat. Off him, down the walk, white in the dark, faster than he’d ever seen her move, between Niko and the thing coming at him; and she reared up, small, a snake the length of a man’s arm, in front of a hunter the height of two men, and hissed.

The hunter stopped.

It looked at her. It had no face to look with, but it looked. Yan felt it. The cold point of it turning on the small white light in front of it, very bright, the way she was bright when she was frightened; and something passed between them that he couldn’t feel. Something old. Something about cold things and the things that eat the cold, or can’t.

It stood there for a count of two.

And then Rhea was there. From the south stretch, along the walk, at a run, with her light going down into the stone with every step and coming up again, and she didn’t stop or slow or set her feet. She cut from five paces. Through the hunter, at the waist, a hand and a finger past her edge, and the top half of it went one way and the bottom half went the other, and Suzu was still standing on the walk between them with her head up, hissing at nothing.

Yan picked her up. She was shaking.

Don’t ever, she said. Don’t ever let me do that again, Yun.

“I didn’t let you.”

I know. She went round his throat so tight he could barely breathe. I know. I know.

Niko was sitting up against the merlon with his hand on his ribs and blood on his mouth, looking at his right hand. It was burned again. Across the palm, the shape of the grip, on top of yesterday’s.

“The fire was first,” he said. To nobody. “It was first again.”

They held the wall for an hour. Then the hunters stopped coming.

Not pulled back. Yan had felt probes pull back: the cold points turning, loping away east, the scouts scattering. These didn’t. Nine hunters left, out on the red below the east wall, and they stopped, all at once, wherever they were, the way a dog stops when somebody whistles. And lay down.

And behind them, the field of nothing came forward.

Yan stood on the wall with Suzu round his throat and felt it come. A hundred. More. He’d never been able to count holes; they didn’t have candles, there was nothing to count. Only the shape of where people should be and weren’t, walking, in the moonlight, across the red, toward the fort.

“The grave,” said Aldous, very quietly, beside him. “They’ll go for the grave. Like the fog.”

Yan felt for the knoll. For the stakes. The ring Wendy had measured all winter with a knotted rope, a hundred and forty paces round, a hundred and thirty-nine on the east once. Where the threadless had stood in the fog, forty of them, facing in, at exactly the stakes.

They didn’t go for the grave.

They walked past it.

Yan felt them do it. He felt the holes come across the red, a hundred and more, toward the fort and the knoll and the stakes; and he felt them reach the line of the stakes on the east side, a hundred and forty paces from the black stone. And not stop. And not turn in. They walked along the outside of it, round, the way you’d walk round a pond, and on, past the knoll on both sides, past the fort, west, a long way. A mile, maybe. On to the open red between Cinderhold and the old west fort that nobody had manned for twenty years.

And there they stopped. All of them. In a line, a long one, curved a little, like the edge of something very big.

And turned round.

Not to face the fort. Not to face the grave.

East.

They stood on the red in the moonlight with their backs to Cinderhold and their faces to Kharvos, a hundred and more of them, a mile off, in a curve; and the nine hunters lying out in front of the wall got up, one at a time, and walked out to them, and lay down again among them, at their feet, facing east too.

Like dogs. At the feet of people waiting for somebody to come home.

Nobody on the wall said anything for a long time.

“What are they doing,” said Tobin Ferris at last, very young, on the south stretch.

Nobody answered him.

Yan stood with his hand on the merlon and felt them. The holes. He couldn’t feel what they were doing. He couldn’t feel anything about them; that was what they were. But he could feel the shape of them, the long curve of where people should be, a mile off, and he could feel which way it faced, the way you can feel which way a crowd’s looking even with your eyes shut.

East. All of it. Toward the black. Toward the far side of Kharvos, past the edge of everything, further than Suzu could taste.

And under the knoll, a hundred and forty paces from the wall, the cold under the grave had its head up too. Listening the same way.

Yan read it with Suzu round his throat and didn’t know what to write.

He didn’t know what was east. Nobody did. The Redmarch, and then the black, and then Kharvos, weeks of it, a whole country of nothing that walked; and somewhere past that, the place a boy from this fort had walked to once, at nineteen, before the bell, with a horse and some bread, and come back from at the end of the summer dragging a sword.

Everybody at Cinderhold knew that much. The old soldiers told it. The Hero went east once, when he was a lad. Came back with that blade. Nobody knew where. Nobody had asked. And the Hero had planted it on his wife’s grave; and it was the old point of it, now, driven through the grave into the dark, that Yan had sat beside every dusk since the snow.

He looked east, past the curve of holes a mile off with their backs to him, at the dark.

Whatever it was, it was the way the sword came from.

They stood there till noon.

All night, and all the morning, with the sun coming up in their faces. The fort watched them from the wall. Nobody went out. Aldous wrote to the king, in his own hand, the way he had in the winter: I do not write that we think so. Yan felt it. So did I. And then, after a long time, a second line, which he showed Yan before he sealed it: They faced east. I do not know what is east of Kharvos. I have never known anyone who did, except one, and he has never said.

At noon, the second bell, they turned round.

Back to face the fort. Every one. A hundred and more holes in a curve on the red, and nine hunters at their feet getting up.

And walked east. Past Cinderhold on both sides, wide of the knoll, wide of the stakes, not looking at the wall, the way you’d walk past a house on your way home without looking in the windows. Across the red. Toward Kharvos.

Yan felt them go until he couldn’t any more.

Under the knoll, the cold put its head down.

He sat on the knoll that dusk with his back to the headstone, and Salty sat beside him with his arm in a sling, and didn’t say anything, and ate.

Niko came out at full dark with his ribs bound and his right hand wrapped, and sat on Yan’s other side. He’d found his sword. It had gone over the parapet and down into the red, and he’d gone down a rope in the morning with Wendy holding it and found it point-down in the dirt by the foot of the wall like somebody had planted it there.

“Forty-two,” he said.

“I know.”

“The fire was first. Into its face. It flinched. And the steel went where it had been.” He looked at the wrapped hand. “If the steel had been first it’d have cut it. If the fire had been first and the steel with it—“ He stopped. “It’s not speed. I’ve been doing it faster and faster since the autumn. Forty-two times. It’s not faster.”

Yan felt his candles. Both of them. Hot and steady, side by side. And in the middle of the hot one, the hard small knot. Smaller than last night.

“What is it, then,” said Yan.

Niko didn’t answer for a long time.

“I don’t know,” he said. “Not yet.” He looked at the black stone beside Yan’s shoulder. At the spike, going down into the grave, saying the same word it had said for ten years. “But I don’t think it’s faster.”

They sat there.

Yan put his hand flat on the earth. The cold under it was lying still again. Head down, small and dense, the ash’s roots round it, the spike through it. Saying nothing. Listening to nothing. As if it had never lifted its head at all.

The iron said stay.

He felt it say it. Tired. The way it was always tired. And under his hand, on the cold earth of the grave, for the first time since the snow, he felt it stop. Not the word. The tiredness. For a breath. The way a man carrying a heavy thing stops for a breath when somebody comes up beside him and takes one corner, without asking, without saying anything.

Yun, said Suzu, very small.

He didn’t move.

That was you, said Suzu. You took the corner.

“I didn’t do anything.”

No, said Suzu. You sat here all winter and said yes. And tonight it was tired and you were here and you didn’t move. Her tongue touched his jaw. That’s the second half of carrying. The first half is sitting with it. The second half is still being there when it’s heavy.

He opened his window. He didn’t decide to.

Ninety. Four points since the snow. Still locked.

Not yet isn’t never, said Suzu. Tell yourself, Yun.

“Not yet isn’t never,” said Yan, out loud, on the knoll, to the grave, with Salty on one side eating and Niko on the other holding his ribs, and the red going dark round them, and somewhere far east, past the edge of everything, a thing a hundred people with nothing inside them had stood all night to face.

He didn’t know what it was.

He sat with it anyway.
