The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 8: The Third Seal

Chapter 219

Harmony

5,179 words · 23 min read

Deston had sung in a choir from the age of thirteen, and the thing nobody ever told you about a choir was that you couldn’t hear yourself in it.

You thought you would. You stood in the second row of the tenors in a school hall in Singapore with the air-conditioning roaring and forty other voices round you, and you opened your mouth, and you waited to hear your own voice come out, the way it did in the shower or in the car. And it didn’t. It went into the others. It went in like a thread into cloth, and what came back to your ears wasn’t you at all; it was the whole thing, all forty, one sound, with you somewhere inside it, holding up a part of it you couldn’t hear.

The first week he’d hated it. He’d gone home and told his mother he couldn’t hear himself and she’d said good, then you’re doing it right, which was the kind of thing his mother said.

The second week, a boy in the row in front with a voice like a cracked bell had turned round in the middle of a rehearsal and said, you’re flat, bro, and Deston had said, I can’t hear myself, and the boy had said, I can. You’re flat. Lift it. And he’d lifted it. And the whole of the tenor line had suddenly fitted, the way a key fits, and the boy had turned back round and gone on singing as if nothing had happened.

That was Declan. Thirteen. The first thing he’d ever said to him.

Thirteen years. And then a phone call at three in the morning.

He stood under the bracket on the first morning of the Swiss with his head tipped and listened to the whole of Valcrest at once.

It was too much. It was always too much, the first morning, in a place where everyone was; it had been too much last summer in the gate square when the twelve had gone gold on one downbeat and he’d had to sit down on a mounting block. He’d learned to let it come in without trying to hear any of it, the way you’d let a big hall’s noise come in before a concert: not listening, just letting it be there, until it settled into parts.

It settled.

Threads. That was how his window had given it to him, in the first week, on the river road out of Solmere, and that was how it still came: everyone in the world had threads going out of them to the people they loved, and he could hear them. Not see; he’d tried to explain that to Thea, in the winter of the first year, and she’d wanted a diagram. Hear. Every thread had a note. Warm ones and cold ones, close and far, loud and faint and frayed. When two people who loved each other stood in a room, the threads between them hummed, and when there were forty people who loved each other in one place it was a chord, and when there were forty thousand, in a bowl of stone, at midsummer, under paper fishing rods, it was the biggest choir in the world.

He listened to it settle into parts.

Thirty-three of the thirty-five. He found them one at a time, the way you’d find your friends’ voices in a crowd: Gabe, beside him, enormous, his thread to Declan so tight and bright it rang like a struck string, and to Rachael, steady, and to a smith called Harl in Kingsferry, and to a king. Rachael, at the rail, flat and practical and warmer than she’d ever admit, threads going out of her to all of them in Group 4 like spokes, and to a woman called Maud in an infirmary on a river, and to a man she’d never met, frayed and faded and grey, going out of the world entirely, toward a mother in a flat in Singapore who didn’t know where she was. Dennis. Calvin. The rest of the thirty-three, all through the stands and the tunnels and the side sand, in their fives, in their friendships, crossing and re-crossing like the lines on Chris’s wall.

And two that weren’t here. Going north-west, faint, a long way, to a tower. Hyvred’s. Tiger’s. Still bright. Still turned toward everyone.

And the fox girl.

He’d heard her come in by the sea gate a week ago, from the other side of the city, the way he’d heard her come down the river past Kingsferry: one thread, very bright, going out of her toward the north, toward the lake, toward a jetty with the lamp lit by somebody else. It went into its other end like a nail. He’d told her so on the Rope Street stairs. Yours goes into his like a nail.

He could hear where its other end was now.

He didn’t listen. He’d sworn not to. Not to anyone; he’d sworn it to himself, in the long room of the Lance a week ago when Group 1 had sat down at their table and said nothing, very loudly, and he’d heard a thread come into the city at dusk by the north road and go and sit behind a door off the Street of Coopers, a quarter of a mile away, so familiar he’d nearly stood up. He’d put his hand flat on the table and not stood up. Rule five. Keep it.

So he didn’t listen. But you couldn’t not hear. That was the thing about a choir. You could choose where to put your ear. You couldn’t choose what was in the room.

It was in the north stand. High up. At the back, under the overhang, where the shade was and the seats were cheapest. On its own.

He turned his head the other way, toward the south stand, and listened very hard to forty thousand people who weren’t him.

“Bye,” said Chris.

He was standing at the right-hand end of the west wall with the morning’s pairings in his notebook, reading them off the bracket in his flat clear logging voice to whoever was standing under it, which was most of them. Thirty-six boxes. Eighteen bouts. The bye in the first round sat in the thirty-sixth box, the one with no name, and by the sums it would walk down the wall one box a round, and nobody sat in it twice.

“Who’s in it?” said Gabe.

Chris looked at his notebook. He looked at Deston.

“You,” said Chris.

Deston looked up at the wall. At the right-hand end, the unlettered box, the same size as all the others, with no gold and no fishing rod, and his own name, which Lucan’s painters had lettered in gold in the box beside it, and a line Chris had ruled in lampblack that morning from the one to the other.

“I’m the bye,” said Deston.

“By the sums,” said Chris. “It’s not a ranking. It’s a hat. Wil drew you.”

“I know,” said Deston. “I’m not upset.” He wasn’t. He found he was smiling. “I’m sitting in his box.”

Chris didn’t say anything for a moment. Then he wrote something in the notebook, very small, and didn’t read it out.

“Round one,” said Chris, to everyone, in the logging voice. “Eighteen bouts. Best of one. It’s swords, or it’s whatever you’ve got, and the marshals call it. Ten on the main sand and eight on the side. First touch.” He looked down the list. “Milli and Dennis, main sand, first bout.”

Dennis made a noise.

“Oh, no,” said Dennis. “Oh, he’s going to wait.”

He sat the first round on the side sand, at the rail, on the healers’ bench, because Rachael was on it.

She’d volunteered. Of course she had. The Lists had its own surgeons, two old men with leather bags who’d been patching up the losers of the Lists since before Lucan was master, and they’d looked at a small dry woman from another world with her sleeves rolled up and an apron on and a healing window, and been offended, and then she’d closed a lord’s son’s split lip on the first morning of the Fledgling in about the time it took them to open their bags, and they’d stopped being offended and started bringing her the hard ones. She had her own end of the bench now, by the water barrel, with a stack of clean linen and a jar of salve and a look on her face like a woman at a post office who’s been told the queue is very long.

She looked at him when he sat down.

“You’re the bye,” said Rachael.

“I’m the bye.”

“Good,” said Rachael. “Then you can hold things.” She handed him the linen. “Don’t drop it in the sand.”

He held it. He sat beside her on the bench, a pace off, the way they all sat near Rachael, near and not on. He’d known her since they were seventeen, since he’d introduced her and Dennis to Declan at a hawker centre over a plate of chicken rice that nobody had eaten because they were all talking. He’d sat a pace off her for twelve years.

On the main sand the marshal said begin, and Milli and Dennis stood in the chalk ring, and nothing happened for a long time.

That was Milli. Milli had been impatient on Earth, Hana said, and impatient here, and Kestra had spent two years in a courtyard in the rain saying again to him every morning until the impatience went somewhere else. It went into his feet. He stood in the ring now with his light curved sword low and his tail very still behind him and his whole body crouched and quivering the way a cat’s does on a wall before it jumps, and he didn’t jump. He waited. That was Pounce: the longer he waited, the faster his first strike. One strike. If it missed, he was wide open.

Dennis knew all of that. Dennis had read every line Milli had ever written in the channel. Dennis stood in the ring with a blunted short sword he obviously didn’t know how to hold and his face perfectly pleasant, and then there were three of him.

The crowd made a noise.

Three Dennises. Standing in a row in the ring, a pace apart, identical, all with the same perfectly pleasant face and the same badly held sword, all of them breathing. Deston could hear them; that was the strange thing. Not the threads. Dennis’s thread was only in one of them, the middle one, warm and wry and going out to all of Group 4 and to a sword-woman in an old mercenary’s coat at the Lance who’d been his business partner for two years and had a cut in his boat. But the other two rang. Faintly. Like glass. The way Dennis always said a lie rang.

Glamour. A lie you could see.

Milli waited.

The three Dennises waited. The crowd waited. Somewhere on the fourth tier of the east stand somebody shouted GET ON WITH IT, which was Tiger’s job when Tiger was there, and somebody else had taken it up for him.

Milli went.

Not at the middle one. Not at any of them. He went between the left one and the middle, very fast, impossibly fast, the cat off the wall, and the curved sword came round in a flat arc through the place where the left one was and the left one went to nothing like breath on a window, and the middle one, the real one, flinched toward it the way you’d flinch toward a friend, and the arc carried on and caught him.

On the forehead. Above the left eyebrow. Blunted steel, but a light curved sword with a cat behind it at full Pounce, and Dennis went down on the sand on his back with his hands over his face, and the right-hand Dennis went to nothing too, and the marshal said touch, and then, in a different voice, healer.

There was a lot of blood. Foreheads bled. Deston knew that; everybody knew that. It didn’t help. It came out between Dennis’s fingers and down the side of his face into his ear and onto the sand, very red in the sun, and Milli was on his knees beside him with his sword dropped and his tail flat and his face gone white saying sorry sorry sorry I didn’t mean it I went too fast.

Rachael was already over the rail.

Deston went with her because he was holding the linen.

She knelt in the sand beside Dennis and took his wrists and pulled his hands away from his face, not gently, and looked. A long cut above the eyebrow, deep, to the bone at the end of it, the edges gaping. Blood everywhere. Dennis’s eyes, open, looking up at her, and his face doing the thing it did when he was frightened and making a joke about it, before the joke had arrived.

“Don’t,” said Rachael.

“I wasn’t going to,” said Dennis. Muffled.

“You were going to say how does it look,” said Rachael. “It looks like a cut. Hold still.” She looked up, at Deston, at the linen. “Pad. Fold it. Press. There. Harder than that. You’re not going to hurt him, he’s already hurt.”

He pressed. He knelt on the other side of Dennis on the sand with a pad of folded linen pressed to his oldest friend’s head and the blood coming through it warm under his palm, and forty thousand people in a bowl of stone gone suddenly quiet, and Milli at Dennis’s feet with his hands over his mouth.

“May I,” said Rachael. To Dennis. Quietly.

She always asked. It was a thing nobody had told her to do; she’d done it from the first week, in the infirmary at Kingsferry, with a docker’s arm opened to the elbow and Brother Tamsin peeling her fingers off the linen. She didn’t like touch. She’d never liked it, on Earth or here. But healing was work, she said, and you asked before you put your hands on somebody’s work.

“Yes,” said Dennis.

She put her hand flat over Deston’s, over the pad, over the cut. And her window opened, and Deston heard it.

Mend. She’d had it since the first spring, since the night she knelt over Gabe’s forearm, burned to the elbow at the forge, and felt for whatever came after a finger’s width at a time, and found it, and her window had opened in front of her face for the first time. It was good. It was very good. It closed things. But it was slow. Not slow the way the Lists’ surgeons were slow, opening their bags; slow the way water is slow, the way a river closes over a stone you’ve thrown into it. A cut like this, to the bone, would take her three minutes. Four. With her hand on it and her eyes shut and the whole of her going down her arm into it, and Dennis bleeding under her the whole time.

He heard her start.

Her thread went down her arm. Warm. Steady. Thinner than it should have been; he heard that too. She’d been on this bench since the Fledgling started, five days, closing lips and knuckles and a cadet’s ear and a lord’s son’s nose that had been broken in two places, and she was tired, and she’d never once said so. She went down into the cut the way she always did, steady, slow, like water.

And Deston heard, very clearly, the way you hear a single flat note in a chord, that it wasn’t going to be enough. Not fast enough. The blood was coming through the pad under his hand faster than she was closing it.

He didn’t decide anything.

He’d tell Gabe afterwards that he’d decided; Gabe would ask, and he’d say I just thought I could. He hadn’t thought anything. He heard Rachael’s thread going thin, the way he’d heard his own voice go flat at thirteen in a hall in Singapore and a boy turn round and say lift it. And he heard, at the rail, ten paces off, white-faced, with his hands on the top bar and his kettle tied to his belt, Calvin.

Calvin’s thread was wind. It always had been. It went out of him in every direction at once, restless, quick, light, the way Calvin did everything; it went to all of Group 4 and to a hundred people he’d talked to once on a barge and to the sky. He was a wind mage. Tailwind. He’d got it on the deck of the Patient Widow in the second spring, up a mast, screaming at Haskett, with a sail full of a wind that shouldn’t have been there. He had more of it than he could ever use. You could hear it coming off him like heat off a road.

And Rachael was running dry.

Deston put out his other hand. Not toward Calvin. Toward the thread. The way you’d put out your hand to the boy beside you in the second row of the tenors when the part went too high for him alone, so he’d know you were there and could lean.

He took Calvin’s thread in one hand. And Rachael’s in the other.

And he lent.

It didn’t feel like magic. That was what he’d say afterwards, to Thea, who’d want to know. It felt like the second week of choir. It felt like a whole line of tenors suddenly fitting.

Calvin’s wind went out of Calvin, through the threads, into Deston, and out of Deston, and into Rachael. Not all of it. A breath. A gust. The way a breeze comes into a room when somebody opens a window on the other side. He felt it go through him and he didn’t keep any of it; you didn’t keep it, you weren’t meant to, it wasn’t yours. You just stood in the middle and held both ends and let it go across.

It went into Rachael’s hand, over his, on the pad, on the cut.

He heard her gasp.

And he heard the cut close.

Not like water. Like a door. All at once. In one breath. Under his palm, under the pad, under her hand, the edges of a cut to the bone above a man’s left eyebrow came together and closed, the way you’d close a book, so fast and so clean that Dennis, on his back in the sand, said oh in a small surprised voice, as if somebody had shut a window he hadn’t known was open.

Rachael didn’t move.

She knelt there with her hand on Deston’s hand on the pad, and her eyes open, and her face doing something he’d never seen Rachael’s face do in twelve years, which was nothing at all, because all of it had gone somewhere else.

Her window was open in front of her face. He couldn’t see it. Nobody could see anybody’s window. He didn’t need to.

He’d heard it. Her thread, all of it, the one that went out to all of Group 4 like spokes, and to Maud, and to a frayed grey one going out of the world to a mother in a flat in Singapore: it had gone up. Not louder. Up. A whole step. The way the tenor line had gone up when he lifted it, and fitted.

And his own had gone up with it.

He felt it. In his chest. Where the threads came out of him and went everywhere, to Gabe and Declan and Rachael and his mother in Singapore and a fox girl’s nail in the north and thirty-three people in a bowl of stone. They’d all gone up a step together, at once, like a choir modulating, and he hadn’t done it on purpose, and he’d never in his life felt anything so much like singing.

He looked at it. He started to laugh.

“Harmony,” said Deston. Out loud, on his knees in the sand, with blood on his hands. “Oh, that’s so corny. Declan would’ve—“ He stopped. “He’d have been so annoying about it.”

“Don’t,” said Rachael.

She was looking at her own window. She hadn’t taken her hand off his.

“Don’t make it a thing,” said Rachael.

It was a thing. Forty thousand people in a bowl of stone had watched a small woman from another world put her hand on a man’s head that was bleeding everywhere and close it in one breath, and they were standing up. Section by section. The way a crowd stands, not all at once, but in a wave. And Milli was sitting on the sand at Dennis’s feet with his hands over his face, crying with relief. And Dennis was lying on his back with his eyes shut saying I’m going to have a scar, aren’t I, I’m going to have a scar, it’s going to look so good. And Calvin was at the rail with both hands on the top bar, white as paper, staring at his own hands as if somebody had picked his pocket and left something in it.

“What’s it say?” said Deston. Gently.

Rachael didn’t answer for a long time.

“Close,” said Rachael.

She said it very quietly, to the window, the way you’d say a word you’d been carrying a long time and hadn’t known was yours. Deston heard it go into her like a key.

He knew why. Everybody in Group 4 knew why. It had been her word, before. All through the first spring at Kingsferry, a finger’s width at a time, every rope burn and cut and bargeman’s arm, she’d said it under her breath as the edges came together: close. Her line. And then, the night of Gabe’s arm, her window had given her Mend instead, and she’d never said the old word again. And last spring, on the deck of the Patient Widow below the Narrows, with the hat man gone grey where she’d cut him with Gabe’s knife, she’d sat with the knife in her lap and said, to nobody, I think healers don’t just mend. I think we close. She’d meant something more than cuts. She’d never said what. Deston had heard the thread under it go cold with something she was frightened of.

Close.

“It’s just a cut,” said Rachael. To the window. As if arguing with it. “It’s a cut on Dennis’s head.”

“It was a cut on Dennis’s head,” said Deston. “Now it’s closed.”

She looked at him. Sideways. Not at his face; at the pad, at his hand under hers.

Then she took her hand away. All at once, the way you’d take it off a stove. And looked at it. And then, because she was Rachael, and because forty thousand people were standing up for her and she’d never once in her life been able to bear being looked at, she stood up herself, and wiped her hands on her apron, and looked down at Dennis on the sand.

“You can get up,” said Rachael. “There’s nothing wrong with you. Stop lying there like a painting.”

“I’ve got a scar.”

“You’ve got a pink line. In a week you won’t have that.” She looked at Milli. “And you. Stop crying. You won. You were meant to hit him. That’s what the bout’s for.”

“I went too fast,” said Milli.

“Then go slower next time,” said Rachael. “Or don’t. He’ll heal.” She picked up the bloody linen off the sand and folded it, very precisely, the way she folded everything, and walked back across the side sand to the healers’ bench and sat down on her end of it, by the water barrel, and put the linen in the bucket and her hands in her lap.

Her hands were shaking. Deston could see them from the sand.

He went and sat on the bench. A pace off. Near and not on.

They sat there. On the main sand the marshals were raking the blood into the chalk and calling the next bout, and the crowd was sitting down again, slowly, in sections, still talking. It went round the bowl like a wave going round a pond. The healer. The small one. One breath.

“That was you,” said Rachael. Not looking at him.

“It was Calvin.”

“It was Calvin’s wind,” said Rachael. “It was you that put it in my hand.” She was looking at her hands in her lap. “I felt it come. Like somebody opening a window behind me.”

“That’s what it felt like from the other end, too.”

Rachael didn’t say anything for a while.

“I don’t like being lent things,” she said.

“I know.”

“I don’t like being helped. I spent twenty-six years being helped. I came here and I could finally do it myself.” She looked at her hands. “And then I couldn’t. Not fast enough. And you heard it. Didn’t you. You heard me running out.”

“Yes.”

“In front of everyone.”

“Nobody else heard it,” said Deston. “Just me.” He was quiet. “And you didn’t run out. You were a breath short. A breath. Calvin had about four hundred spare. It’s not help. It’s—“ He stopped. He didn’t have the word. Then he did, because it had been in his window for a quarter of an hour and he’d been laughing at it. “It’s a part. You were singing a part and it went too high for one voice. So somebody stood next to you. That’s all. That’s what a choir is.”

Rachael looked at him for the first time. Sideways. At his face.

“That’s so corny,” said Rachael.

“I know.”

“Declan would’ve been so annoying about it.”

“I know,” said Deston, and laughed, and she didn’t, quite, but her mouth did the thing at the corner that was Rachael’s version, and her hands stopped shaking.

Calvin came round the end of the rail at the change of bouts, with the kettle bumping on his hip, and stood in front of the bench and looked at both of them.

“Did I do that?” said Calvin.

“You lent it,” said Deston.

“I didn’t decide to lend it.”

“Neither did I,” said Deston. “I just heard you had some.”

Calvin looked at his hands again. He’d been looking at them for twenty minutes.

“It went out of me,” said Calvin. “Like a sneeze. Through you. Into her.” He looked at Rachael. “Did it help?”

Rachael looked at him for a long moment. At Calvin, who asked the question everyone else was too polite to ask. Who’d come along to Malacca once because Deston said you’ll like him, and had liked him, and had been the last of them to meet Declan and the one who’d talked the most about him since. Who kept the kettle. Who’d lent her a breath of wind without deciding to, in front of forty thousand people, and was now standing in front of her asking if it had been any use.

“Yes,” said Rachael. “Thank you.”

Calvin went bright red to the hair.

“Okay,” said Calvin. “Okay. Cool. Good. Okay.” And went away very fast toward the tunnel under the east stand, and they heard him, a moment later, on the other side of the rail, say I’m CANDLE ADJACENT to somebody, very loudly, and Gabe’s laugh.

They put it in the channel at noon. Rachael wrote hers in four words. Deston wrote his in more than that, because Deston always did.

He read it on the healers’ bench with Rachael a pace off reading it too, and Dennis, on the sand at their feet with a pink line over his eyebrow, reading it upside down over his shoulder and complaining.

Seven of thirty-five.

He closed the window. He sat on the bench in the sun with blood drying on his hands and listened.

The threads had settled again. The whole of Valcrest, the whole choir, in its parts. Thirty-three in the bowl. Two on a tower, north-west, a long way, bright. A fox girl on the Fledgling sand on the other side of the Lists, her thread going north like a nail, toward a jetty where somebody else had lit the lamp.

And in the north stand. High up. At the back, under the overhang, in the shade, where the seats were cheapest. On its own.

He’d sworn not to listen. He didn’t. He kept his head turned toward the south stand, the way he had all morning.

But he’d heard it. When Rachael’s hand went down on his, and he took Calvin’s thread in one hand and hers in the other and lent, and the whole of him went up a step like a choir modulating: just for a breath, in the middle of that, every thread he had had gone up together. All of them. And one of them, the oldest, the brightest, tight as a struck string, going up into the north stand to a man in the shade at the back who’d been a boy with a voice like a cracked bell in the row in front of him in a school hall in Singapore, had gone up a step too.

And something had come back down it. Very faintly. The way a note comes back to you off the far wall of a hall.

Not a word. A sound. Like somebody, a long way off, under their breath, laughing.

Lift it, thought Deston.

He didn’t look at the north stand. He kept his face to the south.

But he was smiling, and Rachael, a pace off on the bench, looked at him sideways, and at the north stand, and back, and didn’t say anything at all, which was how Rachael kept things, and folded the clean linen in her lap, very precisely, into a square.
