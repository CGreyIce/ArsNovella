The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 1: Thirty-Four Winters

Chapter 7

Where They Buried Them

2,529 words · 11 min read

The ground was red.

Yan had known it would be. Aldous had told them, on the long road east, and the soldiers at every waystation had told them, and the name told them: the Redmarch. But knowing a thing and standing on it were different. On the first morning at Cinderhold he climbed the fort’s eastern wall at dawn and looked out, and the land below ran away toward the mountains of Kharvos in long low waves the colour of old rust, and nothing grew on it but a thin grey grass that looked as if it had given up halfway through.

“It was green,” said Aldous, beside him. The chaplain had come up quietly, the way he did everything. “Before. There were farms out there. A river. The battle was fought from that ridge to the far trees, and when it was done the earth was this colour, and it has never changed back.”

“Blood?” said Yan.

“That’s what people say.” Aldous looked out over it for a long moment. “I think it is something else. Something that was done here that the land has not forgiven. But I am a superstitious old man, and you should not listen to me.”

Around Yan’s neck, Suzu had gone tight and still. She hadn’t moved since they’d crossed into the red.

Cinderhold was a square stone fort with a garrison of two hundred soldiers, a well, a forge, a chapel with no priest, and a training yard that Rhea had claimed on the first day as her personal kingdom.

“Push-ups,” she announced, on the first morning. “Whoever does the most gets the good bunk.”

“There is no good bunk,” said Wendy. “They’re all the same bunk.”

“Then whoever does the most gets to say they’ve got the good bunk.”

Salty was already on the ground.

That was how it went, for a month. Everything was a contest. Rhea raced them around the walls and wrestled them in the sand and timed them, loudly, with a sand-glass she’d taken from the quartermaster. She arm-wrestled Salty every evening after supper, in front of an audience of increasingly invested soldiers, and the score after four weeks stood at fourteen to thirteen in her favour, with one draw that both of them disputed. Maigo kept the tally on the wall of the mess hall in charcoal, and also ran the betting, and was, by the end of the month, the second-richest person in the fort after the quartermaster.

But the contests were the outside of it. The inside was the same as it was everywhere. You sat, and you breathed, and you tried to feel something move.

“I feel nothing,” Salty announced, on the tenth morning, with his eyes shut and his face screwed up.

“You’re trying too hard,” said Rhea. “Aura’s not a weight. You can’t lift it.”

“Everything’s a weight.”

“That’s your problem,” said Rhea, and then, surprisingly gently for her, “Stop pushing. Let it come up on its own. Like a bruise.”

Salty, who had never in his life stopped pushing anything, frowned and tried to stop pushing.

Wendy didn’t need to be told. She’d picked up a bow on the second day, a plain soldier’s longbow from the armoury, because she’d said the practice swords were stupid, and she’d put three arrows into the centre of the straw target at fifty paces before anyone had shown her how to hold it. The garrison archers came out to watch. She ignored them. She was still wearing her lavender headphones, silent now, around her neck, and sometimes when she was concentrating she put them on, as if listening to nothing helped.

“Aura archers are rare,” said Aldous, watching her. “Very rare. Most people think aura has to go into a blade. It doesn’t. It goes into whatever you trust.”

“I don’t trust anything,” said Wendy, and loosed another arrow into the centre of the target.

“Then you’ll be very good at it,” said Aldous mildly, “once you start.”

Every night after supper, before Rhea and Salty’s arm-wrestling and Maigo’s betting, the five of them sat in a corner of the mess hall and compared their windows. They’d been doing it since the night before they left Solmere. It had stopped being a novelty and become something closer to a check-in.

“Seven,” said Maigo, at the end of the fourth week. He didn’t look smug, exactly, but he didn’t look not smug either. “I can feel the ground. Like, really feel it. I put my toes in it and it’s like putting your hand on someone’s chest.” His pixel sunglasses had slid down his nose. He pushed them back up. “This ground hurts, though. Out past the wall. It’s like touching a burn.”

“Five,” said Salty. “It moved today. Something did. Just for a second. Rhea says that’s it starting.”

“Rhea says a lot of things,” said Wendy.

“Three,” she said, when they looked at her. “It’s the bow. It’s always when I’m drawing the bow. The second before I let go, something goes in.” She frowned at the table. “I didn’t say it was good.”

“Four,” said Yan.

Mashi was last. He always was. He opened his window with the expression of a man reading a bill he already knew was too big.

“One,” he said. “And one.”

“Still?” said Salty.

“Still.” Mashi shrugged, entirely unbothered. “One percent aura. One percent magic. Everyone keeps telling me I’d be on six by now if I picked one. But I don’t think the point is to pick one.” He closed the window. “Hyvred said my light was two colours that don’t mix. I want to know what happens if they do.”

“Nothing,” said Salty. “The Advisor said nobody’s ever done both.”

“Yeah,” said Mashi. “He did say that.”

There was a letter, that night, too. The first. It had come with the courier from Solmere, by way of a dozen other forts and the long east road, and it was from Kei, and Yan read it aloud because Maigo begged him to.

Thornwall is cold and full of sheep and Corvin has a map for everything, including, I think, the sheep. Hyvred sees lights in people. Bee drew a bird yesterday and swears it moved. I believe her, mostly because Hisui says it did.

Five percent. My path’s going to be poisons and medicine, I think. Hisui can taste the air and tell me what’s in it, every herb, every mushroom, which ones will kill you. He’s very smug about it.

Hisui says to tell Suzu hello, and that she’s being rude by not writing back. I told him snakes can’t write. He says that’s a human problem. I’m not passing on the rest of what he said.

Okay, he made me. He says she’s pretty. Please don’t tell her I said that, I didn’t, he did.

Maigo laughed so hard he had to put his head on the table. Salty grinned. Even Wendy smiled.

Around Yan’s neck, Suzu lifted her head, looked at the letter, and hissed softly, once.

“She says thank you,” said Yan.

“She did not,” said Wendy.

“She said something,” said Yan, and folded the letter away.

It was in the fourth week that he first felt the dead.

He’d been going out past the wall in the evenings, with Aldous’s permission, to sit on the red ground and do his breathing there instead of in the yard. He didn’t entirely know why. Something about the quiet. Something about the way Suzu went still when he crossed the line from the grass into the rust, as if she were listening.

That night he closed his eyes, and breathed, and waited, and something waited back.

It wasn’t a ghost. He would be very clear about that later, to the others, because Maigo immediately said ghost. It wasn’t a shape or a voice or a cold hand. It was more like walking into a room and knowing, without seeing anyone, that people had been in it recently. That they had been afraid. That some of them had been very young.

There were so many of them. Under the ground, in every direction, out to the ridge and the far trees.

Yan opened his eyes. His hands were shaking. Suzu was wound so tight around his throat that he had to put two fingers under her coils to breathe.

“You felt them,” said Aldous.

The chaplain was standing a few paces behind him, his staff in his hand, his face grave and unsurprised in the dusk.

“How did you know?”

“I’ve seen that look before. On old soldiers, mostly. Never on someone who wasn’t here.” Aldous came and sat down next to him on the red ground, slowly, the way an old man sits. “I buried a great many of them, after. Soldiers. Farmers who didn’t leave in time. People from the villages over the ridge. For two years after the battle I came out here every day with a shovel and a book. I put up markers. There are a thousand of them, out past those stones. More, maybe. I stopped counting.”

Yan didn’t say anything.

“Your path,” said Aldous, after a while. “It’s going to be the soul, isn’t it. That’s what the snake means. That’s why it frightens me.”

“I don’t know yet.”

“I think you do.” Aldous looked out over the dark. “Come with me tomorrow. All of you. There’s something I want you to see.”

He took them out at dawn: Yan and Salty and Wendy and Maigo and Mashi, and Rhea, who refused to be left behind. They walked for an hour across the red, past the markers Aldous had put up, rows and rows of them, plain wooden posts with names burned into them, and then no names, only posts. Maigo went quiet at the edge of the first row and stayed quiet the whole way.

Near the far trees, on a low rise above where the river had once run, Aldous stopped.

There was a grave there. Only one, set a little apart from the rows. A cairn of grey stones, carefully stacked. Around it, in the dead red earth where nothing grew, there were flowers.

Small ones, white and blue, low to the ground. Not many. Enough.

“Someone planted those,” said Wendy. Her voice had lost its edge.

“Years ago,” said Aldous. “They come back every spring. Nothing else out here does.”

At the head of the cairn, in the earth, there was a hole. A long, deep, narrow wound in the ground, as if something enormous had been driven in there and then pulled out again. The edges had started to crumble.

“There was a sword here,” said Aldous. “Planted over the grave as a marker. Black iron, taller than a man. I said prayers beside it for seven years. The soldiers wouldn’t come near it. They said the man who put it there was called the Black Blade.” He looked at the hole for a long moment. “Last season, the Advisor’s people came with a winch and six men and took it away.”

Nobody spoke.

“The sword in the hall,” said Salty slowly. “The one standing in the circle.”

“Yes.”

“They took a sword off a grave,” said Rhea, sounding genuinely shocked for the first time since Yan had met her. “To do the summoning? Off a grave?”

“Whose grave is it?” asked Mashi.

“A healer’s,” said Aldous. “That’s all I was ever told. A healer who died in the battle.” He hesitated. “People who were here say she meant something to the man who planted the sword. More than something.”

Yan knelt down at the edge of the cairn.

He didn’t mean to. He didn’t decide to. He just knelt, and closed his eyes, and breathed, and waited the way he’d waited the night before, and the ground waited back.

It was different here.

Out in the rows, the dead had been many and frightened and very far away, like voices in another room. Here there was only one, and it wasn’t frightened at all. What was left in the ground here was grief: old and deep and patient, like water at the bottom of a well. Someone had stood on this rise and grieved for a very long time. Someone still did. He could feel the shape of it, worn into the earth like a path.

And under the grief, very far down, there was something else.

Something cold. Something that had been done, here, to someone who hadn’t wanted it done. It was faint, very faint, as if most of it had been scoured away. But it was there, and Suzu felt it too: she went so tight around his throat that his vision greyed, and she hissed, loud and sharp, at nothing at all.

Yan opened his eyes.

The others were all looking at him.

“You okay?” said Salty.

“Yes,” said Yan. He wasn’t. He stood up, and brushed the red dust off his knees, and put two fingers under Suzu’s coils until she let him breathe. “It’s fine.”

He didn’t tell them about the cold thing. He didn’t know what it was yet. He would think about it first, on his own, until he did.

“When the anniversary comes,” said Aldous, “the Advisor will send the sword back. I’ve asked to be the one to bring it. I’d like to see it standing here again.” He looked at the five of them. “It’ll have to be carried, the last part, across the red. A winch can’t come out here. I’m hoping one of you will be able to lift it by then.”

They all, without any discussion, looked at Salty.

“Me?” said Salty.

“You,” said Wendy. “Obviously you.”

“I bet it’s me,” said Rhea.

“You’re not even summoned.”

“I’m still betting.”

Maigo, who had been quiet for almost two hours, made a small sound that might have been a laugh, and wrote something on the back of his hand with a stick of charcoal: the first entry in a new book.

That night, Yan wrote back to Kei.

He told him about the fort, and Rhea, and the score, and Wendy’s three arrows, and Maigo getting rich. He told him that Suzu had said thank you, which was very nearly true. He told him about the red ground and the rows of markers and the feeling of walking into a room where people had been afraid.

He told him about the grave with the flowers, and the hole where the sword had been.

He didn’t tell him about the cold thing under the grief.

Then he sat for a long time at the little table in the barracks, with the pen in his hand and Suzu asleep around his neck, and thought about someone he’d never met, standing on that rise every year for seven years, planting flowers in ground where nothing grew.

He thought about his mother, too. He didn’t mean to. It just happened sometimes, at night, when the work was done. A small kitchen, a long way away. He wondered if she’d planted anything.

He put the thought away, and blew out the lamp.
