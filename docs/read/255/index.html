Chapter 255

Every Night, West

5,659 words · 25 min read

That you could sit in one place and somebody a long way off could see you. Right now. As it happened.

John had thought, at seventeen, that it was the closest thing the world had to magic. He’d thought it in a poly common room with a cheap laptop and a borrowed headset, in the corner of a friend’s facecam stream at two in the morning, telling Declan he was wrong about something he was right about. He’d thought it every night for years.

He’d done it now. Two seconds of a red plain at sunset, into thirty-five windows at once, from the top of a rise on the way home from a grave. Whenever there’s something you want them to see, Declan had said, on the rise, with the red going down behind him. Not half past nine.

So he’d stopped doing it at half past nine.

He did it when there was something. Every night, on the road west, there was something.

The first night was the hill above Duskhollow.

He hadn’t planned it. They’d walked all morning and stopped at the top of the first long rise for Malcolm to check the route and Azac to rearrange the packs for the fourth time; and Petra had turned round. Petra never turned round. He’d walked a thousand miles with her by now, east and back, and he’d never once seen her look behind her on a road.

She turned round on the hill and looked down at the town for a long time. And John, because he was John and couldn’t help it, sent a mote down after her look. Down the hill, a mile, gold, small, over the roofs, over the quay, to the gravel bar and the line of people doing the forms on it and a broad man in grey with a stick; and up, to the quay wall above them, and the row of small figures sitting on it.

In coats. Nine. Swinging their legs.

He opened the square in the air beside the road, small, the size of a book, and there they were in it. Petra didn’t look at the square. She was looking at the real thing. But Xav did, and Azac, and Malcolm looked up from his book and looked at it for a long moment and then went back to his book without saying anything, which was Malcolm’s way of saying something.

That night, at the fire, John opened his window under the square.

He didn’t push. He’d learned that on the road east. You didn’t push, and you didn’t try to make it go in; you just held it there, the thing you wanted them to see, under the window, and wanted them to see it, and let it go along the threads the way Deston said it went, knocking on the windows like a moth on a lamp.

It went.

He felt it go. And then, for the first time, he felt it hold. Not two seconds of colour and a shape going out like breath on a window. A picture. Nine children on a quay wall, in coats, with a grey river below them and a man with a stick; held there, still, in thirty-four windows, for as long as it takes a heart to beat once.

Then it went.

He read it by the fire with his knees up. Across the fire, Petra was sitting with her back to a stone and her sword across her knees and her empty left hand going, every few minutes, to the place under her arm where a stick wasn’t.

He didn’t tell her. She couldn’t see the channel. But Xav leaned over and said something to her, quietly, and she looked at John across the fire, and her face did the thing at the edges that she did instead of smiling.

He didn’t know why it held now.

He thought about it on the road. He’d had three years of not knowing why things worked; you got used to it, here. But he’d been John all his life, and John was the one who said no, look, here’s why, with valid points, until you believed him. He wanted a why.

It wasn’t the strength of it. He was no stronger than he’d been on the rise. Glimpse, his window said, and Spark, and Progress to Candle with a number that had crept up from seventy-nine since the grave, a point at a time, like water rising in a lock. It wasn’t the distance; Duskhollow was a mile, the rise had been nothing at all, the motes went where they went.

It was the wanting. He was almost sure. On the rise, he’d wanted them to see the red because it was enormous and terrible and beautiful and he was standing in it. It had gone in like a shout. Two seconds and gone.

The children on the wall he’d wanted them to see because they were small, and warm, and Petra’s, and nobody else would ever see them.

That was quieter. It held.

He wrote it in Malcolm’s book that night, on the back page, where Malcolm let him write things: IF it’s for them THEN it holds. IF it’s for me THEN it doesn’t. And Malcolm read it over his shoulder and wrote under it, in his own small square hand: IF John is right THEN he’ll be insufferable. And under that: Backup: he already is.

The third night it rained.

Malcolm had said it would. IF it rains on the downs THEN it rains for three days, page four of the list he’d written Garrick before they left, and the backup, page eight: Azac.

So on the third night, on the open downs with the rain coming in sideways off the west and nothing taller than a sheep for ten miles, Azac planted his feet in the middle of the camp, and put up his big round shield from the outfitters, and opened his hand, and the Ward came up.

John had seen it a hundred times. In the Hollow, on the road, on the red. Azac’s ward was a dome of pale blue light about the size of a tent, a little lopsided on the left where Azac always overreached, humming faintly, like a glass somebody had run a finger round. It kept the rain off. It kept the crawlers off, in the Hollow. Azac could hold it for an hour if nobody hit it, and for about a minute if somebody did, and he narrated the whole time, because Azac narrated everything; it was the only way he could hold it.

“And the rain’s coming in off the west,” said Azac, to nobody, in the voice he used in the Hollow, “and it’s heavy, it’s very heavy, and the ward is holding, ladies and gentlemen, the ward is holding, look at it go—“

“Nobody’s watching, Az,” said Rui Xiang, from somewhere.

“You’re watching,” said Azac. “John’s watching. John’s always watching.” He shifted his feet. “And it’s coming in harder now, and the left side’s going a bit, as it does, as it always does, it’s a known issue—“

Petra was looking at the ward.

She was sitting under it, on her pack, with her knees up, out of the rain for the first time all day, and looking up at the pale blue dome over her head. She’d never sat under it before. On the road east she’d always been the one on watch, outside, in the weather, with her back to the camp. John watched her look at it, the way he watched everything. Her face was very still.

“It’s from inside,” she said.

Azac stopped narrating.

“Yours,” said Petra. “It’s from inside. Round. It keeps the weather off everybody under it. All at once.” She looked at him. “Mine’s in front. Flat. It only keeps the weather off what’s behind it.”

Azac thought about that, in the rain, holding his shield up.

“Yours is better,” he said.

“No,” said Petra. “Different.” She looked at the lopsided left side of the dome, where the rain was coming through in a fine spray. “Yours goes there because you’re reaching. You’re trying to cover the side of the camp where Malcolm’s sitting, because Malcolm’s always on the left. You’re holding it toward him.”

Azac opened his mouth. Then shut it. On the left side of the ward, Malcolm looked up from his book.

“Don’t reach,” said Petra. “Stand in the middle and let it be round. He’ll still be under it.”

Azac stood there.

Then he shifted his feet. A half-step. Into the actual middle of the camp. And the ward hummed, and went round; not lopsided; the left side coming in to match the right; and the fine spray of rain on Malcolm’s book stopped.

“Huh,” said Azac.

He didn’t narrate for a whole minute. John had never known him not narrate for a whole minute.

John sent it. Azac’s ward in the rain on the downs, round for the first time, pale blue, with six people under it and Malcolm’s book dry.

It held a heartbeat.

He watched Rui Xiang disappear on the sixth night.

Rui Xiang did it all the time. He’d done it on the gravel bar and the road east and in a shepherd’s barn halfway through a game of Ditch, to cheat, and been caught, and done it again. Unseen. He pulled his aura in, the way Ars pushed his out, until there was nothing left of him for anybody to feel; and if you weren’t looking straight at him, he wasn’t there. If you were looking straight at him he was a slight young man with quick hands and a polite face who said please a great deal, and then you blinked and he wasn’t.

On the sixth night he came and sat by John at the fire and said: “Can the motes see me?”

“Yes,” said John. “They see light. You’re not light. You’re just a person. Light bounces off you.” He thought about it. “Unless you’re doing the thing.”

“I’m doing the thing now,” said Rui Xiang.

John looked at him. Sitting there by the fire, a foot away, with his knees up and the firelight on his face, perfectly visible.

He sent a mote up. Over the fire, ten feet, looking down. And opened the square.

The fire, from above. The six packs. Azac asleep on his back with his arms out, like a starfish. Malcolm with his book. Xav and Petra on the far side, not talking. John, by the fire, cross-legged, looking up.

And beside him, a foot away, nothing. Firelit stones. A patch of trampled grass.

“Oh,” said John.

“Thought so,” said Rui Xiang, pleased. “Can you send it?”

John sent it.

Rui Xiang let go. He was there again, a foot away, by the fire, with his knees up. He looked at the square, at the place where he’d been and hadn’t been, for a long time.

“Deston said,” said Rui Xiang, “about the ones at Thornwall. The threadless.” He wasn’t smiling now. “He said he couldn’t see them at all. Not even the gap.” He turned his quick hands over in the firelight. “When I’m Unseen, he can still see the gap. He told me. On the road east. He said I leave a hole in the threads the shape of me, like a man standing in a doorway in the dark.” He looked at the empty patch in the square. “They don’t leave a hole.”

John didn’t say anything.

“I’d like to not leave a hole,” said Rui Xiang. “If I’m going up there. One day.” He said it very politely, the way he said everything, as if he were asking for the salt. “I’d like it to look at me and see nothing. Not a gap. Nothing. The way it sees them.”

“You’re not going up there,” said John.

“Not yet,” said Rui Xiang. “Please.”

On the eleventh night they read about the bridge.

Drew had written it. Short, the way Drew wrote things when they’d cost him. Tiger tried to go up the pass road alone at night. I stopped him on the millstream bridge. He’s fine. I’ve got two again. Hild says bridge.

Malcolm read it out by the fire, for Petra. Petra listened with her face very still.

“He was going for the trooper,” she said. “The freckled one. In the third ring.”

“Ossie,” said Malcolm.

“Ossie,” said Petra. She looked at the fire. “Good,” she said. And when Malcolm looked at her: “Good that he wanted to. Good that the other one stopped him.” She pulled her coat round her. “You need both.”

On the thirteenth night, Higu wrote one line, and Malcolm read that out too.

Bram. First ice. A reed fell over, a hand and a half past his edge. He didn’t say anything for as long as a kettle takes to boil. Then he said Reaching.

Petra sat up.

“Bram,” she said. “The big one at the lake. In the militia coat. Who talks.”

“Yes.”

“He’s been Gleaming since I was a child,” said Petra. She was quiet for a long time. “He fished. All winter. On the stool next to him.” She looked west, along the dark road, toward the mountains they couldn’t see yet. “I stood on the shingle all spring with a stick, and Bram sat on a stool.”

“Same teacher,” said Xav.

“Same teacher,” said Petra. “Different lesson.” And almost smiled.

John didn’t send anything that night. He thought about a man he’d never met properly, sitting on a stool on the ice at the edge of a lake a long way north-east, not saying anything for as long as a kettle takes to boil, and thought that some things were better as one line in Higu’s hand than as any picture he could make.

The mountains came up on the sixteenth day.

Out of the haze at the end of the long road over the downs, grey, then white at the tops; the Greyspine, Malcolm said, checking his book; the western border; the edge of the kingdom. They came up so slowly that for a whole morning John thought they were cloud. And then the cloud didn’t move, and the haze cleared at noon, and there they were, from one side of the sky to the other, a wall of grey stone and white snow along the whole of the west, with the sun on it.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Somewhere in there, Malcolm said, pointing at a notch in the wall that John could barely see, was the pass. And at the foot of the pass, on this side, a valley. And at the near end of the valley, under the mountains, a town with a north wall and a tower.

And at the far end, the rings.

John didn’t send a mote. He wanted to. He’d wanted to all day. He had three of them in his pocket, more or less, the way you’d have a hand on a camera in your pocket; and he kept his hand off them. Hyvred looked at the valley every night, Thea said. Thea had looked through her, once, and come back with a nosebleed and six pages of Higu’s book and a word in a box. NONE.

He didn’t think he wanted to see it from here. He thought he wanted to see it from the tower, with Hyvred beside him, the first time. The way you don’t want to see the end of a film from the corridor.

He walked.

They came into Thornwall on the twenty-third day, at dusk, in sleet.

Not much of it. Thin and grey, coming in off the mountains onto the road, onto the fields, onto Azac’s hair, where he was trying to catch it on his tongue and narrating it. The town came up out of the dusk ahead of them, grey stone and slate and smoke, smaller than John had thought, with its south gate open and lamps lit on either side; and at the far end of it, beyond the roofs, against the white wall of the mountains, the dark finger of a tower.

There were people at the south gate.

Not soldiers. Or not only. A big grey man in a smith’s apron with his sleeves rolled up and his arms crossed, standing in the middle of the road in the sleet like a man waiting for a cart he’d ordered: and John knew him, though he’d only seen him once before, on a shingle beach, with a red-hot sword in his tongs. The king. Without a crown, without a coat, with sleet in his grey hair, standing at the south gate of a border town at dusk.

Beside him, a woman. Fifty-something, tall, in a soldier’s coat, with a staff in one hand and a leather patch over one eye. Standing very still, with her head turned a little, the way you’d turn your head to listen to something in another room.

And behind them, in the gate, a little apart, five people John knew only from the channel. A man with blond-and-black hair and a red streak in it, and his chest bound under his jacket. A tiger demihuman with his ears flat and his tail wrapped round his own leg. A bee girl with her wings folded and her arms folded and her face like a shut door. A cow girl with gold eyes and glasses pushed up into her hair, standing very close to the bee girl. And a man with teal-ended hair and no snake round his neck, sitting on the mounting block by the gate because he obviously couldn’t stand, grey in the face, with a satchel of flasks on his knees.

Group 5.

They looked like people who’d been on the wrong side of a door for a very long time.

The king came forward.

He didn’t look at John. He didn’t look at Xav or Malcolm or Azac or the space where Rui Xiang probably was. He walked down the road in the sleet to Petra, at the back, where she always walked, and stopped in front of her.

He was very big. John had forgotten. On the shingle at the lake he’d been a big man at a forge; here, in the road, at dusk, with the sleet on him, he was a mountain.

“You came,” said the king.

“You asked,” said Petra.

The king looked at her for a long time.

“I did,” he said. “Thank you.”

Petra nodded, once. Then, because she was Petra:

“What’s for supper?”

The king laughed. It was a big laugh, the forge laugh, a laugh John remembered from the shingle, that made the soldiers on the gate turn round; and it went up into the sleet and the dusk over the south gate of Thornwall, and somewhere behind him in the gate the tiger demihuman flinched at the sound of it, and then, slowly, put his head up.

“Mutton,” said the king. “Cook’s stew. And bread. There’s always bread.” He looked at the woman with the eyepatch, beside him. “Hild.”

The woman with one eye had been standing perfectly still all this time, with her head turned. Listening. Not to the king. To Petra.

Petra looked at her.

John watched it, because John watched everything. Two women in the sleet at the south gate, one with a staff and one with an empty hand where a stick used to be, both in old soldiers’ coats; looking at each other. Not hostile. Not anything. The way two people look at each other across a room when they’re the only two in it who’ve done a thing nobody else has done.

Neither of them said anything for a long time.

“Later,” said Hild.

“Later,” Petra agreed.

Malcolm made them eat.

That was the first thing. John hadn’t known he would; he should have. Malcolm took one look at Group 5 in the gate, at the bound chest and the sling and the shut-door face and the grey man on the mounting block, and opened his book to a page John hadn’t seen, and read it, and closed it, and said to the king, quite politely, “Where’s the kitchen?”

He made them eat in the guardhouse, at the long table, all five, while Cook’s mutton stew went round. He didn’t talk. He didn’t say it’ll be all right or we’re here now or any of the things John would have said. He sat at the end of the table and watched every bowl, and when a bowl stopped going down he looked at it until it started again. Bee’s stopped twice. Tiger’s stopped four times. Malcolm looked at it every time, until Tiger, with his ears flat, picked up his spoon.

Kei didn’t eat. Kei sat at the end of the bench with his satchel and his grey face and said, very quietly, that he was testing something and couldn’t, not tonight; and Malcolm looked at him for a long time and then wrote something in his book, and didn’t argue. John read it later, over his shoulder. IF Kei won’t eat THEN ask Hisui why. And then, crossed out: Hisui isn’t here. And under that: WHY isn’t Hisui here.

Rui Xiang sat down beside Tiger. Not close. On the bench, a hand away. Tiger didn’t look at him. Rui Xiang didn’t look at Tiger. They sat there, two people not looking at each other, while Malcolm watched the bowls. And after a while John saw Tiger’s tail, which had been wrapped tight round the leg of the bench all through supper, unwind a little.

Xav sat by Drew. Azac stood in the doorway, because Azac always stood in the doorway, with his shield on his back, narrating nothing at all.

Petra sat by Hild. Neither of them said anything.

John looked at all of it, the way he looked at everything, and didn’t send any of it. It wasn’t for anybody. It was theirs.

Hyvred took him up the tower at full dark.

A hundred and twelve steps, in the dark, in a spiral, with the wind coming in at the arrow-slits. She went up ahead of him with a lantern, slowly; she was very thin, John thought, thinner than she looked in the channel, if you could look thin in a channel, which you couldn’t. At the top there was a trapdoor and then the roof, flat, with a parapet all round and Corvin’s maps weighted down under a sheet of oilcloth against the weather, and a blanket on the north side where she obviously slept, and nobody else. The king came up at dusk, she said. Not tonight. Tonight he was feeding six more.

She went to the south parapet and stood with her back to the north and shut her eyes.

“I don’t look,” said Hyvred. “Not with my eyes. Not since One Pour. I feel it.” She didn’t turn round. “You’ll want to look. Everybody does. Look. I’ll tell you what you’re seeing.”

John went to the north parapet.

He put his hands on the cold stone. The sleet had stopped. The cloud had broken, and there was no moon; but the stars were out over the mountains, hard and many, and the frost on the valley gave their light back.

Eleven miles of it. Long and pale and narrow, white with frost, between the dark walls of the mountains on either side, going north-west from the foot of the wall below him to a notch in the far end where the pass road went in. He could see the road. A dark line through the white, straight, old, paved, going up the valley floor. Swept. Even in the frost. A dark clean line, swept, all the way up to the far end, as if somebody had walked down it ahead of the frost with a broom.

And at the far end, at the foot of the pass, the rings.

He took a mote out of his pocket and sent it.

He didn’t decide to. His hand did it. North, out over the parapet, over the north wall and the frosted fields, gold, small, a spark going away up the valley in the starlight, two miles, five, nine; and he shut his eyes and looked through it, the way he looked through all of them, and opened the square in the air beside him, on the roof of the tower, the size of a door.

And there they were.

People.

Standing in the frost. In rings. Hundreds of them. A small close knot of them at the middle, and rings round it, wider and wider, thirteen, he counted them, the way you’d count the rings in a cut tree; all of them standing perfectly still in the starlight with the frost on their shoulders and their hair, the way frost settles on fence-posts, and their faces turned up toward the pass.

He could see their faces. The mote was close enough. He’d never wanted to see anything less in his life. A woman in an apron. A man with a beard and frost in it. An old woman. A boy. A soldier, in the third ring, with freckles, and his face turned the wrong way, a little, not toward the pass but back down the valley, toward the town; toward the tower John was standing on. Toward a shed against the inside of the north wall with a tiger on its roof.

At the edge of the outermost ring, a girl with a plait.

And outside them, on the left, curving round, a line of more. Standing the same way. In the same frost. They looked the same as the others, in the square. Exactly the same; people, standing. He could see their coats and their hair and the frost on their shoulders. But they didn’t have faces turned up toward the pass. They didn’t have faces turned anywhere. They stood the way furniture stands.

“Twenty-six,” said Hyvred, behind him. With her eyes shut and her back to it. “At the fourteenth place. Twenty-six since last night. You’re looking at them. They look like people to you.”

“Yes.”

“They’re not anything,” said Hyvred. “That’s what’s horrible about them. Your motes see light bouncing off coats. I see what’s inside the coats.” She was quiet. “Nothing. Holes. The shape of people.”

John looked at them in the square for a long time.

Then he opened his window.

He had it there. The whole of it. In the square on the roof of the tower and under his open window: thirteen rings of people in the frost and a curve of holes and a freckled boy turned the wrong way and a girl with a plait. All he had to do was want them to see it.

And they’d see it. Thirty-four of them. In Wrenhaven and Solmere and Valcrest and Kingsferry and Cinderhold and Duskhollow. At their tables, in their beds, on their roofs. A heartbeat of it, held, steady, the way the children on the quay wall had held, every face clear.

He’d come all this way to do exactly that. The king had asked for him for exactly that. So that when the time comes, the rest of them see what you see.

He held it there under his window and didn’t send it.

He didn’t know how long. Long enough for the sleet to start again, lightly, out of the dark. Long enough for Hyvred to open her eyes and turn round and look at him, and at the square, and at his face.

“You’re not sending it,” said Hyvred.

“No.”

“Why?”

He thought about it. He was John. He wanted a why.

“Because it’s for me,” he said at last. “If I send it now. I want them to see it because I’m frightened and I don’t want to be the only one looking at it.” He looked at the square. “That’s not for them. That’s for me. It’d go in like a shout and they’d all be up all night with it, all thirty-four, in their beds, and there’s nothing any of them could do. Not tonight.” He shut his eyes. “It wouldn’t hold. It shouldn’t.”

Hyvred looked at him for a long time.

“I’ve been the only one looking at it for three years,” she said.

“I know.”

“It’s horrible.”

“I know,” said John. “I’m sorry. I’m here now.” He opened his eyes. “You’re not the only one any more. I’m looking too. But I’m not sending it to them till they need it. Till there’s something they can do with it.”

“When’s that?”

He didn’t know. He thought about a man on a rise at sunset with a red plain going down behind him. Whenever there’s something you want them to see.

“When it comes down,” said a voice from the trapdoor.

The king. He’d come up after all. He stood at the top of the tower stair with sleet in his hair and a bowl of mutton stew in each hand, one for Hyvred and one, it turned out, for John; and he came across the roof and gave them one each and sat down against the north parapet with his back to the mountains, the way Hyvred said he did, and looked at the square in the air with the rings in it.

“When it comes to the mouth of the pass,” said the king. “When it moves. Then they’ll need to see it. All of them, at once. So that whatever they’re doing, wherever they are, they know.” He looked at John. “Not before. You’re right. Not because you’re frightened.” He looked at the square for a long moment, at the freckled boy turned the wrong way, and then away. “Put it out, son. Eat your supper.”

John let the square go.

It went out. The mote, nine miles up the valley, went out with it, a small gold spark in the frost among the rings, and was gone.

He sat down on the roof of the tower beside the king, with his back to the north, and ate his supper. It was very good stew.

He did send one thing, that night.

At the end of it. When the king had gone down, and Hyvred was asleep in her blanket with Bee’s coat over her, because Bee had come up at some point and gone down again, the way Bee did, without saying anything. He stood at the south parapet with the town below him.

Thornwall at night, in the sleet. The roofs going down the hill in the dark, slate and thatch, wet and shining. Smoke going up from every chimney. The lamps in the windows. The guardhouse, lit, with Azac’s shape still in the doorway. The forge at the bottom of the north lane with its hearth banked and red. A small dark shed against the inside of the north wall with a tiger sitting on the ridge of its roof, and, beside him, a little apart, a hand away, a slight young man sitting perfectly still, who might have been there and might not.

And on the granary wall, in the light from the guardhouse window, a running dog drawn in one line of white chalk.

He sent a mote up over it, high, and opened the square, and opened his window under it, and wanted them to see it. Not because he was frightened. Because it was there, and warm, and small, and theirs, and nobody else would ever see it.

It held a heartbeat.

John read it on the roof of the tower in the sleet with his back to the valley. Then he closed his window and lay down on the roof beside Hyvred’s blanket, not close, with his own coat over him and his hands under his head, and looked up at the sleet coming down out of the dark.

North, behind him, eleven miles off, thirteen rings of people stood in it with their faces turned the wrong way, and waited. He didn’t look.

He’d look tomorrow. With Hyvred. Every night, until it moved.

And then he’d show them all.
