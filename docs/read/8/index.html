The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 1: Thirty-Four Winters

Chapter 8

The River Road

2,597 words · 11 min read

On the first night out of Solmere, the two wagons stopped at the same waystation, and the guides agreed, after some discussion, that ten people and four minders could share one fire.

It wasn’t really a discussion. Emrys asked Garrick if he minded, and Garrick looked at the ten of them already dragging logs together without waiting for an answer, and said, “Does it matter?”

So there was one fire, in a field off the north road, with the wagons drawn up behind it and the horses cropping grass in the dark. Sabine had produced a skin of something from her pack that she wouldn’t name and wouldn’t share, except with Dennis, who had somehow already earned it. Petra sat a little apart with her back to a wheel, her sword across her knees, saying nothing. Garrick lay on his back with his hat over his face. Emrys was humming.

And for the first time since they’d woken on the stone floor of the hall, the people from Declan’s life were all in one place with nothing to do but talk.

It took them a while to get there. They talked about the hall, first, and the king, and the Advisor, and the sword. They talked about the guides and the guards and the places they were going. They talked about Dennis’s pay rise, at length, because Dennis wanted them to.

Then Calvin said, out of nowhere, “He showed me his kettle.”

Everyone looked at him.

“In Malacca,” said Calvin. “The visit. I’d met him, like, an hour before. And he walks me into his kitchen and points at this kettle and goes, ‘This is the kettle,’ like it was a trophy. Like it was the best thing he owned.” He laughed, a little helplessly. “I didn’t know him at all. And I remember thinking, I like this guy.”

“He was so proud of that kettle,” said Rachael.

“It was a bad kettle,” said Dennis.

“He knew it was a bad kettle. That’s why he was proud of it.”

And then they were talking about Declan.

Not carefully. Not the way people talk at a funeral, or in the weeks after, when everyone is trying to say the right thing. They talked about him the way you talk about someone who’s in the next room: what he’d said, what he’d done, how annoying he’d been. Rachael told the story about asking him for advice, years ago, about something so embarrassing that she refused to say what it was, only that he’d answered her completely seriously and without laughing once, and then laughed for about ten minutes afterwards. Gabe told a story from school that made Deston put his face in his hands. Dennis did an impression of Declan losing at something that was so accurate it was briefly hard to watch.

Group 6 listened at first. They’d known him in a different way: from a classroom, from long nights of games, from a voice in a call. But the stories found them too.

“I kept meaning to visit,” said Malcolm, quietly, when there was a lull. “After he moved. I kept saying next year.” He stared into the fire. “We drifted. Not a lot. Enough.”

“Same,” said RX. “I was going to go up for his birthday.”

“I was on every stream,” said John. “Every single one. And then he moved and he stopped doing facecam, and I sort of… didn’t know where to put it.”

Azac was turning a stick over and over in the fire. “I took over tank,” he said. “After. In everything. It felt wrong, standing in front. Like I was in his spot.” He shrugged. “Still does, a bit.”

Xav hadn’t said anything. Deston had been watching him. Xav was like that: he waited until he had something to say, and when he said it, people listened. Deston had known him a long time, through Declan, and he’d never once seen him rush.

“He’d have figured this place out in a week,” Xav said at last.

“He’d have broken this place in a week,” said Gabe.

They laughed. All ten of them, at once, around the fire in a field in another world, and for a moment it was very nearly like being in a call at two in the morning with someone saying something completely outrageous and finishing it with trust.

Deston looked around at their faces in the firelight, and felt something he couldn’t name. As if there were something stretched between all of them, holding them in place.

The next day, in the wagon, Emrys taught them to sing.

“A chant isn’t a list of words,” he said, as they rocked along the north road. “That’s what most teachers will tell you, and they’re wrong. A chant is a shape. It has pitch, and breath, and rhythm, and the mana follows the shape. Get the shape right and it’ll go where you send it. Sing it flat and it’ll go nowhere at all.” He smiled ruefully around at them. “We’ll start with breathing. Everyone. From the bottom of the chest.”

Rachael closed her eyes and breathed. Calvin breathed so enthusiastically he had a coughing fit. Dennis breathed while looking sceptically at Emrys, and then at Sabine, who was riding beside the wagon, as if to check whether this was the kind of thing she’d pay for.

Gabe breathed too, even though he’d chosen aura and wouldn’t be chanting anything.

“You don’t need to,” said Emrys.

“I know,” said Gabe. “Feels weird not to.”

Deston knew what he meant. They had stood in the back row of a school choir for years, the two of them and Declan, learning to breathe on the same count. Some things you didn’t unlearn.

Emrys sang a single long note, low and steady, and then a phrase of four notes after it, simple as a nursery rhyme. “Like that. All together.”

They sang it. It was terrible. Calvin was sharp, Dennis was flat, and Rachael was laughing too hard to finish.

“Again,” said Emrys. “Gently.”

On the fourth try, something happened to Deston.

It started in his throat: a warmth, as if he’d swallowed something hot. It spread down into his chest and then out, not into the air but sideways, and suddenly he was aware of the people around him in a way he hadn’t been a moment before. Not seeing them. Feeling them. Gabe on his left, solid and familiar as a wall he’d leaned on his whole life. Rachael, bright and new. Calvin, all restless energy. Dennis, careful and watchful under the zest. Each of them a fixed point, and between him and each of them a kind of tension, like a held breath, like a string pulled taut.

He stopped singing.

“Dest?” said Gabe.

“I’m fine.” He wasn’t sure he was. The warmth was fading already. “I felt something.”

Emrys was looking at him with sharp interest. “What did it feel like?”

“Like…” Deston didn’t know how to say it. “Like I knew where everyone was. Without looking.”

Emrys was quiet for a moment. Then he nodded slowly, as if Deston had confirmed something he’d suspected.

“Write it down,” he said. “Everything you felt. Exactly. That’s the first time. You’ll want to remember it.”

That evening, round the fire, they compared windows: both groups together, the way they had on the last night in the castle.

“Two,” said Deston, and told them about the singing.

“Three,” said Rachael. “And I think I can feel when someone’s hurt. Calvin stubbed his toe on the wagon this morning and my hands went warm.”

“I did not stub—“ said Calvin. “Okay. Two. And I always know which way the wind’s about to go. Before it goes. Is that something?”

“It’s something,” said Emrys.

“Two,” said Dennis, “and I can tell when someone’s lying. Not magic. I could always do that.” He glanced at Sabine. “She’s lying about what’s in that skin.”

“I’m not lying,” said Sabine. “I’m withholding.”

“One,” said Gabe. “Aura’s slow.”

From Group 6: Xav two, Malcolm two, Azac two, John three (“I notice light. Where it’s coming from. When it changes. I always did, but now it’s loud”). RX was three as well, and he talked about his skill page for a while, very fast, about how it was empty now but there were faint grey shapes behind the emptiness, like slots in a game that hadn’t been unlocked yet.

Malcolm listened to all of it and then said, quietly, “We’re going to be at this for years.”

Nobody argued.

On the second evening, Gabe wanted to spar.

Xav understood the impulse. Three days in a wagon did something to your legs, and to your head, and Gabe had always been someone who thought better when he was moving. So they found two straight sticks, and cleared a space in the waystation yard, and squared up.

Gabe was big. He’d always been big, and strong in the way of someone who’d never needed to think about it, and he came in hard and fast and direct, the way he did everything.

Xav waited.

That was all it was, really. That was all it had ever been. Gabe swung, and Xav stepped back. Gabe swung again, closer, and Xav moved half a step to the side, and watched. He watched Gabe’s feet, and his shoulders, and the little hitch in his breathing before each swing. He wasn’t looking for an opening. He was waiting for the moment Gabe committed to something he couldn’t take back.

On the fifth swing, Gabe overreached. His weight went onto his front foot and stayed there, just slightly too long.

Xav stepped in and tapped him on the ribs with the end of his stick.

“Ow,” said Gabe, mostly on principle. “Again.”

They went again. And again. Gabe got better at not overreaching; Xav got better at waiting. They were both laughing by the end, breathless and sore. When Xav finally turned to put the sticks against the wall, he found Petra standing at the edge of the yard.

She’d been watching. He had a feeling she’d been watching the whole time.

“Where did you learn to fight like that?” she said.

It was the most she’d said to him in three days.

“Counter-Strike,” said Xav.

Petra looked at him blankly.

“It’s a game,” he said. “From where we’re from. You hold an angle, and you wait, and the other person has to come to you. Whoever moves first usually loses. A friend made me play it, when we were seventeen.” He hesitated. “He was better at it than me. At the waiting. He’d sit on one spot for a full minute without blinking and then take one shot and win the round.”

“Your friend fights like that?”

“Fought.”

Petra was silent for a long moment. Xav had the strangest feeling that she wasn’t really looking at him anymore. That she was looking at something behind him, or before him, in a place he couldn’t see.

“Where is he?” she said.

“He died,” said Xav. “Three years ago.”

Petra nodded, slowly. She didn’t say she was sorry. She just stood there a moment more, with that faraway look, and then turned and went back to her wagon.

Xav watched her go. He didn’t know what that had been about.

He filed it away anyway.

They reached the river on the third day.

The Wren was wide and brown and slow here, running south between low green banks, with a little ferry town clinging to the near side of it: a jetty, a tavern, some warehouses, and a dozen flat-bottomed barges tied up along the water. Group 4 would go downriver from here, to Kingsferry. Group 6 would go the other way, upriver along the bank, to a town called Duskhollow that Garrick described only as “where the work is.”

They said goodbye on the jetty, in the wind.

It was awkward, the way goodbyes are when nobody wants to be the first to make it real. Dennis and Sabine had to be physically separated from a negotiation with the bargemaster. Rachael hugged everyone, including Garrick, who submitted to it with the expression of a man being sat on by a large dog. Calvin promised John he’d write first and John promised Calvin he’d write better. Gabe and Azac shook hands, then gave up and hugged.

Deston found Xav at the end of the jetty, a little apart from the others, looking upriver.

“Write,” said Deston.

“I will.”

“I mean it. Not just to Gabe. To me.”

“I said I will.” Xav glanced at him, a flicker of a smile. “Since when do you have to tell me twice?”

Deston opened his mouth to answer, and the warmth came back.

It was stronger this time. It came up from his chest and into his throat without any singing at all, and then into his eyes, and suddenly he could see it.

A thread.

A thin, fine line of light running from somewhere in the centre of his own chest, across the space between them, to Xav. It was gold. Not bright, but very clear, and taut, as if it had been there all along and he’d simply never had the eyes for it. When he turned his head, there was another, thicker one, running back down the jetty to Gabe: so thick it was almost a rope. Fainter ones, a dozen of them, to the others. Rachael. Calvin. Dennis. John and Malcolm and RX and Azac. Even a thin grey one to Emrys, who’d been teaching him for three days.

He stared.

“Dest?” said Xav.

Deston made a decision. He wasn’t sure, afterwards, why it had been so easy. Maybe because they’d promised, that last night in the castle, to tell each other everything. Maybe because it was Xav.

“I can see something,” he said. “Between us. Right now. Like a string.”

Xav didn’t laugh. That was the thing about Xav. He just looked at Deston for a long moment, with the steady attention he gave everything, and then looked at the empty air between them where Deston’s eyes were fixed.

“What colour?” he said.

“Gold.”

“To me?”

“To you. And Gabe. And everyone.” Deston swallowed. “Gabe’s is thicker. Like… like it’s been there longer.”

Xav thought about that. Deston could see him thinking about it, turning it over, filing it away.

“Okay,” Xav said. “Write to me when you know what it is.”

“You’ll be the first.”

“I’d better be.”

The bargemaster was shouting. Sabine was shouting back. Gabe was waving at Deston from the deck. Deston clasped Xav’s arm once, hard, and walked back along the jetty and stepped down onto the barge, and the rope came free, and the slow brown current took them.

He stood at the stern and watched the jetty get smaller. Xav was still standing at the end of it, one hand raised.

And the thread didn’t break. That was what Deston would remember, years later. It stretched as the water widened between them, thinner and thinner, finer than a hair, until he could barely see it against the light on the river. But it held. It ran back upstream, over the water, all the way to the man on the jetty, and it held.

He turned around, slowly, and looked at the others on the deck. At Gabe’s rope of gold. At Rachael’s and Calvin’s and Dennis’s finer threads. And he wondered, for the first time, what else was out there that he’d never had the eyes to see.
