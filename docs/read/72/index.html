The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 3: The Anniversary

Chapter 72

Astra

4,077 words · 18 min read

On the first morning, the king measured her hands.

Alea hadn’t expected that. She’d expected hammering. She’d lain awake half the night after the cut, listening to the ting, ting, ting coming up from the shore in the dark, slow and steady, and the river song under it, two voices, one deep and one she’d never once heard sing, and she’d thought: tomorrow he’ll make it. Tomorrow I’ll have it.

Instead she came down to the forge at dawn with her father’s tea and the king’s, and the king put his down on the anvil stump without drinking it and said, “Hands.”

She looked at him.

“Your hands,” said the king. “Hold them out. Palms up.”

She held them out. He took them in his.

His hands were enormous. Square and thick-fingered and hard as wood, with a scatter of small white scars across the backs of them, old burns, dozens of them. He turned her hands over in his, the way you’d turn over a tool to see how it was made, and pressed his thumb into her palm, and spread her fingers, and measured the span of them against his own. Then he did her wrists. Then he had her make a fist, and looked at the fist, and had her open it again.

“Good,” he said. “Strong. Long fingers for a fox.” He let go. “Now stand.”

He watched her do her forms on the shingle for an hour.

With the practice sword, the plain wooden one she’d had since she was thirteen. The first form, and the second, and the long slow third one her father had made her do on the ice every morning for three years until she could do it asleep. The king sat on the anvil stump with Captain the hen in his lap and watched her the way her father watched the water before a cast: not her face. Her feet. Her shoulders. Where her weight went. When.

Kestra watched too, from the jetty. Alea could feel it.

“Again,” said the king, after the third form. “Slower.”

She did it again, slower.

“Your weight’s on your back foot,” said the king. “Good. Who taught you?”

“My dad.”

“Of course he did.” He looked across at her father, who was at the far end of the hearth, building up the fire. “He used to stand on his heels. At Cinderhold. I told him about it every day for a month.”

“You did not,” said her father, without turning round.

“I did. You just didn’t listen.” The king looked back at Alea. “He listens now, apparently. He taught you better than he stood.” He put Captain down on the shingle, and stood up, and came over, and took the practice sword out of her hands, and weighed it, and gave it back. “Your arms are a hand’s width longer than his were at your age. Your shoulders are narrower. You’ll never have his reach in the swing. You’ll have more in the thrust.” He nodded, to himself, as if he’d decided something. “Shorter than his. Not much. Narrower. Not much. Same weight in the hand, near enough, once you’ve grown into it.” He looked at her. “Too big for you now. You’ll be cursing it for a year.”

“Good,” said Alea.

The king looked at her for a moment. Then he laughed, a short surprised bark, and went back to the forge.

It took five days.

She’d thought it would take one. You heated it, you hit it, it was a sword. That was what she’d thought, before. She’d watched Garrow at the forge in the square a hundred times on the way to market, making a horseshoe in a quarter of an hour.

This wasn’t like that.

The long half went into the fire at dawn and came out at noon and went back in again. The king worked it in pieces. A hand’s width at a time. Heating the part he meant to shape and nothing else, so the rest stayed cool and remembered what it was. And then on the anvil, with the small hammer, ting, ting, ting, slow, patient, moving the iron a hair at a time, and then back into the fire before it cooled too far, because, he said, iron worked cold was iron that cracked.

He drew it out first. Narrower. The broad black slab of the blade, the part that had been her father’s, coming in under the hammer by a finger’s width along its whole length, so slowly that you couldn’t see it happen, only that it had. Then the new point, where the cut had been. A day on that alone. Shaping the end of the long half into a point it had never had, a broad heavy wedge of a tip, not sharp like a needle, sharp like an axe.

Her father struck for him.

That was the part Alea would remember. For the big blows, the ones that moved a lot of iron at once, the king held the work on the anvil with the tongs and the small hammer, and pointed with the small hammer where he wanted the blow, and her father stood across the anvil from him with the big sledge and brought it down exactly there. Ting went the king’s hammer, on the spot. BANG went her father’s sledge, on the same spot. Ting. BANG. Ting. BANG. Like a heartbeat with an echo. The king said a striker was the hardest job at the forge, harder than the smith’s, because you had to hit exactly where you were told, exactly as hard as you were told, and not one hair more, and not think. Her father didn’t think. He hit exactly where he was told. The king said, on the second day, that he’d never had a better striker in his life, and her father said, I was always good at doing what I was told, and the king laughed so hard he nearly dropped the tongs.

Alea worked the bellows.

With Garrow, at first, and then on her own, while Garrow went and stood beside the king and watched every blow with his mouth open. She worked them for five days. Slow and even, the way the king said. Like breathing. Her arms ached at night in a way they’d never ached from forms, and in the morning she came down and did it again.

She didn’t mind. She’d never in her life felt so much like she was part of something.

The town came every day.

Not down to the shore. Rowan stood at the top of the shingle every morning with his arms folded, and the town stopped behind him along the field wall in a long line, and watched from there. But they came. Every morning. Old men with their pipes. Women with their knitting. Children sitting on the wall with their legs swinging, cheering every time her father’s sledge came down, until their mothers hushed them, and then cheering again.

On the third day, a boy of about seven came down the lake road on his own and stood at the edge of the shingle, just past Rowan’s elbow, holding a stone the size of his fist. He held it out toward the forge.

Rowan looked down at him. Then he looked at Alea’s father.

Her father put down the sledge, and walked up the shingle, and went down on one knee in front of the boy, and took the stone, and looked at it carefully, and said thank you. And the boy went bright red and ran back up to the field wall, where his mother caught him, and the whole town laughed.

After that, there was a stone every morning. Sometimes two. Her father took every one. He set them in a little pile by the anvil stump, and by the fifth day the pile was knee-high, and the king said what are you going to do with all those? and her father said I’ll think of something.

On the second day, while the iron was in the fire, the king asked her about her mother.

He asked it plainly, the way he asked everything. They were sitting on the shingle at the edge of the hearth, the two of them, while her father worked the far bellows and the half-made sword lay in the heart of the charcoal, going from red to orange.

“Do you remember her?”

Alea looked at the fire.

“Some things,” she said. “I was nine.” She thought about it. “Her hands. She always had cold hands. She said it was the healing. It took the warm out of her. She used to put them on the back of my neck when I was being stupid, and I’d shriek.” She stopped. “Her voice. Not what she said. Just the sound of it. Mostly I remember her laughing at him.”

“At your father?”

“At everything he did. He’d do something, and she’d laugh at him, and he’d pretend to be offended, and then he’d laugh too.” Alea looked down at her hands. “He hasn’t laughed like that since. Not until…” She stopped. “Not until the other night. At the bellows. With you.”

The king didn’t say anything for a while.

“She stitched my arm once,” he said.

Alea turned and looked at him.

“At Cinderhold. The year before.” He held up his left forearm and turned it so the firelight caught it, and there, under the burn scars, running from the wrist halfway to the elbow, was a long thin white line. “Something came over the wall in the night. I was careless. She was the only healer awake.” He looked at the scar. “I was the king. I was Ascendant. And she sat me down on a barrel in the yard in the middle of the night and told me to hold still or she’d stitch my mouth shut too.”

Alea’s breath caught.

“I held still,” said the king. “I’ve never held so still in my life.” He lowered his arm. “She had cold hands. You’re right. I remember thinking she must have had them in a bucket of snow.” He looked at Alea. “Your eyes are hers. Did you know that? You look at people the way she did. Like she was deciding whether you were worth the trouble.”

Alea found she couldn’t say anything at all.

“She decided I was,” said the king. “Eventually. I’ve always been proud of that.”

He got up, and went back to the fire, and took the iron out, and laid it on the anvil. And Alea sat on the shingle with her knees drawn up, looking at the long white scar on the king’s forearm going up and down with the hammer, ting, ting, ting, and cried, without any noise, until her father looked round from the bellows, and then didn’t look away until she’d stopped.

On the fourth day they quenched it.

The king said it would be loud. It was louder than that.

He heated the whole of it, this time. Not a hand’s width. All of it, from the new point to the start of the grip, in the long hearth, for hours, with Alea on one bellows and Garrow on the other and her father building the fire up and up until the heat coming off the hearth was a wall you could lean on. The iron went red, and then orange, and then a colour Alea had never seen iron go, a deep glowing gold, the colour of the sun going down behind the Redmarch, the king said, the right colour, the only colour.

Then the king and her father lifted it out.

Together, with two pairs of long tongs, one at each end. It hung between them, glowing, the light of it lighting both their faces from below, and they walked it down the shingle, slowly, step by step, into the lake.

Into the water. Up to their knees, up to their thighs, the two of them, in their clothes, with the golden iron between them. And then the king said now, and they plunged it.

The lake screamed.

That was the only word for it. The water round the iron exploded into steam, a great white roaring column of it that went up past the jetty, past the cabin roof, twenty feet, thirty, boiling and hissing and shrieking, so loud the whole town on the field wall flinched back as one. The surface of the lake for ten paces round the two men went white and boiled. The steam rolled out over the water in a great cloud and hid them completely.

Alea stood at the bellows with her hands over her mouth.

Then the steam began to thin. And she could see them again, standing waist-deep in the lake in their soaked clothes, with the long black shape between them, quiet now, steaming gently in the cold water. Her father’s face was wet. The king’s was too. She couldn’t tell if it was the steam.

“There,” said the king, in the sudden silence. His voice carried all the way up the shore. “Now it’s hers.”

On the fifth day, the king rewrapped the grip.

He’d unwound the old leather on the morning of the cut, before the sword ever went into the fire. Carefully, a turn at a time, the whole long strip of it, dark and smooth and soft with eight years of weather and years of her father’s hands before that, and rolled it up and put it in his own coat, next to his chest, where it wouldn’t dry out. He’d shortened the grip since. Not by much. A hand’s width. Enough that it was a grip for a girl’s two hands and not a man’s.

Now he took the leather out of his coat and unrolled it and wrapped it back on, a turn at a time, tight and even, with Alea watching every turn. It didn’t all fit. It was too long now, by a hand’s width. When he got to the end of the grip he cut off what was left, very carefully, with his knife, and held it out to her.

“Keep that,” he said.

She took the little strip of old dark leather and held it in her hand. It was warm from his coat.

And there, just above where the leather ended, at the base of the blade, on the black iron, the crooked letters. Scratched with the point of a knife, eight years ago, by a man kneeling in the red dirt. Some deeper than others. One gone over twice.

The king hadn’t touched them. Five days of hammering and heat and the lake, and he’d worked round them, every time, the way you’d walk round a sleeping child.

Then he gave it to her.

He didn’t make a speech. He wasn’t that kind of king. He just picked it up off the anvil, by the grip, in one big scarred hand, and turned, and held it out to her across the shingle.

Alea looked at it.

It was still enormous. That was the first thing. It was still a slab. Black, from the new axe-sharp point to the pommel, the old iron pommel like a fist that the king had kept, unchanged, on the end. With its point on the shingle, the blade alone came up to her shoulder. Broad as her hand and a half across. Thick at the spine. It didn’t shine. It lay across the king’s palm and drank the morning light, the way it always had, the way it had leaned by the cabin door when she was too small to stand.

It was shorter than her father’s. Narrower. Lighter. A little. It was still the most dangerous-looking thing she’d ever seen in her life.

And it was hers.

She reached out and took the grip in both hands. The old leather. It fit. Her fingers closed round it and met, the way they hadn’t met round her father’s.

The king let go.

The weight came into her arms all at once.

She’d been expecting it. She hadn’t been ready for it. It pulled at her shoulders, at her back, at her knees, the whole black length of it trying to go down to the shingle and take her with it. For a moment she thought it would.

She let the aura come up.

Not pushing. Rising, like water in a jar. Down through her back foot, so it had somewhere to stand. Out on the breath. And then, because she was Tempered now, the second tier, the thing the second tier was for: in. Into her bones. Into the long muscles of her back and her arms and her legs, hardening them, the way the lake had hardened the iron. She felt herself go hard all over, like a tree in winter.

The weight was still there. It would always be there. But she was standing up under it.

She lifted it.

Up off the shingle. Up to her waist, the way she’d lifted her father’s on the rag rug, where it had stopped. Past her waist. Up. Her arms shook. Her teeth were clenched so hard her jaw hurt. Up to her shoulder, and over, the blade going back behind her, the whole black weight of it hanging there over her shoulder, and her father’s voice in her head from the ice, every morning for three years: don’t throw it. Let it fall. Your job is to stay out of the way.

She let it fall.

It came round in a long flat arc and roared.

There was no other word. The air in front of her split open with a sound like a sheet being torn in half, and the black blade went through the place where the air had been and kept going, round, dragging her with it, and she went with it, half a turn on the shingle, her feet sliding, and nearly fell. Nearly. She didn’t.

She stood there, breathing like a bellows, with the point of the sword on the shingle at her feet where it had finally stopped, and her whole body shaking.

Nobody said anything.

She lifted it again. Up over her shoulder. Let it fall. This time she knew where it was going, and moved her feet before it went, and it came round and roared and stopped, and she was still standing exactly where she’d started.

The third time, she didn’t think at all.

She just lifted, and let it fall, and the aura went down into her back foot and up into her bones and out along her arms into the old leather and the black iron, and the blade came round, and it didn’t roar, this time. It sang. One long low clean note, like the note on the day of the cut, but deeper. The air didn’t tear. It just opened for it, and closed behind it, and the sword stopped exactly where she meant it to stop, a hand’s width above the shingle, perfectly still.

The field wall went mad.

She heard it before she understood it. Two hundred people, the whole of Wrenhaven, standing along the wall above the shore, and all of them, at once, roaring. Children shrieking. Women banging their knitting needles together. Old men on their feet with their pipes in the air. Rowan in front of them with his arms still folded and his big grey face split in half.

Alea stood on the shingle with the sword in her hands and looked up at them, astonished.

And then past them. Up the hill, above the town, to the long timber hall with the yard in front of it, and the upper window.

Five faces. Crammed into it. All five of them, the way they’d been at that window every day for a week. Too far away to see properly. She didn’t need to see. She knew which one was which. She knew Gigichi would have her arms folded and her eyes wet and be pretending she didn’t. She knew Xola would be bouncing. She knew Higu would be writing. She knew PenguChi would be very still, with her hand pressed flat on the glass. She knew Thea would be looking at her window, and then at the lake, and then at her window again.

She lifted one hand off the grip, and waved.

All five hands went up at the window and waved back.

“It needs a name,” said her father.

It was evening. The king had gone up to the shed with the hen. Kestra was on the step. The fire was banked. Alea was sitting on the end of the jetty with her feet over the water and the sword across her knees, the way she’d seen her father sit with the old one on the rug, and her father was sitting beside her.

“A name?”

“It’s yours,” said her father. “You name it.”

Alea looked down at the black blade across her knees. In the last of the light, she could just see the letters by the grip.

“I could call it Alea,” she said.

“Please don’t.”

She laughed. She couldn’t help it. “You called everything Alea. You told me. Every horse. Every pet. Every companion in every game you ever played.”

“I know.”

“And then you called me Alea.”

“I’d been practising,” said her father. “For a long time.” He looked out at the lake. “You’re the only one who ever deserved it. The sword can find its own.”

Alea sat with that for a while.

Then she put her finger on the crooked letters by the grip.

“What does each one mean?” she said. “Not all together. I know all together. Each word.”

Her father leaned over and looked at them. He didn’t touch them.

“Ad,” he said. “To. Toward.” His finger hovered over the next word. “Astra. The stars.” The next. “Per. Through.” The last, the one gone over twice. “Aspera. The rough things. The hard places.” He sat back. “To the stars, through the hard places.”

Alea looked at the four words for a long time.

“You had the hard places,” she said at last. “You’ve had them for eight years. You carried them out there and stood them over Mum.” She traced the letters with her fingertip. “And half of it’s going back to her now. The half with the hard places in it. That’s where it should be. With her.” She looked up at him. “This half isn’t going to the hard places. This half’s going the other way.”

Her father didn’t say anything.

“Astra,” said Alea.

She said it carefully. In the language nobody in this world could read. Her father’s language. The language of his statuses and his Steam groups and the words he’d scratched into iron, kneeling in the red, the only thing he could think of that wasn’t sorry.

“Astra,” she said again. “The stars. That’s where it’s going.”

Her father looked at her for a long time, in the dusk.

“That’s where you’re going,” he said.

“That’s what I said,” said Alea.

She took it to bed with her.

Not into the bed. Even she wasn’t that stupid. But she leaned it against the wall beside the bed, where she could reach it, the way it had leaned by the cabin door when she was very small. And she lay there in the dark, with her arms aching from five days of bellows and three swings that had nearly killed her, and looked at the long black shape against the wall in the moonlight.

She opened her window before she slept.

Five. Two since the rag rug. She’d have to tell PenguChi in the morning. PenguChi would want to know exactly how.

Downstairs, her father was sitting by the hearth. She could hear him, through the floor. Not reading. Not writing. Just sitting. And after a while, very quietly, so quietly she wasn’t sure at first, humming. The river song. The king’s.

She fell asleep before he finished.

Outside, on the shingle by the jetty, beside the banked forge, the other half of the sword lay on the warm stones at the edge of the hearth, broad and black, waiting its turn. Beside it, knee-high, a pile of stones the size of a child’s fist.
