The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 1: Thirty-Four Winters

Chapter 20

The Infirmary

2,734 words · 12 min read

The crane broke on a Tuesday, a little after noon, and Rachael heard it from the infirmary: a crack like a tree going over, and then the long rattling roar of a chain running out, and then the screaming.

By the time they carried the man in, she’d already washed her hands.

She didn’t remember deciding to. She’d simply been folding linen in the back room, and then she was at the basin, scrubbing, and then she was at the door with her sleeves rolled up, and Sister Maud was beside her saying who taught you that in a voice that didn’t expect an answer.

Nobody had taught her. She’d just watched it done, for years, from the other side of the bed.

They put him on the long table by the window. He was a dockworker, big and grey-bearded, and the crane’s falling arm had caught him across the legs and the lower part of his belly, and there was so much blood that for a second Rachael couldn’t see where it was coming from.

Then she could.

Not with her eyes. With her hands. She put them on him, flat, the way she’d been doing for weeks with feverish children and coughing old men and a bargeman with a gashed arm. And under her palms the warmth came up, and with it the knowing: where it hurt, where it was wrong, where the life was leaking out of him like water through a cracked jar.

“Here,” she said. “And here. It’s worse here.”

Sister Maud didn’t ask how she knew. She just pressed a wad of linen into Rachael’s hands and said, “Then hold it. Hard. Don’t let go until I tell you.”

So Rachael held it.

There were two healers in the river-gate infirmary who could actually heal: Sister Maud, who was old and fierce and Candle-tier, and Brother Tamsin, who was young and nervous and had reached Spark the year before. Everyone else, the dozen sisters and brothers and volunteers who came and went, did what Rachael did. They washed and fed and held and carried. They sat with people.

The healers worked on the dockworker for two hours.

Rachael held the wound in his belly for all of it. Her arms started shaking in the first half hour. By the second hour she couldn’t feel her hands at all, except for the warmth in them, which never went away, and the knowing, which never went away either. She knew, every second, exactly how much was leaking out of him and from where. When it got worse she said so, and Sister Maud moved, and chanted something low and fast, and a faint golden light came off her fingers and went into him, and it got better.

Rachael watched the light. She couldn’t help it. It came off Sister Maud’s hands the way heat comes off a stove, and went into the man, and found the torn places, and sat in them like water settling into cracks. It didn’t fix them. Not all at once. But it held them, the way her own hands were holding the wound, until his body could start to hold them itself.

That’s it, she thought. That’s what I’m for.

She’d never been so sure of anything.

At the end of the second hour, Sister Maud sat back on her heels, and wiped her forehead with her wrist, and said, “You can let go now.”

Rachael couldn’t. Her hands wouldn’t open. Brother Tamsin had to peel her fingers off the linen one at a time, very gently, while she sat there on the floor by the table with tears running down her face that she hadn’t noticed starting.

“He’ll live,” said Sister Maud. “He’ll walk with a stick, but he’ll live.” She looked down at Rachael. “He’ll live because you held that for two hours without moving, and because you told me where it was getting worse before I could see it.” She paused. “How did you know?”

“I could feel it,” said Rachael. “In my hands.”

Sister Maud looked at her for a long time.

“Come with me,” she said.

Sister Maud’s room was very small and very bare: a bed, a desk, a window over the river, a shelf of books with cracked spines. She sat Rachael down on the bed and put a cup of something hot into her shaking hands and sat down at the desk across from her.

“What’s your number?” she said.

Rachael blinked. “My…?”

“Your window. The summoned all have them. Everyone knows.” Sister Maud’s voice was blunt and not unkind. “What’s your number?”

“Fourteen,” said Rachael. “It went up three today.”

Sister Maud closed her eyes briefly.

“Do you know how rare healers are?” she said. “Real ones. Not volunteers, not sitters, not people who can wrap a bandage. People with a healer’s mana.” She didn’t wait for an answer. “One in a few hundred mages. Maybe fewer. I trained thirty years ago with eleven others. Three of us made it past Spark. One of us made it past Candle, and she’s dead now.” She opened her eyes. “And you have a window that tells you exactly how close you are to the next tier, and hands that can feel a wound I can’t see yet, at fourteen percent.”

Rachael didn’t know what to say.

“Every healer’s order in the kingdom will want you,” said Sister Maud. “Every lord with a sick child. Every general with a war coming. And you’re going to have to learn to say no to most of them, because there’s only one of you.” She leaned back. “I’m telling you this now, while you’re still an Initiate, so that when they come, you won’t be surprised.”

“When who comes?”

Sister Maud was quiet for a moment.

“Healers are valuable,” she said at last. “To everyone. To us. To the king.” She looked out of the window at the river. “Seven years ago, on the Redmarch, the demons took a healer. Just one. Out of a whole army. They didn’t kill her. They took her somewhere they could all see, and did something to her that nobody understood.” She turned back. “Nobody knows why they chose her. But I’ve always thought it was because she was a healer. Because there’s something about our mana that… takes things in. Holds them.” She shrugged. “That’s just an old woman’s guess.”

Rachael sat with her cup going cold in her hands and thought about the letter that had come from Thornwall two weeks ago. Someone wearing someone. Families going visiting at night and never coming back.

“I’ll be careful,” she said.

“Be more than careful,” said Sister Maud. “Be watched.”

The stranger came eight days later.

It was late afternoon, and quiet. Rachael was sitting with the little girl from the first week, who had come back with a cough. A man came in at the river door, and stood there for a moment, looking around, the way people did the first time they came into the infirmary and weren’t sure where to go.

He was well dressed. Not like a lord, but like a lord’s man: a good dark coat, clean boots, a neat grey beard, a pleasant open face. He had a hat in his hands, and he turned it round by the brim while he waited.

Brother Tamsin went to him. They talked for a moment. Then Tamsin looked over at Rachael, and back at the man, and said something, and the man smiled and came across the room toward her.

“Forgive me,” he said. His voice was warm and courteous and very slightly accented, in a way she couldn’t place. “Are you the summoned girl? The healer?”

Rachael’s skin prickled.

She didn’t know why. He was perfectly polite. He’d stopped a respectful distance away. He was smiling in a friendly, slightly apologetic way, like a man who knew he was interrupting.

“I’m a volunteer,” she said. “Can I help you?”

“I hope so. I’m here on behalf of my master. He has a household in the hills, west of here, a large one, and he has heard a great deal about the summoned. About you, in particular.” He turned his hat round once more. “He’d very much like to offer you a position. A healer’s position, in his house. Under his protection. You’d want for nothing.”

“I’m not looking for a position,” said Rachael.

“Of course. Of course. Not yet.” He smiled again. “But perhaps you’d let me tell you a little more about it? My master is very generous. And very patient. There’s no hurry. We could walk, perhaps, by the river, and I could—“

“Rae.”

Deston was standing in the river door.

He’d come to walk her home. He’d been doing it every day since Thornwall’s letter: someone from the group, every evening, without fail, even though Rachael had told them it was ridiculous. Tonight it was Deston, tall and easy in the doorway with his hands in his pockets, smiling his friendly, networking smile.

Except his eyes weren’t smiling. They were fixed on the stranger, and they’d gone very wide.

“There you are,” said Deston pleasantly. “We’re late. Emrys will kill us.” He came across the room, unhurried, and put a hand on Rachael’s shoulder, and turned his smile on the stranger. “Sorry. Did we interrupt something?”

“Not at all,” said the stranger, smiling back. “I was just telling the young lady about an opportunity.”

“How kind.” Deston’s hand tightened on her shoulder, very slightly. “She’ll think about it, I’m sure. Won’t you, Rae? Come on. Emrys.”

And he walked her out of the infirmary, past the stranger, out of the river door and into the street, without hurrying and without looking back. He didn’t let go of her shoulder until they’d turned two corners. Then he stopped, and leaned against a wall, and let out a long shaky breath.

“Dest,” said Rachael. “What—“

“He didn’t have any,” said Deston.

“Any what?”

“Threads.” Deston was staring at the street, back the way they’d come. His face had gone pale. “Rae, everyone has threads. Everyone. Strangers who walk past each other in the street have them, thin grey ones, just from being near each other for a second. You’ve got dozens. Tamsin’s got hundreds; he knows everyone in that infirmary. I’ve never seen a single person with none.” He swallowed. “He had none. Not to you, not to Tamsin, not to me. Not to anyone. He was standing in a room full of people and nothing in the world was tied to him at all.”

Rachael felt something cold go all the way down her back.

“Someone wearing someone,” she said quietly.

Deston looked at her. He didn’t say anything. He didn’t need to.

They told the others that night, all of them, round the table in the rooms above the chandler’s, with the lamps lit and the shutters closed.

“Every word,” said Dennis, when Deston had finished. “Every single word that man said was a lie. I’d bet my share of the salt on it.” He was turning a coin over and over on the table. “My master in the hills. He didn’t even try to make it sound real.”

“He didn’t need to,” said Sabine. She was leaning against the wall by the window, arms folded, the way she stood at the bargemaster’s when a deal was going wrong. “He wasn’t trying to convince her. He was trying to get her outside. Alone. By the river.” She looked at Rachael. “I’ve guarded caravans for fifteen years. I know what that is.”

“The hills,” said Calvin. “West. That’s where Coldwater is. That’s where the families went visiting.”

Nobody said anything for a while.

Emrys had been sitting very still in the corner, his fingers steepled. “I’ll write to the Advisor,” he said. “Tonight. By the fast courier.” He looked at Rachael. “And you won’t go to the infirmary alone again. Not once.”

“I’m not stopping,” said Rachael.

“I didn’t ask you to stop. I asked you not to go alone.”

“I’ve been protected my whole life,” said Rachael. She was surprised by how steady her voice came out. “My whole life. Stuck in rooms. Not allowed to go places, in case I caught something. Watched over. Kept safe.” She put her hands flat on the table. They’d stopped shaking hours ago. “I held a man’s insides together for two hours today. I’m not going back into a room.”

“Nobody’s putting you in a room,” said Gabe.

He’d been quiet all evening. He was sitting across from her with his big forge-scarred hands on the table, and he reached into his coat and took something out, and put it down in front of her.

It was a knife. A small one, plain, with a wrapped grip and a short straight blade. The steel had a faint ripple in it, like water.

“Made it at the forge,” said Gabe. “Last week. After the letter. I was going to give it to you anyway.” He shrugged, slightly embarrassed. “It’s not very good. It’s my first one.”

Rachael picked it up. It fit her hand exactly.

“Nobody’s putting you in a room,” Gabe said again. “We’re just going to be in it with you. Wherever you go.”

They made a plan. It wasn’t much of one. But it was theirs.

Someone would walk her to the infirmary every morning and home every evening: Deston, mostly, or Gabe, or Calvin, who had taken to sitting on the roof of the warehouse opposite the river gate with his legs dangling, watching the street like a lookout in a crow’s nest. Dennis and Sabine would ask around. Sabine knew every bargemaster on the river and Dennis knew how to tell when they were lying, and between them, if a man in a good coat with a pleasant face and no threads had come into Kingsferry, somebody would have seen him come.

And Deston would look. Every face, in every street, every day. For anyone standing in a crowd with nothing tied to them.

“That’s a lot of faces,” said Rachael.

“I’m good at faces,” said Deston. “It’s my whole thing.”

Three days later, Dennis came back from the docks with a face like thunder.

“Nobody knows him,” he said. “Nobody. Not one bargemaster, not one ferryman, not one innkeeper on the whole waterfront. Nobody saw him come in on a boat. Nobody saw him come in on the road. Nobody rented him a room.” He sat down heavily. “Sabine asked a man she’s known for ten years, and he said, what man?”

“He was right there,” said Rachael. “Tamsin talked to him. I talked to him.”

“I know. I’m not saying he wasn’t there.” Dennis rubbed his face. “I’m saying that as far as the whole of Kingsferry’s concerned, he was never anywhere.”

That night, Rachael sat on the edge of her bed with Gabe’s knife in her lap and her window open in front of her.

Fifteen. It had gone up again, after the dockworker. After two hours of holding.

She thought about Sister Maud saying be watched. She thought about the stranger turning his hat round and round by the brim, smiling, with nothing in the whole world tied to him. She thought about the healer on the Redmarch seven years ago, the one the demons had taken, out of a whole army, and done something to that nobody understood.

She thought about her whole life before this, in rooms, being kept safe.

Then she closed her window, and put the knife under her pillow, and lay down, and listened to the sounds of the house: Calvin snoring through the wall, Dennis and Sabine still arguing softly about salt in the kitchen, Emrys humming something low and steady downstairs as he wrote his letter to the Advisor. Deston’s door was open a crack, and she could see the light under it, and she knew he was sitting up, looking out of the window at the street, watching for a face with nothing tied to it.

For the first time in her life, she wasn’t being kept safe in a room.

She was being kept safe in a house. By people who’d decided to.

It turned out to be a completely different thing.
