The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 2: Letters

Chapter 58

Firewood

3,872 words · 17 min read

Wendy didn’t believe in much.

She’d have told you that herself, if you’d asked, and probably if you hadn’t. She didn’t believe in luck, or fate, or horoscopes, or people who said everything happens for a reason. She didn’t believe the Advisor had told them the whole truth in the summoning hall. She didn’t believe there was a way home, not really, not the way Yan did. She didn’t believe in the Hero of the Redmarch, or the songs about him, or the soldiers who lowered their voices when they said his name.

She believed in Salty. That was about it.

She’d come through the floor because Salty had. She’d come to Cinderhold because Salty had. She stood on the wall in the cold at night with a bow in her hands and arrows that curved round posts because Salty was on the wall too, and somebody had to watch his back, and it might as well be her. If you go, I go. She’d said it in the hall, the first day, and she’d said it on the wall in the winter, and she’d meant it both times.

It didn’t mean she believed him. It meant she loved him. Those weren’t the same thing, and she’d always been very careful to keep them apart.

She still wore her headphones. That was the stupid thing. Lavender over-ears, round her neck, the way she’d been wearing them on stream the night the floor went soft. They’d come through because she’d been wearing them, the way Hyvred’s glasses had and Ina’s mask. They didn’t do anything. There was nothing in this world to plug them into. They were just silent.

She wore them anyway. She didn’t believe in much. But she believed in keeping things that had come through with you.

The sword was late.

It should have been at Cinderhold a month ago. Aldous had said so, in the spring: the winch party leaves the capital at the start of summer, a month on the road, and it’ll be here by high summer, with weeks to spare. Instead, the summer had gone on, and on, and the letters had come from the road one after another, each one more apologetic than the last. A broken axle outside the capital. Rain for a fortnight in the hills, and a ford that couldn’t be crossed. A second axle. A bridge that wouldn’t take the weight, and a three-day detour round it. Six soldiers and a winch and a slab of black iron that six men could barely move, crawling east across the kingdom at the speed of an ox.

“It doesn’t want to come,” said Maigo, in the chapel, after the fourth letter. Half joking.

Nobody laughed.

It came at last on a hot still afternoon in the last weeks of summer, a week before the anniversary. Wendy was on the wall when she saw it: a cart, far down the west road, crawling, with a great timber frame on its back like the skeleton of a ship. Six figures walking beside it. Dust hanging in the air behind it, gold in the low sun.

She watched it come for an hour. It took that long to cover the last mile.

The whole fort came out to meet it.

Wendy hadn’t expected that. She’d expected Aldous, and Rhea, and the five of them, and maybe the gate sergeant. Instead, by the time the cart creaked in through the west gate, there were soldiers on every wall and in every doorway and lined up along both sides of the courtyard, dozens of them, in silence. The cook had come out of the kitchen with flour on his arms. The smith had come out of the forge. The old woman who mended the uniforms had come out of the laundry and was standing at the front of the crowd with her hands folded, very straight.

Nobody had told them to. They’d just come.

Wendy understood it a moment later, looking at their faces. Half the men in Cinderhold had been on the Redmarch seven years ago. The rest had served under men who had. Every one of them knew what was on that cart. Every one of them had looked out across the red, at some point, on some cold night, at the black shape standing over a grave in the middle of nothing, and felt whatever it was you felt, looking at that.

And then one day it hadn’t been there. And now it was coming back.

The cart stopped in the middle of the courtyard. The six soldiers of the winch party stood beside it, filthy, exhausted, sunburned, looking as if they’d walked across the whole kingdom, which they had. One of them, a grizzled corporal with a broken nose, went up to Aldous and saluted.

“Brought it, Father,” he said hoarsely. “Bloody thing.”

“Thank you, corporal,” said Aldous quietly. “I know.”

The corporal turned and pulled the canvas off.

It was bigger than Wendy had imagined.

She’d heard about it all year. From Aldous, from Rhea, from the soldiers, from Yan. She’d read Cursiffe’s lines about it in the channel. She’d imagined something big. She hadn’t imagined this.

It lay on the bed of the cart like something that had fallen out of the sky. A blade of black iron as long as a man was tall, and as broad across as Wendy’s shoulders, and as thick as her wrist along the spine. Its edge was notched in a dozen places, deep notches, as if it had been used to cut through things that weren’t meant to be cut. Its grip was wrapped in old leather gone dark and smooth with handling, long enough for two big hands. There was no guard to speak of. Just the slab of the blade, and the grip, and the plain iron pommel at the end like a fist.

It didn’t shine. It wasn’t the kind of iron that shone. It just lay there in the late sun, black and heavy and still, and drank the light.

Nobody in the courtyard said anything.

Then Maigo, who could never stand a silence, went up to the side of the cart, and leaned over, and looked at the place just above where the grip met the blade.

“There,” he said. Very quietly, for Maigo. “There it is.”

They all went and looked. One after another. Wendy went last.

It was scratched into the iron. Not engraved. Not carved properly, the way a smith would have done it, with a chisel and a hammer. Scratched, rough and uneven, as if someone had done it with the point of a knife, or the point of another blade, kneeling in the dirt. The letters were crooked. Some of them were deeper than others. One had been gone over twice, as if the first time it hadn’t been deep enough.

But they were letters. Their letters. She could read them. Of course she could; she’d been reading them all her life.

AD ASTRA PER ASPERA.

She’d read it in the channel a month ago, in Cursiffe’s lines. It was different, seeing it. It was very different. Seeing it scratched into black iron by somebody’s hand, somebody who’d knelt over a grave on the red earth seven years ago and dug these letters into his own sword one by one, crooked, going over one of them twice because it wasn’t deep enough.

To the stars, through hardships.

Without meaning to, Wendy put her hand up to her necklace. The long chain, with the little star on it and the crescent moon. She’d worn it on stream for years. She’d never thought about it much.

She thought about it now.

“Ice used to put that kind of thing on everything,” said Salty, beside her. Very low. “Gabe said. In the channel. His statuses. His Steam groups.” He was staring at the scratched letters. “I never knew that. I played CS with him for years. I never once looked at his status.”

“Neither did I,” said Wendy.

“Yan?”

Yan was standing on the other side of the cart. He hadn’t come close. He was looking at the sword from a pace away, with his pale eyes very still and Suzu wound tight round his neck. He didn’t answer.

Aldous said it had to come off the cart before dark. It couldn’t stay out in the courtyard overnight; there were too many soldiers, and too many of them wanted to touch it, and Aldous wouldn’t have it. It would go into the chapel, on trestles, under guard, until they left.

The winch party started to rig the frame. Six of them, with ropes and pulleys and the great iron hook, the way they’d done it in the summoning hall and in every inn yard across the kingdom for two months.

“I’ll do it,” said Salty.

Everyone looked at him.

He’d stepped up to the side of the cart without seeming to decide to, the way he did most things. He stood there, short and square and enormous across the shoulders, looking down at the sword with his blunt face set.

“Lad,” said the corporal with the broken nose, not unkindly. “It took six of us and the frame to get it on there. You won’t shift it.”

“I’m Drawn now,” said Salty. As if that settled it.

“I’m Gleaming,” said Rhea, from the crowd, “and I wouldn’t try.”

“You’d try,” said Salty. “You’d just lose.”

Rhea opened her mouth, and closed it, and then, because she was Rhea and could never resist a contest, shoved her way through to the cart and grabbed the end of the grip with both hands and heaved.

The sword moved. Wendy saw it. The pommel end came up off the bed of the cart, a finger’s width, two, with Rhea’s face going red and her aura flaring hot round her like a forge being blown. She held it there for three heartbeats. Then she let it down, very carefully, and stepped back, breathing hard.

“Fine,” she said. “Fine. Your turn. You’ll lose.”

Salty didn’t say anything. He climbed up onto the cart.

He crouched down beside the sword. He looked at it for a long moment, the way he looked at a weight in the yard before he lifted it, measuring. Then he put one hand on the grip, high up, near the blade, and the other hand under the flat of the blade itself, halfway along.

Wendy felt it, from where she stood. She couldn’t feel aura the way the swordsmen could, not properly. But she’d been standing next to Salty for a year, and she’d felt it come up in him in the mess hall the night he broke the table, whump, like oil on a fire. She felt it now. Not all at once, this time. Slowly. Rising. Up from the very bottom of him, steady and deep, like water filling a jar.

He stood up.

With the sword.

Not all the way. Not easily. His arms were shaking and his face had gone dark red and the veins stood out in his neck like cords. But he stood up, on the bed of the cart, with the black slab of it across his body, the grip in one hand and the blade resting on his other forearm, the way you’d carry a long heavy plank. And then he stepped down off the cart, very carefully, one foot and then the other, onto the cobbles of the courtyard.

And walked.

Three steps. Four. Five. Across the courtyard, toward the chapel door, with the whole of Cinderhold watching in total silence, and the sword across his body, and his boots scraping on the stones.

On the seventh step, he stopped, and knelt, very carefully, and laid it down on the cobbles. And stayed there, on one knee beside it, with his hands on his thighs, breathing like a bellows.

Nobody made a sound.

Then Aldous laughed.

Not loudly. A small, cracked, astonished sound, as if it had been surprised out of him. He was standing by the chapel door with his hand over his mouth.

“Like firewood,” he said.

Everyone looked at him.

“The man who planted it,” said Aldous. His voice was unsteady. “Seven years ago. On the Redmarch. The morning after. I told you, in the spring. Six men and the winch to bring it here. But he…” He shook his head. “He carried it out there alone. Across the red. Miles of it, to her grave. On his shoulder. I watched him go from the wall.” He looked at Salty, kneeling on the cobbles beside the black sword, with his chest heaving. “He carried it like firewood. Like it was nothing. Like a man carrying a bundle of sticks home for the stove.”

Salty looked up at him.

“It’s not nothing,” said Salty, between breaths.

“No,” said Aldous. “No. It never was.” He wiped his eyes. “But you carried it, lad. Seven steps. Drawn, and you carried it seven steps.” He shook his head again. “I didn’t think anyone could, but him.”

Wendy stood at the edge of the crowd and looked at Salty kneeling beside the sword.

She didn’t believe in much.

But she found, standing there, that she was wondering.

Not about the sword. Not about the Hero. About the man. The one who’d carried it two miles across the red on his shoulder, like firewood, the morning after the worst night of his life. Who’d knelt over a grave and scratched four words into his own blade with the point of a knife. Crooked. Going over one of them twice.

To the stars, through hardships.

Who did that? What kind of man carried his own sword to a grave, alone, and then wrote that on it? Not rest in peace. Not I’m sorry. Not her name. To the stars. As if he were still going somewhere. As if, standing over her grave, he’d made her a promise.

And who carved a motto in a dead language from another world, that a boy Wendy had shared a Discord call with for years used to put in his status?

She didn’t believe it. She couldn’t. It didn’t fit. Milli had said so, in the channel, and Milli was right, and Wendy had always been on Milli’s side about things like that.

But she was wondering.

They got it into the chapel in the end, with the frame and the winch, the way it was meant to go. They laid it on two trestles in front of the altar, under the painted ceiling, with a guard on the door. And one by one, as the evening came on, the five of them went in and looked at it again.

Wendy was there when Yan went in.

She hadn’t meant to be. She’d gone in to look at the letters again, on her own, and was standing by the trestles when he came in. He didn’t see her at first. He walked up to the sword very slowly, with Suzu wound round his neck so tight Wendy could see the snake’s muscles standing out under her white scales, and he stopped, a pace away from it, the way he’d stood in the courtyard.

And then he reached out, very slowly, and put his hand on the grip.

Wendy saw his face change.

She’d known Yan for years. She’d watched him carry things. That was what Yan did: he carried things, quietly, on his own, until he’d worked out what they were, and only then did he set them down in front of anyone. He’d carried the cold under the grave all winter. He’d carried the cold one that crossed the red for seven nights, alone on the wall. He’d carried his mother, every day, for a year, and never said a word, and Wendy only knew because she’d seen his face, once, on the wall, at two in the morning.

She’d never seen him look like this.

He took his hand off the grip as if it had burned him.

“Yan,” said Wendy.

He turned round. He hadn’t known she was there. For a moment his face was completely open, the way it never was, and she saw something in it she couldn’t name. Shock. And something under the shock that might have been fear, and might have been something else entirely.

“What?” said Wendy. “What is it?”

He looked at her for a long time.

She thought he wasn’t going to tell her. He never told anyone. He’d tell them later, when he knew, when he’d turned it over and over on his own until it had a shape. He’d tell Salty, or Aldous. Not Wendy. Never Wendy. Wendy was the one who didn’t believe.

Maybe that was why he told her.

“The night the cold one went out,” said Yan. His deep slow voice was very quiet. “I told you. I felt for whatever killed it. I couldn’t feel it. Not because it wasn’t there. Because it was too big. Like trying to see the sea from inside it.”

“I remember.”

“It’s in the sword,” said Yan.

Wendy stared at him.

“Not much. Faint. Old.” He looked back at the black blade on its trestles, and his hand, at his side, closed and opened. “Like the smell of someone on a coat they haven’t worn in years. Worn into the iron. Into the grip, where the hands went.” He swallowed. “But it’s the same, Wendy. The same stillness. The same size. The thing that killed the cold one, a week’s walk north-west of here, in one stroke, without me being able to feel it at all.” He turned back to her. “It held this sword.”

Wendy didn’t say anything for a long time.

The chapel was very quiet. The light coming in through the high windows had gone red and gold, the way it did at the end of a summer day, and it lay across the black iron of the sword and didn’t shine on it at all.

“That’s the Hero,” said Wendy finally. “The man who planted it. The Black Blade. Whatever he is. That’s who you felt.”

“Yes.”

“Then he’s alive.”

“Yes.”

“And he’s somewhere north-west. Somewhere along that line on Aldous’s map. And he killed a demon a week’s walk away, in one stroke, and you couldn’t even feel him do it.” She heard her own voice, very flat. “And he carved that on his sword. In Latin. Like…” She stopped.

Yan didn’t finish it for her. He just looked at her.

“I’m not telling anyone else,” he said. “Not yet. Not until I know what it means. I’m telling you because you saw my face.” He paused. “And because you won’t believe it unless it’s true. So if you ever do believe it, I’ll know it’s true.”

Wendy laughed. She didn’t mean to. It came out short and startled and a bit wet.

“That’s the nicest thing anyone’s ever said to me,” she said. “And it’s an insult.”

“It’s both,” said Yan. “Most good things are.”

The scouts came at dusk.

Wendy was on the wall with Salty when they came. She saw them first, the way she always saw things first: shapes, far out on the red, past the last row of Aldous’s markers, coming out of the dark the way they always came. Tall and thin and bent forward. Moving in bursts. Stopping. Moving again.

But there were more of them than she’d ever seen. Twenty. Twenty-five. More, coming up out of the dusk behind those.

And they weren’t counting.

All year, every time they came, they’d done the same thing. Stopped at the edge of the red, and pointed. At the towers. At the gate. At the well, and the chapel, and the patched place in the wall. Counting. Every time.

They weren’t pointing at anything tonight. They were just standing there, in a long ragged line at the edge of the red, in the dusk, perfectly still. And all of them, every single one, were facing the chapel.

“They know,” said Salty, very quietly, beside her.

“Know what?”

“What came in today.” Salty’s huge hands were tight on the parapet. “They can feel it. Like Yan can. They know it’s here.”

Wendy nocked an arrow. She didn’t draw. She just held it there, on the string, the way she always did, and felt for where it wanted to go.

The scouts stood at the edge of the red for a long time, facing the chapel, in silence. And then, one by one, they turned and went back into the dark. Not running. Not the way they’d run in the winter, when they’d seen the horns over the gate. Walking. Slowly. Like people who’d seen what they came to see, and would come back.

“We leave in six days,” said Salty. “Aldous said. Last week of summer. Me and the sword and the five of us and Rhea and ten soldiers. Half a day across the red to the grave.”

“I know.”

“With that out there.” He tilted his head at the dark, where the scouts had gone. “Watching.”

“I know.”

Salty didn’t say anything for a while. Then he turned his head and looked at her, with his blunt square face, in the last of the light.

“I’m carrying it,” he said. “The last bit. By hand. I’m the only one who can.”

“I know,” said Wendy.

And then, because she’d said it twice before and meant it both times, and because she meant it more now than she ever had:

“If you go, I go.”

Salty looked at her for a long moment. Then he reached over and took her hand off the bow, very carefully, arrow and all, and held it.

“That one I believe,” he said.

She lay awake that night, in the barracks, with her silent headphones round her neck and her hand on the little star on her necklace.

She thought about a boy she’d shared a Discord call with most nights, for years, who she’d never really talked to on his own. Who’d been Yan’s friend, and Salty’s friend, and so by default hers, the way friends of friends are. Who she’d been sad about, when he died, mostly because Yan and Salty were so hurt by it. Who’d put Latin in his status, apparently. She’d never looked.

She thought about a man who’d carried a sword across the red like firewood, and knelt over a grave, and scratched to the stars into the iron with the point of a knife.

She didn’t believe they were the same person. She couldn’t. It didn’t fit.

But she lay there in the dark, turning the little star on its chain between her fingers, and found that for the first time since the summoning hall, she very much wanted to be wrong.

Six days, she thought.

Out on the red, an hour’s walk away, a ring of white and blue flowers and a young green tree were waiting by a grave for a sword that, though none of them knew it yet, would not come back to it the way any of them expected.
