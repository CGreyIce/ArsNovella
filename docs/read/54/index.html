The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 2: Letters

Chapter 54

Mask

4,756 words · 21 min read

Ina had worn a mask for most of his adult life, in one way or another.

The real one came later. The grey oni face with the red-rimmed eyes and the wide fanged grin and the red cords hanging from its mouth: that had been his model’s, on stream, something he’d hang at his shoulder or pull down over his face when he wanted to be someone louder than himself. He’d loved it. It had come through the floor with him, the way worn things did, and he’d woken up in the summoning hall with it lying on the stone beside his hand and a single curved purple horn rising out of his forehead, and a soldier six feet away drawing his sword.

But the other kind of mask, the kind you wore without anyone being able to see it, he’d been wearing much longer than that.

He’d worn it for a long time, with someone who didn’t like his friends. Who didn’t like Yan, especially, and said so, and kept saying so, until it was easier to stop seeing Yan than to keep having the argument. And then easier to stop seeing the others. And then one day he’d looked up and realised he’d been wearing a pleasant, agreeable, perfectly reasonable face for so long that he wasn’t sure what was under it anymore.

When it ended, he’d come back. Carefully. Like a man walking into a room where he wasn’t sure he’d be welcome. He’d joined a call one night, late, without warning, and said hello, and waited for the silence.

There hadn’t been one. Ice had said, Ina! Finally. Okay, you’re on support, we’re losing, as if Ina had been gone ten minutes instead of the better part of forever. As if nothing had happened at all.

He’d taken the mask off that night. He’d never quite put it back on.

Valcrest held its midsummer festival at the Lists, and it was the loudest thing Ina had ever heard.

The whole city came. Every stall in the Street of Armourers moved down onto the great sand of the Lists, under strings of lanterns that ran from the top of the stands to the masts of the master’s platform, so that the whole arena was a bowl of coloured light. There was music, four different bands playing four different songs at once. There was food, mostly on sticks. There were jugglers and fire-eaters and a man with a trained bear that Newish had taken an instant and passionate dislike to, and children everywhere, thousands of them, in paper horns.

And at the centre of it, at midnight, under the torches, there would be an exhibition bout. Lucan had been planning it for a month. The summoned of Valcrest, against a team of champions from the old days: veterans of the Lists, grey-haired and scarred and slow and very, very good. Not to win. Just to show the city what its summoned could do.

“A celebration,” Lucan said, rubbing his hands. “Not a contest. A dance. You’ll lose beautifully, and they’ll love you for it.”

“We’re not going to lose,” said Newish.

“You’re going to lose beautifully,” said Lucan.

Ysolde, on the fence, said nothing at all. But Ina saw her look at the stands. All round the arena, slowly, the way she’d been looking at every crowd for the last week, ever since the letter had come from the capital.

The Advisor’s letter. Lucan had read it to them in the Broken Lance with the door shut. Short and plain and very frightening. Something came for the summoned at Wrenhaven. A hunter, out of Kharvos, of the fourth tier or the fifth. It is dead. It will not be the last. Every posting must be watched. Every night.

It hadn’t said how the thing had died. Ina had noticed that. So had Chris.

So Ysolde watched the stands. And Lucan, under all his showman’s cheer, had doubled the guard on the Broken Lance and put a pair of the city watch at every gate of the Lists. And Ina, standing on the sand in his indigo coat with the mask hung at his shoulder, looked up at ten thousand people under coloured lanterns and wondered which of them, if any, wasn’t a person at all.

The bout started at midnight.

It was, Ina had to admit, beautiful. The old champions were slow, but they’d forgotten more about fighting than any of the five of them had ever known, and they fought like a single creature, smooth and patient and economical, giving ground and taking it back. Group 7 fought the way they’d learned to fight here: split, let them commit, punish the commit. Hako on the far angle with his spear. Chris in the middle, calling. Winter’s frost, which she could bring out now on purpose, a hand’s width of white on the sand that made a man’s foot slide just when he didn’t want it to. Ina’s heat, warm at his sternum, not yet a flame. And Newish, at the edge, listening for everyone’s beat, calling now and left and he’s going, half a heartbeat before anyone else could have known.

The crowd loved it. They roared at every exchange. The children in the paper horns screamed themselves hoarse.

And then, in the middle of a long slow exchange between Hako and a grey old spearman who’d been champion of the Lists before Hako was born, Newish stopped calling.

Ina noticed. Everyone on the team noticed; Newish’s voice was the thread they all moved on. One moment she was calling Hako’s timing, wait, wait, now. The next she wasn’t. She’d gone completely still, at the edge of the sand, with her head turned toward the stands.

Toward the east stand. High up. Near the back.

“Newish,” said Chris.

She didn’t answer. Her face had gone white.

“There’s a beat,” she said. Very quietly. Ina barely heard her over the crowd. “Up there. In the stand. It’s… it’s wrong. It’s so slow. Like a bell. Like a huge bell, a long way down, going once every…” She swallowed. “It’s been there the whole time. I thought it was the music. It’s not the music.” Her voice cracked. “Chris. It’s gathering.”

Ysolde was off the fence before Newish finished speaking.

It came down out of the east stand like a stone falling.

Ina saw it go. A dark shape, rising out of the crowd near the top of the stand, much too tall, throwing off a cloak it had been wearing as it rose. And then it was in the air, over the heads of a thousand screaming people, over the rail, falling, jumping, a leap no man could have made, thirty feet down onto the sand of the Lists.

It landed in a crouch, ten paces from Winter, and the sand jumped. Ina felt it through his boots: a thud like a full barrel dropped off the back of a cart, and a ring of sand thrown up all round it that pattered down on the old champions’ shoes.

It was like the things Group 5 had written about, and not like them. Long, too long, black-skinned, its limbs folded up beneath it the way a spider folds. Too many joints. A head that was the wrong shape, narrow and blind-looking, turning slowly. And cold. Ina could feel it from where he stood: a cold that came off it like the cold from an open door in winter, that made the torches round the arena gutter and lean.

The old champions scattered. The crowd screamed. Up in the stands, somebody was shrieking demon, demon, DEMON, and Ina had a single, absurd, perfectly clear thought: Oh. So this is what one actually looks like.

Then it moved.

It went for Winter.

Of course it did. Ina understood it later; he understood it in the moment, too, with the cold part of his mind that had always stood a little back from everything and watched. Winter was closest. Winter was small. And Winter had horns.

It went for her the way a cat goes for a bird. All at once, with no warning, from stillness to full speed in a single heartbeat. One long black arm unfolding toward her like a blade.

Ysolde got there first.

She’d come off the fence and across the sand faster than Ina had known anyone could move, and she didn’t draw her sword so much as arrive with it, already drawn, already moving, between Winter and the thing. Her aura came out of her in a rush that Ina felt on his skin like heat from an oven. The light ran out past the edge of her blade, a hand’s width, two, the Reaching edge that only a dozen swordsmen in the kingdom could make. It met the black arm halfway, with a sound like a wet sheet torn down the middle.

The arm came off at the elbow. It went spinning past Winter’s head, close enough to lift her hair, and hit the sand behind her, and kept moving: the long black fingers clenching and opening in the dust like a crab on its back. Black stuff came out of the stump in a thick slow rope and steamed where it met the air, and the smell reached Ina a heartbeat later, snuffed candle and wet iron.

The thing didn’t scream. It didn’t make any sound at all. It just folded up, very fast, away from Ysolde’s blade, and unfolded again on her other side. A second arm whipped round. Ysolde turned it with her sword, and turned the next, and the next, and then it found a gap, and Ina saw the black edge of its limb go across Ysolde’s sword arm above the elbow with a sound like a knife through canvas. Her sleeve opened, and the arm under it, and the blood came, bright in the torchlight, down to her wrist in a heartbeat. Her sword hand opened on its own.

Ysolde didn’t make a sound either. She caught the hilt with her other hand before it reached the sand, and stood her ground in front of Winter, and kept fighting.

Everything after that happened at once.

Lucan was on the master’s platform, and fire was coming off him. Real fire, Torch-tier, great rolling gouts of it, thrown down onto the sand like a man throwing buckets of water. The heat went across Ina’s face like an oven door opening, and where it hit, the sand went black and glassy and cracked, and the thing had to fold and twist and dodge to stay out of it. The city watch were coming through the gates with spears. The crowd was a single roaring screaming mass, half of it trying to get out and half of it frozen in its seats.

And the five of them moved.

They didn’t decide to. They didn’t talk about it. They just did what they’d been doing in the Lists for a year, every week, against every kind of team Valcrest could throw at them. They split.

Hako went to the far angle. He always went to the far angle; it was his now. He put himself between the thing and the edge of the sand, between it and the way out, with his spear levelled, and held it. Not attacking. Just there. A line it couldn’t cross without going through him.

Chris went to the middle, calling. Ysolde left, Ysolde LEFT, Lucan cover the right, Hako hold, Winter—

Winter was already moving. She’d fallen back behind Ysolde, out of reach, white-faced, and now she was crouching on the sand with both hands flat on it, and Ina felt the cold come off her. Her cold, not the thing’s. Clean and sharp and clear, like water dripping in a cave. Frost went out from her hands across the sand in a fan, a pace, two paces, three, white and glittering, right under the thing’s feet.

It slipped.

Not much. A long black foot went out from under it on the white, with a squeal like a boot on wet marble, and a joint bent the wrong way with a crack Ina heard over ten thousand people. For a heartbeat it was off balance, and Ysolde’s blade went into the gap and took the leg off at the knee. The leg dropped onto the frost and lay there. The thing came down on the stump with a jolt that shook the sand, and kept coming, on three.

“Ina,” said Newish.

She was right beside him. He hadn’t seen her come. She had her hand on his sleeve, the way she’d had it on Chris’s at the junior lists in the spring, and her face was white and her eyes were enormous and absolutely certain.

“It’s going to go for the gap,” she said. “Between Ysolde and Hako. In a moment. I can hear it gathering. When it goes, it’ll lift its head. Right before. I’ll tell you when.” She swallowed. “You need to be ready.”

“Ready for what?”

Newish looked at him. Then at the mask, hanging at his shoulder.

“You know what,” she said.

He put the mask on.

He’d done it a hundred times. In the Lists, mostly: the crowd booing, the noble boxes sneering, demon, demon, and him reaching up very deliberately and pulling the grey oni face down over his own, so that they could see exactly what they thought they were looking at. It had become a kind of joke between him and the crowd. A dare. The children loved it.

It wasn’t a joke tonight.

The mask came down. The world narrowed to the two red-rimmed eyeholes. And the heat at his sternum, the heat that had been there all year, that he’d been coaxing and nursing and holding like a coal in his cupped hands, rose.

It always did, with the mask on. Hotter. Brighter. As if the face of the thing everyone feared gave it permission. But it had never been more than heat. Warmth at his breastbone. A minute of it, at the most, if he concentrated.

He felt for the gap.

He’d been feeling for it since Thea’s letter. All year. He’d found it, once or twice, in the dark, alone, and never known what to say into it. He’d tried Burn, once, in the yard behind the Broken Lance with the mask on and nobody watching, and the heat had gone into his own hands instead of out of them: two fingertips blistered white, and he couldn’t hold a cup for a week, and Winter had wrapped them for him without asking a single question. He’d tried Fire, and nothing had happened at all. It was a label, not a word. He was a man who cared about words, who’d always cared, who’d spent years finding the right ones for every occasion; and he’d never found the right one for this.

He found it now. With a demon on the sand in front of him, and a horned girl crouched behind his guard with her hands in the frost, and Newish’s hand on his sleeve.

It was the simplest word there was. It was what fire did, when it was ready. What a coal did, when you’d breathed on it long enough. What a heart did, sometimes, when someone you’d thought you’d lost called you by your name.

Catch.

“Now,” said Newish.

The thing lifted its head.

And Ina’s fire caught.

It came out of him like breath.

Not a gout, like Lucan’s. Not a wall. A single flame, the size of his hand, that rose up out of his chest and down his arm and leapt from his open palm across ten paces of sand, straight and quick as a thrown thing. It wasn’t orange. It was violet: pale and bright and cold-looking at the edges, the colour of his horn, the colour of wisteria at dusk. It burned without smoke.

It went into the thing’s face. Into the narrow blind-looking head, just as it lifted, just as it gathered itself to go for the gap between Ysolde and Hako.

The thing recoiled.

It made a sound, at last. A high thin shriek, like metal dragged on stone. Its head jerked back, wreathed in violet fire that clung to it like wet paint, smoking, and a stink of burnt hair rolled across the sand; and for one heartbeat it was blind and burning and off balance, all its long folded limbs flailing.

One heartbeat was all Ysolde needed.

She came in from the side, her sword in her off hand, her wounded arm hanging. Ina saw her plant her back foot in Winter’s frost and turn her whole body into it, the way she’d shown them on a hundred cold mornings: the blade low and flat, and the light running two hands past the edge.

It went through the thing’s neck with almost no sound at all. A hiss, like a hot iron put into water.

For a heartbeat nothing happened. Then the head tipped, very slowly, off the end of the long black neck, still burning violet, and fell, and rolled, and stopped against Hako’s boot. Hako looked down at it and very carefully didn’t move his foot.

The body stood a moment longer without it. Then it folded up on itself, once, twice, joint by joint, like a cheap chair collapsing, and lay still on the sand; and the cold went out of the air all at once, as if a door had been shut.

And the violet fire, on the head, went on burning for a moment, very quietly, in the sudden enormous silence of the Lists.

The silence went on and on.

Ten thousand people. Lanterns swaying overhead. Somewhere, very faintly, one band still playing, a jaunty tune, because nobody had told them to stop. The torches round the arena, straightening up again now that the cold was gone.

And on the sand, in the middle of it all, a guard with curled ram’s horns and her arm running red, standing over the thing she’d killed. And a man in an indigo coat, in a demon’s mask, with violet fire dying in his open hand. And a girl with black horns and white hair, crouched in a fan of frost.

Ina took the mask off.

He didn’t know why. It just seemed like the right thing to do. He reached up and pulled it off his face and let it hang at his shoulder again, and stood there in the torchlight with his own face showing: pale, too pale, the single horn, the fanged mouth, his hair stuck to his forehead with sweat.

He looked up at the stands.

The crowd looked back.

And then a child, somewhere high up in the east stand, near where the thing had been sitting all evening, stood up on his bench. Ina could see him clearly in the lantern light: a small boy of perhaps seven, in a paper crown of horns, with a little white mask stitched on the front.

The boy reached up, and took off his paper horns, and held them up over his head in both hands. High. As high as he could reach.

Then the girl next to him did the same. And her mother. And the old man beside her.

And then, all round the Lists, like a wave going round the bowl of the arena, ten thousand people were standing up and taking off their paper horns and holding them up over their heads, in silence. Thousands of them. Rows and rows. Paper horns, raised in the lantern light, toward the sand.

Ina stood there, and looked at them, and couldn’t speak.

Beside him, Winter had stood up out of the frost. She was looking at the stands too. Her face was doing something Ina had never seen it do.

“They’re…” she said. And stopped.

“Saluting,” said Ina. His voice didn’t sound like his. “I believe that’s a salute.”

Then somebody, somewhere, started to shout. Not demon. Ina’s name. And then Winter’s. And then the whole of the Lists was roaring it, ten thousand voices, over and over, so loud it shook the lanterns.

Ysolde’s arm needed stitches. Eleven of them. The field surgeon of the city watch put them in on a bench under the master’s platform while Lucan stood over him wringing his hands and Ysolde sat perfectly still and looked bored.

Ina came and stood in front of her when the surgeon had finished. He didn’t know what to say. He’d always known what to say. He found, for once, that he didn’t.

Ysolde looked up at him with her strange goat’s eyes, under her curled ram’s horns.

“There,” she said.

That was all. There. The word she gave out perhaps once a season, to someone who’d finally done something right. She’d said it to Winter in the winter, the first time the frost came. She’d never said it to Ina.

Ina found his eyes were stinging.

“I didn’t kill it,” he said. “You did.”

“You made it lift its head.” Ysolde flexed her stitched arm, and winced, and didn’t seem to care. “And then you made it look the wrong way.” She looked at him for a long moment. “I’ve been spat at for these horns for twenty years. By people like those.” She tilted her head at the stands, still roaring. “I never once heard them do that.”

She stood up, and put her good hand on his shoulder, briefly, and walked away toward Lucan, who was shouting at the city watch about something.

He opened his window on the sand, in the end, with the crowd still chanting and the torches burning down.

[ Ina · Tier 1: Spark · Skills: Onibi ]

Onibi. Demon-fire. The ghost lights of the old stories, the ones that floated over graveyards and marshes on summer nights. The window had chosen the word itself. He’d never told it. He didn’t know how it knew.

Seventh. After Thea and Maigo and Higu and Salty and Rachael and Hyvred.

He thought he ought to laugh. A mage path whose tiers were named for fire, and he was the one climbing it literally. Spark, Candle, Lantern, Torch. He was the fire. He’d found that funny all year, in a quiet sort of way. He didn’t find it funny now. He found it very large and very strange and very quiet, like standing in an empty cathedral.

He closed the window, and went to find Winter.

She was on the roof of the Broken Lance, later, when the festival had finally broken up and the streets were full of people going home in their paper horns, still singing. He’d guessed she would be. She went up there when she wanted to be alone, and he’d learned, over a year, that sometimes alone meant alone and sometimes alone meant not with anyone who’ll make it into a thing.

He climbed up the ladder and sat down on the other side of the chimney from her, a good distance away. She didn’t really like men. He’d known that from the first week. She wasn’t cruel about it; she was just guarded, the way a cat is guarded, and she’d let him closer than most over the year, but slowly, and on her terms. He’d never once pushed. He wasn’t going to start now.

They sat there for a long time, not talking. Below them, the city sang.

“It went for me,” said Winter, at last.

“Yes.”

“Because of the horns.”

“I think so,” said Ina. He didn’t lie to her. He never had. “I think it saw horns and thought you were the most dangerous. Or the most valuable. Or simply the most like itself.” He paused. “It was wrong on all three counts.”

Winter was quiet for a moment.

“Ysolde stood in front of me,” she said. “Hako held the angle. Chris called. Newish heard it. Lucan threw fire at it. And you…” She stopped. “Everyone stood in front of me.”

“That’s rather the point of a team.”

“I know.” She pulled her knees up and wrapped her arms round them, her long white hair falling over her shoulders, silver in the moonlight. “I just never had one before. Not like that. Not people who’d…” She stopped again. Ina waited. “In Vietnam, when he died. I was offline. Two months. Nobody could reach me. I came back and he’d been gone two months and I’d missed all of it. The funeral. The calls. Everyone crying together. I just came home to a group chat that had gone quiet, and nobody to cry with.” Her voice was very steady. “I told myself I’d never be the last to know anything again. I told myself I’d always be watching. That nobody would ever have to stand in front of me, because I’d see it coming first.”

“And tonight you didn’t,” said Ina gently.

“And tonight I didn’t.” Winter laughed, a small dry sound. “Newish did. And it was fine. Because everyone else was there.”

Ina didn’t say anything for a while.

“When I came back to the group,” he said at last, “after my… difficult time. I joined a call one night without warning. I’d been gone for years. I fully expected silence.” He looked out over the city. “And Ice said, Ina, finally, you’re on support, we’re losing. As though I’d stepped out for a cup of tea.” He smiled, a little. “I’ve thought about that a great deal. He didn’t make it into a thing. He just made room. As if there’d always been room, and I’d simply forgotten where it was.”

Winter turned her head and looked at him, round the side of the chimney, with her mismatched eyes.

“That’s what tonight was,” she said. “Wasn’t it. Everyone making room.”

“I think it might have been.”

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she unwound one arm from round her knees and held out her fist, round the side of the chimney, halfway across the gap between them. The way you’d hold it out to a teammate at the end of a round you’d somehow won.

Ina looked at it. Then, very carefully, the way you’d approach a cat that had decided, for the first time, that you might be all right, he reached out and bumped it with his own.

Her knuckles were cold. Of course they were. She was frost.

His were warm. He was fire.

Neither of them said anything. Winter took her fist back and tucked it round her knees again, and they just sat there, on the roof of the Broken Lance, on either side of the chimney, two people who looked like the enemy, while the city sang under them and the last of the lanterns went out over the Lists.

It wasn’t anything more than that. It didn’t need to be. It was a great deal, for her. He knew that.

Chris put it in the channel. Ina let him. Chris was better at plainly.

The channel lit up. From everywhere. Maigo in capitals, SEVENTH. Yan: Ina. I’m proud of you. Hyvred: VIOLET FIRE?? Gigichi, from Wrenhaven: good. now go to sleep.

And at the bottom of it, after a while, one more line, from a name that hadn’t said much all year.

[ Winter: Thank you, everyone. For standing in front. ]

Ina read it, lying in the dark in the Broken Lance, with the grey oni mask hanging on the bedpost and the violet fire still warm, somewhere, at the bottom of his chest.

He thought about a call, years ago, late at night. Ina. Finally.

Thank you, he thought. For making room.
