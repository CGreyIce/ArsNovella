The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 5: The Lists

Chapter 148

Everyone

4,975 words · 22 min read

Tiger had a plan, and the plan was everyone.

He’d made it at breakfast, on the last day, the sixty-ninth, sitting at the long table with his elbows in the porridge and Dennis’s ledger open in front of him, upside down, because Dennis had refused to turn it the right way round. Dennis had been keeping the ledger since the north gate. Hugs. Every one. Who, where, when, in a column, in Dennis’s very neat hand, because Dennis said if something was going to be counted it ought to be counted properly.

The ledger said nine. For one person.

It said a great many other things too. Tiger had hugged most of the thirty-five at least twice since the north gate. He’d hugged Maren three times, which the ledger noted with a small exclamation mark. He’d hugged Lucan more times than Dennis had been able to keep up with, and there was a note in the margin that said Lucan (est.). He’d hugged Rhea, who’d tried to turn it into an arm-wrestle, and Ysolde, who’d let him, and Petra, who’d pushed him off and then hugged him back, and a horse.

But there were gaps. Tiger had seen them, reading the ledger upside down. People he’d missed. People he’d only hugged once. People he’d meant to hug and then got distracted by something, which happened a lot.

“Tomorrow we go,” said Tiger. “Four roads.”

“Four roads,” said Dennis.

“So today,” said Tiger, “I’m going to hug everyone.”

Dennis looked at him over the top of the ledger for a long time.

“Define everyone,” said Dennis.

“Everyone,” said Tiger.

Dennis sighed, and turned to a fresh page, and wrote at the top of it, in his neat hand: DAY 69. EVERYONE.

He started with Drew, because he always started with Drew.

Drew was in the yard behind the Lance, sitting on the edge of the horse trough in the early sun, with his 88EXP nameplate catching the light and his eyes shut. Not asleep. Drew was never asleep. He was just sitting there being the person still smiling at the end of a long week, and Tiger knew him well enough after twelve years to know that meant he was tired.

Tiger sat down beside him and put his arms round him and his head on his shoulder.

“One,” said Dennis, from the back door, writing.

“You don’t have to count Drew,” said Tiger.

“I count everyone,” said Dennis. “You said everyone. I’m being rigorous.”

Drew opened his eyes.

“You’re doing the thing,” he said. “Aren’t you.”

“I’m doing the thing.”

“Everyone?”

“Everyone,” said Tiger.

Drew looked at the sky for a moment. Then he put his own arm round Tiger’s shoulders, the way he had on the ridge of a shed roof at Thornwall in the autumn, with the black notch of the pass against the stars and a freckled face in the third ring at the bottom of it.

“Good,” said Drew. “Start inside. Lucan’ll be up.”

Lucan was up. Lucan was always up. He was in the kitchen doorway of the Broken Lance in his red cloak, which he’d apparently slept in, directing the landlord on the subject of breakfast for thirty-five people and their guides and guards for the last time, and weeping, gently, the way he wept at most things now, as a kind of background.

Tiger hugged him.

Lucan made a sound like a kettle, and then hugged him back so hard Tiger’s feet left the floor, and then stood in the kitchen doorway holding him and weeping into his hair and saying something that sounded like two summers ago and were brought and I’ll miss the kettle.

“Two,” said Dennis.

“I’m not going anywhere,” said Lucan, into Tiger’s hair. “You’re going. Everyone’s going. Group 7 are the only ones staying and they don’t count, they live here, I see them every day.” He let Tiger go and held him at arm’s length and looked at him with his red, wet, enormous showman’s face. “Do you know what I’m going to do tomorrow? When you’ve all gone? Out of four gates?”

“What?”

“I’m going to sit in the empty Lists,” said Lucan, “in the master’s chair, which Drew’s leaving me, and I’m going to look at that bracket with the spider on it for about a week.” He sniffed. “And then I’m going to start planning the next one.”

“There’s going to be a next one?”

Lucan looked at him as if he’d said something very stupid.

“Of course there’s going to be a next one,” he said. “Thirty-five people who’ve stood next to each other. Drew said. You don’t do that once.” He wiped his eyes on his red sleeve. “Midsummer. Next year. Or the one after. I’ll write to all of you. I’ll write to him.” He lowered his voice. “Just in case.”

Tiger hugged everyone in the common room.

That took a while. They were all there, at the long table and on the benches, packing and not packing, the way they’d been not packing for three days. He went round. Down one side of the table and up the other. Group 1, at the end, round a basket. Xola, and her squid, which wrapped an arm round his ear. Higu, who let him and then went back to her moon chart. Gigichi, who said don’t and got hugged anyway. Thea, who held on longer than he expected and said still here into his shoulder for no reason he understood. PenguChi, who let him, and her penguin, which didn’t, again.

“Eight,” said Dennis.

Group 4. Gabe, who picked him up. Deston, who hummed. Rachael, who said if you’re doing this because of the apple I will hit you and then let him anyway. Calvin and the kettle. Dennis himself, who put the pen down very carefully first and then wrote Dennis (self) in the ledger afterwards with great dignity.

Group 6. All five at once, which shouldn’t have been possible and was, and Azac laughed so loudly the landlord looked round from the kitchen. Xav, on his own afterwards, who said okay and meant it.

Group 2, who’d come down the hill from the king’s house at breakfast to say goodbye before the king’s household packed. Aleaka, with her hawk, who bit Tiger very lightly on the ear on principle. Cursiffe, slow and relaxed. Jared, who stood in front of him a moment first, the way Jared stood in front of everyone, and then put his arm round his neck. Milli, Drawn and still, whose tail went round Tiger’s ankle without Milli seeming to know it had.

And Hana.

Hana wasn’t at the long table. She was at the small table by the window, on her own, with a great heap of something in front of her that Tiger couldn’t make out from across the room. Cords. Beads. Knots. She was working on them very fast, with her head down and her tail very still, and didn’t look up.

Tiger went over.

“Don’t hug me yet,” said Hana, without looking up. “I’ll drop a knot.”

“What are you doing?”

“Pairs,” said Hana.

She held one up, so he could see. A short knotted cord, purple and gold, with a small bead in the middle, cut neatly in half, so that there were two short cords, each with half a bead on the end. She laid the two halves side by side on the table. Then she picked up one of them and held it in her fist.

Tiger watched the other half. Nothing happened.

“Hold it,” said Hana, and gave him the half on the table.

He held it.

It went warm. Just slightly. The warmth of somebody else’s hand, as if somebody had reached across the table and laid two fingers on his palm. He nearly dropped it.

“Oh,” said Tiger.

“That’s the first thing they do,” said Hana. “Warm. When the other half’s held. That’s what I made in the winter. For everyone.” She took his half back and laid it on the table. “This is the second thing.”

She picked up a strip of something. Thin. Blue. Cursiffe’s ink, on a strip of the oiled paper he’d used on the circle. Tiny English letters on it, too small to read from where Tiger was standing. She wrapped it round the knot on one half of the cord, once, very carefully, and tied it off.

Then she picked up the other half and held it.

Tiger saw something move on the strip on the other half. The blue ink. The tiny letters on it went faint, and came back, and they weren’t the same letters.

“It carries words,” said Hana. “Now. Not many. A few. Whatever’s written on one half, when you hold it, comes out on the other. Cursiffe’s ink and my knots.” She put both halves down. “We worked it out last night. At the king’s house. It took four hours and Oswin cried twice.”

Tiger stared at the cord.

“It’s a channel,” he said.

“It’s a very small channel,” said Hana. “For two people.” She looked at the heap in front of her. “So I’m making a lot of them.”

She’d made fourteen.

Tiger counted them, on the table. Seven pairs. Purple and gold cords, each with half a bead and a strip of Cursiffe’s blue ink knotted round it, in two neat rows. One row of seven halves on the left. One row of seven on the right.

“The guides,” said Hana.

Tiger looked at her.

“We’ve got the channel,” said Hana. “The thirty-five. We’ve had it since Thea built it. We can talk to each other from anywhere in the kingdom. Every night. We always know where everyone is.” She touched the row on the right. “The guides haven’t got anything. They’ve been writing letters for two years. By rider. Twenty days to Thornwall, a month to Cinderhold. Maren writes to the Advisor every second day and it gets there a week later.” She was quiet. “And they’re the ones who’ll have to hold the edges. With us. When it comes.”

“So you’re giving them one each.”

“One each,” said Hana. “Maren. Oswin. Aldous. Emrys. Lucan. And Corvin and Garrick, who aren’t here; Hyvred and Xav will take theirs.” She touched the row on the left, the seven other halves. “And these come with me.”

“All seven?”

“All seven,” said Hana. She picked up a longer cord from beside the heap, plain purple, and began threading the seven left-hand halves onto it, one after another, so that they hung from it like charms on a bracelet. “On my wrist. With the gold heart.”

Tiger looked at the cord with seven halves on it.

“You’re the middle,” he said.

Hana’s hands stopped for a moment.

“I was always the middle,” she said. Quietly. “On Earth. Everyone came through me. Ice met Jared through me, and Milli, and Aleaka. And Cursiffe through Jared, through me.” She threaded the last half on. “I was the one people talked through. I liked it.” She tied the cord off and held it up, seven half-beads with seven strips of blue ink, swinging. “Here I’ve been the one who doesn’t use it. The dangerous half. The charm. Two years of pushing it down.” She looked at the seven halves. “This is the other half. The safe one. Making things that let other people talk.”

She put the cord round her wrist. Beside the purple cord with half a gold heart on it, that had been warm since a riverbank.

“Now you can hug me,” said Hana.

Tiger did.

“Twenty-six,” said Dennis, from the long table, writing. “Hana. With the bracelet. I’m noting the bracelet.”

He took Hana’s pairs round to the guides with her. He hadn’t been asked. He just went, because he was going round everyone anyway, and it seemed sensible to go round with the thing that was making everyone cry.

Maren cried. Tiger had never seen Maren cry. She stood in the yard by the cart for the north with her list against her chest and her half of the cord in her other hand, and Hana showed her how to read the blue strip and how to write on it with a damp finger, and Maren looked at it for a long time and then said oh in a very small voice and turned round and faced the cart.

Tiger hugged her. Four. Dennis put two exclamation marks.

Aldous, in the stable, with Group 3’s cart, took his half in both hands, palms up, the way he did over the dead on the red, and looked at it, and said thank you very gravely, and put it inside his coat over his heart.

Emrys sang. Of course he did. A little bit. Two lines of something, holding the cord, and then stopped and laughed at himself and said sorry, sorry, it’s a habit.

Oswin didn’t say anything at all. He took his half and held it against his chest with both arms, the way he’d held a signed book on a river path, and Tiger realised he’d seen Oswin do that twice now in a week, and that it meant Oswin had been given something he would keep until he died.

Lucan wept. Obviously. But he wept with the cord held up to the light, reading the blue strip, and when Hana held her end on the other side of the yard and wrote hello on hers with her finger and the letters came up on his, he stopped weeping, for once, and just looked at it. And then he looked at Hana, and at Tiger, and said, quite quietly:

“Just in case.”

He found Kestra on the roof.

He’d known she’d be there. She was always on a roof or a wall or a step, somewhere with her back to something solid, watching. She was on the flat bit above the back stairs where Winter and Ina sat, with her back against the chimney and one boot up and her little black book in her coat, and she watched Tiger come up the stairs the way she watched everyone: feet first.

“No,” said Kestra.

“I haven’t said anything.”

“You’re going to hug me,” said Kestra. “No.”

Tiger stood at the top of the stairs.

“Everyone,” he said. “I said everyone. Dennis is writing it down.” He thought about it. “You’re everyone.”

Kestra looked at him for a long time. Then at his feet. Then at his face.

“You stopped your friend going down a mountain,” she said. “In the summer. At the pass. Drew did. Hands on your chest.” She was quiet. “And you didn’t go.”

“No.”

“You wanted to.”

“Yes,” said Tiger. “More than anything.”

“And you didn’t.” Kestra took her boot down. “That’s harder than going.” She looked at him a moment longer. Then she said, very dry, very final, the voice that barely spoke: “Fine. Once. Don’t tell anyone.”

He hugged her. She was very still, the whole time, the way a cat is still when it’s decided to allow something. Then she put one hand flat on his back, between the shoulder blades, for exactly as long as it took to breathe in, and took it away.

“Don’t tell anyone,” said Kestra.

“I won’t,” said Tiger.

Down in the yard, Dennis, who couldn’t possibly have seen, wrote Kestra!!! in the ledger and underlined it three times.

The king came down the hill at the end of the afternoon.

He came the way he’d come the first time, to the Lance, in a plain coat with no guard, with Halvard beside him and Kestra three paces behind. To say goodbye. The king’s household was going north-west in the morning, to Solmere, four hundred people and two hundred horses and eleven wagons of somebody’s luggage. He stood in the yard of the Broken Lance with soot still in the creases of his knuckles and looked round at all of them.

Tiger looked at him.

Dennis, at the back door, with the ledger, said very quietly: “Tiger. No.”

Tiger went.

He walked across the yard of the Broken Lance to the king of Aurelan, the only Ascendant alive, the man who’d sat beside a grave on the Redmarch for an hour the morning after and stood on the wall at Cinderhold with his hand on his chest, and stopped in front of him.

The king looked at him.

“You’re the one who built the gate,” said the king.

“Yes.”

“It fell over.”

“It stood long enough,” said Tiger.

The king looked at him for a moment longer. Then he laughed. The short deep sound, like a hammer on an anvil. And held out his arms.

“Go on, then,” said the king. “Dennis is waiting.”

Tiger hugged the king of Aurelan in the yard of the Broken Lance.

It was like hugging a mountain. A warm one. The king’s arms went round him, very briefly, very hard, the way a smith’s would, and then let go.

“THE KING,” said Dennis, from the back door, and dropped the pen.

He didn’t hug Halvard. He looked at him, and Halvard looked back, thin and grey and very upright, with his hands behind his back and something in his face that hadn’t been there a week ago. And Tiger understood, the way he sometimes understood things under all the noise, that Halvard was a man who’d been carrying something very heavy for a very long time and had only just started to think about setting it down, and that a hug would knock it out of his hands.

So he held out his hand instead.

Halvard looked at it. Then he shook it. Carefully. As if hands were a thing he’d forgotten how to use and was remembering.

“Thank you,” said Halvard. Quietly. Tiger didn’t know what for.

“For what?”

“For not,” said Halvard.

Yan and Kei were on the roof, after dark.

Tiger had gone up to find Kestra again, to tell her Dennis had written her down in capitals, which he’d thought she ought to know. She wasn’t there. Yan and Kei were. Sitting against the chimney, side by side, in the warm dark, with the lamps of Valcrest going down the hill below them and the moon up, fat, waxing, over the east wall.

Neither of them was talking. But Tiger knew, from the way Kei’s head was tipped, that somebody was.

Suzu was on the scythe. The scythe was leaning against the chimney beside Yan, the long dark blade with its thousands of tiny moons, and the white snake was wound three times round the top of its shaft with her head on the curve of the iron. And at the bottom of the shaft, small and green, his own head tipped up toward her, Hisui.

Tiger stopped at the top of the stairs.

He wasn’t going to hug them. He could see that. Not yet. He sat down on the top step instead, with his back against the doorframe, very quietly, which was hard for him, and waited.

“She says,” said Kei, very softly. To Yan. Not to Tiger. “She says this is the last one for a while.”

Yan didn’t say anything. His hand was resting on the shaft of the scythe, just below Suzu.

“She says,” said Kei, and listened. “She says she’s going to miss being heard.” A pause. “She says it was nice. For a week. To have somebody to say things to who could pass them on.” He listened again. “She says she’s not going to stop talking. She’s been talking since before the hall. She’ll go on.” A pause. “She says you’ll just have to go back to guessing.”

“I stopped guessing,” said Yan.

“She says you’ll have to start again,” said Kei. “She says guess better this time.”

Yan laughed. Low and slow and very quiet.

Tiger couldn’t hear Hisui. Nobody but Kei could, and Yan. But he could see him. At the bottom of the scythe-shaft, very small and very green in the moonlight, with his head tipped up toward the white snake above him and his tongue going in and out very fast, the way it went when he was tasting something enormous.

“He says,” said Kei, and stopped. And something happened to Kei’s calm, trustworthy, relaxing face that Tiger had seen happen to it about four times in two years. “He says he’s going to miss her.”

Suzu didn’t move.

“He says don’t tell her he said that,” said Kei.

There was a pause.

“She heard,” said Kei.

At the bottom of the scythe-shaft, Hisui made a sound that Tiger couldn’t hear and could somehow see: a small green snake shrinking, very slightly, into himself, the way a person does when they’ve been caught.

At the top of the shaft, Suzu lifted her head off the blade.

She looked down at Hisui for a long time, with her two green eyes like lamps. Then she came down the shaft. Slowly. One coil at a time. All the way to the bottom, where he was. And put her head down, very lightly, beside his, on the wood. Just for a moment.

Then she went back up.

Kei didn’t say anything for a while. Yan didn’t either.

“What did she say,” said Yan eventually. His voice had gone.

Kei listened.

“Nothing,” he said. “She didn’t say anything. Hisui says she didn’t need to.”

Tiger went down the stairs again, after that, very quietly. Without hugging anyone.

Dennis was at the bottom, with the ledger.

“Well?” said Dennis.

“Not them,” said Tiger. “Not tonight.” He sat down on the bottom step. “They’re busy.”

Dennis looked at him for a long moment. Then he wrote something in the ledger, very small, and showed it to him. In the column where the hugs went, beside Yan and Kei, he’d written: Excused. Snake business.

He found the water-carrier last.

He’d been saving him. He hadn’t meant to. He’d just found, going round everyone all day, that every time he saw the man with the rod across the yard or at the end of the long table, he’d gone somewhere else first. Somebody else. As if some part of him that nobody saw was keeping the last one for last.

He was in the yard, on the edge of the horse trough, late, with the rod across his knees and the moon on him. On his own. Everyone else had gone in, or up, or to bed, or to the long table to not pack for the last time. Alea was asleep on a bench by the window inside with her sword against the wall.

Tiger sat down on the trough beside him.

They didn’t say anything for a while. The moon was very bright. Tomorrow there’d be four roads. North, for Groups 1 and 4 and 6. North-west, for Group 2 and the king. West, for Tiger and Group 5. East, for Group 3 and the man with the rod and his daughter.

West. Back to Thornwall. Back to the pass, and Corvin and Hild and two hundred soldiers holding it, and an absence the size of a hill at the foot of it, waiting, not in a hurry. And rings of people standing in the dark round a circle burned into a valley floor, with their faces turned up. And a freckled face in the third ring.

And a shed. Against the north wall. With a roof. That stood.

“I’m going back,” said Tiger.

“I know.”

“To the pass. To Ossie’s shed.” He picked at the edge of the trough. “I’m going to sit on the roof every day, like I did in the winter. And look at the pass. And not go.” He was quiet. “Drew’ll stand at the bottom of the ladder.”

“He will.”

“Will you come?” said Tiger. “One day. To Thornwall. To see it. The shed.” He didn’t look round. “It’s not much. It’s a shed. Three walls and a door from a ditch and a roof a carpenter put on. There’s a dog on the wall inside that Bee drew and it says OSSIE’S and there’s a shirt on a nail.” He swallowed. “It’s the only thing I ever built that stood. Until the gate. I want you to see it.”

The man with the rod was quiet for a long time.

“When the pass is quiet,” he said. “I’ll come.”

“It’s never quiet.”

“Then I’ll come anyway,” he said. “And sit on the roof with you. And look at it.” He turned the rod in his hands. “And not go.”

“Did it stand?” said Tiger.

He said it to the moon, not to the man. The way he’d say it to himself, sometimes, at Thornwall, at night, on the ridge of a roof.

The man beside him didn’t answer straight away.

“The gate?”

“Everything,” said Tiger. “This week. All of it. The gate, and the north gate, and the sand at midnight, and the Lists, and the closing.” He looked at his hands. “It’s all going to fall over tomorrow. Isn’t it. Four roads. Everyone going back to the edges. Like the gate, when Gabe pulled the sword out.”

The man with the rod looked at the moon.

“It stood long enough,” he said.

Tiger laughed. It came out wet.

“You always say that.”

“It’s always true.” He was quiet. “That’s all anything’s for, Tiger. A gate. A shed. A week. You build it, and it stands long enough, and then it falls over, and you’ve got the thing it was standing for.” He turned his head. “The gate fell over. Squad B still held it for six glasses. The shed’s still standing. Ossie still raced you every morning for three months. This week’s going to end tomorrow at four gates.” He paused. “Thirty-five of us still stood on the sand at midnight and said here.”

Tiger didn’t say anything for a long time.

“I’m going to build one that stands longer than long enough,” he said eventually. “One day.”

“I know you are.”

“A house. A real one. With walls. And a roof I put on myself.” He wiped his face. “And it’s going to stand.”

“I know,” said the man. “I’ll come and see that too.”

Tiger hugged him.

On the edge of the horse trough, in the moonlight, in the yard of the Broken Lance, on the last night. Properly. With nothing in the way. No buckets, no yoke, no forty thousand people. Just a tiger boy from a Minecraft server whose house had blown up, and a man who’d been dead for three years, sitting on the edge of a horse trough.

The man let him. He put one hand on the back of Tiger’s head, the way he put his hand on Alea’s at night, and left it there.

At the back door, Dennis, who’d been there the whole time, very quietly, with the ledger and a lamp, wrote one more line.

Then he closed the ledger.

He came out into the yard and held it out to Tiger. Open. At the page that said, at the top, DAY 69. EVERYONE.

Tiger took it and looked at the list. Every name. All the way down. The thirty-five, minus two excused for snake business. The guides. The guards. Lucan (est.). Kestra, in capitals, underlined three times. The king, with the pen-mark where Dennis had dropped it. A horse.

And at the bottom, on its own:

Ten.

“That’s ten,” said Dennis. “Him. Total. Since the north gate.” He paused. “It’s a record.”

“It’s not a record,” said Tiger. “It’s not a competition.”

“Everything’s a competition,” said Dennis, “if somebody’s keeping the ledger.” He took it back. “I’ll keep it. In Kingsferry. In case you want to beat it next time.”

“There’s going to be a next time?”

“Lucan says midsummer,” said Dennis. “Next year. Or the one after.” He tucked the ledger under his arm. “And Lucan, it turns out, is extremely persuasive.”

Drew was on the roof when Tiger went up, at the very end.

On his own, against the chimney, with his eyes shut and the moon on his 88EXP nameplate. Yan and Kei had gone down. The scythe was gone. There was just Drew, being the person still sitting there at the end of it all.

Tiger sat down beside him. They didn’t say anything for a long time.

“Everyone?” said Drew eventually.

“Everyone,” said Tiger. “Except Yan and Kei. Snake business.”

“Fair.”

“And Halvard. I shook his hand.”

“Good call.”

“Ten,” said Tiger. “Him. Dennis says it’s a record.”

Drew laughed, very quietly, with his eyes still shut.

“You put some of it down,” said Drew. “This week. I watched you. The thing from the pass. You’ve been carrying it since the summer.” He opened his eyes and looked at the moon. “You put some of it down. In a yard, in the moonlight, in front of a gate.”

“Yeah,” said Tiger.

“How much?”

Tiger thought about it. He wanted to give him a true answer. He’d been Drew’s friend for twelve years and he’d always given him true answers, even the stupid ones.

“Enough,” he said. “Long enough.”

Drew put his arm round him. Twelve years. The way he had on the ridge of a shed roof with the pass against the stars, and on top of the pass with both hands on his chest, and at a postern gate in the autumn. Not here. Not on your own. Not ever.

“Eleven,” said Tiger, into his shoulder.

“What?”

“You. Today. Eleven.” He sniffed. “I lost count. Dennis’ll have it.”

“Dennis definitely has it,” said Drew.

And somewhere below them, in the dark, at the back door of the Broken Lance, very faintly, a pen scratched once more on a page.
