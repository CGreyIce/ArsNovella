The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 4: Threads

Chapter 105

Brothers

7,307 words · 32 min read

The kettle was terrible.

He’d bought it in Wrenhaven market the first autumn, from a tinker who was leaving town and wanted rid of it, for two coppers and a pike. It was dented down one side where somebody had dropped it on a flagstone. The lid didn’t sit right. The handle had come off in the second winter and he’d put it back on with wire. It whistled on two notes at once, and neither of them was a nice one. Alea had been asking him to buy a new one since she was ten. Odette had offered him hers, twice.

He’d never replaced it. He’d never let himself think about why.

He thought about it now, standing at his own hearth at midnight, waiting for it to boil. A kitchen in Malacca. A boy Deston had brought along, whom he’d known for an hour, standing in the doorway looking polite. This is the kettle.

He’d bought the same one. In another world. Without noticing.

The kettle began to whistle, both notes, and on the floor under every blanket the cabin owned, Gabe stopped snoring. Just like that. In the middle of a snore. The way he’d always stopped, at the first sound of anybody anywhere making anything to eat.

“Is that tea,” said Gabe, into the floor.

“It’s tea.”

“Is it real tea.”

“It’s what they’ve got. It’s leaves off a bush in the south. It tastes like a hedge.”

Gabe considered this, without opening his eyes. “Okay,” he said, and sat up.

Xav was already sitting up. He’d been sitting up the whole time, against the wall by the door, with a blanket round his shoulders and his eyes open in the dark. He hadn’t been asleep. Xav was never asleep when you thought he was. You learned that, after a few hundred games, the hard way.

He made the tea. He owned three cups and a bowl. He gave Gabe the big cup, and Xav the one with the chip, and kept the bowl for himself, and took the last cup out to the step, where Deston was sitting in the dark with his window closed and his arms round his knees, looking at the lake.

Deston took the cup without looking round.

“I felt you put the kettle on,” he said.

“You can feel that?”

“I can feel you thinking about putting the kettle on.” Deston drank, and made a face. “This tastes like a hedge.”

“I know.”

“Why does it taste like a hedge?”

“Come on,” he said. “Bring your blanket. Let’s go down to the end.”

They’d spent the whole day not talking about it.

It had been a strange day. The strangest of both his lives, and he’d had some strange ones. After the jetty, after the song, after the minute he’d asked for that had turned into an hour, he’d stood on the shingle with the three of them and hadn’t had the faintest idea what to do next. So he’d done what he always did when he didn’t know what to do. He’d gone to see about lunch.

They’d eaten fish on the step. They’d talked about nothing. About the barge, the Patient Widow, and an old man called Haskett who’d taken his cap off when they came in. About the worst inn on the Wren. About a dancing bear at a midwinter market in Kingsferry and whether it had been happy. About the hens, who had come out from under the cabin one at a time in the afternoon to look at Gabe and decided, all three of them, that they liked him. They’d talked around the thing in the middle of the day the way you walk around a hole in a floor that everybody in the room knows is there.

Gabe had fallen asleep in the afternoon on the end of the jetty, in the sun, all at once, like a coat somebody had dropped. He’d slept for three hours. Deston said he hadn’t slept properly in a fortnight. Since the Narrows, Deston said, and didn’t say what the Narrows were, and Ars didn’t ask, because he already knew.

Alea had come down at noon.

She’d stood at the end of the lake road and looked at the four of them on the step, eating fish. She’d looked at him for a long moment. And then she’d turned round without a word and gone back up the road to where Group 1 were waiting with ten people who’d walked a very long way, and taken them all up into the town, and come back at dusk, and gone straight up the stairs to the loft without looking at anybody.

It was the kindest thing anyone had done for him in years. She was her mother’s daughter. She knew exactly when to leave a room.

The lake was still.

It was the first truly still night of the spring. The ice had gone out of the middle a week ago and the last of it had drifted down to the outflow at Wrenhaven and broken up against the mill weir. What was left was black water, perfectly flat, from the end of the jetty to the far shore, with every star in the sky lying on it. You couldn’t tell where the jetty ended. You sat on the edge with your feet hanging over and it was like sitting on the edge of the sky.

They sat in a row. Him in his place, at the very end, where he always sat. Gabe on his left. Deston on his right. Xav on the far side of Deston, a little apart, the way he always sat, so that he could see everybody.

Nobody said anything for a while.

He could feel them waiting. He’d felt them waiting all day. Not pushing. Not asking. Just there, beside him, the way you’d sit beside somebody in a hospital corridor. They’d agreed it, he thought, on the barge, somewhere on the long brown river. We don’t ask. He tells it when he wants to. He could see them agreeing it. Gabe saying it first, very firmly. Deston nodding. Xav saying okay.

“You can ask,” he said.

“We’re not going to ask,” said Gabe.

“We said we wouldn’t,” said Deston. “On the barge. We said you’d tell it when you wanted. Or never. That’d be fine too.”

“I’ll wait,” said Xav.

He laughed. He hadn’t meant to. “You’d wait all night.”

“I’m good at it now,” said Xav. “Somebody taught me.”

He looked at the water for a while. At the stars on it. At the place, out in the middle, where the big pike lived, the one he’d been not-catching for eight years.

“I want to tell you,” he said. “All of it. Once. Properly. And then I don’t think I want to tell it again for a long time.” He turned the bowl round in his hands. “Is that all right?”

Gabe put his big hand on the back of his neck, the way he used to on the risers, when Gabe was thirteen and Declan was fifteen and had just been told off by the choirmaster again, and left it there.

“Go on,” said Gabe.

So he told them.

He started at the beginning, because that was the only place he knew how to start. With a fan turning in a room in Malacca, and going to sleep, and not waking up. With waking up instead in a cottage on the other side of the world, a baby, with twenty-six years in his head and no way to tell anyone. Six years of not knowing what was wrong with him, and then one night under his blanket, whispering Status in English, just to hear the word, and a window opening in front of his face. He’d cried. A father who farmed. A field behind the house, where he’d crossed the first edge at fourteen, alone, and lain in the grass afterwards laughing like an idiot, with nobody in the world to tell.

He told them about the garrison. Fifteen, with a borrowed sword and a temper, because he’d been angry, he said, for about five years straight: angry at being small again, angry at being somewhere he hadn’t asked to be, angry at everything and at nothing and mostly at the sky. He told them about the one time he’d gone east past the border, alone, too far, much too far, at nineteen, because he’d been angry and an idiot, and found a black sword standing on a hill in the middle of nowhere like somebody had left it there for him.

“You went into demon land,” said Gabe. “On your own.”

“I was nineteen.”

“That’s not an answer, Declan.”

“It’s the only one I’ve got.” He looked at Xav. “You’d have pushed too. If you were bored.”

“I wasn’t bored,” said Xav. “You were angry. It’s different. You always played worse angry.”

“I know.”

“You’d peek.”

“I know, Xav.”

He told them about the windows. About finding out, slowly, over years, that nobody else in this world had one. And then, slowly, over more years, that some of them did. People who’d stood near him. People he’d trained. A garrison sergeant, a boy who carried water, a smith. As if something in him had spilled over onto everyone he’d ever stood next to for long enough.

And then he stopped.

He’d known he would stop there. He’d been walking towards it all day, the way you walk towards the edge of something.

He looked at the lake.

“Her name was Diwa,” he said.

He didn’t know how long it was before he said anything else.

Long enough for Deston to put his cup down on the boards. Long enough for the hand on the back of his neck to tighten, just a little. Long enough for Xav, on the end, to stop moving altogether.

“She was a healer,” he said. “At the garrison. At Cinderhold, on the Redmarch. I was seventeen. I kept getting hurt, because I kept doing stupid things, because I was angry. And she kept stitching me up. Three times in one month.” “She had cold hands. Freezing. Always. She said the healing took the warm out of her. The third time, she sat on the end of my cot with her cold hands in her lap and asked me why do you keep doing this to yourself. Not angry. She just wanted to know.”

“And you told her,” said Deston.

“I told her.” He breathed out. “Not all of it. Not then. But enough. Where I was from. That I’d been somebody else, once, somewhere else, with people I missed so much it made me stupid.” He looked at the water. “And she didn’t laugh. She just listened. And at the end she said, Show me. How you write, where you’re from.”

“The letters,” said Gabe quietly. “On the stone.”

“I taught her the letters. All twenty-six. On a slate, with a nail, in the infirmary at night while the garrison slept. She learned them in a week. She was cleverer than me. She always was.” Something happened in his chest that he let happen. “She only ever really used them for three words. In the flour on the table when she’d been baking. In the frost on the window. On the back of my hand once, in ink, while I was asleep, so I’d find it in the morning.”

He was quiet a moment.

“She laughed at me,” he said. “All the time. Not with me. At me. She thought I was the funniest, stupidest man she’d ever met, and she told me so about four times a day. She’d never tell you anything until she was ready, and then she’d tell you everything, all at once, in one breath, at the worst possible moment. In the middle of a fight. At a funeral. Once while I was halfway up a ladder.” He found that he was smiling, and that his face was wet, and that both were all right. “Her ears went flat when she was cross with me. Which was often.”

“Ears,” said Gabe.

“She was a fox. Like Alea.” He looked down at the bowl in his hands. “Alea got her eyes. And her ears. And her way of looking at you like she’s deciding whether you’re worth the trouble. And her hands. Alea’s hands are cold too. You’ll see. She doesn’t heal. She just has her mother’s hands.”

Nobody said anything.

“She was the love of my life,” he said. “Of both of them.”

He’d never said it out loud. Not once, in eight years. Not to Alea, who knew. Not to Rowan, or Odette, or the king. He’d cut it into iron, in a language nobody here could read. He’d never said it.

It went out over the water, and lay there with the stars, and didn’t sink.

“I’d had a whole life before,” he said. “You know that. You were in it. Twenty-six years. I thought I knew things.” He shook his head. “I didn’t know anything. I didn’t know you could feel like that about a person. Like the ground. Like the floor you stand on. I didn’t know you could build a whole life on somebody without noticing you were doing it, until one day you look down and there’s nothing under you but her.”

“Alea was born when I was eighteen. Here, I mean. This body.” He almost laughed. “Eighteen. Can you imagine me, at eighteen, with a baby?”

“No,” said Gabe, Deston and Xav, together.

“Nobody could. Diwa couldn’t. She used to hand Alea to me and then just stand there watching, waiting for me to drop her.” He wiped his face with the heel of his hand. “I never did. Not once. I was so scared of dropping her I held on too tight for about three years.”

“We found this place when Alea was small. For her. Somewhere quiet, a long way from the border. Diwa chose it. She stood on the end of this jetty and said here, and that was that. The cabin was half a ruin. We rebuilt it. She planted the pumpkins. I went east when the border needed me, and came home when it didn’t, and she came east with me when they needed healers, which was usually, because she was the best one on the Redmarch and she’d never once let me go anywhere on my own.” He looked at the cabin, dark at the top of the shingle, with Alea asleep in the loft. “Nine years. It was a good life. It was the best life. I didn’t know it was going to be nine years.”

He stopped for a while then.

The others let him. Gabe’s hand stayed where it was. Deston didn’t move. Somewhere behind them in the dark, one of the hens muttered in its sleep and settled.

“Then the war came,” he said.

“Alea was nine. The demons came over the Redmarch at the end of the summer. More of them than anyone had ever seen. More than anyone believed. The king called everybody east, and I went, and Diwa came, because they needed healers more than they’d ever needed anything, and she’d have walked there on her own if I’d said no. Alea stayed here. With Rowan and Odette.” He looked up the shingle, towards the road. “That’s how I know them. Rowan was a soldier then. He stayed with my daughter so I could go. He’s never let me pay him back for it. I’ve tried for eight years.”

“Cinderhold. The first nights were bad. The second night was worse. The line broke in the south, out on the red, and forty recruits got cut off in a ditch. Forty children, most of them. Surrounded.” He looked at his hands. “So I went. That’s what I was for. I went out to the ditch and I held it. All night. I waited, and I only moved when I had to, and in the morning I walked forty recruits back to the fort.”

He felt Xav go still.

Not still the way Xav was usually still. Still the way a person goes still when they’ve heard a story before, from the other side, and they’ve just realised where it ends.

“And the infirmary wall was open,” he said.

“Not broken. Opened. Neatly. Like a door. The orderlies were dead. Six of them. Four healers were gone.” His voice did something and he waited until it stopped. “She was gone.”

“They hadn’t wanted the ditch,” he said. “They’d never wanted the ditch. The whole south line, the whole night, was to get me out of the fort. They couldn’t beat me. They’d tried, for ten years, and they couldn’t. So they’d stopped trying to beat me. They’d started trying to break me. And somebody, somewhere, had worked out how.” He turned the bowl in his hands. “They’d learned she was mine. That’s all. That was all it took. They found out who she was, and they waited until I was somewhere else, holding a ditch for forty strangers, and they walked in through the wall and took her.”

“Petra,” said Xav.

It came out of him very flat. Very quiet. He wasn’t looking at anyone. He was looking straight ahead, at the dark water.

“She told me. In the summer, in the yard. Forty of them, in a ditch, the second night. One man. He waited. He only ever moved once.” He swallowed. “She was in the ditch. She was twenty-three.”

“I know,” he said. “I saw her. On the shingle this morning.” He breathed out. “I’d know her anywhere. She was the one at the end who wouldn’t put her sword down. All night. Her hands were shaking so hard she couldn’t have hit anything with it. She wouldn’t put it down.”

“Declan,” said Xav.

“Don’t tell her,” he said. “This part. Please. It isn’t hers. It was never hers. I’d hold that ditch again. Every time. That’s the worst part, Xav. I’ve had eight years to want to have done it differently, and I can’t. Forty of them. I’d hold it again.” He looked at Xav, past Deston. “Don’t tell her.”

Xav looked back at him for a long time.

“Okay,” said Xav.

“In the afternoon,” he said, “they showed her to me.”

He didn’t look at any of them now. He looked at the water, and said it to the water, because he couldn’t have said it to a face.

“There’s a rise out on the red, an hour’s walk beyond the east wall. A low hill. Their general was on it. A big one, old, with horns like a bull’s. And a circle drawn on the ground in something dark. And her, in the middle of it.” He breathed. “They waited until I was on the wall. They’d lit fires all round the rise, so I could see. That was the whole point. The general wanted me to watch.”

“They call it a turning. I didn’t know that then. Nobody did. Nobody had ever seen one. It’s…” He stopped, and started again. “It’s like watching someone pour out a cup. Slowly. All of it. Everything that’s in it. And pour something else in, while it’s still going out. Into a living person. While they’re awake.”

He felt Gabe’s hand go rigid on the back of his neck.

“A healer’s best,” he said. “That’s what they found out. That’s why her. A healer’s mana is the purest kind. A healer holds things. A healer makes the best…” He couldn’t say vessel. He’d never been able to. “The best one.”

“Rachael,” said Gabe.

It wasn’t a question. His voice had gone somewhere very low and very far down.

“Yes,” he said.

“The hat man. At the night market. In the Narrows. That’s what he wanted her for.”

“Yes.”

“You knew.”

“I heard,” he said. “Alea brings things home. Group 1 tell me things at supper. I heard about the hat man the first winter. I heard about the Narrows.” He made himself look at Gabe. “I was afraid, Gabe. I’ve been afraid every day since. Do you understand? Every day. And you stood in front of her. Every time. All of you.” He breathed. “Thank you.”

Gabe looked at him for a long time. His jaw was working.

“They’re not having her,” said Gabe.

“No,” he said. “They’re not.”

It wasn’t a hope. It was something he’d decided in the winter, on this jetty, with a letter in his hand, and decided again every morning since. Gabe heard that. He saw Gabe hear it.

“Go on,” said Gabe, very quietly. “Sorry. Go on.”

“I went over the wall,” he said.

“It was a trap. Of course it was. That was the other half of it. A hero who sees that either breaks where he stands, or comes running on his own into whatever you’ve got waiting. They’d got everything waiting. The whole host, between the wall and the rise. They thought I’d break, or they thought I’d die trying. Either was fine with them.”

“I didn’t care. I went through them. I was Shaped then. Tier six. I shouldn’t have been able to, and I didn’t care about that either. It took me all afternoon. An hour’s walk, any other day. It was the longest walk of my life.” His hands were steady on the bowl. He looked at them, surprised. “By the time I got up the rise, she was halfway gone.”

He was quiet for a long moment.

“She knew me,” he said. “That’s the thing. She was halfway gone and she still knew me. Her eyes were still hers, nearly. She looked at me and she was still deciding whether I was worth the trouble.” He laughed, and it wasn’t a laugh. “And she asked me to do it. Before she stopped being her. She begged me. She said, I can feel it, I can feel it filling up, please, while it’s still me.”

“And she gave me something to carry home. For Alea. She made me say it back to her, twice, to make sure I had it right. Because I always got things wrong when I was upset, and she knew it.” He closed his eyes. “Stay healthy. Live well. Listen to your father. I’m sorry, and I love you.”

The words went out over the water.

“And then I did what she asked,” he said.

He opened his eyes.

“That’s all I’m going to say about that part. That’s all there is to say about it.”

Nobody moved. Nobody said anything. Deston, beside him, had put both hands over his mouth. Gabe’s hand on his neck was shaking. Xav sat on the end with his face turned away, towards the dark, and his shoulders were very straight.

“And then my heart hollowed out,” he said.

He’d never found a better way to say it. He’d tried, for eight years, lying awake. There wasn’t one.

“All on its own. That’s the thing I’ve never been able to… They’d spent a whole afternoon on her. A circle, and a general, and whatever they’d been learning, wherever they’d learned it. A whole afternoon to hollow out one person.” He touched his chest, just once, with two fingers, over his heart. “Mine did it by itself. No circle. Nobody had to do anything. One breath, standing on that hill, and everything that had been in there for nine years just… went. Poured out. I could feel it going. And there was nothing left. Just a hole, the shape of her. And the wind going through it.”

“And then the rage came,” he said. “Into the hole.”

He looked at the water.

“I didn’t know a person could be that angry. I’d been angry before. For five years, at sixteen, at everything. That wasn’t anger. That was a boy sulking. This was…” He shook his head. “It came up from somewhere I didn’t know I had. At them. At the general, standing right there with his horns. At whoever had taught them how. At the sky. At myself, for the ditch, for leaving her, for bringing her east, for being me, for being the kind of man you’d do that to somebody to break. At this whole world, for putting me here at all. For giving me nine years and taking them back.”

“And under the rage there was the pain,” he said. “And the pain was worse. The rage at least was doing something. The pain just sat there. Like somebody had put their hand into my chest and was holding on. It didn’t stop. It never stopped. All that afternoon and all that night it didn’t stop for one heartbeat.”

“I don’t remember most of what came after. I remember the noise. I remember the red. I remember the general running, at the end, and how surprised I was that something that big could run. I remember his horns.” He breathed. “The Redmarch was red before. That’s why it’s called that. It’s redder now. They say it’ll be red for a hundred years. That was me. One night.”

“I cut his horns off in the morning,” he said. “I don’t know why. I couldn’t think of anything else to do with my hands. They’re over the east gate at Cinderhold now. Yan and Maigo walk under them every day.”

Deston had stopped crying.

Not because he’d finished. Because he’d stopped breathing, almost. He was sitting very still on the boards with his hands in his lap and his face turned to the side, and Ars knew, without having to be told, that he was listening to something. A thread. His thread. Running out of Deston’s chest a hand’s width to his own.

He wondered what it looked like right now. He didn’t ask.

“Tell them the other part,” said Xav.

His voice was rough.

“Petra said. In the yard. The fisherman that came to the ditch was strong. Everybody knows how strong you were. But the one in the stories after, the one who did that.” He jerked his chin at the dark, east. “That wasn’t the same. That was more. Everyone says it. He wasn’t that strong before. Tell them.”

He was quiet for a moment.

Then he opened his window.

He did it the way he’d done it for Severin, in the winter, on this jetty. With the whole thing showing, for once. Every line. He turned it so that it hung in the air between the four of them over the black water, and he let them see.

[ Ars · Tier 8: Dominion · Skills: Slice, Cut, Maim, Split, Pierce, Sunder, Rend, Annihilate ]

They looked at it for a long time.

“Eight,” said Gabe. Barely a word.

“I went in at six,” he said. “That morning. On the wall, before I went over. I’d looked. I always looked, before a fight, out of habit, like checking your ammo.” He touched the air beside the line, not quite touching it. “Shaped. Not even a fifth of the way to seven. Six skills. Slice to Sunder. That’s all I had when I went over that wall.”

“And in the morning, on the rise, with his horns in my hands, I looked again. And it said that.”

He let them look.

“I skipped almost two full tiers,” he said. “In one night. I never was Unbound. Not for a heartbeat. I went straight through it like it wasn’t there. Rend and Annihilate turned up on the same line, the same morning. I’d never had two at once. Nobody has. Nobody’s ever heard of it.”

“How,” said Deston. Very softly.

“I don’t know.” He looked at the window, at the eight words in a row. “I’ve had eight years to work it out and I still don’t really know. It was something to do with…” He stopped. Tried again. “A strong feeling. Something so big it didn’t fit. The rage. The anger. The pain. And the grief, after, that came in when the rage went out, and stayed. That lingered. All of it together, too much of it, more than a Shaped man could hold. So I stopped being one. I think that’s all it was. I had to get bigger to fit it in.”

“The world wants you to write one line in its language to cross an edge. Every edge I’d ever crossed, I’d done it that way. Slowly. Like you’re all doing it now. Waiting. Listening. That night I didn’t write a line. I screamed one. And it heard.”

“They meant to break me,” he said. “That was the whole plan. And it didn’t break me. It made me stronger instead. Two tiers stronger, in a night.” He closed the window. “That’s the joke. That’s the whole joke. You want to know the thing I’ve never told anybody, not even Alea?”

“Yes,” said Gabe.

“I’d give it back.” He said it to the water. “All of it. Every tier. Both of those. Annihilate, Rend, Sunder, all the way down. I’d be Initiate again in a field behind my father’s house with nothing at all, if it would give me one more year of her laughing at me.”

Gabe made a sound.

“Could we,” Deston started. He stopped. “No. Sorry. I didn’t…”

“Ask it,” he said. “It’s all right. I’d ask it.”

“Could we do it? Like that? If we had to. If it came to it.”

He looked at Deston for a long time.

“It isn’t a door,” he said. “It’s a wound. Don’t you dare go looking for it. Any of you. I wouldn’t give it to the general.” He held Deston’s eyes. “If you ever feel it coming, if you ever feel something that big coming up in you, you find me. Wherever I am. You find me first. Promise.”

“I promise,” said Deston.

“Gabe.”

“Yeah,” said Gabe, thickly. “Promise.”

“Xav.”

“Okay,” said Xav. And then, because it was Xav: “I’d find you anyway. I always know which door you’re behind.”

He told them the rest quickly, because the rest was easier.

The morning after. Walking back across the red, through what he’d done, to the place on the rise where she was. Burying her there himself, with his hands, because nobody else could get near him and he wouldn’t have let them. Planting the black sword over her, point down, the only thing he had that was big enough. Carving the line into it, by the grip, in a language nobody in this world could read, because it was the only thing he could think of that wasn’t sorry.

“Ad astra per aspera,” said Xav. “It was in the channel. Last summer. We all knew whose it was the second we saw it. Nobody else we knew would carve Latin on a grave.”

“To the stars,” he said. “Through hardships.” He looked up at them, all those stars, lying on the lake. “I thought she’d like it. She was always looking up.”

The walk home. A month, west, alone, with a sentence in his mouth that he said over and over so he wouldn’t get it wrong. Coming down the lake road in the autumn, with the leaves turning, to a nine-year-old fox girl on the end of this jetty who’d been waiting for two people and only saw one. Saying the sentence to her, in the right order, all of it. Her face.

“She didn’t speak to me for a month,” he said. “Then she did. She forgave me, slowly. Over years. I don’t know that I’ve forgiven me. But she did, and she’s the one who got to decide.” He breathed out. “And I gave it up. The sword, the war, the king. All of it. I came here and I fished. And I was stuck at forty, on the road to Ascendant, for six years. Forty percent. It didn’t move. Not once. I didn’t want it to.”

“Then this last year,” he said. “Forty to fifty. And on.”

“Why,” said Gabe.

“Because it’s healing,” he said.

He sat for a while, looking at the water.

“I still carry it,” he said. “I want you to know that. I’m not going to sit here and pretend it’s done. I carry it every day. To this day. I wake up some mornings and reach for her before I remember. I hear a laugh in the market and turn round. I still can’t eat pumpkin without…” He stopped. “Alea grows them now. Every year. For her. And I eat them, every year, and it hurts every time, and I eat them anyway.”

“It’s a wound,” he said. “Here.” He touched his heart. “And deeper than that. Here.” He didn’t know where to touch for that one. He put his hand flat on his chest, over the bone, and held it there. “To the soul. Whatever that is. Whatever bit of me it is that came across from the other side and woke up in a cottage. It’s there too.”

“But it’s healing,” he said. “It is. I can feel it. Slowly. So slowly you wouldn’t notice it from one day to the next. The lake helps. The fish help. The quiet helps. Alea helps more than anything in either world. And I think…” He looked at the three of them. “I think you coming helps. You coming today. You singing it too loud and making the hens run away. I think that helped more than a whole year of fishing.”

“It’s healing,” he said again. “But it won’t ever be the way it was. It heals the way a deep cut heals. It closes. It stops bleeding. One day you notice it hasn’t opened in a while. And there’s a scar, and the scar’s yours, and you can feel it every time the weather turns.” He took his hand from his chest. “That’s all right. I’ve decided that’s all right. I don’t want it to heal smooth. If it healed smooth, it’d be like she was never there.”

Gabe took his hand off the back of his neck.

And put his arm round him instead, round his shoulders, the whole heavy weight of it, and pulled him sideways against his own big chest, and held on, and didn’t say anything at all. And on the other side, Deston leaned in, and put his head on his shoulder, the way he used to on the bus home from a concert when they were thirteen and too tired to sit up. And Xav, on the end, shifted along the boards without a word until his knee was against Deston’s, and sat there. Touching. Just touching.

They sat like that for a long time.

The stars went round, a little, on the water.

“Alea,” he said.

He didn’t say it loudly. He didn’t need to.

There was a small sound at the land end of the jetty. A shift on the shingle. And he felt the others look round, and heard Gabe’s breath catch.

She’d been sitting on the shingle at the foot of the jetty in a blanket. She’d been there a while. He’d felt her come down from the loft, an hour ago, very quietly, the way she’d come down the stairs when she was small and couldn’t sleep and wanted to listen to him and her mother talking by the fire. She’d sat on the shingle with her knees up and her tail round her feet and listened to all of it, the whole thing, every word, for the first time in her life from the outside.

She knew it already. She’d always known it. But she’d never heard him tell it.

She stood up. And came along the jetty, barefoot on the grey boards, past the nets, with the blanket round her shoulders and her ears forward and her face very wet and very calm, the way her mother’s face went when something mattered more than crying.

He stood up to meet her.

The other three scrambled up too. Gabe nearly knocked his cup into the lake.

“We’ve met,” said Gabe. His voice was in pieces. “At the top of the road. She told us her name.”

“Not properly,” he said.

He put his arm round her shoulders. She let him. She’d grown again since the autumn. When had that happened? He turned her, gently, so that she was facing them. The three of them. Standing in a row at the end of a jetty in another world, in their blankets, at one in the morning, with their faces wrecked. Gabe and Deston and Xav.

“Gabe,” he said. “Deston. Xav.”

“This is Alea. My daughter.”

He felt her lean against him.

“She’s Diwa’s,” he said. “She’s the gift Diwa left me. The last thing. The best thing anybody’s ever given me, in either world. They took everything else that morning on that hill. They couldn’t take her. She was nine, and she was here, on this jetty, and she was waiting, and she’s been the reason for every single day since.”

Alea looked up at him.

“Dad,” she said, very quietly. In the voice that meant you’re embarrassing me, and also don’t stop.

He didn’t stop.

“She was Tempered at sixteen,” he said. “Faster than I was. She turned seventeen the week a king came to forge her a sword, and he never knew it was her birthday. She carries half my old sword on her back. She grows the pumpkins. She feeds the hens, which hate me. She knows every one of you. Every story. I told her about you every night for eight years, because I didn’t want to forget, and because she asked. She’ll tell you she knew you before you knew she existed. She’s right.” He breathed. “She’s the best thing I’ve ever done. And I didn’t even do most of it. Her mother did.”

Nobody moved for a moment.

Then Deston stepped forward.

He stood in front of her and looked at her, and Ars saw his eyes go somewhere just below her chin, to the middle of her chest, the way they went when he was looking at something nobody else could see. Whatever he saw there made his face crumple.

“Your thread,” said Deston. “To him. It’s the thickest one I’ve ever seen.”

“I know,” said Alea. “I don’t need to see it.”

Deston laughed, wetly, and held out his hand. She took it. He felt her cold fingers close round Deston’s and saw Deston feel them too, and look down, and look up at him.

“Her mother’s hands,” he said.

Then Gabe.

Gabe didn’t hold out his hand. Gabe stood there for a moment, enormous, in a blanket, with his face doing something complicated. And then he went down on one knee on the boards, which put his face about level with hers, and looked at her.

“He told you bedtime stories,” said Gabe. “About us.”

“Every night.”

“Which was your favourite?”

Alea didn’t even have to think about it.

“The one about the choir,” she said. “And the quiet part of the song. And the three of you in the back row, trying not to laugh. And the choirmaster making you stand at the front, one at a time, until you stopped.” Her mouth twitched. “And you never stopped. And he made you all stand at the front together, and you laughed even harder, and the whole choir went with you, and it was the worst concert in the history of the school.”

Gabe put his face in his hands.

“That’s true,” he said, through them. “That actually happened. He actually told you that.”

“He did the voices,” said Alea.

“Of course he did the voices.”

And then Gabe took his hands off his face and opened his arms, and Alea, after a moment, went into them, and Gabe held her the way you’d hold something you’d been told about your whole life and never once believed you’d get to see.

Xav was last.

He waited. Of course he waited. He stood a little apart, on the end of the jetty, until Gabe let go, and Alea straightened up and wiped her face on her blanket, and turned round, and looked at him.

They looked at each other.

“You’re Xav,” said Alea. “You wait.”

“Yes.”

“He said you were the best at it. Better than him. He said you just didn’t know it yet.”

Xav didn’t say anything for a long moment.

“He never told me that,” he said.

“He wouldn’t,” said Alea. “He tells me things so that I’ll tell you. He’s always been like that. It’s very annoying.” She held out her hand, cold, steady. “Hello, Xav.”

Xav looked at her hand.

Then he took it, and shook it, once, very properly, the way you’d shake hands at the start of a match. And held on a moment longer than you would at the start of a match.

“Okay,” said Xav.

And then, very quietly, to her, not to him, the thing that was the closest Xav had ever come, in all the years Ars had known him, to saying a whole paragraph:

“Your dad’s the best player I ever played with. He carried me for years. I never said thank you.”

“I know,” said Alea. “He knows. He said you never had to.”

She sat down between Gabe and Deston, at the end of the jetty, with her feet over the water and the blanket round all three of them because it was big enough, and asked Gabe whether it was true that he’d once eaten an entire birthday cake on his own on a dare, and Gabe said that was a lie and he wanted to know who’d told her, and she pointed at her father, and everyone looked at him, and he looked at the lake.

He sat down on the end, in his place.

Xav sat down next to him. Not a little apart this time. Right next to him. Shoulder to shoulder.

They didn’t say anything. They didn’t need to.

Later, much later, when the others had gone up to the cabin and the stars had gone halfway round and the first grey was coming up behind the hills at the east end of the lake, he sat on the end of the jetty on his own, and opened his window, out of habit. The way you check your ammo.

It had said fifty-two since the winter.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he closed it, and looked at the lake instead, at the grey coming up on the water.

He’d thought, for eight years, that it was the fishing. That the slow days, the quiet, the not-wanting, were the road. And they were. They were part of it.

But he’d put something down tonight. On this jetty. Something heavy, that he’d been carrying on his own for so long he’d forgotten it could be carried any other way. He’d put it down in front of three people who’d known him when he was thirteen and fifteen and seventeen, and they’d picked it up between them without being asked, the way you pick up the other end of a table. It wasn’t any lighter. It was exactly as heavy as it had always been.

There were just more hands on it now.

He touched his chest, once, over the scar that wasn’t on his skin.

Then he got up, and went in, and stepped over Gabe, who was asleep on the floor again with Alea’s blanket over his feet, and put the kettle on.
