Chapter 141

Daughter of Diwa

5,875 words · 26 min read

I didn’t teach her that.

He’d thought it standing at the edge of the near ring, with a paper fishing rod in one hand and his real one in the other, watching his daughter go at a hook instead of away from it. He’d thought it so loudly that he’d been half sure he’d said it, and looked round, and found Xav beside him with his practice sword still in his hand, looking at him the way Xav looked at a round he’d just watched somebody else win.

He thought it again now, at the end of the afternoon, sitting on the bank of the river outside the west gate under the willow where Group 2 had sat with him four days ago. It was a good willow. He’d decided that on the first day. It hung over the water the way the old alder hung over the end of the jetty at home, and if you sat under it with a rod across your knees and looked at the water you could almost believe you were on Lake Ilse.

I didn’t teach her that.

He hadn’t. He’d taught her everything else. How to stand, how to wait, how to look at the whole thing. How to let a sword fall. How to move while it was still deciding to. Four years of dawn on the shingle and wet sand and ice. He’d taught her to be the one with the hook.

He’d never once taught her to be the fish.

“You taught her pike,” said Xav, beside him.

Xav had come out of the west gate an hour ago and sat down on the bank without saying anything, the way he’d sat down on the end of the jetty in the spring with a rod in his hands, waiting for a fish that wasn’t going to come. Gabe and Deston were a little way along the bank, lying in the grass. Gabe was asleep. Deston was humming, very quietly, the first bar of the middle part, and stopping, and humming it again.

“No,” said Ars. “That one’s her mother’s.”

Xav didn’t say anything for a while.

“Okay,” he said.

He’d known Diwa ten years.

He’d met her at seventeen, in the infirmary tent at Cinderhold, with a cut down his forearm that he’d got being stupid on a wall. She’d stitched it. She had cold hands; she always had. She’d looked at the cut and then at him, with her ears very slightly flat, and said how did you do this, and he’d told her, and she’d said that was stupid, and stitched it.

Two weeks later she’d stitched another one.

The third time, she’d put down the needle and looked at him across the tent with her hands in her lap and her ears all the way flat and said, why do you keep getting hurt? Not crossly. Like a question she actually wanted the answer to.

And he’d told her.

Not about the wall, or the fight, or whatever had cut him that time. About the other thing. The field behind his father’s house. The window. The word under the blanket at six. Twenty-six years of somewhere else, and a box of light, and a voice in his ear at two in the morning saying love you guys, and a room with a fan going in it. He’d told her all of it, in an infirmary tent at Cinderhold, at seventeen, to a healer with cold hands he’d known for three weeks, because she’d asked the right question in the right voice, and he’d been carrying it alone for seventeen years, and he couldn’t any more.

She’d listened to all of it. She hadn’t said anything until the end.

Then she’d picked up the needle again, and gone back to his arm, and said, without looking up: well, that explains the way you look at the stars.

He’d loved her from about that moment. He hadn’t told her for two years.

And she’d done it to him all the time. That was the thing he’d never once worked out. He’d have her on the line, in an argument, about anything, the garrison or the war or whether he should stop getting hurt, and he’d pull, the way he always pulled, the way he’d been pulling things to him with a hook since he was fourteen. And she’d just come at him. All at once. Toward the pull instead of away from it. Fine. You’re right. Let’s do it your way. And the line would go slack, and he’d stand there with nothing to pull against, and find that somehow, without either of them deciding it, they were doing it her way.

Ten years. He’d never once worked out how she did it.

His daughter had worked it out in a chalk ring with her heels on the line. From a pike.

“She’s at the well,” said Deston, from the grass, without opening his eyes. “With the coast girl. And Group 1. And Aleaka. Her thread’s very loud.”

“I know.”

“Sorrel’s is too. They’ve got a thread now. Between them. It wasn’t there three days ago.” Deston was quiet for a moment. “It’s gold already. On both ends. I’ve never seen one go gold that fast.”

Ars looked at the water.

“She’s never had a friend her own age who wasn’t one of the five,” he said. “Or Petra. Wrenhaven doesn’t have many girls who carry swords.”

“She’s got one now.”

“She has.”

“A fisherman’s daughter,” said Deston. “From the coast. With her dead dad’s boat-hooks.” He opened one eye. “That’s quite something, Declan.”

“I know,” said Ars.

Gabe, asleep in the grass, said something that might have been seriously.

The sun went down toward the plain. He felt it go, the way he always felt the end of a day, by the light changing on the water. He felt the city behind him, too, through the west gate and over the walls: the noise of it, the hum of forty thousand people going back into the Lists for the closing, filling it up again for the last time. He felt the candles in it. Thousands. And among them, the ones he knew.

He felt Halvard, on the hill, in the king’s house, coming down. A thin careful flame that didn’t burn quite so carefully any more.

He felt the king, already in the royal box, the way you feel a mountain from a long way off.

He felt thirty-four of the thirty-five, going into the Lists in pairs, never alone.

And he felt the thirty-nine. The gaps. Going in too. Separately. Through different gates. Taking their places in the stands, all round the bowl, one at a time. Counting.

He put it away. Not far. He kept it where he could reach it, the way you keep a knife in your coat.

And he felt Alea, coming up from the well behind the Lists, with Sorrel on one side of her and Gigichi on the other, and the five girls from the militia hall round her like a guard, and Aleaka with a hawk. Her candle, the one he’d know if he were blind and deaf and a hundred miles off, coming toward the tunnel under the east stand, very bright, very quick, the way a sword sounds when it’s being swung by somebody who’s just found out it fits.

Frightened, a bit. Not much.

“It’s time,” said Xav.

Ars stood up. He put the rod on his shoulder.

“Okay,” he said.

The Lists at sunset were gold.

He’d come in by the side gate with Gabe and Deston and Xav, and gone up the east stand the way he’d gone up it every day this week, to the fourth tier, to the end of a bench, where nobody looked. Somebody was sitting in his place. A woman with a baby. He sat down beside her instead, at the very end, and she moved her knees to let him past without looking up, and the baby looked at him for a long time with very round eyes, the way babies did. Babies and hens. They always could.

The bowl was full. Fuller than the opening. Fuller than the Banner or the Gate. Every seat, and people standing at the back of every tier, and children on shoulders, and people sitting on the rails, and up on the very top of the wall where the stands met the sky, a ragged line of boys silhouetted against the gold, who’d climbed up from outside.

And the paper. Everywhere. Horns and squids and penguins and kettles and moths. And fishing rods. More fishing rods than he’d ever seen. In every hand, it seemed, all round the bowl, swaying in the warm evening wind off the plain, like reeds along a lakeshore. Ten thousand in the windows of the city, and Lucan had sold another ten at the gates this week, and people had made their own out of sticks and string.

Nobody knew what they were for. People were still asking each other. He could hear them, on the tiers round him. What are the rods? Why rods? Lucan won’t say.

For the Hero, a girl had said, in the winter, to a man who wept easily. Just in case.

He looked at the bottom tier of the east stand. Group 7 were there, in a row: Chris with his notebook, Ina with his mask on his shoulder, Hako with his spear, Winter very straight. And Newish, at the end, with her knees up and her hands in her pockets.

She wasn’t looking at the sand. She was looking at the paper fishing rods. All round the bowl. Twenty thousand of them, swaying.

He watched her look at them for a long time.

The closing was on the master’s platform.

Lucan had built it up for the week, the way he did every midsummer: a raised wooden stage at the edge of the sand under the royal box, hung with red cloth and gold fringe and paper fishing rods, where the master of the Lists sat in his rocking chair and the marshals stood and the champions were called up one by one to have their names read into the roll. Drew was in the chair. Lucan stood beside it, in his best red cloak, the one with the most gold on it, which he’d been saving.

And behind them, on the east wall, the bracket.

Forty feet long. Black paint on whitewash, gold on the lines. Every box filled in now, all week, a trial at a time. Five squads down the left with their names: THE KETTLE. THE STRONGHOLD. THE COMMITTEE. WOLF, with a small dog. EXCEPT NEWISH. The three trials across the top, with the winners in gold: THE KETTLE. IV for the Banner. THE STRONGHOLD. VI for the Gate, with a small drawing, beside it, in Bee’s hand, of a gate lying flat. The Trios tree, all the way to the end, and at the end, ROOM FOR BOTH, in Chris’s square capitals, painted over ONE DOWN. The Singles tree, and at its end, XAVIER.

And in the top right-hand corner, the last box. Over all of them. The box for the whole thing.

Empty.

Except for the spider.

“Master of the Lists,” said Lucan.

His voice went out over the bowl the way it always did, like a kite, without pushing. He’d been doing this for fifteen years. He’d stopped weeping for the closing about an hour ago, Ars thought; there was a stillness in him, standing on the platform, that hadn’t been there all week.

“The bracket,” said Lucan. “Is full.” He turned and looked up at it. At forty feet of it. “Every box. Every trial. Every squad. Every name.” He paused. “Except one.”

He turned to Drew.

Drew was sitting in the rocking chair with his arms on its pale-worn arms and his 88EXP nameplate catching the last of the sun. He’d been sitting there all week. He’d run every trial from it, in his calm voice, the voice that made rooms listen. He’d run a tournament once from a bedroom and muted the casters in the final because a man was dying in every round and narrating it. He’d been waiting all week to fill one box.

“Whose name,” said Lucan, “goes in the top box?”

The Lists went quiet.

Drew stood up.

He didn’t say anything. He went down off the platform, and across the sand to the foot of the east wall, where the painter’s ladder was still standing from the morning, with a pot of black paint and a brush hanging off the top rung. And he climbed it.

Slowly. Unhurried. The whole Lists watching. Up the ladder, forty thousand people silent in the gold light, to the top right-hand corner of the bracket. To the last box. Where the spider sat in the middle of its web, a big brown garden spider with a cross on its back, top seed, where it had been since the second day.

Drew looked at the spider for a long moment.

Then he took the brush out of the pot, and very carefully, without touching the web, without disturbing the spider, he painted round it.

Ars watched him do it. Letter by letter. In black capitals, in the box, in this world’s script on one line so the city could read it, and underneath, smaller, in English, for thirty-five people:

THE THIRTY-FIVE.

The spider sat in the middle of it, in its web, between THE and THIRTY, and didn’t move.

Drew climbed down the ladder.

He stood at the foot of the wall for a moment, looking up at it. Then he turned round to the stands.

“Everyone played,” said Drew. He didn’t raise his voice. It went out over the bowl anyway. “Everyone held something. A hill. A gate. A slot on someone’s left. A pole. A grudge. A word they’d been rehearsing for three months.” He looked up at the box. “That’s the whole bracket. That’s the only name that goes at the top.”

On the platform, Lucan put his face in his hands and wept.

The Lists went up.

Ars didn’t stand up for it. He sat on the end of the fourth tier beside a woman with a baby and watched forty thousand people roar for a box with a spider in it, and found that he had his hand over his mouth.

The baby was still looking at him.

Lucan read the champions.

He did it the old way, from the roll. One at a time. Called up to the platform, and their names read out, full, the way they were written. Three hundred years of it. The city stood and listened.

“The Banner. The Kettle.” And seven of them went up onto the platform: Gigichi with her watch, scowling, and Jared, and Calvin holding his dented kettle over his head, and Kei, and Azac, and Hako, and Wendy in absence, because she was in the stand with Group 3 and wouldn’t come down. Lucan read their names. Every one. The city cheered every one.

“The Gate. The Stronghold.” Tiger first up the steps, at a run. Gabe, very red. PenguChi with her penguin. Salty, with the king’s father’s hammer still in his belt. RX, already talking. Milli, Drawn for three days. And Ina, with his mask on his shoulder, last, at the pace of a man taking the air, who bowed to all four stands from the top step and got the loudest cheer of the night so far.

“The Trios,” said Lucan, and paused, and looked at the roll. “Room for Both.” Chris, Winter, Newish. Up the steps together. Winter in the middle. Newish on her left. Lucan read their names, and when he read Winter, the north stand started chanting it again, and didn’t stop for a long time.

“The Singles. Xavier, of Duskhollow.”

Xav went up on his own. He stood on the platform with his hands at his sides and his face doing nothing, and Lucan read his name, and the city cheered, and the old soldiers on the third tier of the south stand stood up and put their hands on their chests. Xav looked at them for a long moment. Then he looked up at the fourth tier of the east stand.

Ars lifted his hand. Xav lifted his.

“Okay,” Xav said, on the platform, to nobody, and went back down.

“The Fledgling Lists,” said Lucan.

The Lists went very quiet.

Ars felt it. The whole bowl, forty thousand candles, leaning forward at once. Toward the platform. Toward the tunnel under the east stand, where a fox girl had walked out at noon with a sword the size of a door and come at a hook like a pike. They’d learned her this week. The lake girl. Let it fall. They’d made a bedsheet.

“Alea,” said Lucan, “of Wrenhaven.”

She came out of the tunnel.

She walked across the gold sand in the last of the light with Astra on her back and her red-gold hair tied up and her ears up, very straight, the way she’d walked out every day this week. Not fast. Not slow. Behind her at the mouth of the tunnel, in a row, the people who’d walked her there: Petra, with her sword on. Sorrel, barefoot, with her hooks on her shoulders. And five girls from a militia hall in Wrenhaven, who’d waited at the top of a road for twenty months, and Aleaka with a hawk.

Alea went up the steps of the platform.

Lucan was holding the roll. The great leather-bound roll of the Lists, open, with the pen laid across it. He didn’t read her name out yet. He held the roll, and looked at her, and Ars saw his face go very still, the way it had gone still at noon by the near ring.

“For three hundred years,” said Lucan, to the stands, “the champions of this sand have been read into the roll by their full names. Where they’re from. And who their people are. So that the city knows.” He looked down at the roll. “This one has a box left blank.”

The Lists were silent.

“She asked me to leave it,” said Lucan. “Until she’d won something that was hers.” He held out the roll. And the pen. “She’s won it.”

Alea took the pen.

Ars watched her take it. From the fourth tier, from the end of a bench, three hundred feet away. He’d watched her do a great many things in her life. Take her first steps on the boards of the cabin, holding onto her mother’s tail. Catch her first perch off the end of the jetty and haul on it and lose it. Stand in the door of a militia hall at sixteen in front of five strangers she’d known all her life from bedtime stories. Swing a black sword on a frozen lake. Come at a hook.

He’d never watched her hold a pen in front of forty thousand people.

She held it the way her mother had held a needle. Very still, for a moment. Thinking. With her ears flat.

Then she wrote.

He couldn’t see the page. He was too far away, and the roll was held against her arm, and the light was going. He saw her write. Slowly. Carefully. A word. Another. More. The pen moving across the page in the gold light, in the box her guide had left blank by a fire on the shingle in the spring, while her father nodded across the flames and didn’t say anything.

Let her.

She stopped.

She looked at it for a long time.

Then she held the roll out to Lucan, open, so that he could read it. And Lucan took it from her, and looked down at the page, at what she’d written in the box.

And Ars watched the master of the Lists, a man who’d wept at everything for a week, go perfectly, completely still.

Lucan didn’t read it straight away.

He stood on the platform with the roll in his hands and looked at the page, and Ars saw his eyes go along the line, and back, and along it again. As if he were reading something in a language he’d known all his life and had just found out had a word in it he’d never heard.

Then he lifted his head.

He didn’t look at the girl beside him. He didn’t look at the stands. He looked, very slowly, up past the royal box, along the east stand, to the fourth tier.

To the end of a bench.

Ars felt it land. Lucan’s eyes, coming to him. And sliding off, the way everyone’s did, the way they’d slid off all week. And coming back. And sliding off. And coming back again, as if Lucan had decided, for once in his life, to keep looking at a thing until it let him see it.

He couldn’t see Ars’s face. Nobody could.

But he looked at the man on the end of the bench with a fishing rod across his knees, and a woman with a baby beside him, in a stand full of twenty thousand paper fishing rods hung up for the Hero, just in case, at the word of a girl with a ragebait voice. And Ars saw it go across his face. All of it at once.

Of course he is.

Lucan turned back to the stands. He held the roll up. And he read it out, the way he’d read out every name for fifteen years, the way champions had been read for three hundred: full, and clear, letting his voice go out over the bowl like a kite.

His voice didn’t break. Ars would always remember that. For the only time that week, Lucan’s voice didn’t break at all.

“Alea,” read Lucan, “of Wrenhaven.”

A breath.

“Daughter of Diwa, a healer.”

Another.

“And of Ars.”

Nobody in the Lists made a sound.

Ars felt it. Forty thousand candles, all round the great bowl, going still. Not one at a time. All at once. The way a field of barley goes still when the wind drops.

Ars.

It was the name. The world’s name for him. Not Ice, not Declan: those were names for thirty-five people and a fan going in a room in Malacca. Ars was what the kingdom called the Hero of the Redmarch, when it called him anything but the Black Blade. It was in every ballad. Every soldier’s story. Every song in every inn from Solmere to the sea, about a man seven feet tall with eyes like coals and a horse called Midnight, who’d routed an army and dyed a plain red and disappeared. Ars. Nobody knew where he’d gone. Nobody had seen him in eight years.

And of Ars.

And the other name. The one nobody had ever said. The ballads said the healer. The soldiers said his healer, or the woman, or nothing at all, and changed the subject. The city had known for eight years that the Hero had loved someone on the Redmarch and that she’d died, and it had never once known her name.

Daughter of Diwa, a healer.

Mother Breck had said it once, in a market, in the first winter. Just the name. And then gone on buying her onions. She was the only person in Wrenhaven who’d ever said it to his face.

His daughter had just said it to the kingdom.

Ars stood up.

He didn’t decide to. He found he’d done it. He was standing at the end of the fourth tier of the east stand, with the rod in one hand, the way he’d stood up after round one and round five, in a section of the stand where nobody looked at him.

And then somebody did.

On the bottom tier, Newish had turned round. She was looking straight up at him, with her hands out of her pockets for once, and her face white. Beside her Chris had turned. And Winter. And Ina, and Hako.

And then all of them. Thirty-four of the thirty-five, on the bottom tiers of the east stand, turning round at once, one after another, the way grass turns when the wind changes. Group 1, and Group 2, and Group 3, and Group 4 and Group 6 who were his brothers and his class, and Group 5 and Group 7. Thirty-four faces, in the gold light, looking up at the fourth tier.

At the end of a bench.

And the people round the thirty-four saw them turn. And turned too, to see what they were looking at. And the people round them. And round them. Ars felt it go up the stand toward him like a wave going up a beach. A whole section of the east stand turning round on its benches to look up at the fourth tier, at a man standing on the end of a bench with a fishing rod in his hand.

Their eyes went to his face, and slid off.

All of them. Thousands. Sliding off, the way they always had, the way they’d slid off for thirty-four years. They looked at him and couldn’t see him. They looked for a man seven feet tall with eyes like coals, and couldn’t find one.

But they could see the rod.

He felt it happen.

He’d felt a lot of things happen in his life. A war turning. A woman’s candle going out on a rise inside a ring of fires. Thirty-five threads going gold. He’d never felt this.

Forty thousand people, in the Lists, at sunset, looking at a fishing rod in a man’s hand. And then looking down at the ones in their own hands. Paper ones. Twenty thousand of them, swaying in the wind off the plain. That they’d been holding all week without knowing why. That a man who wept easily had hung in every window in their city in the winter, at the word of a girl, for the Hero, just in case.

He felt the city understand.

It didn’t happen all at once. It went round the bowl the way the paper horns had gone round it on the first day, from a single point, outward. From the woman beside him with the baby, who’d turned round on the bench and was looking at the rod in his hand and then at the paper one in her own, and whose face was doing something very slow. From her, to the people next to her. To the tier below. To the whole east stand.

And then somebody, somewhere, held a paper fishing rod up.

Over their head. In both hands. As high as they could reach. The way a small boy had held up paper horns on a bench last summer.

And then the east stand did.

And the south. And the west. And the north, where the men in grey and blue sat, every one of them holding one up now, the one in the farmer’s paper crown at the front. Twenty thousand paper fishing rods, held up over forty thousand heads, all round the great bowl of the Lists, in the last gold light of midsummer. In silence. Toward the fourth tier of the east stand. Toward a man none of them could see.

For the Hero. Just in case.

On the bottom tier, Newish had both her hands over her face.

He didn’t know what to do with his hands.

He stood on the fourth tier with the real rod in his hand and twenty thousand paper ones held up toward him, and didn’t know what to do. He’d known what to do with people who were angry with him for thirty-four years. He’d known what to do with people who were frightened of him, or wanted something from him, or wanted him to go away. He’d said that to his daughter on a hill above this city, at midnight, a week ago. I don’t know what to do with twelve people being happy at me all at once.

It wasn’t twelve.

He looked down at the platform.

His daughter was standing there beside Lucan, with Astra on her back and her ears all the way up, looking at him. Across three hundred feet of air and forty thousand people. With her mother’s face, and her mother’s ears, and her mother’s habit of deciding whether he was worth the trouble.

She’d decided. She’d decided in the spring, by a fire, with a blank box.

He did the only thing he could think of.

He lifted the rod.

Not high. Not over his head. Just up, off his shoulder, a little, the way you lift a rod at the end of a long evening on a jetty to show the person on the shore that you’ve seen them. The way he’d lifted it at the first bend of a road out of Wrenhaven, three weeks ago, to a man standing in the square who’d said nobody follows.

The way a coast girl had lifted a hook to his daughter across the Lists. Like somebody on a quay lifting a hand to a boat.

On the platform, Alea lifted her hand back.

Somewhere in the royal box, a big grey man had stood up.

Ars felt him do it. A mountain, standing. And beside him a thin careful flame, standing too, slowly, as if its knees didn’t trust it, with one hand on the rail. And behind them, on the rail, a small hard bright point, Reaching, very still, that didn’t stand because it had been standing all along.

The king of Aurelan stood in the royal box of the Lists with forty thousand people holding up paper fishing rods round him, and looked at the fourth tier of the east stand, and put his hand flat on his chest.

Over his heart.

Then the old soldiers on the third tier of the south stand did it too. The Redmarch men. Seven or eight of them. Their hands on their chests. And then other men, all round the bowl, older ones, ones who’d been young nine years ago, one at a time. Hands on chests, over paper fishing rods held up in the other hand.

Ars didn’t know what it meant. He’d never seen it before.

He’d ask Petra later. She’d know. She’d been in a ditch.

He felt the thirty-nine, too.

He made himself. He kept it where he could reach it, the way you keep a knife in your coat. Thirty-nine gaps in the field of candles, spread round the bowl of the Lists, one at a time. They hadn’t stood up. They hadn’t held anything up. They were sitting in their places among forty thousand people with their paper fishing rods held over their heads, and they weren’t counting any more.

They were all looking at the same place.

At the fourth tier. At him. All thirty-nine at once.

Not counting. Not leaning. Just looking. The way the absence at the foot of a mountain pass had turned once, Hyvred said, and looked at a town for fifty heartbeats, and then turned back as if it had seen what it wanted.

Good, he thought. Look at me. Not at her.

He looked back at them. At all thirty-nine. One at a time. The way he’d looked at five girls on a market road on the first day, and Group 3 on the red, and thirty people at a north gate. As if he were reading something written on each of them and wanted to get every word.

There was nothing written on any of them.

He looked anyway. So they’d know he had.

He sat down, in the end. He didn’t know when. The paper rods were coming down, slowly, all round the bowl, the way the horns had come down; people sitting down, wiping their faces, holding the rods in their laps now, looking at them. The noise was coming back. Not a roar. A hum. Forty thousand people all talking at once, to the people next to them, about a girl and a name and a fishing rod.

The woman beside him with the baby was crying. She didn’t look at him. She looked at the paper rod in her lap and cried, and the baby looked up at him with very round eyes, and reached out one fat hand, and took hold of the end of his real one.

He let it.

He opened his window. Out of habit. The way you check your ammo.

He looked at it for a long time.

It had said fifty-eight on the Last Look, the night before the north gate. Somewhere this week it had moved. He didn’t know where. In a gate square, perhaps, when a girl said you’re late. In a small room facing north, in the dark, with a chair he’d picked up off the floor. On the sand at midnight, saying here to thirty-five people. On a step, with a king humming a river song.

Or just now. On the end of a bench. Lifting a rod.

He closed it.

Below him, on the platform, Lucan had stopped weeping.

He’d closed the roll. He was holding it against his chest with both arms, the way Oswin had held a signed book. Drew was standing beside him with his hand on his shoulder. The marshals were looking at each other. Alea was standing at the front of the platform with Astra on her back, looking up at the fourth tier, and beside her, very suddenly, there was a coast girl, barefoot, who’d come up the steps without being asked and put her arm through Alea’s and was grinning at the stands like a gap in a fence.

Sorrel turned her head and said something into Alea’s ear. He couldn’t hear what. He didn’t need to.

Now tell me the story.

Alea laughed.

He saw her laugh. From three hundred feet. Her head going back and her ears going straight up and her whole face doing the thing her mother’s face used to do, on a jetty, at the end of a long day, when he’d said something stupid. And Ars, on the fourth tier, sitting at the end of a bench with a baby holding onto the end of his rod, put his hand over his face and sat there.

Look at her, Diwa, he thought.

He’d said it out loud, in English, at the foot of the stairs, the night Gabe fitted her grip. Look at our girl.

He said it again now. Not out loud. Into his hand. In a language nobody in the Lists could understand, on the last night of midsummer, with forty thousand people round him who’d just heard his daughter say her mother’s name.

Look at our girl.

And he thought, very clearly, for the first time in nine years, sitting in the gold light at the end of the Lists, that she probably was.

End of Part Three.
