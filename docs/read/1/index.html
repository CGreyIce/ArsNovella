The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 1: Thirty-Four Winters

Chapter 1

Thirty-Five

3,451 words · 15 min read

Three years after Ice died in his sleep, thirty-five people vanished from Earth in the same instant.

Some of them were mid-sentence. Some were asleep. One was halfway through a sandwich. There was no light and no voice, no tunnel, no goddess, no truck. One moment there was a world, and the next there was cold stone under their cheeks and a ceiling none of them had ever seen.

Hyvred saw all of it.

That was the first thing, before the cold or the voices or the strange new weight on her head. She opened her eyes and the ceiling was simply there: the whole of it, edge to edge, a vast painted dome of gold suns and blue night held up by ribs of white stone. She didn’t have to hunt for it. She didn’t have to sweep her head in slow, careful arcs, stitching the room together from the small bright circle her eyes allowed her. It was all there at once, the way people had always told her the world was.

She started crying before she knew why.

“Hyv.” Someone was shaking her shoulder. “Hyv, look at me. Are you hurt?”

Bee. Bee’s voice, blunt and frightened, which was how Bee sounded when she was working very hard not to sound frightened. Hyvred turned her head and found her best friend kneeling over her, and she could see Bee’s face and the room behind it and the people behind that, all at once, all of it sharp.

Bee had antennae. Bee also had wings, two long sheets of something like veined glass trembling behind her shoulders, and two round, fluffy yellow shapes were bobbing anxiously beside her ear.

“Your Pollen Puffs,” Hyvred said thickly. “Bee. They’re real.”

“I know they’re real. One of them is in my hair.” Bee squinted at her. “You have horns.”

Hyvred reached up. Smooth and curved and warm as bone. Her glasses had slid down her nose, and when she pushed them back into place the world went soft and wrong, so she pushed them higher, up into her hair, and the world came back: wide and bright and impossibly whole.

“I can see the ceiling,” she said.

Bee opened her mouth, shut it again, and then, because Bee never said anything she didn’t mean, said, “Okay. Then cry. I’ll watch for both of us.”

So Hyvred lay on the floor of a place she didn’t know and cried at the ceiling. And because she could see everything now, she saw what nobody else seemed to: the lines carved into the floor were still glowing. A thin silver light ran in rings and spokes across the whole hall, toward the centre, where something tall and black stood planted in the stone.

The light was fading. Slowly, like a pulse winding down.

On the other side of the circle, someone shouted “Demon!” and thirty swords left their sheaths at once.

Chris had been awake for perhaps twenty seconds. He had used them to establish three facts: that he was lying on a floor, that a translucent panel of text was hovering in front of his face like an unrequested pop-up, and that whoever had chosen its font had excellent taste. He had not yet got as far as where.

Now he got as far as who. Soldiers in grey and gold ringed the hall, and they were pointing their swords at Winter.

She was on her knees a couple of metres away, one hand pressed to her head, where two short black horns curved back through her silver hair. She looked exactly like her model, which was impossible, and she looked exactly like a demon, apparently, which was worse. Beside her, Ina had risen to one knee, slow and careful. His single purple horn caught the light. The grinning grey mask at his shoulder caught it better.

“Don’t,” Ina said quietly to the nearest soldier, in the unhurried British voice people always listened to. “Whatever you’re about to do, I’d reconsider.”

It did not seem to help.

Chris stood up. He wasn’t sure why. He was thirty-three, the oldest person he could see, a man whose most dangerous habit was pushing code to production on a Friday afternoon. He stepped between the swords and his friends, put both hands up, and said the first thing that arrived in his head.

“Hi. Hello. Okay. These are my friends, and they are not demons, and I say that as someone who has no idea what a demon is in this context.”

Thirty swords did not lower.

“Love that the first thing this world did,” said Newish, somewhere behind him, “was try to stab Winter.”

“Newish,” said Hako. “Maybe don’t.”

Winter said nothing. Chris could feel her behind him, very still: the stillness of someone who had already decided not to trust anybody in this room and was waiting to be proved right. He kept talking, because talking was the only weapon he had.

“Here’s what I’m thinking. You summoned us. I’m pretty sure that’s what the glowing floor is about. Which means you meant to. And if you meant to, stabbing the first two people you see is a bug, not a feature. So let’s not ship it.”

“Lower your blades.”

The voice came from a dais at the far end of the hall. It wasn’t loud. It didn’t need to be. The swords came down as one.

A man in grey robes stood at the top of the steps: grey-haired, grey-bearded, his hands folded into his sleeves. He looked at Winter and Ina for a long, careful moment. Then he looked at Chris, and something that might have been an apology crossed his face and was gone.

“They are not demons,” he told the soldiers. “They are guests. Treat them as such.”

Chris let his hands fall. They were shaking. Behind him, very quietly, Winter said, “Thanks.”

“Anytime,” said Chris, and then, because his brain had apparently decided this was the moment: “Do you think there’s a changelog?”

Yan woke under the weight of a snake, which was new.

She was enormous: a white serpent longer than he was tall, coiled across his chest, her head raised above his face, her green eyes fixed on his. He knew those eyes. He had spoken for them on a hundred streams.

“Suzu,” he said.

She hissed at him. Then, as he watched, she shrank, length pouring away like water, until what lay across his collarbone was a slim white snake no thicker than a scarf. She slid once around his neck and settled there, cool and dry and unmistakably offended.

His hands, when he lifted them, were not quite his own: long fingers in black gloves, a cuff of crimson sleeve. When he touched his ear it went on for longer than an ear should, up into a point.

“Am I a tree?” said someone nearby, very loudly.

A small figure in a mustard-orange hoodie was sitting up beside him, two leafy branches poking out through the hood like antlers, his skin the rough brown of bark. He was wearing pixelated black sunglasses that should not have been able to exist outside a meme.

“Yan.” Maigo held up his hands, wooden fingers ridged with grain, and turned them over. “Buddy. Honest answer. Am I a tree?”

Yan considered him with the seriousness the question deserved. “Yes.”

“Sick,” said Maigo.

Salty was already on his feet, arms a little out from his sides as if he expected the floor to try something: exactly himself, short and broad and unimpressed. Wendy sat behind him with her lavender headphones pressed to her ears, her face going blank as she realised nothing was coming through. Mashi sat cross-legged, thin and calm as ever, reading something in the air that nobody else could see.

“Status window,” he said, before Yan could ask. “You’ve got one too. Think about it and it opens.”

Yan thought about it. It opened.

For a moment, very far down, he thought of a small kitchen, and the woman who would be standing in it tomorrow morning, wondering why he hadn’t called.

He put the thought away. He was good at that. He would think about it later, when there was time, and when nobody was looking to him to see what to do next.

Because they were. All four of them, glancing his way, the way they always did at the start of a round.

“Okay,” Yan said, in the voice that called rotations. “Nobody touch anything. Stay together.”

A few metres away, a man in a vivid green coat sat very still with a small green snake wrapped around his wrist, saying through his teeth, “I am not talking to you. You’re not talking. Snakes don’t talk.”

“Kei?” Yan called.

Kei looked up, saw him, and sagged with relief. Then the snake said something nobody else could hear, and Kei shut his eyes.

“His name,” Kei said, “is Hisui. Apparently. I didn’t ask.”

Thea’s status window didn’t look like a window. It looked like code.

Everyone else around her—Xola, Higu, PenguChi, and the last person on Earth Thea had wanted to wake up next to—was staring at a neat little panel of text. Thea could see the panel too. But she could also see under it: a fine, shifting lattice of light, lines nested inside lines, the way a page has structure beneath its words if you know how to look. Values. Conditions. Something like a function, calling itself over and over.

She didn’t know the language. She knew it was one.

“Thea. Thea, Thea, Thea.” Xola had her by the sleeve. “Look at my hair.”

Xola’s hair was lavender melting into pink, and it went down past her knees. Feathery purple gills fanned out behind her ears. And sitting on top of her head, blinking at the hall in open alarm, was a small round purple blob with little ear-flaps.

“Squiddles is real,” Xola said, her voice wobbling between delight and something much bigger. “Squiddles is real, and I’m an axolotl, and I don’t know if I’m going to scream or cry or—“

“Breathe,” said PenguChi.

It was just a word. But PenguChi said it the way she said everything, slow and low and steady, and it went into Thea’s chest like warm water. Xola breathed. Thea breathed. Even Higu, sitting very straight with her eyes on the soldiers, let her shoulders come down an inch. In PenguChi’s lap, a small yellow penguin pressed itself to her knee and made a noise like a squeaky door.

“Waddles,” PenguChi murmured. “Hi, baby.”

“Okay,” said Higu, calm and decisive, already finished panicking. “We don’t move until someone tells us what this is. We stay together.”

“Together,” said a voice behind Thea. “Sure. Great plan.”

Gigichi.

Thea didn’t turn around. She knew exactly what Gigichi’s face was doing: the blunt, defensive set of the mouth that said I already know how this ends. They hadn’t spoken in months, not since Thea had finally said it out loud: that she was tired of being the only one who ever showed up.

Of all the people in all the worlds to be dropped beside.

“Gigi,” said Xola, very small. “Please. Not now.”

Nobody answered. Thea looked back at her window instead, at the lattice under the words, and followed one line down and down until it ran into something that wasn’t hers at all. A single thread that went out: out of her window, out across the floor, toward the centre of the hall.

Toward the sword.

She blinked, and lost it.

Xav read his whole window twice before he said a word. Rules first. You didn’t run out of spawn until you knew what the buttons did.

[ Xavier · Tier 0: Initiate · Path: none · Skills · Inventory ]

“Bro,” said Azac. “Bro. We have inventories.”

“Mine’s empty,” said RX, already up on one knee, eyes moving: exits, soldiers, dais, exits. “Two big doors at the end, a small one behind the stage, guards on all three, windows too high. If this is a dungeon it’s a bad one.”

“It’s not a dungeon,” said John, with total confidence, turning in a slow circle to take it all in. “Dungeons don’t have stained glass. It’s a tutorial zone.”

Malcolm sat with his elbows on his knees, staring at his window like a man doing arithmetic he didn’t like. “Tier zero,” he said. “Which means there’s a tier one, and a two, and probably a ten. And we’re at the bottom.” He looked up. “Nobody puts you at the bottom of a ladder unless they want you to climb it.”

Nobody had an answer to that.

Xav looked at his window again. Name, tier, path, two tabs. Clean and sensible, everything you needed and nothing you didn’t: the kind of layout you only got from someone who’d played a lot of games and hated bad menus.

That was what bothered him. Whoever made this has played games.

He filed it away, the way he filed away an enemy’s habits in the first round of a match, and stood up. The others turned to him, because that was how it had always worked.

“We don’t know the rules yet, so nobody does anything stupid until we do. Azac, front. RX, doors. John, keep looking. Mal—“

“I’ll be here,” said Malcolm, “expecting the worst.”

“Good. Somebody should.”

Azac grinned and moved in front of them, big and easy and planted, the way he’d stood in front of them in every dungeon they’d ever run. It had taken him a while to get used to that spot. It used to be somebody else’s.

Hanarisu didn’t say anything at all.

She sat where she’d woken, a fluffy purple tail curled around her ankles (she had a tail; she would deal with that later), and she watched.

She watched the soldiers, and who they looked at for orders: the grey man on the dais. He was studying every face in the hall, one by one, as if he were counting them. As if he were committing them to memory. He stopped on the girl with golden horns, the one crying at the ceiling, and stayed there a long time.

“Honey.” Milli’s hand found hers. There were brown cat ears in her husband’s hair and an expression of deep, impatient disbelief on his face. “You okay?”

“I’m fine. You?”

“I have a tail,” said Milli. “I have opinions about it.”

Beside them, Aleaka had both hands raised to the big black-tipped fox ears that had replaced her own. “I’m— sorry, I’m a fox,” she said, stumbling over it the way she did when something mattered too much. “Hana, that’s not a normal thing to be.”

“Looks good on you,” said Jared, leaning against a pillar, so completely unruffled that Hanarisu knew he was rattled to the bone. His hand kept closing on nothing, as if there should have been something in it.

Cursiffe hadn’t spoken either. He was watching the same things she was, and when their eyes met he gave her one small nod: I see it too.

Whatever this was, it had been planned. Somebody had decided it, and that somebody was standing on the steps with his hands in his sleeves. Hanarisu intended to know exactly what he wanted before she gave him anything at all.

She kept her face pleasant. She was very good at that.

Deston was tall enough to see over most of the room, which was how he saw the grey man come down from the dais before anyone else did.

The man crossed the hall without hurrying and stopped in front of Rachael. He didn’t ask permission. He crouched, laid two fingers against the inside of her wrist, and murmured a single word Deston didn’t catch. A faint warmth moved through the air between them, like heat off summer pavement.

“There,” the man said softly. “That will hold.” Then he rose and walked back toward the steps as if nothing had happened.

“What,” said Rachael, “the hell was that.”

“I don’t know,” said Deston. “I’m going to find out.”

But Rachael had put one hand flat on her chest and was breathing in, long and slow and astonished, like someone stepping out of a stuffy room into mountain air. “Dest,” she said. “I feel good. Like, actually good. Is that weird?”

Gabe came up at his shoulder. He’d been quiet since he woke, which wasn’t like him, and he was looking around the hall with an odd expression: the stone, the soldiers, the painted suns.

“Bro,” Gabe said. “Declan would have lost his mind.”

Deston laughed before he could stop himself. Then he didn’t, because it was true, and it hurt. To most of the people in this hall he’d been Ice, a voice in a call. To the two of them, and a handful of others, he’d always been Declan. And Declan would have been the first one up. He’d already have a nickname for the grey man, a bit about the soldiers, and some completely untrustworthy promise about how he was going to speedrun this place. Trust.

“Yeah,” said Deston. “He would’ve.”

They stood there for a moment, the two of them, in a world their friend would have loved, missing him.

Then Calvin said, “Is that a dragon on the ceiling?” and Dennis said, “Forget the dragon, is that real gold?” and the moment was over.

Deston let his gaze travel the hall. He knew more of these faces than he should have. Yan, with a snake around his neck. Aleaka, with fox ears. And by the far pillar—he looked twice—Xav and Malcolm and RX, Declan’s friends from polytechnic. Something tugged at him, the shape of a thought, like a word on the tip of his tongue.

“YOU WILL FORM GROUPS OF FIVE,” bellowed a herald from the steps, and the shape slipped away.

What followed was chaos.

It turned out that thirty-five people dropped into another world will mostly try to stand next to whoever they love, and half of them loved people in three different directions. Gabe, in particular, seemed to know everyone. Yan waved him over. Jared waved him over. Xav shouted his name from across the hall. For a while Gabe just stood in the middle of the floor, turning slowly, like a man being fought over at a family reunion.

Maigo bounced between Yan’s lot and the girl with the golden horns until Salty picked him up and carried him back. Kei was claimed by four people at once. Someone ended up in a group of seven. Someone else ended up in a group of two and was very upset about it.

Then Drew climbed onto the edge of a stone plinth and clapped once.

“Okay! Hi! Everyone!”

He didn’t shout. He had never needed to. Drew had the kind of voice that turned a room toward it, calm and warm and faintly amused: the voice of a man who had run more tournament brackets than he could count, and was about to run one more.

“If you’re in a group of six, one of you is in the wrong group. Figure out who loves you least.” Laughter, startled and scattered. “Groups of four, hands up. Great, you two, come here. Group of seven at the back, I can see you. You know what you did.”

It took him ten minutes. When he was done, seven groups of five stood on the silvered stone, and the hall had gone quiet again.

In the end, Gabe walked back to Deston, the way he always had.

The grey man waited until the last of them had settled. Then he came to the edge of the dais.

“Welcome to Aurelan,” he said. “And I am sorry.”

He didn’t explain the apology. He lifted one hand instead, toward the centre of the hall, where the silver light in the floor had finally gone out, and for the first time all thirty-five of them looked properly at what stood there.

A sword.

A slab of black iron, crude and enormous, taller than any of them, planted point-down in the stone. Its edge was notched. Its grip was wrapped in old leather, worn smooth by years of someone’s hand.

“Who,” said Salty, frowning at it, “could even lift that?”

Nobody in the hall could have told him.

The only man in the world who could was many days’ ride away, sitting on a jetty on Lake Ilse with his daughter, mending a fishing net. He had no idea, yet, that any of them were here.
