The Steps

7,079 words · 31 min read

Dennis had been a lamp post for thirty-nine nights, and he was getting good at it.

Not a real one. Tanner’s Row didn’t have lamp posts; it had a lamp on a bracket over the door of the Three Coopers and one over the infirmary gate and nothing in between but cobbles and dark and the smell of the tannery pits. So he’d added one. Halfway up, on the corner by the net-mender’s shed, where you could see the whole of the Row from the river at the bottom to the infirmary gate at the top. An iron post with a lantern on it, and the lantern lit, and a little puddle of yellow light on the cobbles round its foot. Every night from dusk till the first grey. And inside it, leaning on the wall of the net-mender’s shed with his arms folded and his eyes doing the thing, Dennis.

It was the best glamour he’d ever made. He was quite proud of it. Nobody had walked into it in thirty-nine nights, which he’d been worried about; a drunk had leaned on it once and been very confused, and Dennis had had to make it lean back. And it didn’t ring. That was the thing he was proudest of. Every glamour he’d ever made had rung cracked in his own ears since the Lists, since Milli’s cut, a little high wrong note like a bell with a split in it, the sound of a lie. The lamp post didn’t. Because it wasn’t one. There really was a light on that corner, every night now, and somebody under it watching the Row, and it was true.

He’d told Sabine that. She’d said you’re a lamp post that’s a man that’s a lamp post and charged him a fee for the joke.

At a quarter past one on the thirty-ninth night the rope walk caught fire.

He heard it before he saw it. A boy, running. Bare feet slapping up the Row from the river, and a voice cracking: “Fire! Fire at the walk! Fire!”

Dennis looked at him with True.

Nothing. No ring. Silence, the clean silence of a thing that was so; the boy was a fish-market boy called Pip with soot on his face and he believed every word and every word was right. And then Dennis smelled it, coming up the Row on the river air: tar. Hot tar and burning hemp. And over the roofs at the bottom of the hill, to the east, where the rope walk ran its long low shed a quarter of a mile along the waterfront, an orange light coming up into the dark like a sunrise in the wrong place.

The rope walk was tarred end to end. Every rope that ever came out of Kingsferry was laid in it, and dipped, and the shed was three hundred yards long and old and dry and soaked in a hundred years of pitch. And at the near end of it, on the bottom of Tanner’s Row, the net sheds. Where the rope-walk families lived. Where Ned Webb’s mother lived, when she wasn’t sleeping in a chair by his bench.

Dennis saw Calvin come out of the infirmary door at a run and go down the Row past him, past the lamp post, and the wind came with him. Not the river wind; his. Out of nowhere, the wrong way for anybody but Calvin, pushing at his back. And at the bottom of the Row, coming up off the waterfront to meet him, a big shape going the other way at a jog with its arms pumping.

Gabe. Off the steps.

Dennis watched him go. He’d known Gabe a long time. He’d watched him stand on those steps every night for five weeks with his back to the infirmary, and he’d never once seen him leave them before the first grey. He saw him leave them now, and turn east along the waterfront toward the orange light, and not look back; because there were families in the net sheds, and Gabe had never in his life been able to stand still while a house burned.

Dennis looked at the steps.

Empty. Pale stone at the bottom of the hill in the moonlight. The moon was most of the way to full and high over the river, and the customs house stood up white against it with its columns and its big shut doors, and the steps went down from the doors to the quay, wide and shallow, twelve of them, the way they’d gone down for a hundred years. Nobody on them. Nothing.

He looked at them with True.

Nothing. Silence. Just steps.

Maud came out of the infirmary gate at half past one.

He saw her come, and Tamsin behind her with the big leather bag and a lantern, and he straightened up inside the lamp post. She was on her stick. She was going fast for Maud, which wasn’t fast. She came down the Row on the cobbles with her shawl over her head and her face set, the face she wore for the dying, and when she got to the lamp post she stopped.

She looked at it. Right at it. Right at him.

“I know you’re in there,” said Maud. “Don’t be an idiot.”

He let the post go. It went out of the air like breath off a window, and he was standing on the corner in his own coat with his arms folded, feeling foolish.

“Sister. You can’t—“

“There’s a rope walk on fire and forty families in the net sheds and every one of them’ll have burns by morning,” said Maud. “I can. It’s my ward’s work.” She looked up the Row, at the infirmary gate, at the window. “The girl’s asleep. Don’t wake her. She did twenty-three today and she’s cold to the bone and she’s no use to anybody till she’s slept. That Sabine’s on the door. The singer’s at my table. Your Deston’s on the stairs.” She looked back at him. “You stay here. It’s her they want. Not an old woman with a bag.”

He looked at her with True.

He did it without thinking. He did it to everybody now; he’d done it to Maud a hundred times. And every word she said was so. The fire was real. The families were real. It was her ward’s work. It was Rachael they wanted.

Nothing rang.

“Go on, then,” said Dennis. “Take Tamsin. Stay by the light.”

“I’ve stayed by the light for forty years,” said Maud, and went down the Row toward the orange sky, with Tamsin and the lantern behind her, and he watched them go till the corner of the Three Coopers took them, and put the lamp post back up round himself, and leaned on the wall.

He’d remember that. Afterwards. That he’d looked at her and nothing had rung and he’d let her go.

Everything she’d said was true. That was the trouble with True. It only told you if a thing was so. It didn’t tell you what was waiting at the bottom of the hill for a woman who was telling the truth.

The fire was bad. He could see it from the corner. The east end of the rope walk was gone, a long roaring trough of orange with the roof falling into it in sheets, and the smoke going up black into the moon and leaning, leaning west, toward the net sheds and the bottom of the Row; and then not leaning. Straightening. Going up. And then leaning east, away, out over the river, against the river wind, as if somebody very stubborn had put his shoulder to it.

Calvin.

Dennis could hear people. Shouting, a long way down. Buckets. A woman screaming a name over and over. The bell of the river-gate watch going. And under it, once, a sound he knew, low and long and ringing: a big man with aura in him taking something heavy on his chest, Wall, a beam or a door or a falling wall, and ringing like a bell, and not breaking. Gabe.

At ten to two, Tamsin came back up the Row on his own.

He was running. He’d lost the lantern. He came round the corner of the Three Coopers with his face white in the moonlight and the leather bag banging on his hip, and ran straight into the lamp post, through it, because he didn’t know it was there, and Dennis caught him.

“We were at the net sheds,” said Tamsin. “At the end. In the smoke. Doing the burns. Silence. Every word.

He didn’t answer. He’d let go of Tamsin. He was standing on the corner in his own coat with the lamp post gone again, looking down the hill.

At the steps.

They were empty.

Pale stone in the moonlight. The white columns of the customs house. The big shut doors. Twelve wide shallow steps going down to the quay, and nobody on them, and nothing on them, and the quay empty, and the river going past grey.

He looked at them with True.

And they rang.

He’d never heard anything like it. Not in his life. Not on Earth, where it had only ever been an itch; not here, not once, not with every glamour he’d ever cast going cracked in his ears since the Lists. This wasn’t a crack. This was a bell the size of the customs house dropped on its own steps. A lie so big and so loud and so close that he put both hands over his ears, in the street, as if that would help, and it didn’t, because it wasn’t coming in through his ears.

The steps weren’t empty.

The steps were saying they were empty. Somebody was telling the whole of Kingsferry, as loud as anything had ever been told, that there was nothing on those steps at all.

“Dennis,” said Deston.

He was in the infirmary gate. Dennis hadn’t heard him come. He had his hand on the gatepost, and he was looking down the hill, and his face was doing the thing it had done on the jetty at the lake when there wasn’t a thread where there should be.

“There’s nothing on the steps,” said Deston. “Nothing. Not a thread. Not the hole. Not anything.” He swallowed. “There was a hole there in the spring. There’s not even a hole now. It’s like the steps aren’t there.”

“They’re lying,” said Dennis.

Up at the end of the long ward, a window banged open.

Rachael was in it. In her nightshirt, with Gabe’s coat over it, and her hair down, and both hands on the sill, and her face white and set and absolutely still, the way it went when she’d seen something she couldn’t mend.

She was looking at the steps.

“There’s a ring,” she said.

She didn’t shout it. She said it in her bones-of-the-hand voice, the exact one, down into the Row, to Deston at the gate and Dennis on the corner, and they heard every word.

“On the steps. On the landing at the top, by the doors. A ring. Drawn. With lines going in, like spokes, and little fires at the end of every line. It’s lit.” Her knuckles were white on the sill. “There are people standing on it. Six. And something tall behind. And somebody sitting in the middle.”

“Rae—“

“It’s Maud,” said Rachael.

Dennis looked down the hill. Empty steps. Moonlight. Nothing.

“It’s for me,” said Rachael. Very flat. “They’ve lit it so I can see it from this window. Only from this window. They did it on the rise. The fires round it so he’d see from the wall.” She didn’t move. “Nobody else can see it. Can they. Nobody on the quay.”

“No,” said Dennis.

He knew what it was. That was the thing.

He’d have known it anywhere, if it hadn’t been so big. It was his. It was the thing he did. A thing that wasn’t there, put over a thing that was, so that people saw what you wanted. A glamour. Not a man’s glamour; nothing a man could make, the size of the customs house steps, ringing like that. But the same kind. A wall where the road is. Empty steps where a ring is.

And he’d spent two years learning the difference between what rang and what didn’t.

Lies ring cracked, he’d told Sabine, the night he got True, in the fog on the river with his hand over his hand. True things don’t ring at all. That’s how I know them. Silence.

He’d never once in two years thought about what that meant you could do. He’d only ever used it to hear.

He put his hands down from his ears.

He looked at the steps. At the lie. And he did what he did every night, on the corner, with the lamp post: he made a glamour. But not a lamp post. Not a wall where the road was. He made a glamour of what was there. Of the thing the lie was lying about. He didn’t know what it looked like; he couldn’t see it. But Rachael could. And she was in the window above him, with a thread to him that Deston said was gold, and she’d said it in her exact voice, every word of it, and every word had been silence in his ears.

A ring. Lines going in like spokes. Little fires at the end of every line. Six standing on it. Something tall behind. Maud in the middle.

He painted it.

Over the lie. On the steps. The way you’d paint a true picture over a false one; the way you’d hold up a lantern in a dark room and say there. Not a thing that wasn’t there over a thing that was. A thing that was there, over a thing that said it wasn’t.

It didn’t ring.

He’d never made a glamour in his life that didn’t ring. Not since the Lists. And this one went out of him and down the hill and on to the customs-house steps, and lay over the great loud bell of the lie like a hand laid flat on it; and the bell stopped.

The steps lit up.

He heard the quay see it.

The fish market. The river-gate watch. The bargemen and the dockers and the women with buckets, coming back along the waterfront from the fire in the smoke with their faces black; forty, fifty people, coming round the corner of the customs house on to the quay. And stopping. All at once. The way a crowd stops when a horse goes down in front of it.

On the top of the customs-house steps, on the broad landing before the shut white doors, there was a ring.

Drawn on the stone. In something dark, that wasn’t chalk and wasn’t paint; it took the moonlight and gave none of it back. A ring, ten paces across, and inside it lines, going in from the edge to the middle, straight, like the spokes of a wheel, with small marks along them like the marks on a ruler. And at the outer end of every line, on the ring, a little fire. Not orange. A pale, flat, sickly light, like a candle seen through dirty glass. Twelve of them.

Six people stood on the ring, evenly spaced, facing in.

River people, by their clothes. A bargeman’s blue jersey. A ferrywoman’s shawl. A boy of fifteen or so in a fisher’s smock. A big woman with her sleeves rolled up. Two men in plain brown. Standing on the ring with their hands at their sides and their faces patient and a little puzzled, like people who’ve walked out to the end of the lane and can’t remember why.

Behind them, at the head of the ring, between it and the doors, something tall.

He’d never seen a demon. Not up close. He’d seen the hunters in John’s pictures from Thornwall, and the scouts, and he’d heard the 35 talk about the rest, in the channel, the way you’d talk about weather in another country. This wasn’t any of those. It was tall, taller than Gabe, and thin, and it stood like a man, and it wore a long dark robe that went to the stone like a priest’s; and its hands were long and grey and very still, and they were on Maud’s head.

Maud sat in the middle of the ring.

On the stone. At the hub, where all the lines came together. With her stick across her lap and her shawl round her shoulders, upright, the way she sat at her table at the end of the long ward, with her hands folded on the stick. Her eyes were open. She was looking down the steps. Down the hill. Up Tanner’s Row.

At the window.

And the long grey hands on her head were pouring.

He didn’t know how else to say it, afterwards. There was nothing in them. No jug, no cup. But something was coming out of them, out of the palms, slow, the way cold comes off ice: a dark that wasn’t a colour, that you couldn’t look at straight, running down over the backs of the fingers like water off a ledge, and down into Maud. Into her hair. Into her face. And where it went in, she went grey. Not the grey of an old woman’s face in moonlight. The grey of the hook. Of Ned’s arm. A line of it, coming down from her hairline on the left side, round her eye, round her cheek, round and down, like a seam.

On the bottom step, at the edge of the quay, with a little wooden box at his feet, a man was sitting.

A good coat. No hat on his head. The hat in his hands. Turning it round, slowly, by the brim.

Smiling.

The river-gate watch went first.

Of course they did. They were the watch; it was their quay; and Sergeant Coyle had been on the river gate for twenty years and had seen worse things than a woman in a ring, he said once, at the Three Coopers, though he never said what. He saw it, and he didn’t stop to look at it. He shouted, and seven of the watch came up out of the smoke behind him with their pikes, and they went at the steps at a run.

Dennis was running too. He didn’t remember starting. He was halfway down Tanner’s Row with Deston beside him and Emrys behind, singing something under his breath, and Sabine, out of nowhere, passing him on the outside like a horse passing a cart, with her sword already out.

He saw the watch hit the steps.

The six didn’t turn round. That was the thing he’d remember. They stood on the ring facing in, all six, looking at Maud, and seven men with pikes came up the steps at their backs, and the six didn’t look at them at all until the pikes were there. And then the big woman with her sleeves rolled up put her hand back without turning her head, and caught the first pike behind the head.

Bare-handed. On the steel. Dennis saw her fingers close on it.

She pulled.

Watchman Benno went past her up the steps on the end of his own pike as if he’d been thrown off a cart, and over the edge of the landing, the side where it dropped to the quay, and down. Twelve feet. On to the stone. Dennis heard it from the bottom of the Row.

He didn’t get up.

The others came on. Coyle put his pike into the bargeman’s shoulder from behind, hard, all his weight, a good thrust, the steel going in to the socket. The bargeman didn’t notice. He turned round, slowly, with a pike in his shoulder and the shaft sticking out of him and his face patient and a little puzzled, and put both hands flat on Coyle’s chest, and pushed.

Coyle went down the steps backwards. All twelve of them. Dennis heard his head on every one.

And then the six turned round.

They came down the steps at the watch the way Hyvred said the turned came at Thornwall: smiling. With their hands up and open and flat. Not fast. Not hitting. Pushing. And every man they pushed went over. The boy in the fisher’s smock pushed a watchman off the side of the quay into the river and stood and watched him go under. The ferrywoman put her hands on two of them at once and walked them backward into the crowd. The big woman took another pike off a man and broke it across her knee and dropped the pieces and kept coming.

Dennis was on the quay.

He didn’t know what to do. He had never in his life known what to do in a fight; he had glamours, and True, and a ledger, and a mouth. He stood at the bottom of the steps with Deston at his shoulder and watched the turned push the river-gate watch off the customs house like a woman sweeping a step, and the crowd screaming and going back along the quay, and a child, a fish-market girl of six or seven, standing on her own at the edge of it with her thumb in her mouth, right in the way.

“Get back,” said Dob. “Get back, lad. I know your mam.”

Dob went off the edge of the quay into the river with the boathook still in his hands, and the river was running fast and black with the tide on the turn, and he went under and came up once, ten yards out, and went under.

Dennis picked the girl up. He didn’t know how. He picked her up and ran back along the quay with her and put her down in the doorway of the fish market and said stay there in a voice he’d never heard himself use, and she stayed, and he turned round.

And put six Rachaels on the quay.

It was the only thing he could think of.

He did it without deciding. Six of them, in nightshirts and an enormous coat, with their hair down and their faces white, standing on the quay at the bottom of the steps in a row with their hands at their sides. Exactly her. Every one. He knew her better than he knew his own face. He made them so well that he heard them ring in his ears, six cracked bells, every one a lie.

The turned stopped.

All six of them. On the steps, among the watch, with their hands up. They stopped pushing and turned their heads, all together, the way the forty at Thornwall turned their heads to the town; and looked at the six Rachaels on the quay.

“Good,” said Dennis, through his teeth. “Yes. That’s it. Over here. Come and get her. Come on—“

Sabine had been a caravan-guard captain for fifteen years and the crown’s sword for four and she was Reaching, and she didn’t make speeches about it; she just cut. It went in. He heard it go in.

Not like a sword going into a man. Like a sword going into a sack of wet sand. A thick dull sound, and the robe opening, and under it grey; and nothing coming out.

The priest didn’t fall.

It took one hand off Maud’s head.

That was all. One long grey hand came off her hair, slowly, the pour from it stopping like a tap turned off; and it turned, at the waist, the way nothing with a spine turns, and looked at Sabine.

Dennis saw its face then. He wished he hadn’t. It had one. That was the worst of it. It wasn’t a hunter’s face, all jaw and nothing. It was a face like a man’s, long and grey and calm, a priest’s face, the face of somebody who’d stood at a great many deathbeds and been patient at all of them. And its eyes weren’t there. Where its eyes should have been there were two places where the moonlight went in and didn’t come out.

It hit her with the back of its hand.

Sabine went across the landing like something thrown out of a window. Ten paces. Into the second column of the customs house, back first, with a sound like a door slammed, and the column cracked, a long split up the white stone; and she slid down it and sat at the bottom with her sword still in her hand.

And got up.

She got up. Dennis would never forget it. She put her free hand on the column and got up off the stone, slowly, with blood coming out of her mouth, and spat it, and set her feet, and lifted the sword.

“Again,” said Sabine.

She went in again. Not low this time. High. From three paces, with everything she had gone down into the stone first, so much of it that Dennis felt it through his boots on the quay, a long shudder in the stone of the steps, like a big bell rung under the ground. And the edge arrived before the sword, and opened the priest’s side from the ribs to the hip.

It made a sound.

The first sound it had made. A crack, like ice going on a pond. It took the other hand off Maud.

And for a heartbeat nothing was pouring.

Maud moved.

Dennis saw it. In the middle of the ring, on the hub of the lines, with the grey seam down the left side of her face from her hairline to her jaw, she moved her head. Not much. A hair. Down the steps. To the quay. To the six Rachaels in nightshirts standing in a row at the bottom.

And past them. Up the hill. To Tanner’s Row.

To the real one.

Rachael was in the Row. Halfway down it, on the cobbles, in her nightshirt and Gabe’s coat, barefoot, with Emrys’s hand on her sleeve and Deston, who’d gone back for her, on her other side. Coming down. Of course she was coming down. Rachael comes or nobody goes. They’d turned it round in the spring and it went both ways.

“Don’t you dare,” said Maud.

Her voice came down the steps very clear. An old woman’s voice, cracked, in the cold. The voice she used for patients who wanted to get up too soon.

“Don’t you dare, girl. Mender sits out. You heard her. You heard the song.” The grey was on her mouth now, at the left corner, and the words came out of the right side. “Go home. Go to bed. You did twenty-three today.”

Rachael stopped at the bottom of the Row. On the edge of the quay. Twenty paces from the steps.

Behind Maud, the priest put its hands back.

Both of them. On her head. Sabine was on her knees by the cracked column with her sword across her thighs and her ribs gone, Dennis could see it from the way she was breathing, short and high; she’d held it for two heartbeats and it had cost her that. And the long grey hands went back on Maud’s hair, and the dark began to come out of them again, slow, down over the backs of the fingers like water off a ledge.

“Emrys,” said Deston.

Emrys was singing.

Dennis heard the seventh word go out of him, low, the one he’d learned all summer burning his own name off his hand. This. And the white thread of fire came out of his finger and went across the quay and up the steps to the ring, to the nearest of the spokes, the line going in from the edge with the ruler marks along it; and followed it. The way it followed the grey. The way it followed a word.

The spoke burned. A hair of it. White fire running along a black line on the stone, toward the middle.

And Maud screamed.

The whole quay heard it. An old woman in the middle of a ring, with her back straight and her stick across her knees, screaming, because the fire running in along the spoke toward her wasn’t burning stone. It was burning the word. And the word went in to the middle, where she was. Where it was written in her.

Emrys stopped.

He stood on the quay with his finger out and his face gone grey in the moonlight, and the white thread went out.

“It’s in her,” he said. “The ring. It’s not round her. It’s in her. Every line goes into her.” His voice was shaking. “You can’t burn the word round her without burning the word in her. I’d burn her out with it.”

“Her thread’s going,” said Deston. Very quietly. “To Rae. It’s the thickest one she’s got. It’s thinning.” He was looking at nothing, at the air between the steps and the Row, the way he did. “She’s holding on to it. Hard. She’s holding on to Rae’s thread with everything she’s got left, and the pour’s pushing her out, and she’s holding on.”

The hat man stood up.

On the bottom step, with his little box at his feet, and his hat in his hands. He stood up and turned round, slowly, the way he’d turned round on the Narrows cliff in the spring and looked down at a girl on the stern rail of a barge. And he looked at the six Rachaels in their row on the quay.

And past them. At the real one.

He smiled.

Dennis felt his six glamours go. Not because he dropped them. Because the hat man looked at them and they didn’t matter any more; nobody was fooled, nobody had ever been fooled, the hat man had always known which one she was. They went out of the air one after another like lamps going out down a street.

“Mender,” said the hat man.

His voice was perfectly ordinary. A pleasant voice. A man asking the way.

Dennis looked at him with True.

Nothing. Not silence; not the silence of a true thing. Not a ring; not a lie. Nothing. The way the hook on the bottom step every morning had been nothing. Like putting your ear to a wall and hearing no house behind it. The most nothing thing Dennis had ever looked at, standing on the bottom step of the customs house in a good coat, turning his hat round by the brim.

Gabe let go of the bargeman.

He came across the quay and stood in front of Rachael. Not near her. In front. Between. With his black arm and his burned shirt and the blood on his face, and his back to her, and his hands at his sides.

“Stay there a minute,” said Gabe, to the hat man.

The hat man looked at him.

He’d looked at Gabe once before. At the Narrows. The first time in two years he’d looked at anybody but her. He looked at him now, a long time, smiling, with his head on one side. And he didn’t come off the bottom step.

He stayed there.

Rachael put her hand in Gabe’s coat pocket. Dennis saw her do it, past Gabe’s arm. She took out the needle case. The black horn one, forty years old, older than that, that Maud had pressed into her hands on the jetty and said come back.

She held it in both hands. She looked at the steps. At the ring. At the old woman in the middle of it with the grey coming down her face like a seam and her eyes on the Row.

“You told me to come back,” said Rachael.

She said it to Maud. Not loudly. In the bones-of-the-hand voice. And Maud heard it, up on the landing, twenty paces off, through everything; Dennis saw her hear it.

“I came back,” said Rachael.

“Girl—“

“If the mender’s in the ring,” said Rachael, “she can close up anything.”

It went very quiet on the quay. The crowd at the fish-market door. The watch, the ones who were still standing. The turned, on the steps, with their hands half up. Sabine on her knees by the column. Calvin, out on the river, low over the black water on his own wind, still looking for Dob.

“That’s why they want a healer,” said Rachael. “Not because we hold it. Because we’re the only ones who could close it. So you fill her first. You fill her where she can see.” She looked at the hat man. “And then there’s nobody.”

The hat man smiled at her.

“Except,” said Rachael, “there’s two of us.”

She looked at Deston. At Emrys. At Gabe’s back. At Dennis.

“Never alone,” she said. “You said. Not to the privy. Not to the end of the street.” She swallowed. “Not into a ring.”

Gabe turned round.

He looked at her for a long time. Dennis watched his face do the thing it did, wet and loud and too much, and then not do it. Go still. Go very still, the way it had gone on the Widow when he stopped being a wall.

“Okay,” said Gabe.

She went up the steps.

Not fast. Barefoot, on the cold stone, in her nightshirt and Gabe’s enormous coat with the sleeves down over her hands and the needle case in both of them. Gabe beside her on the left, near enough. Deston on her right, with his hand on her sleeve, and this time he didn’t take it away. Emrys behind, singing nothing. And Dennis.

The turned didn’t stop them. They stood on the steps with their hands half up and watched her go past, all five of them that were still standing, with their patient puzzled faces; and turned their heads to follow her, the way the forty at Thornwall turned their heads to follow a boy who ran; and didn’t push. Didn’t move. As if they’d been told what she was for and were waiting to see her get there.

The hat man stayed on the bottom step, where Gabe had told him to, and watched her go up. Smiling. Turning his hat.

At the top of the steps, on the landing, the ring was drawn on the stone in a dark that took the moonlight and gave none back. Twelve pale fires on the edge of it. Lines going in to the middle like spokes. And in the middle, on the hub, an old woman on the stone with her stick across her knees and her back straight and the grey down the whole of her left side now, from her hairline to her collar, and her right eye on the girl coming up the steps.

Behind her, the tall thing in the priest’s robe, with its side open from the ribs to the hip and nothing coming out, and both long grey hands on her head. Pouring.

It looked at Rachael.

The two places where its eyes should have been looked at her, and the moonlight went into them, and didn’t come out.

Rachael stopped at the edge of the ring. Her bare toes were a hair from the dark line on the stone. She looked down at it for a moment, the way she looked at a wound before she touched it. Then up, at Maud.

“Can I?” she said.

Dennis looked at her with True. He couldn’t help it.

Silence. The cleanest silence he had ever heard in his life.

She stepped over the line.

And Dennis, who had never once in his life known what to do in a fight, who had glamours and a ledger and a mouth and nothing else, who’d been a lamp post for thirty-nine nights on a corner where a lamp post ought to be, stepped over it after her.
