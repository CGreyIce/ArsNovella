The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 3: The Anniversary

Chapter 86

Midwinter

3,308 words · 14 min read

Maren had been given one order that mattered, and she had kept it.

There had been other orders. Hundreds. Teach them to sense mana. Teach them the script, slowly. Keep them fed and housed and warm, and keep them out of the taverns, and keep the silver-haired one and the one with the pink in her hair from killing each other in the first month. Report to the Advisor every new moon, in full. She’d kept all of those too. She was good at orders. She’d been one of the Advisor’s seven since she was twenty-three, the youngest he’d ever chosen, and she’d never once failed to carry out an order to the letter.

But only one of them mattered.

The night before they left the capital, sixteen months ago, the Advisor had called the seven guides into his study one at a time and given each of them a sheet of paper with a description on it. A man. His height, his build, his hair, the way he stood. A fishing rod. And under it, in the Advisor’s small careful hand, the order.

He is a fisherman, and nothing more. He is not to be disturbed. Tell them nothing.

Maren had asked where he lived. The Advisor had looked at her for a long moment and said, Near you.

She’d understood, a week later, on the road to the market in Wrenhaven, when a plain man in a plain coat had come walking toward them with a girl with fox ears, and her knees had gone to the road before she knew she’d moved, and Bram’s beside her.

A fisherman, she’d said, when the five of them asked. And nothing more.

She’d said nothing more for sixteen months.

It had been the hardest order she’d ever been given.

Not because they asked. They’d stopped asking, after the first few months. Not because she wanted to tell them; she didn’t, or she told herself she didn’t. Because she’d had to watch.

Watch them work it out. A piece at a time. Over a year. Althea at the market with a basket of eggs, coming back white-faced. Higu’s lists. The coats. The fish in the basket, two and then three and then four and then five. The books from the north. The lessons the fox girl carried up the hill. The windows, and the channel, and the night in the summer when all five of them sat up until dawn in the upper room with the door shut, and came down to breakfast the next morning with their faces changed, and never said why.

She’d known they’d worked it out. Of course she had. She reported to the Advisor every new moon, and she was very good at her job.

And she’d watched them, after that, do the thing she’d never expected. Wait.

Not ask. Not go down the lake road. Not tell anyone. Not even each other, out loud, in front of her. Just wait. Every night, at the upper window, with the lamp at the end of the lake road burning in the dark. For a year.

She’d been given an order, sixteen months ago, to tell them nothing. She’d thought it would be the hardest thing she’d ever do.

They’d made it easy. They’d never once made her choose.

She’d stood in the doorway of the upper room, some nights, and watched the five of them at the window, and had to go away down the stairs and stand in the yard in the cold for a while before she could trust her face.

Midwinter came on a clear night, very cold, with no moon.

The bonfire was in the market square, the way it always was. The way it had been last year, when Althea had stood in front of it and seen something in the fire that nobody else could see, and written one line, and a flame had come and sat on her fingertip. Here. Light. The first of all thirty-five, four months in. Maren had been standing three paces away.

She was standing in the same place this year. She’d chosen it without meaning to.

Rowan Hale gave a speech. It was longer than last year’s. Maren had been told it would be; Odette had warned her, with a look of resignation, at the well that morning. He spoke about the harvest, and the king’s visit, and the forge on the shore, and the stones, and the children who’d carried them, and the new well in the east quarter. He spoke about the summoned, the five girls at the militia hall, who’d carried stones down the lake road with everyone else and never asked for anything. He went red when he said that, and coughed, and looked at his notes.

Then he lit the fire.

It went up the way it had last year: a great roaring tower of flame in the middle of the square, higher than the church, and sparks going up into the black sky in their thousands, so many and so high that they looked like stars going home. The whole town in a ring round it, faces lit gold. The fiddler. Somebody’s dog. Children shrieking.

The five of them were at the front. Maren was behind them. She always was.

And then the square went quiet.

It went quiet from the lake side first.

Maren felt it before she understood it. A change in the noise of the crowd behind her, on the side where the lake road came up into the square past the church: the laughter stopping, and the talk, one voice at a time, the way a field goes quiet when a hawk’s shadow goes over it. And then the quiet spreading. Round the ring of the crowd, toward her, toward the fire, person by person, until the only sounds left in the whole of the square were the roar of the bonfire and the crackle of the sparks.

She turned her head.

There was a man standing at the edge of the firelight.

Where the lake road came into the square, by the corner of the church, at the very edge of where the light reached. A plain man in a plain coat. Snow on his shoulders. No rod. Just his hands, at his sides. And beside him, a little in front of him, the way a girl stands in front of someone she’s decided to protect, the fox girl, with her ears very straight and a black sword over her shoulder.

He’d never come to the bonfire. Not once in eight years, Rowan said. Not once since before the war. He watched it every year from the end of his jetty, across the snow, and went to bed.

He was here.

The town didn’t do anything. That was what Maren would remember, afterwards. Two hundred people, three hundred, in a ring round a fire, and not one of them said a word, or pointed, or moved toward him. They just made room. Quietly. The people nearest him stepped back, a pace, two, the way you’d step back to let somebody through a doorway, and turned back to the fire, and went on watching it, as if a man standing at the edge of the light on midwinter night were the most ordinary thing in the world.

They’d learned that from Rowan. Eight years of it. Nobody follows us down. They’d learned not to look.

They looked anyway. Maren saw them do it. Out of the corners of their eyes. All of them.

The five of them had seen him.

Maren watched it happen. She was behind them; she saw the backs of their heads turn, one after another, toward the corner of the church. Xola first, because Xola always saw people first. Then Gigichi, and her shoulders went up. Then PenguChi, very slowly. Then Higu, who’d already known, Maren thought; Higu always already knew.

Althea last.

She was standing nearest the fire. She’d been looking into it, the way she had last year, the way she’d looked into it every time they’d passed a lit hearth for a year, as if she were reading something written in the flames. She turned her head.

And saw him.

He was looking at her. At all of them. One after another. The way he’d looked at them on the road to the market, sixteen months ago, the first day, when Maren had gone to her knees. As if he were reading something written on their faces and wanted to get every word.

He didn’t come any closer. He didn’t say anything.

He just stood there, at the edge of the light, and looked at them.

Althea turned back to the fire.

Maren didn’t understand, at first. She thought the girl had looked away because she couldn’t bear to look at him any longer, the way you’d look away from the sun. And then she saw Althea’s hands.

They were up. In front of her. Open, palms toward the fire, the way they’d been last year. Shaking. And her lips were moving.

Maren was three paces away. She’d been three paces away last year, too. She knew what she was seeing.

“Althea,” she said. Very quietly.

The girl didn’t hear her. Or she heard, and didn’t stop. She was looking into the fire, at the heart of it, where the flames were white, and her face had gone completely still, the way it went when she was Parsing something too big to hold all at once. And her lips were moving. Two words. Maren heard them, over the roar of the bonfire, because she was listening for them.

“Still here.”

The fire answered.

Not the way it had last year, with a small flame on a fingertip. Maren felt it go out of the girl, the way you feel a door open in a cold house: a thread of something, thin and bright and steady, going from Althea’s open hands into the heart of the bonfire. And the bonfire took it.

For one heartbeat, the whole tower of flame went white. Not yellow. Not orange. White-gold, the colour of a candle flame at its very centre, from the base to the top, three times the height of a man, so bright that the whole square went pale and every face in the ring round it was lit like noon.

And every candle in Wrenhaven answered.

Maren saw them. She’d see them for the rest of her life. In every window round the square, on every sill, in every lantern hanging from every porch, every candle and every lamp in the town, for one heartbeat, flared. All at once. All together. As if the bonfire had said something and every small flame in the town had said it back. Up the hill, in the militia hall, in the upper window, the single lamp they’d left burning. Down the lake road, in the windows of the houses, one after another.

And at the very end of the lake road, down by the water, in the window of a cabin with a jetty and a hen-yard: one more lamp, flaring with the rest.

Then the fire was a fire again, yellow and orange and roaring, and the candles were candles, and the square was dark and gold and full of sparks.

Nobody made a sound.

Althea lowered her hands.

She stood there in front of the bonfire for a long moment, not moving. Then she opened her window, the way she’d opened it last year, and looked at it. Maren saw her face. She saw it do the thing it had done last year, at this fire, in this square: go completely still, and then crumple.

Althea turned round. Not to the other four. To Maren.

She held the window out. Maren could see it; the guides could see windows if they were shown, the Advisor had taught them that. She looked at it, in the light of the fire.

Maren looked at it for a long time.

Candle. The second tier. A year to the night after the first. Sixteen months after the summoning. Not one of the thirty-five had reached it. Not one of them was close.

“The first,” said Maren. Her voice didn’t sound like her own. “Of all of you. Again.”

“Patch,” said Althea. She was half laughing and half crying, the way she had been last year. “My skill. It’s called Patch. I can change things now. Small things. In the windows. I can feel it.” She wiped her face with her sleeve. “I wrote still here. I don’t know why. I wasn’t even thinking about the fire. I was thinking—“

She stopped.

She turned her head and looked across the square at the corner of the church. At the edge of the firelight.

He was still there.

And then everything happened at once, the way it had last year: Xola with her arms round Althea’s neck, and Squiddles squashed between them, gone gold. PenguChi with both hands over her mouth. Gigichi saying of course you’re first, of course you’re first in a cracked voice, the way she’d said it last year, as a gift. Higu not saying anything, standing very close, with her hand on Althea’s back. Bram behind Maren, blowing his nose like a trumpet.

And the square, all at once, roaring.

Maren stood three paces back and let them have it. She always did.

But she watched the corner of the church.

He hadn’t moved. He was standing exactly where he’d been, at the edge of the light, with the fox girl in front of him. And the fox girl had turned round, and was looking up at him, and saying something Maren couldn’t hear. He wasn’t answering her. He was looking at Althea. At the five of them, round her, in front of the fire.

Maren had seen his face twice in her life. Once on the road to the market, sixteen months ago, for a heartbeat, before she went to her knees. Once a few weeks ago, at the forge, from the top of the shingle, with the town. She’d never once been able to hold on to it. Nobody could. It was the kind of face your eyes slid off.

She held on to it now.

He was crying. Not much. Just his eyes, wet in the firelight, and his face otherwise doing nothing at all. A plain man in a plain coat at the edge of a bonfire on midwinter night, watching five girls from another world hold each other and shout, with snow on his shoulders and his hands at his sides.

He didn’t wipe his eyes. He didn’t look away. He just stood there and let it happen, and watched.

Maren looked away first. It felt like the only decent thing to do.

He left before the fire burned down.

Maren saw him go. The fox girl took his hand, the way a child takes a parent’s hand, though she was nearly as tall as he was, and he let her, and they turned to go back down the lake road into the dark. The crowd made room again. Nobody followed.

But he had to pass the militia hall’s people to get to the lake road. That was the way the square was laid out: the church, and the corner, and the road going down past the place where Maren was standing, at the back of the five of them, three paces behind, the way she always stood.

He passed her.

He stopped.

Maren didn’t move. She didn’t breathe. She’d been given an order, sixteen months ago, and she’d kept it to the letter, and she’d never once in all that time been this close to him, near enough to touch. She didn’t go to her knees. She’d thought she would. She found she couldn’t move at all.

He looked at her.

Not the way he’d looked at the five of them. Differently. The way one person who’s been carrying something heavy for a long time looks at another person who’s been carrying something heavy, when they pass on a road.

“You’ve done well by them,” he said.

Maren had been one of the Advisor’s seven since she was twenty-three.

She’d been precise, and formal, and correct, and tight-lipped, every day of it. She’d kept every order. She’d taught five frightened girls from another world how to sense mana and how to stand and how to write the script, slowly. She’d kept them fed and housed and warm. She’d stood between them and every curious face in Wrenhaven for sixteen months, and said a fisherman, and nothing more, and watched them work out everything she couldn’t tell them, and wait.

She’d never once been told she’d done well.

The Advisor had never said it. He wasn’t that kind of man; he said thank you at the bottom of letters, and meant it, and left it at that. Her father hadn’t said it. Bram said things like good job, Maren every day, about nothing, which wasn’t the same.

She’d done it all for an order. And for them. She’d never once thought of doing it for him.

You’ve done well by them.

She opened her mouth. Nothing came out.

He didn’t wait for an answer. He nodded to her, once, very slightly, and went on, down the lake road, with the fox girl’s hand in his, into the dark and the snow.

Maren stood at the back of the crowd, three paces behind the five of them, with the bonfire roaring and the sparks going up into the black sky, and found that her composure, which she had kept intact through sixteen months of the hardest order she’d ever been given, had broken entirely.

She didn’t make a sound. She’d never been a woman who made a sound. She just stood there very straight, the way she always stood, with her hands folded in front of her and her face perfectly still, and the tears running down it, and didn’t wipe them away.

Bram saw. Of course Bram saw. He came and stood beside her, big and gruff and warm, smelling of woodsmoke and boot-grease, and didn’t say anything, which was the first time in sixteen months that Maren had ever known him not to say anything. He just stood there beside her.

After a while he put his big hand on her shoulder.

She let him.

Later, much later, when the fire had burned down to a great red heap of embers in the middle of the square and most of the town had gone to bed, Maren climbed the stairs to the upper room of the militia hall to tell the five of them to go to sleep.

She didn’t, in the end.

They were at the window. All five of them. The way they’d been every night for a year. Althea in the middle, with her window still open in front of her, the new word glowing on it, Candle, and the other four round her, close. Looking down the hill, over the snowbound roofs, at the lake.

At the lamp at the end of the lake road.

Maren stood in the doorway for a long time.

Then she went back down the stairs, very quietly, and out into the yard, and stood in the snow under the stars for a while, the way she’d done on so many nights before, until she could trust her face again.

She didn’t think it would be much longer now. The waiting. Hers or theirs.

She didn’t know how she knew that. She’d just seen his face, at the edge of the fire, when the candle in his own window flared with all the rest.
