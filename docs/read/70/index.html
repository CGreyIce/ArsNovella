The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 3: The Anniversary

Chapter 70

Scope

3,940 words · 17 min read

Thea checked her window the way people used to check their phones.

First thing in the morning, before her eyes were properly open. Last thing at night. A dozen times in between, without thinking about it, the way your hand used to go to your pocket. She’d done it for a year. She’d stopped noticing she did it.

She always looked at the same line first.

Not the tier. Not the progress bar, or the skills, or the channel, though she looked at all of those after. The first thing she looked at, every single time, was a line most of the thirty-five had forgotten was there. Down near the bottom, under everything, in small plain letters, the way a file keeps a note of where it came from:

Medium: one blade.

And beside it, the sealed value. The one she couldn’t open. The one that had rattled like a door in a wind the first time a plain man walked past her on the market road, and every time since. The one that had shown her, for a single heartbeat on the night of the bonfire, a shape like a letter from her own alphabet, and then sunk again.

She knew now what the blade was. They all did. She knew whose. She had a fair idea, she thought, what the sealed value would say, if it ever opened.

She looked at it anyway. Every morning. The way you look at a photo on your lock screen.

On the night after the stones, she couldn’t sleep.

None of them could. The five of them were in the upper room of the militia hall, the one with the window that looked down over the town to the lake, and nobody had even pretended to go to bed. Xola was on her cot with Squiddles in her lap, both of them faintly pink still. Gigichi was sitting on the floor with her back against the wall and her arms round her knees. PenguChi had Waddles asleep on her feet and was looking at her own hands, as if she could still feel the weight of the stone she’d carried. Higu was at the window with her notebook, not writing in it.

Thea was at the window too. Looking down at the lake.

You could see the forge from here. A small red glow under a roof of sailcloth, on the shingle beside the jetty, below the cabin with its one lamp. Two figures sitting beside it with their backs to the warmth. They’d been sitting there for hours.

Thank you, he’d said.

She’d carried a stone down the lake road in a line of two hundred people and walked off the end of the road onto the shingle and held it out, and he’d taken it from her, and looked at her. And for a moment, holding a lump of grey field stone between them, she’d felt the sealed value in her window go completely still. Not rattle. Still. The way a door goes still when the person on the other side of it leans against it.

Thank you. The same two words he’d said to every child on the road. And not the same at all.

She’d walked back up the shingle without looking back, because if she’d looked back she’d have said something, and rule six was rule six. And she’d carried it all the way up the hill, and it was still sitting in her chest now, at midnight, like a coal.

“They’re going to cut it,” said Higu.

Thea looked at her.

“The sword,” said Higu. She was still looking down at the forge. “That’s what it’s for. You don’t build a forge that long for horseshoes. The king brought hammers. Alea said half of it in the yard.” She turned a page in her notebook that had nothing written on it. “They’re going to cut it in half. Tomorrow, maybe. Or the day after.”

“I know,” said Thea.

And then she stopped. And stood very still at the window.

“Thea?” said Gigichi, from the floor.

Thea didn’t answer. She’d opened her window. She was scrolling, fast, past the channel, past everything, down to the place where she kept the channel itself: the little piece of the window’s own writing that she’d added, in the spring, the night of the full moon, with Higu’s rules at the top of it. She found the line she was looking for. She read it.

She’d written it herself. She knew exactly what it said. She read it anyway, twice, the way you read a line of code you wrote at three in the morning and suddenly, horribly, remember.

“Oh no,” said Thea.

“What?” said Gigichi. She was on her feet. “What’s wrong? Is it the channel? Is somebody—“

“Nobody’s hurt. Nothing’s wrong. Yet.” Thea turned round from the window with her window still open in front of her, and she could hear her own voice going high and fast, the way it did. “It’s the scope. The channel’s scope. How it decides whose windows it goes into.”

They all looked at her blankly. Except Higu, who’d gone still.

“When I built it,” said Thea, “in the spring. I had to tell it whose windows to go into. And I couldn’t just say the thirty-five, because it doesn’t know what that means. I had to find something every one of our windows had that nobody else’s did. Not Maren’s. Not Alea’s.” She held up her hands. “And there it was. Down at the bottom. Medium: one blade. All thirty-five of us have it. Maren doesn’t have a medium line at all. Alea doesn’t. Nobody born here does. It was perfect. It was the cleanest filter I could have asked for.”

“Okay,” said Gigichi slowly.

“So I told the channel,” said Thea. “Go into every window where the medium line says one blade.”

Silence.

“Says,” said Higu softly.

“Says.” Thea put her hands over her face. “I didn’t write every window that has a medium line. I wrote every window where the medium line says one blade. The value. Not the field. I matched on the value.” She took her hands away. “And they’re about to cut the blade in half.”

Xola sat up on her cot. “So if it stops being one blade…”

“Then maybe the line changes,” said Thea. “I don’t know. I don’t know if it will. It’s never changed. Not by a letter, in a year. But it’s about the sword, and the sword’s about to stop being one sword, and if the line changes, even one word of it, then the channel looks at our windows and says that’s not one blade, and lets go.” She swallowed. “All thirty-five of us. At once. Out of the channel. In the middle of the night, or the middle of a fight, or whenever they do it.”

Nobody said anything.

“And I don’t know if I could put it back,” said Thea. “I built it in a hurry in the spring with Higu holding the lamp. I’ve never touched the scope since. If it drops everyone, I’d have to build it again from nothing. That took me three weeks.” Her voice cracked. “Tiger’s patrols go up toward the pass every week. Rachael’s got hunters looking for her. Group 3 just got their ribs broken. And I’d have to tell them all to go back to letters.”

“Then change it,” said PenguChi.

She said it quietly, the way she said everything. Calm and slow and steady. Thea had heard it do things to rooms. It did something to this one now: the panic that had been climbing up Thea’s throat sat back down, a little, and waited.

“You know what’s wrong,” said PenguChi. “You know what it should say. You said it. Every window that has a medium line.” She looked up at Thea with her golden eyes, with Waddles asleep across her feet. “So change it to that. Tonight. Before they cut it.”

“I’ve never changed the scope with people in the channel.”

“Then you’ll be the first,” said PenguChi. “You usually are.”

Thea stared at her.

Then Gigichi got up off the floor, and went to the door, and came back with the big pot of tea from the kitchen downstairs and five cups, and put them down on the floor in the middle of the room with a thump, and sat down beside them cross-legged.

“You do the code,” said Gigichi. “I’ll do the yelling.”

It was what Thea had said to her in the spring, the night of the 2 a.m. fight, when the channel was a week old and nobody knew how it worked. You do the code. I’ll do the yelling. Gigichi had said it back to her then, the other way round, and they’d both laughed, a little, for the first time in months.

Thea laughed now. It came out wet.

“Higu,” she said. “How long have we got?”

Higu didn’t answer. She’d already opened her window.

Thea watched her do it, at the window, with the lamp behind her: one small line, in the window’s language, the one she’d written the first time on the roof under the full moon. Go and see. And from the palm of her hand, the way it had that night, a moth came up. Pale and soft and the size of a thumbnail, with wings like grey silk. It sat on Higu’s fingertip for a moment, opening and closing. Then it went out through the open window, into the dark, and down the hill, toward the lake.

Higu closed her eyes.

They waited. Thea could hear her own heart.

“It’s not in the fire,” said Higu at last. Very quietly, with her eyes still shut. “It’s on the stones. At the edge of the hearth. Wrapped up still. The canvas.” A pause. “The fire’s banked. Low. Red, not yellow.” Another pause, longer. “The king’s asleep. On the shingle, against the anvil stump, with a blanket. And a hen.” Her mouth twitched. “Ars is sitting up. On the jetty. He’s got his rod. He’s not fishing.”

“Is he looking at the moth?” said Gigichi.

Higu was quiet for a moment.

“No,” she said. “He’s looking at the lake.” She opened her eyes. “Not tonight. They’ll need the fire hot for the whole of a day first, I think. Garrow’s anvil is still wrapped in sacking. It’ll be tomorrow, at the earliest. Late tomorrow.” She looked at Thea. “You’ve got tonight.”

“Then I’ve got tonight,” said Thea, and sat down on the floor with her window open, and began.

It wasn’t hard. That was the stupid thing.

The change itself was one line. It was the same line, almost. Every window where the medium line says one blade became every window that has a medium line at all. A few words different. The kind of fix you’d make in thirty seconds on Earth, at a desk, with a cup of coffee, and push, and forget about.

It was everything around it that was hard.

Because she couldn’t stop the channel to make the change. Thirty-five people were in it. Thirty-four, really, and her. It was midnight here, but midnight here was midnight everywhere, and people were still up: Kei, writing up a poison he’d found; Malcolm, as usual; Mashi on his wall, reporting heartbeats. If she pulled the channel down to fix the scope, every one of them would lose it, and she’d be back to three weeks.

So she had to do it live. While it was running. Change the filter under the channel’s feet without the channel noticing it was standing on something new.

On Earth there’d have been tools for that. Ways to test. A copy of the whole thing she could break in private. Here, there was her window, and her Parse, and the window’s own writing scrolling past her eyes in its endless plain English, and five cups of tea going cold on the floor.

She worked.

She wrote the new line out first, in the margin, the way you’d write it on paper. Read it. Read it again. Gigichi read it over her shoulder and said it says the same thing as before and Thea said no, look, there, it says has instead of says and Gigichi said that’s the whole difference? and Thea said that’s the whole difference and Gigichi said I’m going to yell at it, then, and didn’t, but looked at it very fiercely.

Then she had to find every place the old line was used. There were four. She’d forgotten two of them. The second one she found by accident at half past one, buried in the part of the channel that counted how many windows it was talking to, and she went cold all over, because if she’d changed the other three and missed that one, the channel would have gone on counting thirty-five while talking to nobody.

At two, Xola fell asleep on her cot with Squiddles tucked under her chin.

At half past two, PenguChi started talking. Nothing important. About Waddles, mostly, and a dream she’d had, and the weight of the stone she’d carried, and how strange it had felt to hand it over. She talked slowly, in her calm low steady voice, and Thea’s hands, which had started to shake at two, stopped shaking.

At three, she was ready.

“Count,” said Thea.

Higu opened her window. So did Gigichi. So did PenguChi. They knew what she meant; they’d done this in the spring, the first night, sitting in this room: watching the channel, ready to tell her the second it went wrong.

Thea put her finger on the line.

On Earth you’d have pressed a key. Here you just decided, the way she’d decided on the night of the bonfire to write Here. Light. You wrote it in the window’s language, and meant it, and it was so.

She meant it.

Nothing happened.

Nothing at all. The channel went on scrolling. Mashi, on his wall at Cinderhold: two heartbeats. still no faint. Kei: Hisui says this mushroom is a liar. Malcolm: IF anyone is still awake, THEN go to sleep. I am. The window didn’t flicker. The lines didn’t stop. Nobody said hey, did anyone else just.

“Count,” said Thea again. Her voice came out a whisper.

She looked at the place where the channel counted the windows it was talking to.

35.

She let her breath out.

“Still here,” said Gigichi, from her window. “I can see Mashi. I can see everyone.”

“Still here,” said PenguChi.

Higu said nothing. She just reached over, and squeezed Thea’s shoulder, once, and let go.

Thea sat on the floor of the upper room of the militia hall at three in the morning and put her face in her hands and laughed until she cried, very quietly, so as not to wake Xola. And Gigichi poured her a cup of cold tea, and she drank all of it.

It was a long time before she could sleep. When she did, she dreamed of nothing at all.

She woke late, to Gigichi shaking her shoulder and pointing at the window.

It went on all day.

The fire first. You could see it from the hall: the smoke from the sailcloth roof going up grey and then white and then almost invisible, the way it did when the fire was very hot. Garrow at one bellows and Ars at the other, and the king between them at the mouth of the long hearth, with the sword in it. Thea could see it from the window if she squinted, a long dark line in the glow, the canvas off at last.

They didn’t train. Maren didn’t even try to make them. She stood at the window with them for most of the morning, and so did Bram, and neither of them said anything about it.

Then, in the afternoon, the hammering started.

It wasn’t like the hammering at Garrow’s forge in the square, which went clank, clank all day long, steady and dull, like a clock. This was different. A single blow. A long pause. Another. Thea couldn’t see what they were doing from the hall. Higu could; she sent the moth down, and sat with her eyes closed, and told them.

“The king’s got a chisel,” said Higu. “A big one. On the sword. On the anvil. He’s hitting it.” A blow, from the lake, faint and flat. “He’s been hitting it for an hour.” Another. “It isn’t going in.”

“What do you mean it isn’t going in?”

“I mean it isn’t marking it.” Higu’s face was very still. “He’s red-hot to the elbow. He’s the strongest man in the kingdom. And the chisel’s bouncing off.” A long pause. “He’s stopped. He’s looking at it.”

Nobody said anything.

“He’s saying something to Ars,” said Higu. “I can’t hear. The moth can’t hear.” A pause. “Ars is putting the tongs down.” Another. “He’s taken the chisel.”

They heard it at the hall.

Everybody did. The whole of Wrenhaven heard it, Thea found out afterwards: in the square, in the fields, in the mill by the river, on the boats out on the lake. It came up from the shingle by the jetty and went out over the water in every direction at once.

One note.

High and clear and long, like a bell struck once in an empty church. Not the dull flat sound of the chisel bouncing. Something else entirely. It went on and on, and rang off the hills on the far side of the lake and came back, fainter, and went on, until it seemed to Thea that it had got inside the walls of the hall and was still ringing there, in the timber, after it had stopped everywhere else.

Then silence.

Thea stood at the window with her hands flat on the sill. Behind her, nobody breathed.

“It’s in two,” said Higu, with her eyes shut. Very quietly. “On the anvil. Two pieces.” A pause. “Ars has put the chisel down. He’s walking out to the end of the jetty. He’s sitting down.” Another pause, longer. “The king’s not going after him. He’s just standing there. Holding the hammer.”

Thea opened her window.

Her hands did it on their own. She’d been waiting all night for this, and all day, ever since three in the morning, and her hands knew. She scrolled straight past everything, past the channel, down to the very bottom, to the line she looked at first every morning.

The channel was fine. It was scrolling. Thirty-five. Nobody had dropped. Nothing had flickered. Nothing had happened to anyone’s window at all, as far as she could see; no one in the channel had even noticed. Tiger was complaining about a goat.

But the line had changed.

Medium: one blade · parted.

She stared at it.

Three words. Two of them the same as before. One blade, still. It hadn’t stopped being one blade, then, not to the window. It was one blade that had been parted. As if the window had looked at what had just happened on the shingle by the lake and decided that it was still the same thing, really, just in two pieces now. As if it knew.

If she hadn’t changed the scope last night, every one of them would have been out of the channel right now. In the middle of an afternoon. With no idea why.

And the sealed value sat beside the new line, sealed, the way it had always sat. Not rattling. Not still. Just there. Waiting.

“Thea?” said Gigichi.

Thea turned the window round and showed her. Gigichi read it, and her mouth went tight, and she looked out of the window at the lake. PenguChi read it. Xola. Higu opened her eyes and read it last, and didn’t say anything at all, and wrote something in her notebook, very small.

“Nobody else is going to notice,” said Thea. “Are they. Nobody looks at that line.”

“You look at that line,” said Gigichi.

“I look at it because I’m…” Thea stopped. She didn’t know what she was.

“Because you’re you,” said Gigichi. “Somebody has to look at the line nobody looks at.” She looked back at the lake. “He’d have looked at it too.”

Somebody else did notice. Thea had half known they would.

It came in that evening, after supper, in the channel, on its own, in the middle of Tiger’s goat.

Thea read it and held her breath.

The channel was busy. Tiger’s goat had eaten Drew’s boot. Rachael was asking Kei something about burns. Maigo was shouting about Mashi’s third heartbeat, which had happened at dusk, and everyone was congratulating Mashi. Chris’s line went up the channel in the middle of it all, and most of them, Thea could see, scrolled straight past it.

But not everyone.

That was all. Three people. Out of thirty-five. The rest of the channel rolled on over them, goat and boots and burns and Mashi’s three heartbeats, and their lines went up and up and off the top of the channel and were gone, the way everything went, forty lines at a time.

Nobody asked what the blade was. Nobody needed to; they’d all read Cursiffe’s carving in the spring, and Group 3’s report a month ago. He took his sword back. They could do that sum.

What nobody wrote was where. Or who was holding the chisel. Or the whole town heard it. Those were Wrenhaven’s.

Group 1 didn’t write anything.

Thea sat at the window late that night, after the others had finally gone to sleep, and looked down at the lake.

The forge was still glowing. Lower now. Banked. And on the end of the jetty, a single figure, sitting with his feet over the water, the way she’d seen him from this window a hundred times. He hadn’t moved, Higu said, for an hour after the note. Then he’d got up, and gone back to the forge, and picked up the hammer. They’d heard the king’s hammer start again, at dusk, in a new rhythm. Not single blows. Ting, ting, ting. Steady as a heartbeat. Working the iron.

She looked at her window one last time before bed. The way she always did.

Medium: one blade · parted.

She thought about a man who’d carried that blade across the red alone the morning after the worst night of his life, and stood it over a grave, and scratched to the stars into it with the point of a knife. Who’d left it standing there for eight years. Who’d taken it back out of Salty’s hands, and carried it home, and today, in front of the whole town, had held a chisel to it while a king brought down a hammer.

Whoever’s medium it is, Thea thought, just put something down.

Not all of it. One blade, still. Just parted.

Rule six, she told herself. We wait for him.

But she sat at the window a long time, looking at the single figure on the jetty in the dark, before she could make herself go to bed.
