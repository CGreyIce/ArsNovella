The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 9: The Other Front

Chapter 242

Ten Over the Road

5,604 words · 24 min read

Wendy counted things. Everybody knew that.

She’d counted things on Earth. Spreadsheets, mostly: flights and prices and the hours between time zones, and a column called ELEVEN in a file she’d never deleted, with Ice’s notes beside each dish. She’d counted the days until the trip. She’d counted, very privately, the number of times Salty said Wen in a call before he said what he actually wanted to say, which was always four.

Here she counted everything. Four hundred and eighty-one markers between the east gate and the knoll. Thirty-nine steps on the east stair, and the twenty-third a different height. Two hundred and twelve arrows over the winter, and how many were in her quiver at any moment, without looking. The scouts on the night of the knoll: a hundred and sixty-one, and four big ones. She’d counted them at a quarter to eight in the evening and written 161. 4. in the channel, and then nobody on the knoll had had a hand free to write anything else until seven in the morning.

She counted people. That was the one nobody noticed, because they thought it was the same as the other kind. It wasn’t. She counted people the way you’d check your pockets. Group 3 at supper: five. Six with Aldous. Seven with Rhea if Rhea had come in from the wall. The old ones on the east wall when Ice came in under the gate: twenty-five. She counted them without deciding to, every time, and if the number was wrong she knew before she knew why.

On the morning they left, she counted ten.

She’d been trying to hold four all summer.

Not people. Arrows. Since the Lists. Since a big sandy ring in Valcrest in the middle of a Swiss, where she’d faced Salty in a bout that wasn’t supposed to mean anything and had shot three padded arrows at him and they’d bounced off, the way everything bounced off Salty; and instead of letting them drop she’d held them. All three. In the air. Above the sand, glowing faintly blue, the way she’d held one at a time since the second winter, and then two, and never three.

Three.

She’d been ninety-nine percent to Tempered ever since. She’d looked at her window that night in a borrowed room in Valcrest, and it had said ninety-nine, and she’d laughed, because that was exactly the kind of thing that would happen to her. Not a hundred. Ninety-nine. Skills: Aloft. And a gap under it where the next one would go.

So she’d been trying to hold four.

Every evening. On the east wall at Cinderhold, at the eleventh merlon, which had become, without anybody saying so, the place you went when you wanted to try something hard. She’d stand there with her bow and a quiver of practice arrows, blunt-headed, the ones she made for the recruits, and shoot them up, one after another, straight up, over the red; and hold them.

One. Easy. It hung there over the parapet in the evening light, blue, turning very slightly.

Two. Easy.

Three. She could do three now. Every time. Three blue lines hanging in the air over the red, in a little fan.

Four.

Four was where it went. She’d shoot the fourth up and feel it come into the hold, the soft drag in her chest where the others were, and for a heartbeat she’d have four; and then something would give. Not the fourth. The first. Or the second. One of the ones she’d already had. It would go, the hold on it, like a thread snapping, and the arrow would drop. And she’d catch it, or not catch it, and be back at three.

On a good night she held four for a count of ten.

Once, at the end of the summer, she held five.

She didn’t know how. It was the evening after Maigo’s trees, after the graft; she’d come up onto the wall with her bow still a bit shaky from watching Maigo lie on the knoll crying with his arms out, and she’d shot five up without thinking about it, one after another, and they’d stayed. Five blue lines over the red. She’d stood there with her mouth open, and Salty, beside her, had stopped breathing.

Five. For a count of three.

Then all of them went at once.

That was when the one hit the walkway.

It had been bound to happen. She’d known it would, every evening, the way you know a glass on the edge of a table is going to go eventually. She shot them straight up because straight up was the easiest to hold; and what goes straight up comes straight down. She’d caught most of them. She’d got quick at catching them. But five at once, all dropping, in the evening, with her hands shaking—

She caught three. One went over the parapet onto the red.

One came down point first into the walkway a hand from Salty’s boot.

Blunt. A practice arrow; it didn’t go in. It hit the stone and bounced, with a sharp little crack, and skittered away along the wall, and lay there.

A hand from his boot.

Salty looked down at it.

Then he looked at her, and she saw him decide not to say anything, and then decide that wasn’t fair either, and pick it up, and hand it to her.

“That’s all right,” said Salty.

“It’s not all right.”

“It’s blunt, Wen. It’s a recruit arrow. It’d have bounced off my foot. It did bounce.”

“It’d have bounced off your head if you’d been standing a pace over.” She took it from him. Her hands were shaking so hard she nearly dropped it again. “I’ve been shooting arrows straight up over people for a month. Over the wall. Over the walkway. Over you. I’m going to drop one on somebody. I’m going to drop one on a recruit’s head, or Niko’s, or—“ She stopped. “I’m going to drop one on someone.”

Salty didn’t say anything.

She stopped. For four days. She didn’t go up on the wall in the evening. She went to the butts behind the barracks and shot at straw, flat, the ordinary way, the way she’d shot before she found Aloft, and didn’t hold anything. It was very restful. She hated it.

“You count everything,” said Salty.

It was the fourth evening. They were in the forge, two evenings before the twelfth dinner, the night after the party came in, because Salty was practising, still, the way he’d practised every night since the winter, with the right peppers now, his own, from the plants by the forge wall. The oil was on the second anvil in a skillet the size of a cartwheel. He had a bit of bread on a fork, held over it, waiting for it to sizzle. Waiting for the oil to be exactly right.

“I know I count everything.”

“No, I mean.” He was watching the bread. “You count everything. You’ve always counted everything. On calls. You’d say that’s four when I said Wen the fourth time. You counted the scouts on the knoll and you were right, the commander’s men counted them after from the bodies and the tracks and you were right to the one.” He lowered the bread a hair. “You count without trying. You don’t hold your breath when you count.”

She looked at him.

“When you hold the arrows,” said Salty, “you hold your breath. I’ve watched you. All summer. You shoot one up and you breathe in and you hold it, like you’re carrying something full to the top across a room. And then you shoot the next one and you’re still holding it. And by four you’ve been holding your breath for a count of twenty and you go red and something lets go.” The bread hissed. He lifted it out, looked at it, and put it back in. “Not yet.” He looked at her. “You don’t do that when you count. When you count the markers you just walk and they go past and you know. You don’t hold anything.”

“The markers aren’t in the air.”

“Neither are the scouts. Neither are the old men on the wall. You don’t hold those up, you just—“ He stopped. He didn’t have the word. Salty very often didn’t have the word. He did the thing he did instead, which was put the fork down and show her with his hands: one hand flat, the other going past it, one finger at a time. “You just have them. They go past and you’ve got them. You don’t let go of them till you want to.”

Wendy didn’t say anything.

“Maybe it’s not the holding,” said Salty. “Maybe the holding’s the counting. Maybe you’ve just been counting arrows wrong.”

The bread sizzled. He took it out. It was gold all round and had little bubbles at the edges. He looked at it for a long time.

“There,” said Salty. “That’s it. That’s the temperature.” He looked very pleased, and then very worried. “I hope it’s like this tomorrow.”

“It will be,” said Wendy. Absently. She was thinking about counting.

She tried it the next morning, on the wall, early, before the walk out to the grave.

Not five. One. She shot one up over the red and held it there and didn’t hold her breath. She made herself breathe: in, out, the way she breathed walking out along the markers. And she counted it. Not counted it; she didn’t say one. She had it. The way she had the twenty-third step. It was there, over the parapet, blue, and she knew it was there, the way she knew how many arrows were in her quiver, without looking.

It stayed. Easily. As easily as one always had.

Two. Breathing. Having it. She looked at the red instead of the arrows, the way she’d look at the road instead of the markers, and let them be there at the edge of her eye.

Three.

Four.

She felt the drag in her chest where the fourth went into the hold, and she felt herself start to hold her breath, and she didn’t. She breathed out. Slowly. And looked at the red. And counted the old ones on the wall below her instead, without meaning to: eleven at the gate end, nine at the north end, five down in the yard by Oakes’s door. Twenty-five.

Four arrows over the parapet. Blue. Not shaking.

She didn’t try for five. She was too frightened. She held four, breathing, for a count of forty, and let them down one at a time into her hand like a woman taking pegs off a line, and stood there on the wall with her heart going.

Salty was at the top of the stair. She hadn’t heard him come up. Thirty-nine steps; he’d counted them in his head, she knew, because she’d told him how many there were so many times he couldn’t not.

“Four,” said Salty.

“For forty.”

“For forty.” He came along the wall. “Wen.”

“I know.”

“You breathed. I watched. You were breathing.”

“I counted the old men,” said Wendy. She heard how it sounded. “I had the arrows up and I didn’t count them. I counted the old men on the wall. And the arrows stayed up while I was counting the old men.” She looked at her hands. “I don’t know what that means.”

“It means you were counting,” said Salty.

She looked at him.

“Doesn’t matter what,” said Salty. “You were counting. You’ve got a thing in you that counts. And the arrows stay up while it’s going.” He shrugged, the big slow shrug. “I don’t know. I’m not an archer. I lift things.”

That day was the tenth, and the grave; she didn’t try anything. The night after was the twelfth dinner. She didn’t try anything that night either.

She sat at Oakes’s table in the forge with two dozen people round her and watched Salty stand at the end of the table with his arms folded, watching Ice eat noodles he’d made himself at a forge on the edge of the red; and she found she couldn’t count anybody. She tried. She looked down the table and tried to count and kept losing it at about eight because her eyes had gone strange.

If Wendy and me had come to Malacca, Salty had said. Like we said.

She had a tab open.

Not here. At home. On a laptop in a flat on a desk with the lid shut, the way she’d left it on the last night before the hall: a flight search, two passengers, Kuala Lumpur, and a date three weeks after the date Ice died. She’d opened it the week before he died. She’d been going to book it on payday. She’d never closed it. She’d kept the laptop lid shut for six months afterwards so she wouldn’t have to see it and couldn’t bring herself to close the tab, either, because closing it would be a thing, and she didn’t want it to be a thing.

It was still open. Three years and three months, here; a little over three months, there. She thought about it sometimes, in the night. The laptop on the desk with the lid shut. The tab under it, still open, with the date on it in the past now.

She watched Ice eat the noodles, sitting down, with the garlic burnt, the way it was always burnt at two in the morning; and she thought, quite clearly, as if somebody had said it in her ear: we came.

They had. Not to Malacca. Not on the date in the tab. Three years late, in another world, at the end of a red plain, at a scarred oak table in a forge. But they’d come, she and Salty, and he’d taken them to the eleven, all of them, in order, the way the list said; at a lake, in a hedge, in another summer. And at the end, at his, after the eleventh, when he’d have been hungry again, Salty had made this.

That was the trip.

She’d said it once before, on a road east last summer, to Alea. It’s the trip. She’d meant it then as a kind of sad joke. She didn’t mean it as a joke now.

In her head, in a flat in another world, on a desk, under a shut lid, she closed the tab.

Then she ate her noodles, which were mostly good.

They left at dawn.

By the west gate. The way they’d come in, four days ago, with the twenty-five on the wall over it and the commander at the end of the row with his hand on his chest. Ten of them, going home: back across the red and the grey grass and the downs, to Ashby, to the north road, to the lake. Twenty-two days, if he carried all the water. He’d carry all the water.

Wendy was on the west wall.

She hadn’t meant to be. She’d meant to be in the yard, by the gate, with the rest of Group 3, to say goodbye properly, the way people did; and she had been, for a while. She’d stood there while Salty hugged Gabe, which took a long time because both of them were the size of a door, and Maigo told Deston about the graft again, and Niko and Ice stood a little apart by the gatepost not saying anything, the way they did, and then Ice said something too quiet to hear and Niko laughed. She’d watched Yan say goodbye to Alea. He didn’t touch her. He looked at her, the way he looked at people now, with his pale half-shut eyes, and Alea looked back, and something passed between them that Wendy didn’t understand and Yan wasn’t going to explain; and then Alea put her fingertips together and tapped them, three times, and Yan did it back.

Then the gate had opened, and the ten had started out through it in the grey light, Alea at the front, and Wendy had found she couldn’t stand in the yard and watch them go through a gate. She had to see them go. All of them. All the way to the rise. She had to count them out.

So she’d gone up the west stair, which had forty-one steps and no odd one, and along the wall to the middle, over the gate, where the twenty-five were already standing with their hands on their chests. And stood among them. With her bow over her shoulder and her quiver on her hip, out of habit, full; she didn’t go anywhere without it any more.

And counted them out.

Alea first.

Out from under the gate onto the road, with her black sword on her back and her ears forward and her tail low, not looking back, setting the pace. The way she’d set it on every road for two years. The way she’d walked out of the east gate last autumn going home, and turned at the top of the first rise and waited.

One.

Wendy didn’t decide anything. That was what she kept coming back to, afterwards, in the channel and in the forge and in the nights. She didn’t reach for the quiver. She didn’t think arrow. She stood on the wall and counted the first of them out under the gate, the way she’d counted the riders out in the spring and the scouts on the knoll and the old men on the wall every time Ice came in. One. Alea.

And her hand went back to the quiver and drew, and nocked, and the bow came up, and she shot.

Straight up. Over the road. The arrow went up into the grey air over Alea’s head, blue at the tip, the way they always were when she held them, and stopped. And hung there. A pace above the fox girl walking out along the road, turning very slightly, going with her.

Alea didn’t look up. She didn’t know it was there.

Two. Gabe. With his big sledge over his shoulder in its sling of old sailcloth and his head down, walking like a man walking out of a forge into the cold.

Her hand went back to the quiver.

Three. Deston, beside him. Taller than anyone. Not looking at the road; listening to something.

Four. Xav. Chin down. Practice sword on his back.

Four arrows over the road. Going with them. She felt them in her chest, the soft drag where the hold was, four of them. And she felt herself start to hold her breath, the way she had all summer, and something in her said, in Salty’s voice, you don’t hold your breath when you count. And she breathed out.

Five. John. With a little gold light already up on the end of his finger, out of habit, because he couldn’t walk out of a gate without putting the motes up.

Six.

Rui Xiang wasn’t there.

She looked. She knew he was. She’d counted him in the yard; he’d been there, between John and Malcolm, talking very fast about something. And now he wasn’t. There was John, and then a gap of a pace and a half on the road, and then Malcolm. The gap was very slightly the wrong shape. Your eyes went over it and decided it was empty.

Unseen. Of course. He did it out of boredom. He did it when he was sad and didn’t want anybody to see. He’d been doing it since the yard.

Six, thought Wendy, at the gap, because she’d counted him, because she knew he was there, because she’d known where every one of them was for four days without looking. Six. Rui Xiang.

And shot.

The arrow went up over the empty-looking gap in the road and stopped, and hung there, blue, over nothing, a pace above the place where a person should have been.

And the gap stopped walking.

Rui Xiang came back into the world under it, all at once, the way he did; standing in the road between John and Malcolm with his face turned up, staring at a blue arrow hanging in the air a pace over his head. He looked at it for a long moment. Then he turned round, walking backwards, and looked up at the wall, straight at her, through the grey, from fifty paces; and his whole face split open into a grin.

“You found me,” Rui Xiang shouted. “Wendy. You found me.”

She didn’t answer. She couldn’t. She was counting.

Seven. Malcolm.

Eight. Azac. Big, slow, at the back of the four from Duskhollow, where he always walked, the way Ice used to walk at the back of a party, holding the end.

Nine. Petra. Very straight. With her sword on her hip and a length of grey driftwood under her arm. She’d made a wall in the yard the day before, for a recruit who dropped a practice blade on his own foot; a count of twenty; Wendy had counted it. She walked out under the gate now, and the arrow went up over her, and she looked up at it, once, and then back at the road, and nodded, as if somebody had told her something she already knew.

Nine arrows. Over the road. In a long line, going west, a pace above each of them, turning very slightly as they turned.

Wendy was breathing.

She didn’t know how. She had nine in the hold and she’d never had more than five and she should have been red in the face and shaking with something about to snap. She wasn’t. She was standing on the west wall with the twenty-five round her and the bow in her hand, and breathing in, and out, the way she breathed walking out along the markers. And counting.

And the nine weren’t arrows.

That was the thing. She understood it on the wall, at nine, all at once, the way you understand a sum when the numbers stop being numbers and start being apples. She’d been trying to hold arrows all summer. Four arrows. Five. Arrows were things you made; she’d made two hundred and twelve of them over the winter; they didn’t mean anything; they were sticks with feathers on. She’d been counting sticks.

She wasn’t counting sticks.

She was counting them. Alea, Gabe, Deston, Xav, John, Rui Xiang, Malcolm, Azac, Petra. The way she’d count Group 3 at supper. The way she’d counted the old men on the wall this morning, twenty-five, and the scouts on the knoll, a hundred and sixty-one, and the markers, four hundred and eighty-one. You didn’t hold people up. You had them. They went past and you’d got them. And you didn’t let go of them until you wanted to.

The arrows were just where she was putting the count.

Ten.

He came out last. Of course he did. He always walked at the back.

Out from under the west gate, onto the road, with the rod on one shoulder and the four water skins over the other, full, from Pim’s pump in the yard. Not looking back. Walking the way he always walked, as if there were no reason in the world to hurry or not to. A plain man in a brown coat, whose face she’d never once been able to hold, not on the red two summers ago and not at a lake and not at a scarred oak table last night with noodles in front of him.

She’d counted him every time he came in under a gate for three years. She’d never once counted him out.

Her hand went back to the quiver.

She shot.

The tenth arrow went up into the grey air over the road and stopped, a pace above the head of the man at the back, and hung there. Blue. Turning very slightly. Going with him.

He stopped walking.

He didn’t look up. He didn’t need to. He stood on the road a hundred paces out from the west gate with the skins on his shoulder and the rod, and the arrow over his head, and nine more going on ahead of him in a long line over nine people’s heads, all the way to the girl at the front; and he turned round. And looked back at the wall.

Wendy couldn’t see his face. She never could. She could see his shoulders.

They came down.

Then he lifted his hand. Not the rod. His hand. Flat. Toward the wall. And held it there.

The twenty-five round her put their hands on their chests, all of them, at once, as if he’d been looking at them, which perhaps he had.

Wendy didn’t do anything. She stood there with ten in the hold, breathing, counting.

Then he turned round, and walked on, and the arrow went with him.

She held them to the rise.

She didn’t know how long. She found out afterwards: Aldous had been in the yard below, with Yan, and Yan had counted it the way Yan counted things, slowly, by the candles; he said about seven hundred heartbeats, from the gate to the top of the long low rise west of the fort, where the road went over and out of sight onto the grey grass. Ten arrows over ten people, going away across the red in the dawn, a long line of small blue lights above a long line of small walking figures, all the way out.

She held them breathing. She held them the way you’d hold a list in your head. Alea, Gabe, Deston, Xav, John, Rui Xiang, Malcolm, Azac, Petra. And at the back, at the end of the line, a hundred paces behind the girl setting the pace, a man with the water. Ten.

At the top of the rise, Alea stopped.

She turned round. Wendy saw her do it, very small, on the skyline against the grey: the fox girl at the front, turning, looking back at the fort. And looking up. At the blue light hanging a pace over her own head, that she hadn’t known was there; and then along the line of them, all ten, over all of them, going back down the road to her father at the end.

Alea put her fingertips together.

Wendy couldn’t see it from the wall. She knew it. She knew it the way she knew the number of steps on the stair.

Then Alea turned round again and went over the rise, and the arrow over her went too, out of sight.

One, thought Wendy. Not counting up. Counting out.

Gabe went over. Deston. Xav. John. Rui Xiang, turning at the top to wave both arms over his head at the wall like a man on a beach. Malcolm. Azac. Petra.

And the man at the back, with the water, went up the last of the rise at the same unhurried pace he’d walked every road she’d ever seen him on; and at the top, without stopping, without turning round, he lifted the rod once off his shoulder, toward the fort, and put it back.

And went over.

Ten, thought Wendy.

And let them go.

She felt them go. All ten. Not falling; she’d been afraid of that the whole time, a long way down in the back of her head, that when she let go they’d all come down at once on the people under them. They didn’t come down. She let go of the count, the way you put down a list when you’ve finished reading it, and somewhere out over the grey grass, on the far side of the rise where she couldn’t see, ten small blue lights went out like candles, one after another, gently, in order.

She stood there on the west wall with her bow in her hand.

Her hands weren’t shaking. That was the first thing she noticed. They were perfectly still. She’d held ten for seven hundred heartbeats and she wasn’t red and she wasn’t dizzy and her nose wasn’t bleeding; she was standing on a wall in the early morning with the twenty-five round her and the sun just coming up behind her over Kharvos, and she felt the way she felt at the end of a long walk out along the markers. Tired in her legs. Clear in her head.

She opened her window.

She looked at it for a long time.

Count.

Of course it was. Of course it was Count. It would have been something else for anybody else. For her it was Count, because that was the thing she’d been doing her whole life without anybody noticing, including her; in spreadsheets, on calls, on the end of a red plain. She’d counted a hundred and sixty-one scouts on a knoll by firelight and been right to the one. She’d counted the days until a trip that never happened. She’d counted ten people out of a gate and held an arrow over each of them all the way to the top of a rise, and the arrows had stayed up because she hadn’t been holding arrows at all. She’d been holding them.

She laughed. Out loud, on the wall, so that two of the old ones looked round. It came out of her all at once, a short startled sound, not a nice one, and then her eyes went, and she put her free hand over them.

“Wen,” said Salty.

He was at the top of the west stair. Forty-one steps. No odd one. He hadn’t counted them; she could tell. He’d run up them.

“I’m all right,” said Wendy, through her hand.

“I saw.” He came along the wall. He stopped in front of her, short and square and enormous, with his face red from the stair. “Ten. All the way to the rise. I watched from the yard. Everybody watched. Pim dropped his bucket.” He looked at her. “Ten, Wen.”

“It’s Count,” said Wendy. “The skill. It’s called Count.”

Salty looked at her for a long moment.

Then he started laughing too. Helplessly, the way he’d laughed on the knoll in the spring with his arms round a black stone and seven scouts pulling at it; the big slow laugh that came up out of him like air out of a bellows. He put his hand over his own face.

“Of course it is,” said Salty. “Of course it’s Count.”

“Don’t.”

“Count.” He couldn’t stop. “Wen. You’ve been counting my Wens since we met. You counted the steps. You told me the steps every single day for two years—“

“There are thirty-nine—“

“I know there are thirty-nine,” said Salty, “I’ve never once been able to go up them without hearing you say thirty-nine,” and then neither of them could say anything for a while, and the twenty-five on the wall round them, who’d been on that wall ten years ago and seen a great many things, looked carefully at the red and pretended they hadn’t heard.

She read it on the wall, with Salty’s arm round her and the sun up behind them over the mountains of Kharvos.

She didn’t write anything else. She didn’t need to. She closed her window, and looked out west, over the red, at the rise where the road went over and out of sight, and counted the people on the road beyond it, the way she’d counted them every time they came in.

Ten. She couldn’t see them. She knew.

“Come on,” said Salty. “Breakfast. You’ve been holding ten people up since dawn. You need porridge.”

“I didn’t hold them up.”

“What did you do, then?”

Wendy thought about it.

“I counted them,” she said. “That’s all. They were going, and I counted them, and I didn’t stop until they’d gone.” She looked at her hands. “That’s all it is. That’s all it ever was.”

Salty didn’t say anything to that. He just looked at her, the way he looked at the oil when it was finally the right temperature.

They went down the west stair together. Forty-one steps. She didn’t say forty-one at the bottom, for once.

He said it for her.
