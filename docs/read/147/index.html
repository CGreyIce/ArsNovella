The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 5: The Lists

Chapter 147

The Basket

4,458 words · 19 min read

Higu kept a moon chart.

She’d kept it since the first week at Wrenhaven, on the back of a page Maren had given her, in charcoal and then in ink when she could get ink. Every night, the moon: its phase, where it rose, how bright it was, how much stronger it made her. Because it did. That was her magic. Light and moths and things that weren’t quite there, and all of it stronger when the moon was up and full and weaker when it was dark, the way a tide goes in and out. She’d worked that out in the first month and she’d been charting it ever since.

On Earth she’d been the strategist. In every group she was ever in. The one who looked at the whole board and said here, and then here, and then if they do this we do that. She’d had a man as a mod in her Twitch chat, once, for a while, who kept the room in order for her without ever being asked twice; she’d learned a lot from watching him do it. She’d written rules. Be kind. Everyone here is scared. That had been the first rule of the channel, the first night anyone in this world had ever written anything to anyone else in it. She’d written it because somebody had to, and because it was true.

She was sitting at the end of the long table in the Broken Lance on the sixty-ninth day, the last full day, with the moon chart in front of her and a pencil in her hand, working out the road north.

Twenty-two days, she wrote. Valcrest to Pellow’s Cross. The Sallow. Fennick. Caddow. The river road. Then: Moon waxing. Then, underneath, smaller: Moths can scout the road ahead on clear nights. Pairs on every watch. Nobody alone.

She looked at it.

Then she wrote, at the bottom, the thing she’d been not writing all morning:

He’s not coming.

She’d known since the riverbank. Everybody had.

Alea had come back through the west gate at noon two days ago with a pike over her shoulder and her face doing something none of them had ever seen it do, and her father behind her carrying her borrowed rod, and she’d walked straight into the common room of the Lance and put the pike on the long table in front of Lucan and said east.

That was all. East.

And Group 1, at the end of the long table, had looked at each other.

They’d known he walked east every year. Alea had told them in the first autumn: every year at the end of summer, alone, to the grave, a month there and a month back, and she stayed with Rowan and Odette in the lace room. They’d known it the way you know a thing about a friend’s family that isn’t yours to ask about. And this year was the ninth. And Group 3 was going back east to Cinderhold anyway. And he’d said to Yan in the yard, in a week we’ll go east, with all of you.

So they’d known he wasn’t coming north with them.

They hadn’t known Alea was going too.

East, Alea had said, with a pike on the table, and her ears up. And then, to Group 1, at the end of the long table, in a smaller voice: I’ve never been. I’m going to see her.

Xola had cried. Thea had gone very still. PenguChi had got up and gone round the table and put her arms round Alea from behind and her chin on Alea’s shoulder, and said nothing at all, the way PenguChi said nothing, which was more than most people said with words.

And Gigichi had looked at Alea for a long time, gruffly, with her gold watch closed in her fist, and said you okay?

And Alea had said yes. And for once, Higu thought, it had been the true answer.

So they were going north without him.

Without either of them. That was the part Higu kept turning over, at the end of the long table, with her moon chart. For two years Group 1 had been the ones who were near. Wrenhaven. The militia hall at the top of the lake road. Five girls, a guide and a guard, sent to a quiet town in the north by an Advisor who’d known exactly who lived at the bottom of the hill. They’d blocked his road on the first day. They’d waited at the top of it for twenty months. They’d gone down it in the snow for supper. They’d had a basket of fish at the door every morning for a year and a half, two fish, then five.

And now they were going back to the top of the road, and he wouldn’t be at the bottom of it.

Not for months. A month east. A month at Cinderhold, at the grave, for the anniversary. A month back. Late pumpkins, he’d said. That was the first frost, at the lake. Three months, maybe, before a man with a rod came down the lake road again past the militia hall.

Higu had worked it out on the moon chart. Three full moons.

She’d never once, in two years, been at Wrenhaven without him there.

Groups 4 and 6 were packing round her.

Not well. The way everyone in the Lance had been packing for two days, which was mostly by putting things on the long table and then standing round them talking. Calvin had his kettle on the table in front of him, dented now, from the Banner, and was explaining to Dennis why he couldn’t possibly carry it in a pack, it had to be on the outside, where it could see. Dennis had his ledger open beside the kettle and was going through it with Tiger, who’d come over from the Group 5 end of the table specifically to dispute a figure.

“Nine,” said Tiger.

“Seven,” said Dennis. “I’ve got it written down. Seven hugs. Gate, gate, Lance door, Lance door, the sand at midnight, the forge, and the closing.” He tapped the page. “Seven. I’ve been extremely rigorous.”

“You missed the one on the roof.”

“There was a hug on the roof?”

“There’s always a hug on the roof,” said Tiger, with tremendous dignity. “Nine.”

Malcolm was at the other end of the table with his notebook of contingencies, writing very fast. IF the apple-seller follows us north, THEN. He’d been writing since dawn. He’d filled four pages. Higu had read the first one over his shoulder and found it very reassuring and very alarming at the same time, which was how Malcolm’s pages always were.

RX was talking. John, with three little motes of light circling his head, was listening with his eyes shut. Azac was asleep on the bench. Xav was sitting with his hands on his knees doing nothing at all, which was what Xav did on the day before a road.

And Rachael was at the end of the Group 4 bench, beside Deston, with her arms folded and her face saying she’d had quite enough of everyone asking her if she was all right.

“I’m fine,” said Rachael, to nobody, about every quarter of an hour. “It was an apple.”

“It was a threat,” said Malcolm, without looking up.

“It was a bad apple,” said Rachael. “Winter said. Show apple. Polished. Tasted of nothing. If you’re going to threaten someone with fruit, at least use a good one.”

Gabe, beside her, made a noise that was very nearly a laugh, and shifted along the bench so that he was sitting between her and the door, which was the kind of thing Rachael let people do for her.

Higu went back to her chart.

Pairs on every watch, she’d written. She looked at it. Then she started writing the pairs. That was the strategist’s job: you looked at the whole board, and you put everyone where they should be. Twenty-two people on the road north, minus two. Minus the man who’d carried the water all the way south and taken the third watch from the fourth night. Minus the girl with the door on her back.

She wrote the watches. Group 1, Group 4, Group 6, Maren and Bram and Emrys and Sabine and Petra. Who with who. Who could see what. Thea and Calvin, because Calvin could feel the wind change and Thea could see a spell coming. PenguChi and Xav, because neither of them would ever fall asleep on a watch and both of them would be furious if the other did. Gigichi and Rachael, because Gigichi was the protector and Rachael needed protecting and would hate it, and Gigichi would never once let her know she was being protected, and they’d both like that. Higu and Deston on the full-moon nights, because the moths flew furthest then, and Deston could feel threads, and between the two of them they’d see anything coming a mile off.

She wrote third watch and stopped.

He’d had the third watch. From the fourth night on the road south. He’d asked for it. Give me something to carry, Maren. And Maren had written water, and then, on the fourth night, third watch, and he’d sat on a mounting stone in a posting-house yard at three in the morning with twenty people asleep round a fire in front of him, every night, all the way to the Last Look.

She didn’t know who’d take the third watch.

She wrote Higu beside it, in the end. Because she was the one who’d noticed it was empty.

The moths came in at noon.

She’d sent them out at dawn. Three of them, pale, glowing faintly even in daylight, the way her moths did: out from the window of Group 1’s room, over the roofs, across the city. She’d been sending them out every morning since the threadless left. Round the walls. Out along the four roads. Looking for anything that wasn’t there.

They came back through the window and settled on the moon chart, one on each side of the paper and one on her pencil, and she closed her eyes and let them tell her what they’d seen. It came in pieces, the way it always did, like a dream when you’re waking up: roads, and fields, and carts, and the shape of the Last Look on the north road, and nothing. Nothing that wasn’t there. No gaps. No shapes in the barley that the barley didn’t move for.

Empty roads, for a day out. All four.

She opened her eyes.

“Clear,” she said, to the table. “All four roads. As far as they’ll go.”

“Thank you, Higu,” said Malcolm, and wrote IF the moths say clear, THEN, and paused. “Then what?”

“Then they’re clear,” said Higu.

“That’s not a contingency.”

“It is,” said Higu, calmly. “It’s the one where nothing happens. Write it down. You never write that one.”

Malcolm looked at her for a long time. Then he wrote it down. Higu read it upside down.

IF nothing happens, THEN we go home.

Thea came and sat beside her at about the third hour.

She’d been up at the king’s house all morning, in the archive room, with Cursiffe and Oswin, rolling up Oswin’s copy of the circle very carefully for the road back to Solmere. She had blue ink on her fingers. She had the look she’d had since yesterday, since the archive, since a blank arc on a ten-foot sheet of linen paper: very quiet, very clear, like somebody who’d found the thing she’d been looking for and was now going to have to walk all the way to it.

“Eight tiers,” said Thea.

“I know.”

“I worked it out. On the walk down. If I climb the way I’ve been climbing. One tier a year, roughly, at first. Then slower. They get slower.” Thea looked at the moon chart. “Fifteen years. Maybe twenty. To Sun.” She was quiet. “If I’m the one. If anybody is.”

“Eleven to one,” said Higu.

Thea looked at her.

“Time,” said Higu. “Here and there. He said. Eleven years here for every one at home.” She tapped the chart. “Twenty years is less than two, there. Your mum’s still going to be making dinner.”

Thea didn’t say anything for a while.

“He told me that too,” she said. “Yesterday. In the archive. He said he’d been carrying it round since last year waiting for somebody to need it.”

“He carries a lot of things round,” said Higu, “waiting for somebody to need them.”

Thea laughed. Quietly. Then she leaned her head on Higu’s shoulder, the way she’d leaned it there on the first night in the militia hall at Wrenhaven, two years ago, when nobody knew anything and they were all frightened and Higu had just written be kind.

“I wrote TODO in it,” said Thea. “In the blank half. In Cursiffe’s ink.”

“I know. He told the channel.”

“Was that stupid?”

Higu thought about it. Properly. The way she thought about the moon.

“No,” she said. “It was a strategy.”

Alea came at the end of the afternoon.

She came in through the front door of the Lance, not the side, which was unusual; she always came in by the side, from the yard, the way her father did. She had Astra on her back and her red-gold hair tied up and something in her arms.

A basket.

Higu knew it before she’d seen it properly. Everybody at the Group 1 end of the long table did. They’d seen it every morning for a year and a half, on the step of the militia hall at Wrenhaven, or in Alea’s arms coming up the lake road in the frost. A fisherman’s basket, flat-bottomed, woven from willow, with a lid that tied, and a smell that never quite came out of it however many times you rinsed it in the lake. They’d thought it was at the lake. It must have come south on the dray.

Alea came down the length of the common room with it, past Groups 4 and 6 and their packing, past Calvin’s kettle and Dennis’s ledger and Malcolm’s contingencies, to the end of the long table where Group 1 was sitting. And put it down in front of Higu. On top of the moon chart.

“Dad says,” said Alea.

She stopped. Her ears were doing something.

She tried again.

“Dad says take them five fish,” said Alea.

Nobody at the end of the long table said anything.

Higu untied the lid.

Five fish. River fish, brown and silver, from the slow river outside the west gate, laid in wet grass in the bottom of the basket the way he always laid them, heads all the same way. Perch, two of them. A bream. Something long and silver that Higu didn’t know the name of. And at the end, a little pike, a jack, no longer than her forearm, with its mouth open and its teeth showing.

The basket stays five, he’d said. In the winter. After the supper. When Group 1 started coming down the lake road every evening and somebody had asked whether he’d go back to sending two. The basket stays five.

“He went out before it was light,” said Alea. “Under the willow. He didn’t wake me. I woke up and he wasn’t on the bench and I knew where he’d be.” She looked at the basket. “I sat with him. He caught all five before the sun came up. He didn’t say why. He just put them in the basket in the grass the way he always does and gave it to me and said take them five fish.”

“For the road,” said Xola. Her voice had gone.

“For the road,” said Alea. “He says you can eat them tonight. Or tomorrow, on the first night out. He says the pike’s for Gigichi, because she’s the only one of you who’ll eat pike.”

“It’s full of bones,” said Gigichi, gruffly.

“He says that’s why it’s for you,” said Alea. “Because you’ll complain about it the whole time and eat all of it.”

Gigichi opened her mouth. Then shut it. Then took the gold pocket watch out of her coat, and looked at it very hard, the way she looked at it when she didn’t want anyone looking at her face.

Higu looked at the basket for a long time.

She’d done the sum. On the moon chart. Three full moons until a man with a rod came down the lake road again. Three months of the militia hall at the top of the road and no basket on the step in the morning. They’d been getting fish for a year and a half. They’d stopped thinking about it, the way you stop thinking about the sun coming up. And now it was going to stop, and she’d been sitting at the end of the long table all morning with her moon chart, not writing the thing she’d been not writing, and here it was.

The last basket.

“Is there a note?” said PenguChi. Very calmly.

“No,” said Alea. “He said you’d know.”

Higu did know. She’d known for two years. Be kind. Everyone here is scared. She’d written it on the first night, and a man by a lake had read it, and for a year and a half he’d answered it every morning with fish, without ever once writing anything back.

She tied the lid.

“We’ll keep the basket,” said Higu.

Alea looked at her.

“We’ll take it north,” said Higu. “On the cart. And when he comes back, for the late pumpkins, it’ll be on the step of the militia hall. Empty.” She put her hand flat on the lid. “And then he’ll have to fill it.”

Alea’s ears went straight up.

“He’ll like that,” she said.

“I know,” said Higu. “That’s why I’m doing it.”

She found him in the yard at dusk.

She hadn’t gone looking. She’d gone out to send the evening moths, because the moon was up early, a fat waxing half over the east wall, and her moths flew better with it. She’d stood in the yard behind the Lance and let three of them go off her fingers into the blue air, pale and glowing, out over the wall toward the east road. And turned round, and he was sitting on the edge of the horse trough, with the rod across his knees, watching them go.

“Clear?” he said.

“Clear. All four. A day out.”

“Good.”

She sat down on the trough beside him. Not close. The way she’d sat on the jetty, in the winter, at the far end, with a notebook, copying down his window.

“I’ve still got it,” she said.

He knew what she meant. He always did.

“The copy,” said Higu. “Your window. All of it. Every skill, with the ages I guessed.” She’d written it in the winter, on the ninth evening, while he sat on the jetty with his window open for the first time in front of anyone and Thea asked questions and Gigichi said Maim at seventeen? and he said it was a difficult year. She’d copied the whole thing in a notebook by lamplight. “And two empty lines at the bottom. Nine and ten. For when you find out.”

“I remember.”

“Line nine’s still empty.”

“It is.”

“Sixty?”

“Sixty,” he said. “It moved at the closing.”

Higu nodded. She’d guessed. She’d been watching his window over his shoulder at the long table for a week, the way she watched the moon.

“It’ll fill,” she said.

“Probably.”

“I want to be there,” said Higu. “When it does. I want to write it in.” She looked at the moths, very small now, going away over the wall. “I’ve had the line empty for six months. I’ve looked at it every night. I want to be the one who writes it.”

He was quiet for a long time.

“You’ll be the first to know,” he said. “I’ll tell Winter to tell you.”

Higu laughed. She hadn’t meant to.

“The anniversary,” said Higu, after a while.

“Two moons.”

“I worked it out.” She took the moon chart out of her coat, folded small, and unfolded it on her knee in the dusk. “On the chart. I worked out the moon. For the night. Wherever you are.” She put her finger on a mark, a circle, drawn very carefully. “It’ll be full.”

He looked at the chart.

“The ninth anniversary of the Redmarch,” said Higu. “On the night. It’ll be a full moon. Rising in the east, at dusk, about an hour after sunset. Over the red.” She’d checked it four times. “It’ll be bright enough to see the whole plain. Bright enough to see a long way.”

He didn’t say anything.

“I thought she should know,” said Higu. “Alea. I thought somebody should tell her.” She folded the chart. “When she’s standing there. At the grave. With her mother’s half of the sword in the ground and her own on her back. And looking east at the rise.” She looked at him. “I thought somebody should tell her to look up.”

He was very still on the edge of the horse trough.

“You tell her,” he said eventually. Quietly. “Tonight. You’re the one who worked it out.”

“You’re her father.”

“I’m her father,” he agreed. “And you’re the one who worked out the moon.” He turned the rod in his hands. “It’ll mean more from you. From somebody who looked.”

She told Alea that night. At the end of the long table, with the fish cooking in the Lance’s kitchen because Lucan had insisted, and the moon coming in through the window, and Group 1 round her.

“Look up,” said Higu. “On the night. At the grave. About an hour after sunset. East.” She drew it on the back of the moon chart, a small circle over a line that was the horizon. “It’ll be full. Right over the red. You’ll be able to see everything.”

Alea looked at the drawing for a long time.

Then she took it. The whole moon chart. Folded it very carefully along Higu’s folds and put it inside her coat, against her chest, where Maren had put a letter for Rowan.

“I’ll look,” said Alea.

They ate the fish that night. All five. Lucan’s cook did them in butter with something green, and they sat at the end of the long table, Group 1 and Alea, and ate them with their fingers off the plates, the way they’d eaten fish at the cabin in the snow.

Gigichi ate the pike. All of it. She complained the entire time, about every bone, loudly, to everyone, and ate every last bit of it, and when it was gone she sat back and looked at the empty plate with the expression of a woman who’s been tricked into something and knows exactly who did it.

“Bones,” said Gigichi.

At the far end of the long table, a man with a rod across his knees, who’d been listening the whole time while pretending to talk to Salty about horseshoes, laughed out loud.

Higu opened her window, late, when the others had gone up.

She wrote in the channel. She didn’t usually. She’d written the rules, two years ago, on the first night, and she’d written very little since; she let other people do the talking and kept the room in order. But she’d been thinking all day, at the end of the long table, with the moon chart and the basket. About rules. About the first one. About what the king had said at his table, three nights ago, with goose fat on his fingers: when you need to be together, you come. You don’t ask. You decide.

She looked at the rules. Pinned at the top of the channel, where they’d been for two years. Six of them. Be kind. And the others. And rule five, which said nothing at all to anyone who didn’t know what it was for, and had kept a man’s name and a lake and a basket of fish out of the channel for a year and a half.

She added a seventh.

She looked at it.

Then, because rule five didn’t need to exist any more, and hadn’t since a north gate, she took it down. And the gap where it had been closed up, and the rules were six again, and the seventh was sixth now, and nobody but Higu would ever know there’d been one in between.

Except one person.

At the other end of the long table, in the dark, a man with a rod across his knees had his window open too. She saw it from the stairs. He was reading. He always read everything.

He didn’t write anything.

But the next morning, when Higu came down to the yard before it was light to send the moths, the fisherman’s basket was on the edge of the horse trough. She’d left it on the long table. Somebody had brought it out. Lid tied. Empty.

There was a strip of paper tied to the handle. Three words, in English, in a plain narrow hand she’d seen once before, on the first page of Oswin’s book, under a single letter.

Rule five. Thanks.

Higu stood in the yard of the Broken Lance in the grey before dawn with the basket in her arms and three moths on her shoulder, and read it four times.

He’d never written in the channel. Not once. Not in two years. Not when she wrote be kind, not when Thea wrote still here, not when thirty-four people had written whoever did this i love you at the top of a channel that had somehow grown to two hundred lines in a single night. He’d read every word and written nothing.

He’d written this. On a strip of paper. For a rule she’d taken down last night, that had kept his name out of a channel for a year and a half, because five girls had decided on their own that it wasn’t theirs to give.

She put the basket on the cart, very carefully, under the seat, where it would be safe all the way north, with the strip of paper still tied to the handle.

She’d give it back at the lake. In the frost. When he came down the road for the late pumpkins.

Empty. And then he’d have to fill it.
