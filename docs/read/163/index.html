The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 6: Nine

Chapter 163

The Rings Fill

6,133 words · 27 min read

Hyvred saw the first one come down the pass on the ninety-fifth day, and knew it at once by its coat.

Green. A good green coat, a grain factor’s coat, with brass buttons, buttoned to the neck. She’d seen it in Kingsferry, in Deston’s letters, two years ago. She’d seen it in the Street of Coopers in Valcrest, in John’s square of gold light, walking north under a lamp with no shadow at its feet. She saw it now, through the Scope, from the top of the north tower of Thornwall, at dusk, eleven miles off: a man in a green coat coming down the last switchback of the old paved road at the top of the pass, the road wide enough for armies that nobody had built, at an even pace, alone.

Into the valley.

She didn’t say anything. She stood on the tower with her hands on the cold stone and her left eye doing the thing it did now, the red crosshair in the gold locking on, the world going narrow and very sharp, the way it had gone on Earth through a hole the size of a coin. Only now the hole could reach eleven miles. Now the hole could see everything.

She watched him walk down into the valley.

Down past the shepherd’s hut with the glassy circle in its floor, burned. Down past the miller’s house with no smoke. Down into the town at the bottom, the one with no dogs and no lamps and no voices, where the lights in the windows weren’t lamps. And out the other side, into the middle of the valley floor. To the circle.

It was the size of a fort. Burned into the ground. Rings, with lines in them, the way Yan had said in a letter once: a ring with lines in it, like the one on the rise. And round it, standing, in the dark, people.

Rings of people. She’d counted them so many times. Eleven rings, last winter. Twelve, by the spring. Hundreds of them. Standing in the dark in rings round a burned circle with their faces turned up toward the pass. Not moving. Not blinking. Not breathing, Hyvred was almost sure, though she’d never been close enough to know. Men and women and children. Soldiers in Western Watch coats. A miller’s wife. A freckled boy of nineteen in the third ring, who’d been going to buy his dad a goat called Cabbage.

The man in the green coat walked to the outside of the outermost ring.

And stopped. And turned. And lifted his face toward the pass.

And stood there.

“One,” said Hyvred, out loud, to nobody.

Behind her, on the tower stair, Bee said, “One what?”

Hyvred didn’t take her eye off the valley. She couldn’t. It held you, the Scope, once it had locked on; you had to choose to let go of it, the way you’d choose to let go of a rope. She stood with her hands on the parapet and the wind off the mountains in her hair and felt Bee come up the last of the stairs behind her, the little buzz of Bee’s wings that didn’t work, the small warm weight of a Pollen Puff landing on her shoulder.

“The green coat,” said Hyvred. “From Kingsferry. From Valcrest.” She swallowed. “He’s in the rings.”

Bee was quiet for a moment.

“The thirty-nine,” said Bee.

“One of them.”

“Where are the rest?”

Hyvred looked. Up the pass. Down the road. Along the switchbacks in the dusk, the whole long grey road coming down from the top of the Greyspine into the valley, empty.

“Coming,” she said. “I think. One at a time.” She was quiet. “Like they left Valcrest. All at once, and then each on their own road.”

Bee didn’t say anything. She came and stood beside Hyvred at the parapet, very close, so their shoulders touched, the way they’d stood together on Earth in a hundred calls with nobody able to see them, and looked at the mountains with her own eyes, which couldn’t see eleven miles, and didn’t need to.

“I’ll get Corvin,” said Bee.

“Not yet,” said Hyvred. “Let me count.”

She counted for eleven days.

Every dusk, and every dawn, and in between when she could, from the north tower, with Bee or Drew or Kei on the stair behind her and Hild, sometimes, at the bottom, with her one eye shut, listening. Corvin wrote it down. He’d written everything down for two years, every sighting, every track, every count; two years of reports to a drawer in Solmere nobody else had a key to. He wrote this down too, standing at the top of the tower stair with his board and his ink, very pale, while Hyvred called them.

Ninety-five. Dusk. One. Green coat. Outer ring, north-west.

Ninety-six. Dawn. Two. A man in a hat.

The hat man. She knew him too. From Deston’s letters, from the first autumn, standing on the far quay at Kingsferry while a hunter came through a wall. From Valcrest, the opening, by the north gate. Smiling at Dennis across the water, as if it had learned something. He came down the pass at dawn in his hat, alone, and walked to the outer ring, and stood beside the green coat, and turned his face up.

Two.

Ninety-six. Dusk. Three. A woman in a shawl.

The woman from the fountain in Valcrest, whose reflection hadn’t been in the water.

And on. One at a time. Never two together. Never from the same direction twice: one down the main road, one down a sheep path on the east side, one over a ridge to the west where there wasn’t a path at all, walking down a scree slope at the same even pace as everyone else, as if the slope weren’t there. A boy in a cap. A carter. A girl with a basket of eggs. A soldier in a coat nobody at Thornwall recognised, from some company in the south. Each of them alone, each of them walking down into the valley and through the town with no dogs and out to the edge of the burned circle, and stopping, and turning, and lifting their faces. And standing.

The outer ring filled.

Hyvred watched it fill. It had been the outermost ring, the thirteenth, the one that had been short in the spring, a ragged half-circle with gaps in it like a mouth with teeth missing. She watched the gaps close, one at a time. Thirty-nine people from all over the kingdom, who’d spent a week in Valcrest counting thirty-five and then thirty-six, walking down out of the mountains into the empty places in the ring that had been left for them.

As if the places had been left for them.

They’re going home, Yan had written, in the channel, the night they left Valcrest. They finished counting.

She thought about that, on the tower, on the sixth day, with nineteen in the ring and twenty to come.

Home.

Tiger was on the shed roof.

He’d been on it every evening since they got back. Ossie’s shed, behind the granary, the one he’d built three times and that had stood the third time, with a roof he and Ossie’s father had put on together in the first summer, and Bee’s chalk letters inside it on the back wall, OSSIE’S, and a mended shirt on a nail. Tiger sat on the ridge of the roof with his knees up and looked north-east, toward the pass, toward a valley he couldn’t see.

Drew stood at the bottom of the ladder.

He always did. Every evening. He didn’t go up; Tiger didn’t want anybody up there with him, and Drew had known that for twelve years. He just stood at the bottom with his hands in his pockets and his face doing the thing it did, which was smiling, very slightly, in a way that meant nothing was all right and he was going to be the one still smiling anyway.

Hyvred came down off the tower on the seventh evening, after she’d counted twenty-three, and walked across the yard to them, and stopped at the bottom of the ladder beside Drew.

“How many?” said Drew.

“Twenty-three.”

Drew nodded. He didn’t say anything else. Up on the roof, Tiger didn’t turn round.

“Is he still there?” said Tiger.

His voice was very ordinary. That was the worst thing about it. Tiger’s voice was never ordinary; Tiger wrote in capitals and shouted down channels and ran into rooms. He said this the way you’d ask whether it was going to rain.

Hyvred looked up at him. At the back of his head, and his shoulders, very still against the evening sky.

“Third ring,” she said. “Same place. Face up.”

Tiger didn’t say anything.

“The new ones are on the outside,” said Hyvred. “The outer ring. They’re not near him.” She swallowed. “They’re not doing anything. They just stand.”

“He just stands,” said Tiger.

“Yes.”

“Every morning he raced me.” Tiger was still looking north-east. “Round the yard. Every morning, before drill. He always lost. He never once stood still in his life.” He was quiet. “And now he just stands.”

Nobody said anything.

Drew took his hands out of his pockets. Then he put one foot on the bottom rung of the ladder. Not climbing. Just there. On the rung. Where Tiger, if he looked down, would see it.

Tiger didn’t look down. But after a while, Hyvred saw his shoulders go, a little. And stay gone.

The thirty-ninth came down on the hundred-and-fifth day, at noon.

Hyvred almost missed it. She’d stopped expecting anyone at noon; they’d all come at the edges of the day, dusk or dawn, the way the threadless always moved, at the hours when you weren’t sure what you were seeing. She’d come up the tower at noon only because Kei had asked her to.

“Hisui wants to look,” Kei had said, at breakfast. With the green snake round his neck, with its head up, tasting the air. “At the wind. He says it’s doing something.” He’d looked apologetic, the way Kei always looked when he was passing on something Hisui said, because Hisui never said anything politely. “He says it’s rude.”

So they’d gone up. Hyvred and Kei and Hisui, at noon, into the wind off the mountains. And Hyvred had let the Scope go out, idly, over the pass, the way you’d glance at a clock.

And there was the thirty-ninth.

On the road at the top of the pass. Small and far and alone, in the bright noon light, with no shadow at all on the grey paving. A woman. Old, grey-haired, in a black widow’s shawl, walking very slowly with a stick. She came down the switchbacks one at a time. It took her the whole of the afternoon. Hyvred stood on the tower and watched her the whole of the afternoon, and Kei stood beside her, and Corvin came up with his board, and then Bee, and then Drew, and then Hild, all of them on the north tower in the long afternoon, watching a place eleven miles off that only one of them could see, while Hyvred told them.

She’s at the bottom of the road. She’s at the town. She’s going through it. She’s out the other side.

She’s at the ring.

There was one gap left. Hyvred had watched it for three days. A single space in the outermost ring, on the side nearest the pass, the last tooth missing from the mouth. The old woman in the widow’s shawl walked round the outside of the ring to it, slowly, with her stick, and stepped into it.

And turned. And lifted her face toward the pass.

And the ring was whole.

Hyvred felt it.

She didn’t see it. The Scope showed her the same thing it had shown her all afternoon: a valley, and a burned circle, and rings of people standing round it with their faces up. Nothing moved. Nobody turned.

But she felt it, in the gold eye, under the crosshair. The way you feel a sound in your teeth before you hear it. Something going shut. Not loudly. Like a book closing in another room. Like a sum being finished, the last number written in at the bottom of the column, and the pen put down.

They finished counting.

“It’s full,” said Hyvred. Very quietly.

Nobody on the tower said anything.

And then she saw the absence move.

It was at the foot of the pass. It had been there since the winter, at the near end of the valley, where the old paved road came down off the last switchback onto the valley floor. The size of a hill. She’d never been able to see it properly. Nobody could. It wasn’t a thing you saw. It was the place where seeing stopped: a hole in the Scope, a patch of the valley where her gold eye went to look and came back with nothing, the way your tongue goes to a missing tooth.

Not in a hurry, Fenwick had written, in the first winter, in eleven pages to the capital.

It wasn’t in a hurry now.

But it turned.

Hyvred felt it turn the way you feel somebody turn round behind you in a quiet room. The whole of it. A hill’s worth of nothing, at the foot of the pass, shifting, slowly, the way a sleeper shifts. It had faced the valley all year. The rings. The circle. Its back to the pass.

It turned its back on them.

Toward the pass. Toward the road at the top of it, the old paved road wide enough for armies, going up over the Greyspine and down the other side into the green country, and the farms, and Thornwall.

And stopped.

And stayed turned.

She’d felt it look at Thornwall once before. In the spring, when every chalk dog in the fort barked at once and Drew made her close the Scope. It had looked for fifty heartbeats, and then turned back, as if it had seen what it wanted.

This wasn’t that.

This wasn’t looking. Hyvred stood on the north tower with her hands on the stone and her left eye locked on a hole in the world and knew the difference the way you’d know the difference between somebody glancing at a door and somebody standing in front of it with their coat on.

It wasn’t looking at Thornwall. It had turned to face the way out.

“Close it,” said Drew, behind her. Very quietly.

She didn’t.

“Hyv,” said Drew. “Close it.”

“It’s not looking at us,” said Hyvred. “It’s not—Drew, it’s not looking. It’s facing.” She didn’t know how to say it. “Like the rings. It’s done what they do. It’s turned round and it’s facing the pass and it’s just standing there.”

“Close it anyway.”

She closed it.

The world came back wide. The tower, and the yard, and the mountains blue in the afternoon, and five people standing round her on the north tower of Thornwall, very still, looking at her face.

“It’s ready,” said Hyvred.

Nobody asked for what.

Corvin wrote it down.

He stood at the top of the stair with his board, with the ink drying on his pen, and wrote it, slowly, in his narrow careful hand, while the others went down. Hyvred stayed with him. She watched him write it. She couldn’t read it; the native script slid off her eyes. She watched his hand, which was steady, and his face, which wasn’t.

One hundred and five. Noon to dusk. Thirty-nine. An old woman, widow’s black, with a stick. The last gap in the thirteenth ring, nearest the pass. The ring is whole.

The absence has turned. It faces the pass. It has not moved toward it.

Hyvred says: it is ready.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he put the board down on the parapet and took something out of the inside pocket of his coat.

A short knotted cord. Purple and gold, with half a bead and a strip of thin blue paper wound round the knot. Hyvred had carried it west for him, from Valcrest, in her own pack, for twenty days. It had worked, on and off, since. And then, a fortnight ago, one night, it had gone very hot in Corvin’s hand and said the door is open and then hello, and since then Thea said it worked perfectly.

Corvin held it in his closed hand and looked at the valley he couldn’t see.

“Two years,” he said. “Two years of reports. Every week. By rider. Twenty days to the capital.” He was quiet. “I’ve never once known if anyone read them.”

“Somebody read them,” said Hyvred. “The Advisor did. He sent a whole company west because of them, the first autumn. You cried.”

Corvin looked at her.

“Everybody cried,” said Hyvred. “Hild put her hand on your back.” She looked at the cord in his hand. “Write to her. She’ll read it in a heartbeat. And then she’ll tell everyone who needs to know.”

Corvin looked at the cord. Then he picked up his pen again, and wrote on the strip of blue paper, very small, in his narrow hand. Not much. It didn’t take much.

The rings are full.

He held it. In his closed hand. In the wind on the north tower.

After a moment, he opened his hand and looked at the strip, and Hyvred saw his face change.

“She says thank you,” said Corvin. His voice wasn’t steady. “She says I’m here. Tell me everything.”

So he did. Standing on the north tower of Thornwall in the last of the afternoon, he wrote it all, a few words at a time, and held it, and wrote more. And a long way east, in a castle on a rock, a woman with a purple cat’s tail and seven halves on her wrist sat and read it. And it was the first time in two years that anything Corvin had seen at the foot of the pass got to anybody faster than a horse could carry it.

The wind changed that night.

Kei felt it first. Or Hisui did; with Kei it was always Hisui first. They were at supper, all of them, at the long table in the guardhouse, with the lamps lit and the shutters open on the warm evening, and Hisui, round Kei’s neck, lifted his head off Kei’s collar and stopped.

Hyvred saw it. Everyone at the table did. Hisui never stopped. He was a green tree snake who never once in two years had stopped talking, in Kei’s head, every waking minute, so much that Kei had stopped hearing it, Kei said, the way you stop hearing a river. And now he lifted his head and held it very still, and his tongue went out, and in, and out, tasting the air coming in through the open shutters off the mountains.

And Kei’s face changed.

“What?” said Bee.

Kei didn’t answer at once. He was listening. He had the look he got when Hisui was saying something long and fast and Kei was trying to keep up.

“He says the wind’s changed,” said Kei. “Off the valley. Just now.” He was quiet. “He says it tasted like the bottom of a well all year. Cold. Old. Like that.” He listened again. “He says it doesn’t now.”

“What does it taste like now?” said Hyvred.

Kei listened for a long time.

“Like rain that hasn’t happened yet,” he said slowly. “Like the air before a storm. When everything goes still and waits.” He frowned. “He says it’s very faint. He says nobody else would taste it.” A pause. “He says it’s calling.”

Nobody at the table said anything.

“Calling what?” said Drew.

Kei shook his head. “He doesn’t know. He says it’s not calling him. Snakes don’t get called.” Another pause. “He says it’s the kind of thing that calls people.”

Nothing happened for two days.

That was the worst part, Hyvred thought afterwards. Two days of nothing. The wind off the mountains, warm and dry and ordinary, the way it always was at the end of summer. The fort going about its business. Drill in the yard. Patrols along the foot of the pass and back. Tiger on the shed roof at dusk. Bee’s chalk dogs on the granary wall, one a day, each one a little higher. And Hisui, round Kei’s neck, tasting, and tasting, and saying nothing that Kei would repeat at the table.

Hyvred Scoped the valley twice a day. The rings stood. The absence faced the pass. Nothing moved.

On the third day, a sentry on the north wall stopped walking.

Hyvred didn’t see it. Fenwick told her, at noon, very carefully, the way Fenwick told everything. A young soldier on the north wall, on the morning watch, walking his length of the parapet the way he’d walked it every morning for a year. And at about the third hour, halfway along, he’d stopped. And turned. To face the mountains. And lifted his face toward the pass.

And stood there.

His sergeant had shouted at him. He hadn’t answered. His sergeant had come along the wall and shaken him by the shoulder, and he’d blinked, and looked round, and said what? And hadn’t known he’d stopped. Hadn’t known how long. It had been, his sergeant thought, about a minute.

“One man,” said Fenwick. “Once. A minute.” He was pale. “I’d have put it down to the sun. To sleeping badly.” He looked at Hyvred. “If it weren’t for the way he stood.”

On the fourth day, two more. A groom in the stable yard. A woman at the well. A minute each. Faces up. Toward the pass. Not answering. Then blinking, and what?, and not knowing.

On the fifth day, the dogs.

Every dog in Thornwall, at dusk, all at once, in the yards and the kennels and the streets of the town below the fort, stopped whatever they were doing and turned to face the mountains. Not barking. Not growling. Just standing, with their ears up and their noses lifted. For a minute. And then lay down, and wouldn’t eat.

Bee’s chalk dogs on the granary wall barked all night.

On the sixth night, at supper, Drew stood up.

He’d been talking. He was always talking at supper; it was his job, he said, the one who kept everybody going, the one still smiling at the end. He’d been telling a story about Lucan and a goose, at the long table, with his elbows on it and his hands going, and everyone laughing, because Drew could make anybody laugh about anything, even this, even now.

And in the middle of a sentence, he stopped.

And stood up.

Not quickly. The way you’d stand up to look at something out of a window. He pushed his chair back and stood, and walked round the end of the table, past Tiger, past Bee, past Hyvred, to the open shutters at the end of the guardhouse. And stood at them.

Facing north-east. Toward the pass.

And lifted his face.

“Drew,” said Tiger.

Drew didn’t move.

“Drew.”

Hyvred was already on her feet. She didn’t remember getting up. She was across the room to the window and in front of him, and she looked at his face, and it was smiling.

That was what she’d remember. That was the thing she’d see at night. Drew’s face, lifted toward the pass in the lamplight from the guardhouse, perfectly calm, and smiling. Very slightly. The way he always smiled. The smile that meant nothing’s all right and I’m going to be the one still smiling anyway. Only there was nobody behind it now. Nobody deciding to smile. It was just there, on his face, the way a smile is on a painting.

She looked at him with the gold eye.

She didn’t mean to. It locked on before she could stop it. The crosshair. The true sight. And she saw, on Drew, faint, so faint she’d never have seen it if she hadn’t spent two years looking at the rings in the valley, the thing she’d seen on the miller’s wife on the ridge track, and on the boy in the cap at the Narrows, and on thirty-nine people walking out of Valcrest at night.

The wrong light.

Not much. Not on him. Round him. A faint grey shine on the edges of him, like frost on the outside of a window, the very first fern of it, the way frost starts at the corner of a pane on the first cold night of the year.

“Kei,” said Hyvred.

Her voice came out of her in a way it had never come out before.

Tiger had hold of Drew by both arms.

He’d come round the table so fast that a bench went over. He had Drew by the arms and was shaking him and saying his name, Drew, Drew, DREW, and Drew stood there in his hands with his face lifted and smiling and didn’t answer. Bee had her hands over her mouth. Corvin was on his feet. Hild was in the doorway, from nowhere, the way Hild was always from nowhere, with her one eye open, and her hand on her sword, and nothing to use it on.

And Kei came past all of them with a cup.

Hyvred didn’t know where he’d got it. She’d find out later that he’d been carrying it for three days. In his coat, in a flask, stoppered with wax. Since the first night the wind changed. Since Hisui said calling. He’d been up every night since, in the little room off the guardhouse kitchen that Hild had given him for his poisons in the first month, with Hisui round his neck, tasting things.

He pulled the stopper out with his teeth. He poured something into the cup. It was dark, and it smelled of river water and bitter green things and something else, sharp and cold, that made Hyvred’s eyes water from three paces away.

“Hold his head,” said Kei.

Tiger held his head.

“Poison and medicine are the same thing,” said Kei, very quietly, to nobody, the way he said everything when he was frightened and didn’t want anybody to know. “The difference is the dose.”

And he lifted the cup to Drew’s mouth, and tipped it, and Drew, who was smiling and not there, swallowed, because a body swallows when there’s something in its mouth, whether or not there’s anybody inside it.

Nothing happened.

Hyvred watched with the gold eye. The faint grey frost round Drew’s edges. Not spreading. Not going. Just there.

Kei was looking at the cup. At what was left in the bottom of it. His face had gone very still. And round his neck, Hisui had gone still too. Not tasting. Listening. Both of them. The way Hyvred had seen them listen to things she couldn’t hear.

“It’s not enough,” said Kei.

He said it to Hisui. Not to anyone else. Hyvred could see Hisui saying something back, fast, his tongue going.

“I know it’s not enough,” said Kei. “I know. I know.” His hands were shaking. “I made it for a minute. For the sentry. For the dogs. A minute’s worth. He’s—“ He looked at Drew. “He’s further in than a minute.”

Hisui said something else.

Kei went very still.

“The difference is the dose,” he said. Slowly. “And the intent.”

He put the cup down on the table.

And then he did a thing Hyvred had never seen him do, in two years. He lifted his hand, and put one finger to the air in front of him, the way they all did, the way Hyvred had written scope once and Bee had drawn a dog without lifting the chalk. Not on the cup. On the air in front of Drew’s lifted face. Very carefully. One word, in English, in Kei’s neat sloping hand, the hand he’d labelled two years of jars and flasks and bottles in.

Enough.

The air between Kei’s finger and Drew’s face went green.

Hyvred saw it with both eyes. Faint, and green, the colour of Hisui’s scales, the colour of a leaf held up to the sun. Not a light. A taste. She could taste it, suddenly, sharp and cold and bitter on the back of her tongue, the way you taste the air before a storm. And then, under it, something else. Something warm. Something like the smell of a kitchen when somebody’s making soup for somebody who’s ill.

It went into Drew.

Not through his mouth. Through the air, the way breath goes in. And the dose that was already in him, the dark bitter cupful that hadn’t been enough, changed. Hyvred saw it with the gold eye. Saw the faint grey frost on the edges of him, the first fern of the wrong light, meet the green, and stop. And go back. Like frost going off a window when the sun comes round onto it. Edge first. Then the corners. Then gone.

Drew blinked.

He stood at the open shutters with Tiger holding him by both arms and his face lifted toward the pass. And blinked again. And lowered his face. And looked round. At Tiger, an inch from him, white, with his hands on Drew’s arms. At Hyvred. At Kei, with his finger still up in the air and a green light going out at the tip of it. At the whole of the guardhouse standing round him in the lamplight, very still, looking at him.

“What?” said Drew.

Nobody said anything.

“Why’s everybody looking at me?” Drew looked down at Tiger’s hands on his arms. Then up at Tiger’s face. His own face did something. Not smiling. Something else. “Tiger. What happened?”

Tiger didn’t say anything.

He let go of Drew’s arms. And then, before Drew could say anything else, he put both his arms round him, and held on. Not the way Tiger hugged everybody, loudly and with his whole body and a running start. Very quietly. With his face in Drew’s shoulder. And didn’t let go.

“Oh,” said Drew. Very quietly. And put his own arms round Tiger, slowly, and held on too, and looked over Tiger’s shoulder at the rest of them, and his face asked the question again, and nobody answered it, because nobody could.

Kei’s window had opened.

Hyvred saw it from where she stood. Everybody did. Plain and white, in front of his face, while he stood by the table with his finger still raised and the green going out of the tip of it and Hisui round his neck absolutely silent for the first time since a roof in Valcrest.

[ Kei · Tier 1: Spark · Skills: Dose ]

Kei looked at it.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he lowered his hand, and sat down on the bench, quite suddenly, the way people sat down when their windows opened on something they’d waited two years for. He sat with his hands in his lap and looked at the window and didn’t say anything.

Round his neck, Hisui put his head down on Kei’s collar.

“He says,” said Kei, eventually. His voice was very small. “He says he told me so.” He swallowed. “He says he’s been telling me so since the first week. Poison and medicine are the same thing. The difference is the dose. And the intent. He said it on the first day.” Kei laughed, a little, wet. “He says I only ever heard the first half.”

“What’s the second half?” said Bee.

Kei looked at Drew. At Tiger, still holding on.

“The intent,” said Kei. “You don’t make medicine for a sickness. You make it for a person.”

Corvin wrote to Hana that night.

Hyvred watched him do it. On the north tower again, at midnight, because Corvin said he thought better on the tower and Hyvred thought he just wanted to be somewhere he could see the pass, even though he couldn’t see the pass. Him and the cord and the strip of blue paper. Writing a few words, and holding it, and reading the answer, and writing more.

Drew stood at the window and turned his face to the pass. A minute. More. The wrong light round him, Hyvred says, very faint.

Kei made a dose. It was not enough. Then he wrote a word into it and it was.

Kei has reached Spark. The window says Dose.

Drew is well. He does not remember. Tiger has not let go of him.

And then, after a long time, the last thing, very small:

The rings are full and the thing at the foot of the pass has turned to face the way out, and the wind off the valley is calling people, and the first person it called was the one who keeps everyone going. And we have a dose. One. For one man. For now.

Corvin held it.

He held it for a long time, in the wind, with his eyes shut. Then he opened his hand and read the answer, and Hyvred, beside him, watched his face.

“She says she’s telling the king tonight,” said Corvin. “And the Advisor. And the channel, the parts that are fit.” He was quiet. “She says: you’re not on your own out there any more. Not for one more day.”

Hyvred looked at the mountains.

They were black against the stars. The pass was a notch in the black, very high. And beyond it, eleven miles off, a valley she could have seen if she’d let her eye go. A burned circle the size of a fort. Thirteen rings of people standing in the dark with their faces up, full now. A freckled boy in the third ring. And at the foot of the pass, a hill’s worth of nothing, with its back to all of them, facing the way out, waiting. Not in a hurry. Ready.

She didn’t let her eye go.

She stood on the tower beside Corvin with both her eyes open, the gold one and the other, and looked at the dark the way anybody would, and let it be dark. And found she could stand it. Because behind her, down in the guardhouse, the lamps were still lit, and Kei was sitting at the long table with a cup in front of him and his window open and a green snake asleep on his collar, and Bee was drawing tomorrow’s dog on the back of a supper plate, and Drew was asleep on the bench by the fire with his head on Tiger’s knee, and Tiger was awake, sitting very straight, with one hand flat on Drew’s shoulder, the way you’d keep your hand on something you’d nearly lost in a river.

Not alone. Not for one more day.

Tiger went up on the shed roof the next evening at dusk, the way he always did.

Drew went up the ladder after him.

Hyvred saw it from the tower. She’d gone up to count, the way she still did every dusk, though there was nothing left to count. And she looked down into the yard behind the granary and saw Tiger on the ridge of Ossie’s shed with his knees up, facing north-east, and Drew at the bottom of the ladder with his hands in his pockets, the way he’d stood every evening for a year.

And then Drew took his hands out of his pockets, and went up.

Slowly. He wasn’t good with ladders. Hyvred watched him go up it one rung at a time, very carefully, the way he did everything that wasn’t talking. And at the top he stopped, and Tiger turned round on the ridge and looked at him.

Hyvred couldn’t hear what they said. She didn’t try. She let her eye stay where it was, plain and gold, and didn’t lock on.

Tiger moved up. On the ridge. Not much. Just enough.

And Drew climbed off the ladder onto the roof, and sat down beside him, on the ridge of a shed that had stood, with his knees up, and the two of them sat there, side by side, the way they’d sat side by side in calls for twelve years with nobody able to see them, and looked north-east together at a pass they couldn’t see.

On the granary wall below them, at the top, higher than all the others, there was a new chalk dog. One unbroken line. Sitting. Facing the pass.

With its ears back.

Guarding.
