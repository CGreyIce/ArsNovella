The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 6: Nine

Chapter 166

The Rise

5,782 words · 25 min read

Yan had said together, on a street corner in Valcrest under a lamp, and the man had said together, and Yan had carried it east for fifty-six days like a stone in his pocket.

He didn’t carry many things out loud. He’d never needed to. On Earth he’d been the one who kept everyone going: the calm voice on the call at three in the morning, left, two, behind the gap, the one who said okay at the bottom of everything. Here he’d been the same. Two years on the edge of the red. The candles of four friends round him, the cold candles of the things that came across the plain at night, and under all of it, a thing he’d felt in the first week and never put down. A cold. Under a grave. Still asleep. Something’s holding it.

He’d written that in a letter once, to a man he didn’t know yet. In Cursiffe’s blue ink. And the man had read it, and said nothing, for a year.

And then in Valcrest he’d said we’ll both look.

So on the hundred-and-twenty-sixth day, the day before the anniversary, at the grey hour before dawn, Yan went down to the east gate of Cinderhold with the scythe on his shoulder and Suzu wound round its shaft. And the man was there already. Standing in the gate, under the horns, with the rod over his shoulder and his coat buttoned, looking out at the red.

He turned his head when Yan came.

“Ready?” he said.

Yan thought about it. Properly. The way he thought about everything, slowly, from the bottom up, so that when he said a thing it was the right thing.

“Okay,” he said.

The red went quiet when the man walked on it.

Yan had felt it before. On the red a year ago, under a broken ring of fire, with fifty cold candles round him and a sea coming in. He’d thought then that it was the sea. That the man was so large, so much, that everything else just stopped to let him pass. Wendy had told him what she’d seen at the old south line on the way in, the grey grass going still round his feet in a ring, and Maigo saying it stopped hurting. Yan had nodded. He’d felt it from the cart.

He felt it now. Walking beside the man out of the east gate onto the red in the grey, past the first row of Aldous’s markers, and the second. He wasn’t looking with his eyes. He let the other sight come up, the one that had come with Suzu, the one that saw the living as candles. And under his feet, all round him, the ground.

It wasn’t a candle. The ground had no soul. But it had something. It always had, out here. Not a flame. A kind of ache. Every step on the red for two years, Yan had felt it through his boots like the bruise under a bruise, deep and old and everywhere. The way a field would feel, if a field could feel, where something had been done to it that it couldn’t forget. Maigo said it hurt. Maigo was right.

And round the man, it didn’t.

In a ring. A few paces across. As they walked. The ache went quiet under the man’s boots the way a crying child goes quiet when somebody it knows walks into the room. Not better. Just there. Somebody being there.

Yan walked beside him in the ring of quiet with the scythe on his shoulder and didn’t say anything about it. The man didn’t either.

They went on, east, through the markers. Row after row. Plain wooden posts with names burned into them, and then no names. A thousand. More.

“He told me how many,” said the man, after a while. “Aldous. In the chapel.” He didn’t look at the posts. “Eleven hundred and four with names. Three hundred and nineteen without.”

“He told us he’d stopped counting.”

“He hadn’t.”

“No,” said Yan. “I knew he hadn’t.” He was quiet. “His candle goes very small, every morning, an hour before dawn. For about the time it takes to read twenty names.” He looked at the posts going by. “I worked it out in the first month. I never asked.”

The man looked at him sideways. Then back at the red.

“That’s what you do,” he said. “You work it out. And you never ask.”

“Yes.”

“I do the same.”

“I know,” said Yan.

They passed the grave at a distance.

Yan had known they would. You couldn’t get to the rise from the fort without passing it; the knoll was on the way, an hour out, above the old dry riverbed, by the line of dead grey trees. He’d walked to it a hundred times. He’d sat on the cairn in the snow and counted thirty-six. He’d carried his share of a town’s worth of children’s stones across the red in the white winter, and watched Salty carry the rest of it, and stood at the edge of the ring of green while a black stone went down into its hole like a key into a lock.

He could see it now. Off to the left as they went, in the grey light, a few hundred paces away. The low knoll. The ring of flowers round the top, green in all that red, the only green there was. The young ash, Maigo’s, taller than Salty now, with its leaves just beginning to turn at the edges. And the stone. Black. Rounded at the top. Standing at the head of the cairn, facing west.

The man didn’t look at it.

Yan watched him not look. He walked past it, on the left, a few hundred paces off, at the same even pace, with his eyes on the ground ahead, and didn’t turn his head once. The way you’d walk past a door you’re not going through yet, in a house where everyone’s asleep.

“Tomorrow,” said Yan.

“Tomorrow,” said the man. “With her.”

He didn’t say anything else. They went on past the knoll. And the tree, Yan saw, as they passed, moved. Very slightly. All its leaves at once. Toward the man. The way leaves turn toward the sun. There wasn’t any wind.

Maigo would have shouted. Yan didn’t. He watched it, and filed it, the way he filed everything, and walked on.

The rise was further than the grave. Further out, and a little south. You didn’t see it properly from the fort; you saw the ring, in the low light, if you knew. From down on the red, walking toward it, it was just a long low hump in the plain, like the back of something sleeping. Grey grass on it, the same as everywhere. Nothing special.

They came to the foot of it as the sun came up behind it.

Behind it. That was the first thing Yan noticed, and he’d remember it. The sun came up over the mountains of Kharvos, behind the rise, and the rise stood black against it, a long dark shape against a sky going gold. The way it must have stood nine years ago, in the afternoon, with fires round it, against the smoke.

The man stopped at the foot of it.

He stood there in the grey grass with the rod over his shoulder and looked up at it for a long time.

Yan stood beside him and didn’t say anything. He let the other sight come up. Not at the rise. At the man.

He’d been looking at the man that way for a year. On the red, the first time, he’d seen a sea: something vast and cold and grieving, older than its body, from somewhere else. At the grave in the snow, a long way north-west, the biggest of thirty-six. In Valcrest, close, a thing that wasn’t a candle at all. More like the place a flame had been, he’d thought, on the first night out from Valcrest, by the fire. Warm. Very large. Very quiet.

It was quiet now.

Not the quiet of the jetty, or the road, or the man carrying water. Something else. Very still. The way a lake goes still, not because the wind’s dropped, but because something under it has stopped moving and is waiting to see what you’ll do.

“Nine years,” said the man. Not to Yan. To the rise.

“You’ve never been up,” said Yan.

“I’ve been up once.” The man didn’t take his eyes off it. “The afternoon. And the night. And the next morning, I came back, with a spade.” He was quiet. “And carried her down off it. And west. To the knoll. Away from the ring.” He breathed. “I’ve never been up since.”

“Why not?”

The man was quiet for a long time.

“Because she’s not there,” he said eventually. “Alea said it. On the wall. She’s not there. She’s under the stone. Where you carried her.” He shifted the rod on his shoulder. “I’ve known that for nine years. I still couldn’t come.”

“Why now?”

The man turned his head and looked at him.

Yan couldn’t see his face. Nobody could; your eyes slid off it like rain off glass. But he didn’t need it. He could see the other thing. The large quiet place where a flame had been. And it leaned. Toward him. The way it had leaned toward each of them, at the grave, a year ago, like a fire leaning toward somebody who’s come in from the cold.

“Because you asked to look,” said the man. “And I said together.”

Yan didn’t say anything. He found, for a moment, that he couldn’t.

“Okay,” he said.

They went up.

It wasn’t steep. It was a long low slope of grey grass going up to a flat top about the size of a barn floor. The man went first, and Yan behind, with the scythe, and the ring of quiet going up the slope ahead of them round the man’s boots. And Suzu, on the shaft of the scythe, went cold.

Yan felt it at once, down the cord. Not hunger-cold; he’d fed her a hen last night, in the stable yard, with Wendy beside him, and he’d watched the little pale light go to her the way he watched it every time now, and she’d been warm all night. This was the other cold. The cold she went in the Lists when the threadless were near. The cord between them pulling tight as wire.

She came down off the shaft. All of her, fast, round his wrist and up his arm and round his neck, tight, the way she’d gone round his neck in the hall on the first day. And put her head under his jaw. And stayed there.

“I know,” said Yan, very quietly, to her. “I know.”

They came to the top.

The ring was in the middle of it.

Yan had seen it from the wall. A dark circle on the rise in the low light. He saw it now, at his feet, close, in the first sun.

It was burned into the ground. Not on it; into it. A ring about thirty paces round, a hand wide, the colour of old charcoal. Darker than the red round it. Darker than anything on the red. As if somebody had laid a ring of iron on the earth at a white heat and left it there until it went through. And inside the ring, fainter, other lines. Going across. Spokes, like a wheel’s, from the edge toward the middle, and smaller rings inside the big one, and short marks along the lines like the marks on a ruler.

And nothing growing in it.

That was what Yan noticed most. Grey grass all over the rise, all over the red, everywhere, the grass that didn’t die and didn’t grow. Up to the edge of the ring. Not one blade inside it. The ground inside the ring was bare red earth, packed hard, cracked, the way it must have been nine years ago, as if nothing had happened to it since. As if nothing could.

The man stopped at the edge of it.

Yan stopped beside him.

They stood there in the sun with the wind going over the rise, and looked at it.

Yan reached.

Not for the man. Down. Into the ground. The way he’d reached into the ground on the cairn in the snow, the way he reached for Suzu down the cord. Slowly. Carefully. Not far.

And there it was.

The shape.

He knew it at once. He’d felt it under the grave for two years, faint, asleep. He’d felt it in the Lists, in a crowd, on forty people with nobody inside them. He’d read Hyvred’s letters about the valley below the pass, the circle the size of a fort with rings of people round it. He’d read Malcolm’s about the Hollow, and the deep ring, and the light coming up under the bricked door in spokes. He’d read about a hut above a village called Coldwater, with a circle burned into its floor.

A ring with lines in it.

It was here. Under the burned ring on the surface. The same shape, going down, into the red, the way Kei had said the hut’s circle went down, full of the wrong light. But not full. Not here.

Empty.

He felt it the way you’d feel a cup in the dark with your fingers: the shape of it, the rim, the inside, and nothing in it. A mould. Something had been poured into this shape, once, a long time ago, nine years ago, and poured out of it again into something else, and what was left was the shape, burned into the ground, cold as a cellar, with nothing inside.

“It’s empty,” said Yan.

The man didn’t move.

“The shape’s still here,” said Yan slowly. “The ring. The lines. Like the valley. Like the Hollow. Like under the grave.” He was choosing every word. “But there’s nothing in it. It’s like a jug that’s been poured out. The cold’s in the shape of it. But the thing that was in it isn’t here.”

“Where is it?”

Yan didn’t answer straight away.

He turned his head. West. Back the way they’d come. Down off the rise, across the red, to a low knoll a long way off with a ring of green on it and a young tree and a black stone, small in the morning sun.

The man followed his eyes.

He didn’t say anything.

The man stepped into the ring.

Yan didn’t stop him. He wouldn’t have known how. The man just stepped forward, over the burned edge, onto the bare cracked earth inside it, the way you’d step over a threshold. And the ring of quiet went with him. Yan felt it. The ache of the red went still round the man’s boots, the way it always did, and the cold of the empty shape under the ring went still too. Not warm. Not gone. Just quiet. Waiting to see what he’d do.

He walked to the middle.

Slowly. Across the spokes. To the place in the centre where all the lines met, where the smallest ring was, no bigger than a cartwheel. And stopped there.

And knelt.

He did it the way Yan had seen him kneel by the young tree on the cairn, a year ago, to touch a leaf: unhurried, one knee and then the other, the rod laid down on the red beside him. He knelt in the middle of the burned ring with the sun coming up behind him over Kharvos, and put one hand flat on the ground.

On the bare red earth. In the centre. Where the lines met.

Yan watched him.

Not his face. The other thing. The large quiet place where a flame had been. And he watched it go still. Stiller than he’d ever seen it. Stiller than the lake. So still that for a moment Yan thought it had stopped altogether, the way a heart stops between beats.

“She was here,” said the man.

Very quietly. Not to Yan. To the ground under his hand.

“She was here, and she asked me. And I did what she asked.” He didn’t move his hand. “And then she was gone, and I carried her off the rise, and west, to where the grass grew. And I’ve never come back.” He was quiet. “I thought I’d left part of her up here. On her own. Nine years, I thought that.” He breathed. “I didn’t. I carried all of her.”

He knelt there a long time.

Then he took his hand off the earth. And looked at it, his palm, red with the dust of the ring. And wiped it on his coat. And stood.

“All right,” he said. To the ring. “That’s done.”

Yan saw it then.

He’d remember it for the rest of his life. He’d never tell anyone, not even the man, not even Suzu, though Suzu was there and saw it too, he thought, with her head under his jaw and one green eye open.

In the place where a flame had been, something caught.

Very small. Very faint. Like a coal you’d thought was out, in the bottom of a grate in the morning, when somebody bends down and breathes on it. A point of light. Warm. Not a candle; not yet; nowhere near. Just a spark in the dark of the place where a candle used to be.

It didn’t go out.

Yan watched it, from the edge of the ring, with the scythe on his shoulder and the sun in his eyes, and it didn’t go out. It just stayed. Small and warm and steady in the middle of all that quiet, the way a lamp stays in a window at night in a house where somebody has finally come home and gone to sleep.

He didn’t say anything.

Some things weren’t his to give.

“The grave,” said the man.

He’d come back to the edge of the ring. He stood beside Yan and looked west, at the knoll, small and far in the sun.

“You said the thing that was in the shape isn’t here.” He didn’t look at Yan. “You looked at the grave.”

“Yes.”

“Tell me.”

Yan told him.

He did it the way he did everything: slowly, from the bottom up, in order, the way he’d called rounds on a thousand calls at three in the morning. He told him about the first week. Two years ago, on the red, with Aldous, at the edge of the hole where the sword had been taken out by six men and a winch the season before. The cold he’d felt under it. Faint, and deep, and wrong. A flame burning the wrong way, like the cold candles of the things that came across the plain at night, but not like them. Bigger. Older. And asleep.

“Something’s holding it,” said the man. “You wrote that.”

“I didn’t know what,” said Yan. “Not then. It was asleep, but it was the kind of asleep that turns over. You could feel it shift. Some nights. Like something in the next room that might wake.” He was quiet. “All the first year. While the sword was gone.”

The man was very still.

“And then you came,” said Yan. “On the anniversary. And took the sword out of Salty’s hands. And carried it to the grave, and sat down by the cairn with it across your knees until dusk.” He looked at the knoll. “And it went to sleep. Properly. I felt it go. Like a dog lying down.”

“I felt something,” said the man. “I didn’t know what.”

“And then you took the sword north,” said Yan. “And it didn’t wake. Not quite. It was the tree, I think, all that winter. And the flowers. Maigo’s roots, round it, like fingers.” He paused. “And then the stone came.”

“The spike,” said the man.

He told Yan about the spike.

Yan had seen it. He’d stood at the edge of the ring of green in the white winter and watched it go in. The long black spike under the headstone’s face, that the king had drawn out of the old point of the sword. It had gone down into the hole in the red, Wendy said afterwards, like a key into a lock it was made for. All the way. As if the hole had been waiting eight years for exactly that shape to come back to it.

“Severin kept the point,” said the man. “When he cut the sword. He didn’t cut it off. He drew it out, longer, narrower. A spike. So the stone would go into the same hole the sword had stood in.” He was quiet. “He said a stone needs a root.”

Yan looked west at the knoll.

He reached. Not far. He didn’t need to go far; he’d been reaching for it for two years and he knew the way. Across the red, through the morning, to a low knoll above a dry river, a ring of green, a cairn of stones. And down. Under the cairn. Under the children’s stones. Under the earth.

There.

The cold. Small, now. Dense. Like a coal of ice, deep in the ground, under the place where a woman lay. Asleep. Not turning over. Not shifting. Holding very still, the way you’d hold still with something pressing on you that you couldn’t push off.

And going down into it, through it, from above, a line.

Black. And hot. Not warm; hot, the way iron is hot that’s been in a forge, the way the black sword had been hot to the cold things on the red that tried to drag it and burned their hands. A long straight line of it, going down from the surface, through the cairn, through the earth, through the cold, and out the other side, and on, deep.

Holding it.

Not like a root. Yan saw that at once. A root wraps round. A root drinks. This didn’t wrap round anything. It went through. Straight. Driven.

“It’s not a root,” said Yan.

The man waited.

“It’s a nail,” said Yan. “It’s—it’s pinned. The cold. The spike goes right through it. Into the ground underneath. Like a nail through a moth.” He swallowed. “And round it, the roots. The tree’s. Maigo’s. Round the spike, and round the cold, like fingers round a wrist. Holding the nail in.”

The man didn’t say anything for a long time.

“It held for seven years,” said Yan, eventually. “The sword. Point down, in that hole. Seven years. And the cold slept.” He was working it out as he said it, the way he worked out everything, one piece at a time. “And then the Advisor took the sword. For the summoning. And it woke. Not all the way. But it turned over. I felt it, my first week.” He looked at the knoll. “And then the stone went in. The same hole. And it slept again.”

“Because of the iron,” said the man.

“Because of the iron. And the tree. And you, the day you sat there with the sword on your knees.” Yan was quiet. “I don’t know which. I think all of them.”

The man looked at the knoll.

“Tomorrow,” he said slowly, “Alea’s going to stand at the head of that cairn with the other half of the sword on her back.”

“I know.”

“The two halves haven’t been in the same place since the king split them on the shore.” The man was very still. “Gabe says it’s not from here. The iron. He felt it, at the forge. Folded a thousand times. Waiting, he said.” He turned the rod on his shoulder. “I don’t know what it does when both halves are in one place. Over that.”

“Neither do I,” said Yan.

They stood on the edge of the rise in the sun and looked at the knoll.

“We’ll find out,” said the man.

“We’ll find out,” Yan agreed.

He thought he should say it. He didn’t want to. But he’d carried things on his own for two years, and the man had said together, and Yan had decided, on the road east, somewhere between Ashby and the grey grass, that he was going to stop carrying things on his own when there was somebody who’d said together and meant it.

“It isn’t her,” said Yan.

The man turned his head.

“The cold,” said Yan. “Under the grave. I’ve felt it for two years. I’ve thought about it every night. I know what you’ve been thinking. I’d think it too.” He held the man’s eyes, or the place where they’d be. “It isn’t her. She’s not cold. She’s not anything; she’s gone; I can’t see the dead, I only see what’s leaving. But the cold isn’t her.” He was choosing every word. “It’s under her. Like something she was lying on. Or something that was put into her, and didn’t go when she did.”

The man didn’t say anything.

“Poured,” said the man, after a long time. Very quietly. “That’s what it looked like. From the wall. Like pouring out a cup and pouring something else in.” He looked at the empty ring at his feet. “This is the cup.”

“Maybe,” said Yan.

“And under the grave is what they poured.”

“Maybe,” said Yan again. “I don’t know. I think so. Some of it.” He looked at Suzu, cold and tight round his neck, her head under his jaw. “I don’t know what it is. I don’t know what it’s for. I don’t know why it’s the same shape as the valley, and the Hollow, and a hut at Coldwater.” He breathed. “But it isn’t her. And it’s been held down for nine years by your sword, and your stone, and a tree, and it’s still held.” He was quiet. “I wanted you to know that before tomorrow. So you’d know what you were standing on. And what you weren’t.”

The man looked at him for a long time.

Yan felt it. The place where a flame had been, with its one small new spark in it, leaning. Toward him. All of it. The way it had leaned toward his daughter asleep on his arm on the first night out from Valcrest. Like the whole of a lake toward the place where the water goes out.

“Thank you,” said the man.

Yan didn’t say anything. He didn’t need to.

“Okay,” he said, after a while. Because it was the only word he had. It always was.

They walked back past the grave at the same distance, a few hundred paces off, and the man didn’t look at it again. But the tree moved. All its leaves. Toward him. And this time Yan saw the man’s shoulders go, very slightly, as they passed, the way they’d gone at the gate when the old soldiers put their hands on their chests. Not looking. Knowing.

“How close are you?” said the man, halfway back. Through the markers.

Yan looked at him.

“Your bar,” said the man. “Candle.”

Yan hadn’t told anyone. He’d been watching it for weeks, since Valcrest, creeping. He opened his window, walking, the way you’d check the time.

“Ninety-six,” he said.

“Close.”

“Close,” Yan agreed. He closed the window. “Not tomorrow.”

“Why not?”

“Tomorrow’s hers,” said Yan. “Alea’s. And her mother’s.” He shifted the scythe on his shoulder. “I’m not going to do anything tomorrow except stand at the back with the others and keep quiet.” He paused. “I’ll go the day after. Or the week after. Or whenever. Not tomorrow.”

The man walked beside him for a while through the markers without saying anything.

“You can’t decide that,” he said eventually. Not unkindly. “Nobody can. It comes when it comes. Mine came on the worst night of my life.” He was quiet. “It doesn’t ask whose night it is.”

“I know,” said Yan. “I’m deciding anyway.”

The man made a sound that might have been a laugh. Yan wasn’t sure. He’d never been sure, with the man.

“Okay,” said the man.

Alea was on the wall when they came back.

Yan saw her from the red, a long way out, before the man did. Or perhaps the man saw her first and didn’t say. A small figure on the east wall of Cinderhold, north of the gate, at the eleventh merlon. Not beside it. In it. Sitting on the parapet in the gap with her legs over the drop and her mother’s sword on her back, the way Yan had seen her sit every evening since the man sat there with a letter. Watching them come back across the red.

She didn’t wave. She just watched.

The man didn’t wave either. He walked on toward the gate with the rod on his shoulder and the ring of quiet round his boots, and Yan beside him, and Suzu warming, slowly, round Yan’s neck, the further they got from the rise.

They came in under the gate, under the horns.

The man went straight to the gatehouse stair. Yan didn’t follow. He stood in the courtyard by the well with the scythe and watched the man go up, and along the wall, and into the gap, and sit down on the parapet beside his daughter, with his legs over the drop beside hers.

She didn’t ask him anything. Yan saw her not ask. He saw her look at her father sideways, once, the way she looked at everything, the whole of it, all at once; and see whatever she saw; and put her head on his shoulder. And leave it there.

Yan let the other sight come up.

Two candles on the wall, in the gap, side by side. No; one candle, and one place where a flame had been, with a spark in it. And the candle, the girl’s, very bright, quick and hot and steady, like a sword that had just found out it fit.

Leaning east.

It had leaned east all the way from Valcrest. Every night. Every morning. Asleep and awake. Toward a grave she’d never seen. Yan had watched it lean for fifty-six days.

Tomorrow it would get there.

The moon came up at dusk, a night short of full.

Yan watched it from the courtyard. Everyone did, in the end; it was that kind of evening. The whole of Group 3, and Aldous, and Rhea, standing about the yard doing things that didn’t need doing. Salty at Oakes’s forge door, cleaning a scythe that didn’t need cleaning; Yan’s scythe; he’d taken it off Yan at supper without a word. Wendy beside him, re-fletching arrows she’d fletched yesterday. Maigo at the well, in the dusk, with his hood down and his antlers full of leaves going faintly gold at the edges with the season, talking to a small bundle in his hands that turned out, when Yan went over, to be a cutting from the young ash, wrapped in wet moss, that he’d taken in the spring and grown on in a pot in the barracks window all summer.

“For her,” said Maigo. “Alea. To take home. To the lake.” He looked at the cutting. “So there’s one there too. So the lake’s got one.” He was quiet, for Maigo. “I’m going to tell the tree tomorrow. That I’m giving her one of its children. Trees like being told.”

Niko on the wall, south of the gate, with his eyes shut, breathing, round and round, nineteen, twenty. Rhea arm-wrestling nobody. And Aldous, in the chapel doorway, with the last of the light on him, and over his arm, folded, a cloth.

White. Long. Plain linen, very old, very clean, folded in eight. Yan had seen it once before, in the first year, on the red: laid over a dead soldier for an hour on the morning after a scout raid, while Aldous said the old words in the church tongue nobody spoke any more.

Aldous saw him looking.

“For tomorrow,” said the chaplain. Quietly. “For an hour. Over the stones.” He smoothed the folded cloth with his old hand. “It’s not much.”

“It’s enough,” said Yan.

Aldous looked at him, and then at the white snake round Yan’s neck, warm again now, with her green eyes half closed. Something in his grave old face moved. The thing it had done once, in the first week, by a waystation fire, when Yan told him Suzu would never eat people. Less like warding something off. More like a blessing.

The moon came up over the red.

Yan stood at the east gate, under the horns, and watched it. Big and gold and a night short of round, coming up over the mountains of Kharvos, enormous, the way the moon only ever looked out here, with nothing between you and it. Lighting the red. The markers in their rows, all the way out. The knoll. A tree, small and black. A stone.

And further out, a little south, the rise. With an empty ring on it, that a man had knelt in this morning for the first time in nine years, and put his hand on, and said that’s done.

Tomorrow it would be full. Higu’s chart. Two months ago, at the end of a long table in Valcrest, a girl with moths round her head had worked it out for a fox girl with a black sword. It’ll come up in the east over the red about an hour after sunset. Look up.

Yan counted.

Not with Kin. With his eyes. The way he’d counted on the first night out from Valcrest, round a fire, at the edge of the downs.

Five of Group 3. Aldous and Rhea. A man with a rod. A girl with a sword.

Nine, he’d thought then. We’re nine, going east. And one more waiting.

He looked at the knoll, small and black under the moon. A cairn of children’s stones. A white stone at its head. Four words in English on a black face. A tree. A ring of flowers.

Ten, he thought. Tomorrow, ten.

Round his neck, Suzu lifted her head, and looked at the moon with him for a long time, and put her head back down on his collarbone.

And down the cord, warmth. Not fed warmth. The other kind.

Yun.

“I know,” said Yan, very quietly, to her. “Tomorrow.”
