Chapter 233

Some Frames Are Empty

7,779 words · 34 min read

The first morning after they’d gone, Thea went down to the shingle before it was light and found the end of the jetty empty, and stood there for a while, getting used to it.

She’d known it would be. She’d stood at the top of the lake road the day before and watched ten people go over the first hill, and seen him stop at the top and lift his hand, and seen Gigichi, in the gap of the hedge below, lift hers back. She’d known all night that the jetty would be empty in the morning. Knowing a thing and seeing it were two different functions; she’d learned that in her first week here, standing in a hall in Solmere with a white box in front of her face, knowing perfectly well that it couldn’t be there.

The lamp was out. Somebody had lit it last night, Gigichi probably, and it had burned down and gone out on its own, and the glass was black with it. Thirty-four planks of grey wood going out over grey water into the mist. No rod. No hens. No man sitting on the last plank with his legs over the edge, not fishing, mostly.

She walked out to the end anyway. She didn’t know why. She sat down on the last plank where he sat, with her legs over the water, and it was cold through her skirt, and the mist moved on the lake, and she couldn’t see the far shore.

After a while she opened her window.

Ninety. Since the Lists. Since a night on the road north from the Lists, in the back of the miller’s cart, when she’d sat with her hand over Maren’s on a blue cord and felt Hana, a long way off on her own road, send on all seven at once, and something in her had moved, two points, the way a stuck drawer moves when you’ve been pulling it for a year and finally pull it the right way.

Ninety. And under it, grey, the line that had been there since the night she held a roof up from underneath for twenty heartbeats and bled all down her chin. Handshake. Locked. Requires Tier 3.

She knew what it was. That was the worst of it. She’d known for more than a year. She could have drawn it for anybody who asked, the whole shape of it, the way you can draw the shape of a function you haven’t written yet: two things that don’t know each other, meeting, and agreeing, and staying met. She’d done it once. Twenty heartbeats. And then it had come apart in her hands like wet paper, and she’d lain on the floor of the militia hall with Higu holding a handkerchief to her face, and the window had said locked.

You couldn’t hold what you couldn’t hold. You could understand it all you liked.

She closed the window and sat on the end of the jetty and waited for the mist to go off the far shore. It went. It always did. Trees. Hills. A sky going blue.

The end of the jetty was still empty.

She practised on the steps of the militia hall, the way she had every morning since the snow.

It was very simple. That was what made it hard. She sat on the second step from the top, with her back against the doorpost and her skirt tucked under her, and held her left hand out, palm up, and lit a candle on it.

Not a candle. A Candle. The spell, the first one she’d ever held steady, a year and a half ago, at a bonfire in the square at midwinter: a small flame on her palm the size of a thumb, a little bluer at the bottom than a real one, that didn’t burn her and didn’t flicker and didn’t need anything to burn on. Any Candle could hold one. Every Candle in the kingdom could hold one all day, Maren said, while they did the washing.

Thea could hold one all day while she did the washing.

Then she held her right hand out, palm down, over the step beside her, where she’d chalked a frame.

One frame. Frame seventeen, today; she went through them in order, one a day. A small ring, the size of a cup. A dense unique mark in the middle of it, like a knot drawn by somebody who’d never seen rope. Six lines going out from the ring, each ending in its own mark. And on the right of it, a short arc, curved like a fingernail. And on the arc, six small strokes. Ticks.

And opened Parse.

She’d been able to do that for two and a half years. Before Spark; before she knew what it was. You looked at a thing, and the thing had a shape, and the shape had parts, and the parts had names, and the names were in a language that wasn’t any language at all but was the closest thing she’d ever found to the one she’d spent her whole life in at home, sitting at a desk in the dark, reading other people’s code. Ring: circle, instance. Mark: reference, unique. Lines: six, outbound, terminated. She could read the frame. She’d been able to read the frame since the spring, when the copies came up the south road in a case on a boy’s back at three in the morning.

All of it. Except the ticks.

Ticks: six. [—]

Nothing. A bracket with nothing in it. The way a program tells you there’s something there it can’t show you. Not empty. Not null. Unreadable at this level. A thing written in a hand above her own.

She held both.

The flame on her left palm. Parse open on her right, on the frame, reaching for the ticks. Both at once. The flame steady. The reading steady. That was the whole of it. That was what a Lantern did, Maren said, without thinking: held a spell steady with one hand while it did something else with the other. A Lantern could hold a flame and read a book. A Lantern could hold a flame and talk to you. A Lantern could hold a flame and fall asleep.

“One,” said Higu.

She was on the top step, behind Thea, with her book open on her knees and her pencil. She’d sat there every morning since the snow. She didn’t look at Thea. She looked at the pencil.

“Two.”

The flame was steady. The reading was steady.

“Seven. Eight.”

Thea breathed. In through her nose. Out. The flame didn’t move. The frame on the step lay under her right hand in its chalk, and Parse lay on it like a hand on a page, and the ticks lay in their bracket, unreadable, and she reached for them.

“Fifteen.”

There. That was the thing. That was where it always went. She felt the flame lean. Not much. A hair. The way a candle leans when somebody opens a door in another room. Because she’d reached, with the right hand, for the thing she couldn’t read, and something in her had gone after it, and the left hand had felt it go.

“Twenty.”

Hold it. Not the reading. The flame. Hold the flame and let the reading do what it does.

“Twenty-five. Twenty-six.”

The flame steadied. She didn’t know how. She’d done it before; she still didn’t know how. It was like trying to remember how you’d made a bug go away when you’d changed three things at once.

“Thirty.”

Her nose was hot. She could feel it, the warm thick feeling high up behind her face that came before the blood. She knew it. She knew it the way you know the sound a hard drive makes before it goes.

“Thirty-four. Thirty-five.”

She reached for the ticks again. She couldn’t help it. They were there, under her hand, six of them, small, neat, a builder’s marks on a beam; and the bracket round them with nothing in it; and she knew. She knew. A boy with ink on both hands had stood in a long room in Solmere a month ago and said it out loud, in front of everybody, and then said he was sorry four times. They’re people going home. She knew what they meant. She’d known since she was a little girl, probably, the way you know what a tick means on any list anywhere in the world: done. That one’s done. That one got there.

Ticks: six. [—]

She couldn’t read them.

“Thirty-nine,” said Higu. “Forty.”

The flame went out.

Not leaned. Out. All at once, the way a candle goes out in a draught, and the place on her palm where it had been went cold, and the reading on her right hand went with it, and the bracket, and the frame, and the step came up and hit her in the side of the face, quite gently, because she was already sitting on it.

She lay there with her cheek on the cold stone and her nose bleeding onto the chalk.

“Forty,” said Higu again. And wrote it down. Then she closed the book and came down a step and put a handkerchief on Thea’s face, the way she always did, without saying anything. And sat there with her hand on the handkerchief while Thea breathed.

“Forty,” said Thea. Into the handkerchief. Thickly. “That’s the same.”

“It’s the same,” said Higu.

“It was forty in the spring.”

“It was forty-one once. On the eleventh day after the thaw.”

“That doesn’t count. I sneezed.”

“I wrote it down,” said Higu, “so it counts.”

She didn’t say what the ticks meant.

Not to anybody. Not to Gigichi, who asked her that evening, at the long table in the militia hall, very carefully, the way Gigichi asked things now, as if each question were an egg she was carrying in her bare hands across a yard. Not to Xola, who asked her the next morning loudly in the middle of breakfast with her mouth full, Thea what do the TICKS mean, and then went pink to the ends of her hair and said sorry, and then asked again. Not to PenguChi, who didn’t ask at all, and looked at her in a way that was worse than asking.

“Not yet,” Thea said. To all of them. The same way. “When I can read it.”

“Edwy said,” said Xola.

“I know what Edwy said.”

“He said they’re people going home.” Xola put her spoon down. Squiddles, in her shirt pocket, went a worried sort of grey-blue. “That the ticks are people. Going home. On the arc. And in the first thirty-seven frames there’s a tick for every line, and then there aren’t any, and on ours there aren’t any, and—“ She stopped. “And I don’t understand why you won’t say it, Thea. Everybody knows. Everybody in the channel knows. The Warden sat down on the floor.”

“I know.”

“So why—“

“Because I can’t read it,” said Thea.

The table was quiet.

She hadn’t meant to say it like that. She’d said it like a door shutting. She looked down at her bowl, Gigichi’s porridge, which was still terrible, which Gigichi had been making every morning for three years and getting no better at, on purpose, Thea was almost sure.

“Edwy guessed,” she said. More quietly. “He guessed right, probably. He’s very good. He looked at Oswin’s copy for five days and counted every tick and every line and he guessed, and then he said it out loud because he couldn’t not, and then he said sorry because he knew it was a guess. That’s why he said sorry.” She turned her spoon over. “I know it’s probably right. Everyone does. But I can read the ring, Xola. I can read the reference mark in the middle and the lines going out and where each one ends. I can read all of it except that one stroke. And that one stroke is the only bit that matters. And I can’t read it. It’s written above me.” She looked up. “If I say they’re people going home, and I’m wrong. If it means something else. If it means these ones died or these ones were used for something or these ones are still in the circle somewhere. If I say home to thirty-four people who’ve been waiting three years to hear it, and I’m wrong.”

Nobody said anything.

“I’m not saying it till I can read it,” said Thea. “That’s all.”

Xola looked at her for a long time across the table. Then she picked her spoon back up.

“Okay,” said Xola. Small. “Okay. Sorry.”

“Don’t be sorry. Edwy’s already been sorry enough for everybody.”

Higu, at the end of the table, didn’t say anything. She hadn’t asked, the whole time. She’d never asked. She ate her porridge, which she was the only one who liked, and wrote something in her book, and when Thea looked at her she looked back; and Thea knew, without having to see the page, that it was one word, and that the word was counted.

The frames were on the wall.

All fifty-six of them. Higu had chalked them in the spring, the week the copies came, on the long plastered wall at the end of the militia hall under her old map of the lake road; four to a row, fourteen rows, in her small careful hand, each one copied exactly from Oswin’s copy of the leaves, which were copied exactly from the leaves, which were three hundred years old and had been made by somebody nobody knew. Each one the size of a dinner plate.

Thea stood in front of them every night.

She didn’t try to read them. Not at night. She did her reading on the step in the morning, one frame, forty heartbeats, a handkerchief. At night she just stood and looked at them, the way you’d look at a page of code you’d written a long time ago and didn’t trust any more, not reading it, just letting your eyes go over it, waiting for the thing that’s wrong to come up out of it like a fish.

The first row. Frames one to four. A ring, a mark, lines out. Nine lines on the first one. Nine ticks on its arc.

Frame two. Four lines. Four ticks.

Frame three. Seven and seven. Frame four. Two and two.

Down the wall. Row after row. Eleven lines on frame twenty-two and eleven ticks, which had taken Higu all of one evening to copy because they were so close together. Every arc on the right of every ring, the same shape, the same small fingernail curve; the shape, Oswin had worked out on the floor of a long room in Solmere with a brass half-wheel and a handful of glass pins, of the blank right half of the fourteenth ring of the circle. The way home. Drawn smaller.

And on every arc, one tick for every line.

Until frame thirty-seven. Five lines. Five ticks.

And frame thirty-eight.

Three lines going out from the ring. Three marks where they ended. And the arc on the right, the same fingernail curve, exactly where it should be.

Nothing on it.

Thea had stood in front of frame thirty-eight more nights than she could count. It wasn’t anything. It was a small chalk ring on a wall with three lines coming out of it and an arc with nothing on it. It was the most frightening thing she’d ever seen.

Thirty-nine. Nothing. Forty. Nothing. Down the wall. Fourteen rows. Nineteen frames with lines and marks and arcs and nothing on the arcs at all. Frame fifty-five: two lines. Aldemar’s two. The two summoned in his reign, Oswin said; the king had read it in Aldemar’s own letter. Nothing on the arc.

And at the bottom of the wall, on the right, the last.

Frame fifty-six.

It didn’t look like the others. It looked like a dandelion clock. A small ring with thirty-five lines going out of it in every direction, so many that Higu had had to draw them as fine as she could make chalk go, and at the end of each line its own mark, thirty-five marks, so that the whole frame was a ring with a halo round it. And on the right, the arc.

Nothing on it.

That was them. Thirty-five lines. She’d found her own on the first night: the fourth line from the top, going out to the north-east, ending in a mark she didn’t need Parse to read because she’d felt it the moment she looked at it, in her stomach, the way you feel your own name said across a room. And Gigichi’s next to it. And Higu’s. And Xola’s, and PenguChi’s. The five of them, together, going out from the ring in a little fan, the way they’d come into the hall together, holding hands, in a heap.

It keeps itself, she’d worked out in the spring. The register keeps itself. Every time the circle runs, it adds a frame. Frame fifty-six hadn’t been on the leaves three hundred years ago. It had come when they did.

And on the arc, nothing.

She stood in front of it every night and didn’t say anything.

Bram came to her on the fourth morning.

She was on the step, after. Forty heartbeats again; she’d had forty-one, very nearly, and then a cart had gone past in the square and the flame had leaned at the noise and gone out. She was sitting with her head against the doorpost and Higu’s handkerchief on her face when he came up the square from the lake road with his sword on his hip and his boots wet to the shins, and stopped at the bottom of the steps.

He didn’t say anything for a while. He wasn’t a man who said things first. He’d been their guard for three years, the big grey militia man who’d come down the hall steps on the first day in Wrenhaven with his thumbs in his belt and told five girls from another world, quite kindly, that if any of them ran off into the hills he’d come and fetch them and he’d be very cross about it. He’d never had to fetch anybody. He’d sat with them in the hall through a winter, and walked behind them to Valcrest twice, and stood at the back of the room when the man from the cabin came up the lake road for supper and said it’s me. He’d never asked any of them for anything.

“You can see your bars,” said Bram.

Thea took the handkerchief off her face.

“In your windows,” said Bram. “All of you. The girls tell me. Gigichi’s at eighty-seven. You’re at ninety.” He looked at her. “To the next.”

“Yes.”

“Can you see mine?”

She looked at him. He was a big man, near fifty, grey all over, with a soldier’s hands and a broken nose that had set crooked a long time ago and a face that had never been good at hiding things. He was trying very hard now. It wasn’t working.

“No,” said Thea. “I’m sorry. We can’t. Not yours. Not anybody’s from here.” She thought about it. “Maren can see her own. The guides can. You can’t.”

“No,” said Bram. “Never have. Never thought I needed to.” He looked at the step, at the chalk frame on it, and away. “It’s the reeds.”

She waited.

“There’s a thing,” said Bram. “With the reeds. At the end of the shore. Since the ice went out. I’ve been—“ He stopped. He was going red, very slowly, from the collar up. “He saw it. Yesterday. He came along the shore and stood there and watched me do it, and didn’t say anything, and went.” He breathed out through his nose. “And I thought, he’d know. If anybody would. If it’s anything. And he didn’t say. So it’s either nothing or it’s—“ He stopped again. “And I thought, you’d know. You can see things. Numbers.”

“I can’t see yours,” said Thea.

“No.”

“Show me,” said Thea.

They went down to the end of the shore.

She sat on the stones, a little way back, with her knees up, and Higu sat beside her with the book. Bram went to the edge of the reeds where they stood up out of the shallows, tall and brown at the tops and green at the water, very still; and took his sword out; and stood.

She watched him for a long time before he moved. She’d learned that from the man too, without his ever teaching it: you watched the standing, not the move. Bram stood the way the old soldiers stood, the ones she’d seen on the wall at the Lists and on the road. Feet a bit too wide. Weight a bit too far forward. Like a man who’d been told hold a great many times by people who wanted him to hold a door. And then, as she watched, something changed. His weight went back. Onto his heels. Not much. The way the man’s went back on the end of the jetty when somebody said a thing before he’d had to.

He cut.

Slow. Level. A hand short of the nearest reed.

She saw the sheen come up along the steel, the grey-silver that every Gleaming sword in the kingdom had, that the girl’s had had on the sand at the Lists before it went silver all the way. She saw it go to the edge.

And past it.

A finger. Less. Off the steel into the air at the end of the stroke, a faint silver breath that wasn’t there and then was, and then wasn’t.

The reed bent.

Away from him. Its whole stem bowing, its brown head going over, as if somebody had blown on it from very close. Over. And back. And standing still, swaying a little.

Bram lowered the sword.

“That,” he said. Not turning round. “Two hundred and some times. Since the ice. It does that. It doesn’t cut. It goes over and it comes back.” He was quiet. “Thirty-six years I’ve had a sword. Since I was a boy in a militia coat too big for me. I’ve been Gleaming since twenty-four. You don’t go further. That’s what everybody says. Gleaming’s where you stop, unless you’re one of the ones who don’t. And I’m not.” He breathed out. “And then I sat on a stool on the ice all winter next to him, and something leaned. And now this.”

Thea didn’t say anything.

She was thinking about the jetty. About a man standing on the shingle a pace behind Bram and to the side, yesterday, where Bram could see him if he turned his head, watching a reed go over and come back, and not saying anything at all. She knew what he hadn’t said. She’d watched him not say things to people for two years. Here’s where it gets interesting. And walk away.

And she understood, sitting on the stones with her knees up and her nose still a little sore, why. Not because he was being clever. Because if he said it’s this, it would be his. It would be a thing Bram had been given. And Bram, who’d been a soldier thirty-six years and Gleaming for twenty-five of them and had stopped, would carry it the rest of his life like a coat somebody had lent him that didn’t quite fit.

So she didn’t say anything either.

Not it’s Reaching. She was nearly sure it was. She’d seen Petra’s on the bar at Duskhollow in John’s light, going a hand past the edge into a post; she’d seen it go past the edge at the Lists a hundred times. She knew what Reaching looked like from outside. She didn’t say it.

“I can’t see your bar,” she said instead. “And you can’t. But you don’t need a bar.”

Bram turned his head.

“It’s a distance,” said Thea. “Isn’t it. Whatever it is. It goes past the edge. Some days a finger. Some days less.” She looked at the reed, still swaying. “You can measure a distance.”

Bram looked at her for a long moment.

“Ysolde did it,” said Thea. “At Valcrest. Lucan’s guard. She told us at the Lists. She’s been doing it since the winter before last: every week, the same cut, into the same oak post, and a chalk line where it stops. A hand. Then a hand and a finger. Then a hand and two fingers. She’s got a book.” She hesitated. “She said, I thought it was a number. It’s a distance. You can measure a distance.”

“A post,” said Bram slowly.

“A reed’s better,” said Higu.

They both looked at her. She hadn’t looked up from the book.

“A post doesn’t move,” said Higu. “You have to cut it to know. A reed bends. You can see how far. You can see it from here.” She turned a page. “Mark the stones. Where you stand. Then measure how far over it goes, every time, from the top. With something straight.”

She reached into the satchel at her feet, the one that went everywhere with her, with the book and the pencils and the chalk and a small brass compass that had been Oswin’s and the copies of the frames; and took out a ruler. A long one. Wood, with brass ends, a clerk’s ruler from Solmere, marked in the kingdom’s inches. And held it out to Bram without getting up.

Bram looked at it.

Then he came back up the stones, slowly, with his sword in one hand, and took it with the other. He turned it over. He held it up against the reeds, at arm’s length, with one eye shut, the way you’d hold up a thumb against a hill to see how far it was.

“Every time?” he said.

“Every time,” said Higu. “Write it down. Or tell me and I’ll write it down.” She looked up, at last. “You’ll see it move. Even when you can’t feel it move. That’s what it’s for.”

Bram stood there with a clerk’s ruler in one big hand and a sword in the other, at the edge of a lake, a soldier of thirty-six years, near fifty, Gleaming since twenty-four, and something happened to his face that Thea had never seen happen to it, not in three years. It went young.

“Right,” said Bram. Gruffly. And went back to the reeds.

They watched him mark the stones with his heel. Stand. Lean. Cut. The reed went over. He held the ruler up against it, one eye shut, before it came back.

“Two inches,” he called. “At the top. Bit more.”

Higu wrote it down.

He cut again. “Two. Two and a bit.” Again. “One and a half. Bloody thing.” Again. “Two and a half.”

Higu wrote it all down, in a new column on a new page, under a heading in English that Bram couldn’t read and that said, in her small neat hand, BRAM · REED · INCHES AT THE TOP.

They stayed until the sun was well up. Thirty-one cuts. Higu wrote them all. When they went back up the shingle Bram was still there, at the edge of the reeds, with the ruler in his belt next to his sword, cutting, and holding it up, and calling numbers to nobody.

“You didn’t tell him,” said Higu, on the stones.

“No,” said Thea.

“You sound like you wanted to.”

“I really wanted to,” said Thea, and Higu, very nearly, smiled.

Maren was at sixty-six.

She told Thea that night, at the cord, the way she told her every night now, as if it were the weather. They sat at the end of the long table in the militia hall after the others had gone up, with a lamp between them and the blue cord on the table under Maren’s hand, a plain knotted cord of blue wool with one white bead on it, that ran, if you could see it, which nobody could but Deston and Hana, three hundred miles south to a room in Solmere where a cat-eared girl sat on the floor every night with seven cords round her wrists and the other half of this one tied round her thumb.

“Sixty-six,” said Maren. “This morning.”

She said it the way she said everything. Small and exact. A guide’s voice; a woman who’d written a report a week for three years in a hand so small you needed a glass for it, and never once written anything that wasn’t so.

“That’s four since the spring,” said Thea.

“Five. It was sixty-one at the thaw. I didn’t tell you sixty-one; I thought it might go back.” Maren looked at the cord under her hand. “It didn’t go back.”

“Maren.”

“It said fifty-eight for seven years,” said Maren. Very quietly. “From when I was twenty-six. I’d look every morning. Fifty-eight. I stopped looking after a while, the way you stop looking at a clock that’s stopped. And then I started reading slowly with you last autumn, and it was sixty by midwinter. And sixty-two at the thaw, when the kettle went. And sixty-six this morning.” She took her hand off the cord, very carefully, and put it in her lap. “I don’t know what to do with it, Thea. I’m thirty-three. I’m a Lantern. I’ve been a Lantern since I was twenty. You’re not supposed to—“

“You keep saying that. You’re not supposed to.”

“Because you’re not.”

“Bram’s not supposed to either,” said Thea. “He’s down at the end of the shore with Higu’s ruler. He’ll be there till dark.”

Maren looked at her.

“Read me one,” said Thea. “Slowly.”

So Maren did. She put her hand back on the cord, and the cord went warm under it, the way it did every night at about this hour, when Hana began. And the first strip came through. Thea couldn’t see it; it came out in Maren’s head, in Maren’s script, the kingdom’s small tight letters, because the cords wrote in the hand of whoever was holding the other end; and Maren read it out loud.

Slowly. That was the thing. Every word, on its own, with a space after it. The way Thea had asked her to, the first night, last autumn, in this room. Say it out loud, and stop when you don’t know why a word’s there.

“From. Oswin.” A breath. “Edwy. Has. Asked. For.” She stopped. “Asked. That’s the right word. He did ask. He’d not have demanded; he never demands.” A breath. “For. The. Fourteenth. Leaf. Again. That’s again, not also; he’s asked before.” A breath. “He. Says. He. Wants. To. Count. Them. Once. More. Before. Before.” She stopped, longer. “Before. Before what. Oswin’s not said. That’s on purpose. He’s left it open. He means before the tenth. He means before tomorrow week, before the anniversary. He means he wants to count them again in case they change.”

She read it all. Seven strips, from seven cords, through Hana. Oswin and Edwy and the leaf. Aldous, at Cinderhold, who said only They are waiting. All five. The knoll is green to the riverbeds. Emrys, at Kingsferry, about a barge. Corvin, at Thornwall, about the tower, and the seven in the field, and a girl with glasses who’d stopped wearing them and kept them in her pocket. Garrick at Duskhollow, about nothing, the market, the river, the empty gravel bar. Lucan at Valcrest, about a wall with thirty-six gold boxes on it and a boy who sat under them every evening and wrote in a book.

She read every word. Slowly. Stopping when she didn’t know why a word was there. And somewhere in the middle of Corvin’s, without either of them noticing, the lamp on the table, which had been guttering for an hour because nobody had trimmed it, steadied.

Thea noticed afterwards. She didn’t say anything.

The lamp burned very straight and still all through Lucan’s, and Maren read it to the end, and took her hand off the cord, and sat back.

“It’s late,” said Maren.

“It is,” said Thea, and didn’t look at the lamp.

On the fifth night there was something in the channel that wasn’t text.

She was in bed. Up in the loft over the hall, the five of them in a row under the slope of the roof, the way they’d slept for three years; with her window open in front of her face in the dark, reading, the way all thirty-five of them read every night now, the way the man on the jetty had read it for two years without anybody knowing.

John had been writing every night. Since the first camp. Little lines, at the end of each day, from the road east.

[ 21:14 · John: day 4. hills. sheep. Declan carried the water again. all of it. ]
[ 21:14 · John: and he sat down and ate. with us. fourth night running. ]
[ 21:15 · PenguChi: Good. ]
[ 21:15 · John: trying something tonight. tell me if anybody sees anything. ]

And then nothing. For a long time. Thea lay there in the dark with the window open and watched the line sit at the bottom of the channel, not moving, and the time in the corner going on.

21:31.

And then.

She didn’t see it. That was the first thing. There was nothing to see; no new line, no words, the channel exactly as it had been. But Parse was always half open, these days; she’d stopped being able to close it all the way, the way you stop being able to stop reading the code on a sign once you’ve learned to read code. And Parse caught something.

It came in under the last line. Not a line. A thing in the channel that had the shape of a line, the place a line would go, and nothing in it that was words. The way a packet comes in that your program doesn’t know how to read. [malformed]. She’d have said corrupted, at home. It wasn’t corrupted. It was just the wrong kind of thing for the channel to be holding.

It was warm.

That was the only way she could say it, afterwards. Parse touched it and it was warm, and gold, if a thing you couldn’t see could be gold. It lasted about as long as it takes to say gold. Then it was gone, and the channel was a channel, and the bottom line said trying something tonight.

She sat up so fast she hit her head on the roof.

“What,” said Gigichi, beside her, from inside a blanket.

“Sorry. Sorry.” Thea held her head. She was already writing.

[ 21:32 · Althea: John. Something came through. Not text. ]
[ 21:32 · Althea: In the channel. Under your line. For about a second. It had a shape. It was warm. ]
[ 21:33 · Althea: What did you do. ]

A long pause.

[ 21:35 · John: held the square over the fire. the light. the camp. everyone round it. and opened my window under it and tried to put the square in. ]
[ 21:35 · John: like putting a photo in a chat. ]
[ 21:35 · John: it didn’t go. I thought. ]
[ 21:36 · John: it went? ]
[ 21:36 · Althea: Something went. Not the picture. I didn’t see a picture. Something. ]
[ 21:36 · Hanarisu: My cords went warm. All seven. Just then. I thought it was Thea. ]
[ 21:37 · Chris: logging. 21:31. ]
[ 21:37 · John: ok ]
[ 21:37 · John: ok ok ok ]
[ 21:38 · Malcolm: IF John gets excited THEN he holds it too long and passes out in the fire. I’m watching him. ]
[ 21:38 · John: I’m not excited ]
[ 21:38 · Rui Xiang: he’s so excited ]
[ 21:39 · John: tomorrow night. same time. tell me again. ]

She lay down again, slowly, with her head sore and her heart going.

“What,” said Gigichi again. Not cross. From inside the blanket.

“John’s trying to put his light in the channel,” said Thea.

There was a silence from the blanket.

“Can he?”

“No,” said Thea. “Not yet. Something went through. A shape. Not the picture.” She looked at the roof, at the beam a hand above her face. “It was warm.”

The blanket thought about this.

“You sound like you did,” said Gigichi, “when you found the chat. The first spring. In the hall. You sat up like that then. You hit your head then too.”

“I didn’t hit my head.”

“You hit it on the stair,” said Gigichi. “You had a lump for a week.” A pause. “It’s nice. You sounding like that. You haven’t for a while.”

Thea didn’t say anything to that. She lay in the dark. On her other side, Higu was asleep, very straight, with her hands folded on her book. Beyond her Xola, all hair, and PenguChi beyond that, and somewhere at the foot of the row the penguin, making the small contented noise it made in its sleep.

Gigichi’s hand came out of the blanket in the dark and found Thea’s wrist, and held it, the way it used to on Earth, a long time ago, before they’d drifted, when they used to fall asleep on a call together at four in the morning and wake up with the call still going.

Thea let her.

On the sixth night she tried to read the last frame.

Not the chalk. The real one.

She’d never told anyone she could. She wasn’t sure she could. It had only come to her in the spring, standing in front of the wall the night the copies came, with Parse half open, the way it always was: that the register kept itself. That every time the circle ran, it added a frame. That frame fifty-six hadn’t been on the leaves three hundred years ago and was on them now, in the castle at Solmere, on a page nobody had written on.

So the register wasn’t the leaves. The leaves were a copy. Like Oswin’s sheets were a copy, and Higu’s chalk was a copy. The register itself was somewhere else. Somewhere that could write on a three-hundred-year-old page by itself when thirty-five people fell out of their rooms onto a stone floor.

In the circle. In the binding. In the thing she’d been reading, a little at a time, for two and a half years, every night, from inside the channel. The channel lived inside the binding; she’d found that out in the first winter. She’d built a chat window in the corner of somebody else’s three-hundred-year-old program, and it had held, and she’d never once looked at the rest of the program she’d built it in.

She looked now.

She lay in the dark in the loft with her eyes shut and her window closed and Parse open, all the way, the way she never opened it, because it was like opening every door in a house at once and the wind came through. And she went down. Out of the channel. Into the binding round it. Into the fourteen rings, the thirteen full and the fourteenth with its right half blank, that she’d read on the floor of a room in Solmere on Oswin’s ten-foot linen; except that this wasn’t linen, this was the thing itself, and it was enormous, and it was still running, and she was inside it.

She found the register at the back of it. Where you’d keep a log.

She didn’t know how she knew it was the register. It was a list. A long one. Entries. Each one the shape of a frame: a ring, a reference, lines out, an arc. Fifty-six of them. She went down the list the way she’d gone down Higu’s wall, one at a time. One. Two. Three. Each one there, in the binding’s own hand, the hand the leaves were copied from.

She could read the rings. The references. The lines.

She came to the ticks on frame one and reached for them.

They weren’t strokes. Not here. On the chalk and the linen and the leaves they were little strokes, a builder’s marks. Here they were something else. Something with a shape inside it, like the shape inside a seed, folded very small, that would be something bigger if it opened.

[—]

She reached anyway.

And it swam.

The whole frame. Not just the ticks. The ring and the reference and the lines and the arc, all of it, sliding sideways under her the way a page slides when you’re too tired to read it, the letters going off the lines and back. She held on. She held on to the ring, which she could read, and reached for the tick, which she couldn’t, and the ring slid out from under her hand, and she was holding nothing.

She came up out of it gasping, in the dark, with her nose bleeding onto Gigichi’s blanket.

“Thea.” Gigichi was sitting up. “Thea. Thea.”

“I’m all right.”

“You’re bleeding.”

“I know. I’m all right.” She was. She was shaking. She sat up, carefully this time, with her hand under her nose, and Gigichi was already feeling for the handkerchief that Higu kept by the bed for exactly this. “I’m all right, Gigi. I just— I went too far down.”

Higu was awake. Of course she was. She didn’t say anything. She held out the handkerchief.

Thea took it. She sat there in the dark in the loft with it pressed to her face and her other hand flat on the blanket, and Gigichi’s hand on her back, and Higu’s eyes on her, steady, the way they were when she was counting.

“It’s there,” said Thea. Through the handkerchief. “The real one. Not the leaves. The register. It’s in the binding. At the back. All fifty-six.” She breathed. “The ticks aren’t strokes. They’re something folded. I can read everything but them, and when I reach for them the whole frame swims. Like it’s written for somebody with—“ She stopped. “Better eyes.”

“Than a Candle,” said Higu.

“Than a Candle. Than a Lantern, maybe. I don’t know. Than one person.” She took the handkerchief away and looked at it in the dark and couldn’t see anything. “It’s above me. It’s not locked. It’s not like Handshake. It’s just written higher than I can reach on my own.”

Higu didn’t say anything for a while.

Then she opened her book on her knees, in the dark, and wrote something by touch, the way she could, in the small neat hand she could make without seeing it. Thea didn’t ask what. She knew it would be the time.

She went down to the wall before it was light.

She didn’t know why. She wasn’t going to read anything. She went down the loft ladder in her stockings with a shawl round her and the handkerchief still in her hand, and through the long dark hall, past the table, to the end, where the wall was; and stood in front of it in the grey that was just coming through the high windows.

Fifty-six frames in chalk. Four to a row. Fourteen rows.

Thirty-seven of them with a tick on the arc for every line out.

Nineteen of them with nothing.

And at the bottom, a dandelion clock with thirty-five lines, and nothing.

She stood there a long time.

Higu came down after a while. She didn’t say anything. She stood beside Thea in front of the wall with her book under her arm, in the grey, and they looked at it together, the way they’d looked at it every night since the spring.

“Can you read it?” said Higu.

“No.”

“Then don’t.”

“I’m not.” Thea pulled the shawl tighter. “I’m not saying it. I said I wouldn’t.” She looked at frame thirty-eight. Three lines. An arc. “I can say what’s there, though. I can say what I can read.”

Higu waited.

“Some frames are empty,” said Thea.

That was all. She didn’t say nobody’s gone home since. She didn’t say home. She said what she could read: rings, and lines, and arcs, and, on some of the arcs, nothing. It was true. She could stand behind every word of it.

Higu wrote it down.

Then she turned to a new page, and wrote something else under it, small, and showed it to Thea, which she almost never did. In the grey light Thea could just make it out.

TODO: eyes.

Thea looked at it for a long time.

It was her word. TODO. She’d written it on Oswin’s copy of the circle last summer, in a room in Valcrest, beside the blank half of the fourteenth ring, because she hadn’t known what else to write next to a thing that was meant to be there and wasn’t yet. It had gone round the whole channel. Chris had it at the top of his log. It was the closest thing the thirty-five had to a prayer.

Eyes. Higu hadn’t been told. She’d been in the loft. She’d heard Thea say better eyes, and than one person, and written it down, and sat on it all night, and turned it into a thing you could do.

“More than one,” said Higu. “You said.”

“I said maybe.”

“You said than one person.” Higu closed the book. “So it’s more than one. When you need it.” She looked at the wall. “We’ve got thirty-five.”

Thea didn’t say anything.

Outside, in the square, a cart went by, early, the baker’s; and somewhere down the lake road at the end of the shore, very faint, a man called a number to nobody, and then another, and then a short word old soldiers say. And up the stairs, from the loft, Xola’s voice, sleepy and enormous, asking where everyone had gone.

Thea stood in front of the frames with her best friend’s handkerchief in one hand and a shawl in the other, and looked at the empty arcs, and for the first time since the spring, didn’t feel quite so much on her own in front of them.
