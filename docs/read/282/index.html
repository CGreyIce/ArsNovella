Between

“Again,” said Jared.

Milli hit him again.

The castle yard at first light, in the frost, with the walls going up grey on four sides and the kitchen chimneys smoking and nobody about yet but a boy with a barrow and two of the castle cats on the well-head, watching. Milli had the ash practice sword. Jared had nothing. He stood in the middle of the yard on the packed earth with his hands open at his sides and his feet where Stand put them, a little apart, the weight even, and watched the sword come; and drew everything he had into his chest to meet it, all of it, the whole of his aura, the way you’d bring every man in a fort to the one wall where the ladders were going up.

It landed on his ribs, left side, low.

It hurt. It hurt the way it had hurt every morning since the autumn: more than it should. The shock of it went into his braced chest and had nowhere to go, because everything was braced, and it rang round the inside of him like a stone in a bucket, and his breath went out of him, and he went down on one knee on the frozen earth with his hand on his side.

“That’s thirty,” said Milli. “For today.”

“Again.”

“No.” Milli lowered the sword. He stood over him in the cold with his breath smoking and his tail lashing once behind him, the way it did when he was angry and trying not to be. “Jared. That’s thirty. You went down on twenty-two of them. It’s not working. It’s not going to work. You’re making it worse.”

“I know.”

“Then why are we—“

“Because I don’t know what else to do,” said Jared.

He said it calmly. He said most things calmly. That was the trouble, he’d thought, the last few weeks; he’d been calm about it for so long that nobody had noticed he was stuck, including him. He stayed down on one knee and breathed until his ribs let him, and looked at the frost.

Take a blow that should hurt, Kestra had said. On purpose. And have it not.

She’d said it in this yard, two summers ago, before she went west with the king. She’d stood exactly where Milli was standing with a wooden sword in her hand and hit him across the shoulder hard enough to put him in the dirt, and stood over him, and said it. That’s the second tier. That’s all it is. Everybody thinks it’s hard. It’s the easiest thing there is, and nobody can do it. And then she’d looked at him for a long time with her terse unreadable face and said: You’ll do it when you’re stood in front of something that matters more than your face. Not before.

He’d been past ninety-five since the anniversary. Since the morning of the tenth, when Chris had posted the list in the channel at dawn: Hako, Milli, Jared, Gabe: all past 95. Nearly seven months ago. He’d been ninety-seven at midwinter. He was ninety-eight now. He’d been ninety-eight for a month.

“Okay,” he said. “Okay. That’s thirty.”

Milli put his hand down. Jared took it, and came up.

The theory had been simple. That was why he’d liked it.

Stand was where your feet were. He’d found that in the Lists, two summers ago, on the main sand, in the bout against Chris; that if he put his feet in the right place and left them there, he could take anything that came at him and send it back, because the ground was doing it and not him. The window had named it. He’d been proud of it, quietly, the way he was proud of most things.

So the second tier would be the body. That was the theory. The feet were the ground; the body was the wall. Draw the aura into the body and hold it there, all of it, hard, the whole of yourself made into something a sword couldn’t get through, and when the blow came, it wouldn’t.

Cursiffe had written it down for him, on the back of a page of Oswin’s, in his small careful English, in the autumn. Jared’s theory: bring everything to the wall. And under it, a month later, in the same hand: Jared’s theory: still bring everything to the wall. And under that, at midwinter, the morning after Milli had cracked two of his ribs on the left side and Aleaka had sat with him in the infirmary with her vixen on her knees: It hurts more braced. Why?

Nobody knew why.

“Because you’re carrying it,” Aleaka had said, that night, in the infirmary. She’d had her hand flat on the blanket over his chest, not touching him, an inch off, the way she felt what people carried. “When it’s coming. You pick it up before it gets there. All of it, all at once, every bit of you. Like somebody holding a door shut against a crowd that hasn’t come yet.” She’d taken her hand away. “And then it comes, and you’re already tired.”

He’d thought about that all winter.

He was thinking about it in the castle yard at first light, with his ribs aching, when the cords came down from the west tower.

It was Cursiffe. He came across the yard at a walk, which for Cursiffe was a run, with a strip of linen in his hand and ink on his fingers and the expression he wore when he’d read something he’d have to read again.

“Hana’s had a cord from Thornwall,” he said. “The king. Overnight.” He held the strip out. “And one from Cinderhold, before that, from Aldous. Hana’s copied them both for the Council. The king says send it as it stands.”

Jared read it standing in the yard.

The fog. The east wall at Cinderhold, in the dead of winter. Eight of the big ones and forty scouts. Trooper Ennis on the north stretch. And at dawn, round the grave on the knoll, forty people standing in a ring, facing in, with nobody inside them, and the big ones lying among them like dogs. Both together, on the red, in the open. Rhea saw it.

And under it, in a different hand, the king’s: Send it to the Council as it stands, every word, before anybody tells them what it means. I’ll be home at the thaw.

“They’re going to be scared,” said Milli. He was reading over Jared’s shoulder.

“Yeah.”

“Of Kharvos?”

“Of Kharvos,” said Jared. He folded the strip and gave it back to Cursiffe. “And of whatever’s holding Kharvos’s hand. And they can’t see that. They can’t put a name to it, or a map, or a number.” He looked up at the west tower, where Hana’s window was, at the top, with a light still in it from the night. “So they’ll be scared of the thing they can see.”

Milli looked at him. Then up at the tower. His tail went still.

“Honey,” he said.

“Yeah,” said Jared.

The Council met two days later, at the top of the old keep.

Jared had been in the room four times in three and a half years. Always in the gallery, which was a narrow wooden balcony along the back wall, up a little stair, where people who weren’t the Council could sit and not speak. The Council sat below on the dais in a half-ring of tall chairs: twelve lords and officers of the kingdom, and a thirteenth chair in the middle, empty, with no crown on it, because the king didn’t wear one, and wasn’t in it, and had been at Thornwall since the summer.

Halvard sat at its right hand. Thin and grey and very still, with his hands folded on the table in front of him. Jared had never been able to read him. Aleaka could; she said he carried more than anybody in the castle and had for twenty years, and that most of it was one thing, and she didn’t know what. Jared couldn’t see it. He saw a thin grey man who’d taken thirty-five people out of their lives without asking and said yes when Bee said he’d kidnapped them, and who’d looked, for the whole of the briefing, like a man at a funeral.

Hana sat in the gallery with the rest of them. Front row, at the end, nearest the stair. She had the cords on her, the way she always had them now: a knot of them at her belt, every colour, one for every guide and every group and a dozen more, and two on each wrist, and the grey-and-blue one round her neck that went to Thornwall. She never took them off. Jared had asked once, in the first year, and she’d said they go cold, and that had been all.

The Chancellor read the letters out. Aldous’s first. Then the king’s.

Jared watched the room hear them.

He’d seen a room go like that before. In the summoning hall, on the first day, when the soldiers round the walls had seen Winter’s horns and Ina’s face and reached for their swords. It didn’t happen all at once. It went round. One face, and then the next, and the next, like a draught going round a table and putting out the candles one at a time. The Marshal first, who’d been on the Redmarch. Then the lords of the provinces. Then the Treasurer, soft and careful, going the colour of milk.

People, he heard somebody say, under their breath. Standing round a grave. Walking with them.

And then Lord Vane stood up.

He didn’t say alliance. That was the clever thing. Jared would think about it afterwards, a lot: that Vane had stood up into a room that had just been told the kingdom’s oldest enemy had found a friend, and hadn’t said a single word about the enemy or the friend at all.

“My lords,” said Vane. “Majesty’s chair.” He bowed to the empty chair, very correctly. A tall man with grey at the temples and a voice like a good cloak. “We have just heard two letters read. One from a chaplain on the Redmarch, a good man. One from the king.” He turned, a little, so that he was facing down the room. “I should like to ask how they came to us.”

Nobody said anything.

“I don’t mean the roads. The roads are shut; the roads have been shut for two months; a rider from Cinderhold is a fortnight. These letters came in a night.” Vane let that sit. “They came down a piece of string. And before they came to us, they went through the hands of one woman.” He didn’t look up at the gallery. He didn’t need to. “Every word the chaplain wrote. Every word the king wrote. Every word that every guide in this kingdom has written to every other for a year and more. All of it, through one pair of hands.” He paused. “Hands we have never once been permitted to look at.”

In the gallery, Hana didn’t move.

“I make no accusation,” said Vane. “I have none to make. I’m sure the lady is everything Majesty believes her to be.” He turned back to the table. “But I have read the reports this Council receives, as you all have, and I know what the lady’s path is. Her own window says it. Charm. Not trinkets. Charm. The kind that goes into a man’s head and makes him want what you want.” He let his voice go quieter. “We have just been told that something is walking about on the Redmarch that makes people stand still in a ring and look at a grave. That has nobody inside it. That can walk people out of their houses.” He spread his hands. “And we keep the whole of the kingdom’s word in the hands of a woman whose gift is getting inside heads.”

It went round the room again. Faster.

“Two motions,” said Vane. “Plain ones. First, that the summoned, all thirty-five, be brought to Solmere at the thaw. Under the Crown. Where they can be protected, and counted, and kept safe from whatever this is.” He bowed to Halvard, very slightly. “The Advisor’s guides have done well with them. But a kingdom at war does not leave its best weapons in seven corners of the map.”

“And the second,” said the Chancellor.

“That the cords be held by the Crown,” said Vane. “By the Chancellor’s own clerks, in the Chancellor’s own tower. Read by Aurelan eyes. Until Majesty comes home to decide otherwise.” He sat down. “That’s all. For the lady’s protection as much as ours. If there’s nothing in her hands, there’s nothing to fear in giving them up.”

They voted on the second one first.

Jared didn’t follow all of it. He’d never followed Councils; he’d watched them, the way you’d watch weather. He saw Halvard speak, once, short, the cords are not a box of letters, my lords; they’re hers; she made them; they go cold in any other hands; and he saw the room hear hers and go colder. He saw the Warden of the Summoners’ Hall, very old, very upright, say something about the king, and the Chancellor say something about the king being at Thornwall, and the Marshal say that a kingdom at war could not wait for the thaw to know who was reading its letters. He saw the Treasurer, white as milk, put up his hand.

Seven hands. Five not.

“Carried,” said the Chancellor. He didn’t look happy about it. “The Council will hold the cords for safekeeping until Majesty’s return. Lady Hanarisu, you’ll be so good as to come down and give them to the sergeant.”

The sergeant-at-arms came out from the door at the side of the dais. He was a big grey man of about sixty in the Council’s dark livery, with a staff of office the height of his shoulder: black wood, bound with brass, with an iron shoe on the foot of it. He’d been at that door every time Jared had been in the room. He looked up at the gallery with no expression on his face at all, the way a man looks who’s been told to fetch something and is going to fetch it.

Hana stood up.

She stood at the top of the gallery stair with her hands at her sides and the cords at her belt and her wrists and her throat, and looked down at the room. Jared watched her face. He knew it better than almost anybody’s in two worlds. He’d known it since he was twenty-one: on a stream, at two in the morning, laughing at something a funny boy half a world away had typed into her chat. He knew what it did when she was hurt and what it did when she was angry. It wasn’t doing either.

It was doing the third thing. The one he’d only seen twice. The calm one.

He felt it before he saw it. They all did, in the gallery; Milli’s hand closed on the rail beside him. It came off her the way heat comes off a stone in the evening: very gentle, very warm, very slow. Charm. Not the trinket kind. The other one. The one her window had named in the hall on the first day and she’d spent three and a half years pushing down into the safe half of herself where the cords were.

She was letting it up.

Not all the way. Not yet. Just to the edge of her, the way you’d let a dog to the end of its rope. And Jared saw what she’d do with it, all at once, the way you see a cup going off the edge of a table before it’s fallen. She’d go down that stair. Into a room that was frightened of her because she could get inside heads. And she’d get inside them. All twelve. Gently. One at a time, an hour each, she’d said once, but she was stronger now, much stronger; she was Lantern; she was the middle of the wheel. She’d walk down that stair and across that floor and every frightened man on that dais would stop being frightened, and want to let her keep her cords, and vote again, and she’d go back up the stair with her cords at her belt.

And tomorrow every one of them would wake up and know.

For the lady’s protection as much as ours. Vane hadn’t needed to say what he was really asking for. He’d built a room where the only way to keep her cords was to prove him right.

Hana took a step down.

Jared was on the stair before he’d decided anything.

He went down past her. He didn’t touch her; he went down the outside of the stair, against the wall, and past her, two steps below, and stopped, and turned round. So that he was between her and the room. With his back to the Council and his face to her.

“Alice,” he said.

Quiet. Just to her. Nobody in the room knew the name; nobody in this world had ever said it except him. It had been a funny boy’s name for her, and then his.

She stopped.

The warmth came off her and stopped at him. He felt it. Like the edge of a fire stopping at your shins. Warm, and kind, and so gentle he wanted to lean into it; and he stood on the stair with it against him and didn’t.

“Don’t,” he said.

Her face did something. He couldn’t have said what.

“That’s what he wants,” said Jared. “You know it is. You do it, and you win, and they wake up tomorrow and they know, and they never trust anything you say again. Any of us.” He kept his voice low, and level, the way he kept it in the Lists. “Put it down. I got it.”

“You don’t,” said Hana. Very quiet. “Jared. They’re taking the cords.”

“I know.”

“They go cold. They’ll go cold in his tower in a week. Every guide. Thornwall.” Her hand had gone to the grey-and-blue one at her throat. “I’ll have nobody.”

“You’ll have me,” said Jared. “On the stair. That’s step one.”

He turned round.

The room was looking at him. All of it. Twelve lords on a dais and a thirteenth chair, empty; Halvard, very still; the Chancellor with his papers; the Warden with his hands on his staff; Vane in his chair, with his handsome face showing nothing at all. And at the foot of the stair, three steps down, the sergeant-at-arms with his staff.

“Sir,” said the sergeant. Not unkindly. “Stand aside.”

“No,” said Jared.

He said it the way he’d said alright, what’s step one in the summoning hall, the first day. Calmly. Like a man telling you the time.

“Council’s order, sir. I’ve to fetch the cords from the lady.” The sergeant came up a step. “Stand aside, please.”

“She’s not coming down,” said Jared. “And you’re not going up.” He didn’t raise his voice. “I’m sorry. I know it’s not your vote. I know you’ve been told to. But I’m going to stand here, and you’re going to have to move me.”

“Sir—“

“Move me,” said Jared. “Or don’t. But I’m not going anywhere.”

The sergeant looked up at him for a long time from two steps down. Then, past him, at the dais. At the Chancellor, who didn’t say anything; at Vane, who said, very quietly, the Council has given an order, sergeant.

The sergeant sighed. It was a big man’s sigh, an old soldier’s, a sigh that had moved a great many people out of a great many doorways in its time and didn’t enjoy it.

“Sorry, lad,” he said.

And brought the staff up.

Jared saw it come.

He always saw them come. That was the thing; that had always been the thing, since the first day in the yard with Kestra. He was perceptive. He saw a blow start in the shoulder before it started in the arm. He saw the sergeant shift his grip on the black staff, the right hand sliding down to the brass, the left coming up near the iron shoe; and he knew what it was going to be before the man knew he’d decided. The old way. Not a swing. The butt of the staff, short and straight and hard, into the breastbone. The way the Watch moved a man who wouldn’t be moved. It knocked the wind out of you and sat you down, and you didn’t get up for a while, and it didn’t leave a mark.

It was going to land right in the middle of his chest. On the bone. A hand below his collar.

And everything in him came up to meet it.

He felt it happen. The way it had happened thirty times a morning all winter. All of it, the whole of his aura, rising up out of his feet and his legs and his belly and his back into his chest, every bit of him, to make a wall there; bracing; picking it up before it got there, all of it, all at once, like a man holding a door against a crowd that hadn’t come yet.

And behind him, on the stair, he felt Hana. He felt the warmth come off her again, a little, at his back, frightened. And in front of him, two steps down, he felt the sergeant, who didn’t want to do this, and was going to. And past the sergeant, the room, twelve frightened men in tall chairs. And he was on the stair, between all of it, with the staff coming.

He didn’t have to hold all of it. That was what he saw.

It came to him all at once, between one heartbeat and the next, with the iron shoe of the staff coming up off the step toward his chest. He’d been bringing every man in the fort to the one wall. Every morning. Every blow. Bracing the whole of himself, his ribs and his back and his arms and his legs, against something that was only ever going to land in one place, the size of a hand. And everywhere else he’d braced was just tired, and stiff, and rang like a bucket.

The staff was only going to touch him in one place. He knew where. He’d always known where. He just had to be there. In the place where the iron met the bone, for the length of time it took to meet it, and nowhere else. Not a wall. A hand’s breadth of himself, between the blow and the rest of him, at exactly the moment.

He let the rest go.

All of it. Out of his back and his arms and his legs and his ribs, like letting a held breath out; down, into the stair, into his feet, into Stand, where it belonged. He let himself go loose all over, standing on the step, the way you’d stand on a boat. Everything loose. Everything but one place.

And at the last moment, as the iron shoe came in, he put himself there. A hand’s width of aura in the middle of his chest, on the bone, exactly where it was going to land, exactly when. Between.

The staff hit him.

It made a sound like a mallet on a post.

He heard it go round the room. A flat hard tock, wood and iron on something that didn’t give. The whole weight of a big man of sixty behind it, short and straight, the way it had been done a thousand times in a thousand doorways; and it hit him in the middle of the chest, on the breastbone, a hand below his collar, and stopped.

He didn’t move.

Not a step. Not an inch. His feet stayed where Stand had put them on the stone of the stair, and the rest of him stood loose above them, and the blow came in through the iron shoe and the hand’s breadth of him that was there to meet it and went down, through him, loose, into his feet, into the stair, into the old keep, and was gone. Like water going down a drain.

It didn’t hurt.

He waited for it to. He’d been hit thirty times a morning all winter and every time it had hurt more than it should. He stood on the stair with the iron shoe of the staff against his breastbone and waited for the pain to come up out of the place where it had landed, and it didn’t come. There was a pressure, where the iron was. Like a thumb. That was all.

The sergeant looked at him over the staff.

He was a big grey man who’d been moving people out of doorways for forty years, and his face had gone blank with surprise, all at once, like a child’s. He looked at his staff. At the iron shoe of it, against Jared’s chest. He took it away. There was a little ring of dust on Jared’s coat, where it had been. Nothing else.

“Lad,” said the sergeant.

“I know,” said Jared. His voice came out perfectly level. “I’m sorry. I’m still not moving.”

The sergeant looked at him for a long time. Then he lowered the staff, slowly, until the iron shoe rested on the step, and turned round, and looked up at the dais. At the Chancellor. Not at Vane.

“My lord,” he said. “I can’t move him. Not without I hurt him. And I’ve just tried that.” He stood there with his staff. “You’ll want to give me another order, I think. Or someone else.”

It was very quiet in the room.

Jared stood on the stair. He didn’t turn round to look at Hana. He didn’t need to; he could feel her, at his back, two steps up. The warmth had gone. All of it. She’d put it down. He felt it go back into her, slowly, the way the heat goes back into a stone at night, down into the safe half where the cords were; and she stood behind him on the stair with her hands at her sides and didn’t do anything at all.

Nobody on the dais said anything.

He looked at them. At twelve frightened men in tall chairs. He’d never spoken to a Council in his life. He’d never wanted to; he’d watched them, like weather. But he was standing on the stair now with the whole room looking at him, and the sergeant waiting, and he thought he’d better say something, because otherwise somebody else would, and it’d be Vane.

“My lords,” he said. “I’m Jared. I’m one of the thirty-five.”

They waited.

“You’re scared,” said Jared. “That’s fair. I read the letter. I’d be scared too. Something on the Redmarch with nobody in it, walking with Kharvos. That’s scary.” He kept his voice level. Like a friend, across a table. “But you can’t see that. You can’t put it on a map. So you’re scared of her instead. Because you can see her. She’s right here.” He didn’t look round. “I get it. I do. But she just stood on that stair with a gift that could’ve had every one of you voting the other way by suppertime, and she didn’t use it. You all felt it. I know you did. It was right there.” He looked at Vane. “And she put it down. In front of all of you. And let a man come up the stair with a stick.”

Nobody said anything.

“That’s what’s in her hands, my lords,” said Jared. “That. You want to know if she’s safe, that’s your answer. You just watched it.” He took a breath. “So. Here’s step one. You let her keep her cords till the king gets home. You read every word that comes down them, if you want. Out loud, in this room, every day. She’ll read them to you herself. She’s been doing it all winter.” He shrugged, very slightly. “And when the king gets home, you ask him. That’s all. That’s the whole plan. It’s not clever.”

He stopped. He didn’t know what else to say. He’d said it.

It was the Warden who moved.

The very old man, at the end of the dais, with his hands on his staff. He’d stood on the third ring at the Great Summoning and believed every moment of it that he was doing a good and necessary thing. He looked down the room at Jared for a long time with his old sincere face. Then he looked up at the gallery, past him, at Hana.

“I felt it,” said the Warden. “Lady. On the stair. I felt it come and I felt it go.” He was quiet. “I have been in this Council forty years. I have never once seen anybody in this room hold a weapon like that and put it down.” He turned to the Chancellor. “I’ll change my hand, Chancellor. If the Council will let me. I’d like to wait for the king.”

The Treasurer put his hand up too. Very small. White as milk.

And then the Marshal, who’d been on the Redmarch. And one of the lords of the provinces, a thin one at the end who Jared had never heard say anything at all.

Seven hands, five not, had been the vote. It wasn’t now.

“The Council,” said the Chancellor, after a moment, slowly, in the voice of a man who’d been handed a way out of a corridor and meant to take it, “will wait for Majesty’s return. On both motions.” He looked at the sergeant. “Thank you, sergeant. That’ll be all.”

The sergeant went back to his door. On the way, he looked up at Jared once, on the stair, and something happened at the corner of his mouth under the grey moustache that might, on a less tired man, have been a smile.

Vane hadn’t moved. He sat in his tall chair with his handsome face showing nothing at all. Then, as the room began to break up, as the lords stood and the clerks gathered their papers, he looked up at Jared on the stair, and inclined his head, very slightly. The way you’d nod across a market to a man who’d got the better of you on a price you meant to have back.

“I’ll remember it,” said Lord Vane.

He’d said it to Hana once, three and a half years ago, in this room. Jared knew; she’d told them.

“Yeah,” said Jared. “Me too.”

He didn’t open his window until they were back in the west tower, in Hana’s room, with the door shut and everybody in it.

He sat on the floor by the door with his back against it, the way he always sat. Milli on the bed with his tail lashing. Aleaka in the window seat with the vixen on her feet and Brisk on her shoulder glaring at the room. Cursiffe at the little table with his pen. Hana on the end of the bed with her hands in her lap and the cords at her belt and her wrists and her throat, all of them, still warm.

He opened it.

[ Jared · Tier 2: Tempered · Skills: Stand, Between ]

He’d known. He’d known on the stair, with the iron shoe of the staff against his breastbone and the pain not coming. He’d known it the way you know you’ve caught something you thought you’d drop: not with your head, with your hands. He’d known the window would say something and he hadn’t known what, and he looked at it now and thought that it couldn’t have said anything else.

Between.

“Oh,” he said. Quietly. “Okay.”

“What,” said Milli. “What. Jared. What does it say.”

He read it out.

Milli stared at him. Then he lay down flat on his back on the bed with his arms out and his tail hanging off the end of it and said, to the ceiling, “Thirty times a morning. All winter. I hit you thirty times a morning, and it was a man with a stick.”

“It wasn’t the stick,” said Jared.

“Then what was it?”

He thought about it. He sat against the door and thought about how to say it, because he’d want to say it right, in the channel, for Chris, and for Gabe, who’d be next, and for Hako and Milli, who were stuck exactly where he’d been.

“I was holding the whole thing,” he said. “Every time. All of me. Like a wall.” He put his hand flat on his chest, on the bone, where the iron had been. “But it was only ever going to land here. So I let the rest go, and put myself here. Only here. Only when.” He took his hand away. “In between.”

Cursiffe was writing it down. Under It hurts more braced. Why?, in his small careful English: Because you only need to be where it lands.

Aleaka was looking at him from the window seat. She had her hand up, an inch off the air, the way she did.

“You’re not carrying it,” she said. Softly. “For the first time since I met you. You’re not carrying anything you don’t need to.” She put her hand down. “That’s nice. You should do that more.”

On the end of the bed, Hana hadn’t said anything.

She sat with her hands in her lap. He looked at her across the room. She looked back. Her face was doing something he didn’t have a name for; it had been doing it since the stair.

“You stood in front of me,” she said.

“Between,” said Jared. “Not in front.” He thought about it. “In front’s for things that’ll hurt you. They weren’t going to hurt you. They were scared of you. That’s different. You don’t stand in front of scared people.” He shrugged. “You stand in between. So nobody does anything stupid till everybody’s calmed down.”

“I was going to,” said Hana. “Do something stupid.”

“I know.”

“I was going to do all twelve.”

“I know, Alice.” He leaned his head back against the door. “That’s why I was on the stair.”

She looked at him for a long time. Then she got up off the end of the bed and came across the room and sat down on the floor beside him, with her back against the door too, close enough that he knew she was there. And put her head on his shoulder. And didn’t say anything.

Milli, on the bed, looked at the ceiling and said nothing either, which for Milli was a speech.

He posted it that night.

He closed his window.

He sat on the floor of Hana’s room with his back against the door and Hana’s head on his shoulder, asleep now, and her cords warm against his arm. His chest didn’t hurt. He put his hand on it, on the bone, a hand below the collar, where the iron had been. There’d be a bruise in the morning, probably. A small one. The size of a thumb.

He couldn’t do it all the time yet. He knew that already. He’d tried it once, alone, on the stair up to the tower, an hour ago, with Milli’s ash sword, and it had worked; and then he’d tried it again straight after and braced without meaning to and it had hurt as much as ever. Tomorrow it’d be one in three. Next week, maybe two. He’d have to learn it the way you learn anything. Thirty times a morning.

But he knew where it was now. The hand’s breadth. The moment.

Between.

Across the room, Milli was asleep on the bed with his arms still out and his tail hanging off the end. Aleaka was asleep in the window seat. Cursiffe was at the little table, not asleep, writing something in his small careful English that he’d probably show nobody. Out of the window, over the roofs of Solmere, the sky was going from black to grey in the east.

Somewhere a long way off in that direction there was a fort on the end of the world, and a knoll an hour east of it, and a black stone on a cairn with a ring round it that forty people couldn’t cross. And somewhere a long way the other way, a king at a banked forge, and a pass under four feet of snow, and the thaw coming.

And here, at the top of the west tower, a door, and him against it. Where he’d always sat. Between the room and whatever was outside it.

He’d never been able to do anything from half a world away. He’d thought about that a lot, in the first year. That was the thing he’d carried longest, longer than anything: a funny boy in Malaysia, and Jared on the other side of the world, and nothing he could have done.

He wasn’t half a world away now. He was right here. On the floor. By the door.

He let that go too. Down, into the floor. And went to sleep.
