Chapter 268

Three-Quarters

5,799 words · 25 min read

Alea lit the lamp at dusk.

She did it the way he did it. Thea had watched him do it, on and off, for three years, from the lake road or the end of the reeds or the cabin step, without ever quite meaning to watch; and she’d watched Alea do it every evening for twenty-seven evenings now, on purpose, from the same place on the jetty, a few planks back, where she wouldn’t be in the way. It was the same. Down the jetty in the last of the light with the tinder box in her coat. The lamp on its post at the very end, over the ice. Glass up. Wick trimmed with the good knife, a hair, not more. Flint. The small flame catching and standing up. Glass down. And then she stood there with her hand on the post and looked at it for as long as it took to know it would stay lit; and turned round, and came back up the jetty.

Twenty-seven evenings. She hadn’t missed one.

“You don’t have to come every night,” said Alea, at the top of the jetty, as she said every night.

“I know,” said Thea, as she said every night.

They stood there. The lake was white to the far shore, ice under snow, with the reeds at the north end standing up out of it brown and stiff and the hills going up behind into a sky going green at the edges. The cabin behind them, with smoke coming out of the chimney. The town up the slope, with its lamps coming on one at a time. And the one at the end of the jetty, small and yellow over the ice, that had been lit every night of Alea’s life and every night of her mother’s, and that nobody had ever asked who it was for.

Alea didn’t ask either. She stood with her hands in her coat and looked at it.

And Thea’s window opened.

She hadn’t opened it. It opened on its own, the way it did when John sent something; and she braced for it, standing on the jetty in the cold, the way she’d braced every dusk for fifteen nights, because every dusk for fifteen nights it had opened on its own at about this hour with something in it nobody should have to see. The rings walking. The lamps going out down the long street. A grey thing coming up out of black water. One heartbeat, and gone, and you stood there with it in your eyes.

It didn’t go.

It was a town in the snow, from high up. A tower parapet, close, at the bottom of the picture; beyond it, roofs going down a hill, white, with lamps coming on in the windows. A long street. A wall. An arch. And on the step inside the arch, in a row, small, in the snow: people. She knew them. She didn’t need to be able to see their faces; she knew them the way you’d know your own family across a station. Tiger, against a post. Bee. Kei with something pale round his neck. Drew, very straight. Two at the ends, women. And in the middle, an old man with a blanket on his knees.

And beside him, not close, a man with a rod.

Beyond them, through the arch: fields, white, going away north. A valley. And at the very top of the valley, very small, rings of dark specks on the white, and a smudge above them like a thumbprint.

It held.

She stood on the jetty and looked at it. One heartbeat. Five. Twenty. She waited for it to go out like breath on glass, and it didn’t. It lay there in her window the way a drawing lies on a wall. Steady. Not asking her anything.

“Thea,” said Alea.

Thea realised she’d stopped breathing. She started again.

“It’s John,” she said. “It’s a picture. From Thornwall. It’s holding.” She heard her own voice, odd and high. “It’s not going. It’s just there.”

Alea couldn’t see it. None of them could, the ones born here; they had windows of their own, but nobody born in this world had ever seen what came into a window from someone else’s. Alea stood on the top plank with her hands in her coat and looked at the air in front of Thea’s face, where Thea was looking, the way you’d look at the place somebody was pointing in the dark.

“What’s in it,” said Alea.

So Thea told her.

The roofs, and the snow, and the lamps coming on, not the order, just because it was getting dark. The arch. The step. Who was on it, in order, left to right. The king in the middle with a blanket on his knees, and his beard dark down the front. And next to him.

“He’s sitting down,” said Thea. “On the step. Next to the king. He’s got the rod across his knees.” She looked at it. “The line’s wound in. He’s looking north. At the valley.” She swallowed. “He’s got his old coat on. The brown one. The elbows.”

Alea didn’t say anything for a long time.

“Is he eating,” she said.

Thea looked. There was a pot on the step, at the king’s feet, with a cloth over it; and a cup on the stone beside the man with the rod, with no steam coming off it.

“There’s a pot. And a cup by him.”

“Is it full?”

“I can’t tell.”

“It’ll be cold,” said Alea. “He lets it go cold. He always does.” She looked at the lamp on the end of the jetty. Then back at the air in front of Thea’s face. “Is he all right.”

Thea looked at the man on the step. At his shoulders, and his hands on the rod, still, and his head turned north. She couldn’t see his face. She never had. Nobody under the sixth tier could, not on a jetty and not in a picture.

“He’s sitting down,” she said again. It was the only true thing she had. “He’s not doing anything. He’s just sitting there.”

Alea breathed out. It went on for a long time.

“Then he’s all right,” she said.

The circle was in it. All of it. Small, at the top of the picture, a thumbprint and some specks; but it was there, the way the whole of a town is there on a map the size of your hand. And it wasn’t going.

“You’re going to read it,” said Alea.

“Yes.”

“Here?”

Thea hadn’t thought about where. She thought about it now. Maren’s room over the militia hall, with the cord, and the chair, and the lamp that had swung when nobody touched it. She thought about the last time: six hundred and twelve heartbeats with her eyes gone and her mouth talking, and something at the far end of a line turning round and coming down it toward her, and Deston saying now would be good, and the cut; and three days in bed, and a nosebleed that wouldn’t stop, and Higu sitting on the end of the bed in the dark, drawing.

She didn’t want Maren’s room.

“Here,” said Thea. “If that’s all right. With the lamp.”

Alea looked at her for a moment. Then she nodded, once, the way he did, and went to put the kettle on.

They came down from the town in the dark with lanterns. Higu first, with the notebook and three pencils and her coat buttoned wrong. Xola, with a blanket she’d brought without being asked. PenguChi, slow and enormous and gentle in the doorway, ducking. Gigichi last, with her sleeves pushed up, who sat down on the floor by Thea’s chair without saying anything and took Thea’s left wrist in her hand and put her thumb on the inside of it, where the pulse was. Then Maren, with her new pink hands pushed up her sleeves against the cold. Then Bram, big and grey, who came as far as the door, and stopped there, and leaned his good shoulder on the frame.

“I’ll be the door,” said Bram. “Again.”

“You don’t have to,” said Thea.

“I know,” said Bram, and stayed.

The cabin was small with all of them in it. It was always small; it was built for two, a table and two stools and a shelf by the stove with the cups on their hooks. The sword on Garrow’s two pegs over the hearth, star-iron, where he’d put it back on the morning he went. Under it, the drawing, in its frame. And beside the drawing, on its nail, the list, hung blank side out. Higu’s neat pencil on the other side, every line crossed through but one. Nothing on this side at all.

Thea sat at the table, on his stool, because Alea put her there. The door was open on the dark and the jetty and the lamp at the end of it, small and yellow over the ice. She could see it from the chair.

“How long,” said Higu. She had the notebook open on the table and a pencil in her fist.

“Four hundred,” said Thea.

“You said that last time.”

“I went to six hundred and twelve last time. I said four hundred. I’m saying it again.” She looked at Higu. “Four hundred, and if I’m not out you say door. Not my name. Door.”

Higu wrote it down. 400. DOOR. She underlined door twice.

“And if it looks at you,” said Higu.

“It can’t. It’s a picture.” Thea heard herself say it and made herself go on, carefully, the way she’d say a thing in a code review that she wasn’t sure of yet. “Last time I went through Hyvred. Through her eyes, live, down the cords, through Deston. When it turned round, it had somebody to turn toward. A line with people on it. Hana, Deston, Hyvred, me.” She looked at her window, at the picture in it, still lying there. “This is John’s light. On a tower. Looking north. Nobody’s holding it. He said. It’s just there because it’s true.” She swallowed. “If it turns round, there’s nobody at the end of this. There’s a mote on a parapet. It can look at a mote all it likes.”

Higu looked at her for a long time.

“That’s a theory,” said Higu.

“Yes.”

“Then four hundred,” said Higu, “and door,” and wrote it again, and drew a box round it.

She went in.

Not through anyone. That was the first thing, and it was so different she nearly came straight out again. Last time it had been like stepping into somebody else’s eyes; warm, and moving, and full of Hyvred, her fear and her red mark and the cold on the back of her neck. This was like stepping into a window. Cold glass, and the picture behind it, and nobody there.

Handshake. She’d named it for what it felt like, the first time: two hands, two people’s bindings, meeting and holding long enough to let something pass. She put her hand out now and there wasn’t a hand. There was a mote. John’s light, small and gold and steady on the stone of a parapet at Thornwall, looking north, with nothing in it at all but looking.

She took hold of that.

And the picture opened, the way a map opens when you stop looking at the paper and start looking at the country.

The town. She didn’t stop in the town; she’d promised herself she wouldn’t. She went over it the way a bird goes over roofs, past the lamps and the long street and the arch and the row on the step, and didn’t look down. Out across the fields. Up the valley in the snow. Eleven miles. Her eyes, wherever they were, in a cabin by a lake, went wet with cold.

The rings.

She knew them now. She’d seen them once, through Hyvred: candles in the frost, hundreds, standing in thirteen rings at the top of a valley with their faces all turned the same way. She’d parsed them, then, sitting in Maren’s chair with her mouth running, and found the thing nobody had wanted to find: it’s the hall. The circle on the floor of the hall in Solmere, that thirty-five people had woken up in. Ring for ring. Line for line. The same language, the same hand. Only written in people.

It was still the hall.

She went round it. Slowly, the way she read a file she’d read before, looking for what had changed. Thirteen rings, standing in the snow; every person in them a word, and every word where it had been. The outermost ring, the thirteenth, finished, with a girl at the very end of it with a blue edge to her light: the last word. Elin. She didn’t stop on her. She’d promised Higu that too.

The inner rings.

She’d known what she’d find there. Kei had written it in the channel at seven in the morning, the morning after his dose. Seven went out. She found them anyway, because she was who she was, and she couldn’t read past a thing without seeing it. Seven places, in the inner rings, the oldest, where there’d been a word and wasn’t.

Not holes. She’d thought they’d be holes, like the threadless: places where the light should be and wasn’t. They weren’t. They were blanks. Gaps in a line of writing where a word had been rubbed out, very carefully, and nothing written in its place; and the rings standing round them, still whole, still the shape of rings, the way a sentence still reads if you take out a word from the middle of it. It’s still the hall. Just a little quieter. A hair. Seven hairs.

Ninety, said Higu. A long way off. Counting.

She went on.

Outside the rings. On the left. The fourteenth.

This was the part she’d come for. Last time it had been a bit under half: fourteen threadless standing in a curve outside the thirteenth ring, fourteen holes, fourteen words, and the words were the left arc of the fourteenth ring, and the left arc was the call. Come. Written in people who weren’t there any more. Every new one made it louder.

It had got to thirty-one. Rui Xiang had counted them, by hand, an inch off each shoulder. Thirty-one, the night before it came down.

She found the curve. She went along it the way she’d go along a line of code with her finger, word by word. One. Two. Three. Holes in the snow, standing, the size of people, with nothing in them. Ten. Fifteen. Twenty. She could read them now. Not the words themselves; she’d never been able to read what they said, only what they were, the shape and the grammar of them. But she could read the shape of the sentence they made.

It was very nearly a sentence.

That was the thing that went through her, sitting in a cabin with her eyes gone. It had been very nearly finished. Thirty-one words. She could see, from the grammar, from the shape of the curve and the way it bent toward the end, how many more it had wanted: not many. Two. Three. It had come down the pass with the sentence nearly said, and leaned on Thornwall for fifteen nights saying it, as much of it as it had.

Twenty-one. Twenty-two. Twenty-three.

And then it stopped.

Not trailed off. Not faded. Stopped. The curve went along, word after word, holes in the snow, and at the twenty-third it ended; clean, square, like a line of writing that somebody had cut off the bottom of a page with a very sharp knife. She could see the cut. She’d never seen anything like it. Not in the language. Not in anything she’d ever parsed. The edge of it was straight and quiet and absolutely final, the way the edge of a reed is when it falls a hand past a blade; and on the other side of it there was nothing at all. Not blanks. Not gaps. Just the snow, going on, with no curve in it.

Eight words. Cut off the end of a sentence.

She knew what had cut them. She’d been in the channel at seven in the morning three days ago. He’s here.

She sat in the cut, for a moment. On the edge of it. She looked at it the way you’d look at a weld somebody else had done, better than you’d ever do one. And then she looked at what was left.

Twenty-three. Three-quarters, near enough. Three-quarters of a sentence that had been nearly said.

It wasn’t fading. That was the second thing. She’d half hoped it would be; that a sentence with its end cut off would come apart, the way her own spells came apart when she lost the last line. It didn’t. The twenty-three stood in the snow, holes in a curve, exactly as written; and the end of the curve, at the cut, wasn’t broken. It was open. Like a line that ends in a comma.

It was waiting.

For the rest of it. For eight more words, written in eight more people with nothing in them, to come and stand at the end of the curve past the cut, so it could start again from where it had been stopped; and then the two or three it had still wanted, after that. Come. Three-quarters said. It wasn’t going anywhere. It would stand in the snow at the top of the valley and wait as long as it took.

And it would take until spring. She could see that too, if she looked past the curve, up, at the pass. The words came from the west. They always had; Hyvred had watched them come, one a week, down the switchbacks into the valley from the far side, walking. And the pass was shut. White from side to side, under the snow, the way the old men at Thornwall said it shut every year, and stayed shut till the thaw.

Nobody was coming down it till the thaw.

Two hundred, said Higu.

The right arc was still blank.

She looked, because she always looked. On the other side of the fourteenth place, on the right, where in the hall in Solmere there’d been a curve drawn and left empty on purpose. There was nothing. There’d been nothing the first time. It wasn’t even drawn. Just snow, on the right, going away to the side of the valley; and the left arc, three-quarters said, pointing at it.

She thought of Oswin’s leaves, that Hana had read her through the cords from Solmere in the autumn. The right hand is drawn and left. It is not ours to write. It must be held open from the middle, by a hand that holds and does not close.

Nobody was holding anything open on the right. There was nothing there to hold.

She didn’t stay on it. She’d written it down before. She went up.

The absence.

At the top of the valley, at the foot of the pass, a round place the size of a hill where the snow didn’t lie. She didn’t parse it. She’d promised herself that more than anything. You couldn’t parse it anyway; there was nothing there to read. It wasn’t a word. It wasn’t written. It was the thing the words were being written for, sitting at the top of the page, and the snow coming down all round it and stopping a few feet short, as if it had remembered something.

It was facing the town.

She knew that the way you know which way a person in a dark room is facing. It was facing down the valley, toward Thornwall, toward the arch and the step and the row of people on it and the mote on the parapet. It had faced the town since the thirtieth word walked into the curve. It had turned round once, on the last morning, and faced a man on a ridge road, and then it had turned back.

It was facing the town now. Still. Patient. The way the lake is patient under ice.

It didn’t look at her.

She’d been right. There was nothing at the end of this line to look at; a mote on a parapet, and a picture that was only there because it was true. She sat on the edge of the valley in John’s light, with her eyes gone, and the thing at the top of it sat with its face to the town, and didn’t turn, and didn’t know she was there. A picture doesn’t look back.

She didn’t follow the call. She could feel where it went, the way you can feel a draught from under a door: past the absence, past the pass, west, a long way. Past the edge of the paper, Higu had written, the first time, at the end of an arrow. She didn’t go. Not a hair. She’d promised Higu five things, and the fifth was the only one that mattered.

Three hundred and forty, said Higu.

She closed her door.

Not the room. You didn’t close the room; she’d learned that over the chat in the first winter, without knowing she was learning it. You closed your own door, and the room went on without you, and everybody else in it went on talking. She closed hers. Gently. The way you’d close a door on a sleeping house.

And she was in a cabin by a lake, on a stool, with her face cold and her eyes stinging and Gigichi’s thumb on her wrist, and the door open on the dark, and the lamp.

“Three hundred and sixty-one,” said Higu.

She wrote it down. Then she put the pencil down on the table, very carefully, beside the notebook, and looked at Thea; and Thea saw that her hand was shaking, a little, and that she was pretending it wasn’t.

“Under,” said Higu.

“Under.”

“You came out on your own.”

“Yes.”

Higu looked at the number for a long time. 361, in her small fierce letters, inside the box that said 400. DOOR.

“Good,” said Higu, and didn’t say anything else; and picked the pencil back up, and drew a second line under the box, and then a third.

“Your nose,” said Gigichi.

Thea put her hand up. It came away clean. She looked at her fingers for a long moment. Then she laughed, once, a small cracked sound that surprised everybody including her; and Xola, on the floor, put her face in the blanket and made a noise that might have been laughing too.

“Seventy-four,” said Gigichi, with her thumb on Thea’s wrist. “It was a hundred and ten at two hundred. You came down on your own.” She let go. “That’s a door.”

PenguChi, in the corner by the stove, enormous and quiet, said: “What did you see?”

So she told them.

All of it. Slowly, the way Maren read; every word. The rings, still the hall. The seven blanks in the inner rings, with the sentence still reading round them. The fourteenth: twenty-three, three-quarters of a sentence that had been nearly said, and the end of it cut off clean, the way nothing in the language had ever been cut. Open, at the cut. Waiting. For eight more to get back where it had been, and the last two or three after them, from the west, down a pass that was shut till the thaw. The right arc, blank. The absence, at the top, facing the town; not looking at her.

Higu wrote it down. A new page. Then another. She drew the curve, and the cut, and the snow on the pass. She wrote SPRING at the top of the second page and underlined it once, not twice.

Maren listened with her new hands folded on the end of the table. When Thea got to the cut, she unfolded them, and looked at her palms, pink and smooth and new, where the burns had been; and folded them again.

Bram in the doorway didn’t say anything. But when she said the edge of it was straight, like a reed when it falls a hand past a blade, he looked out of the door at the dark, toward the north end of the lake, where the reeds were, and she saw his right hand close on his hip.

“So it’s coming back,” said Xola. At the end. Very quietly, into the blanket.

“In the spring,” said Thea. “When the pass opens. If they come down it: eight to get back where it was, and two or three more.” She thought about it. “It’s not over. It’s waiting. It’s three-quarters, and the end’s open, and it’s waiting.”

Nobody said anything.

“But it’s waiting,” said Thea. “It’s not doing anything. It’s sat down.”

She looked at Alea.

Alea had been at the stove the whole time. She’d made tea and put it in front of everybody, in the two cups and the jug and whatever else in the cabin would hold it, and not drunk any of her own; and she’d stood by the stove with her arms folded, listening, with her face giving nothing away, the way his did. She was looking at the door. At the jetty. At the lamp.

“Then it’s winter,” said Alea.

She said it the way you’d say the price of flour. A fact; nothing more. And it went through the cabin, through all of them, Thea saw it go: Higu’s pencil stopping, and Xola’s head coming up out of the blanket, and PenguChi’s slow enormous breath going out. Then it’s winter. Not then it’s over. Not then we’re safe. Winter. A thing that came every year, and you got through, and at the end of it the ice went off the lake and the pass opened and whatever was coming came.

“Yes,” said Thea.

“Okay,” said Alea. And went out to look at the lamp.

She posted it at midnight, on the cabin step, with the picture still lying in her window under the words, steady.

She shut it. The picture stayed.

The others went back up to the town at one, with the lanterns, in a line along the lake road: Higu first, Gigichi, Xola, PenguChi. Thea watched the lights go up the slope. Maren stayed a moment at the door, with her sleeves pushed up, looking at the lamp at the end of the jetty.

“Seventy-one,” said Maren.

Thea looked round.

“This morning,” said Maren. “My window.” She didn’t look at Thea. She looked at the lamp. “It said fifty-eight for seven years. It was sixty-six in the summer. Then the hands.” She turned them over, in the light from the door. “I thought Torch was more. I was wrong. I pushed and I burned them. It didn’t move for a month.” She was quiet. “Then it did. Since they healed. A point at a time. I don’t know what for.”

“Maybe for stopping,” said Thea.

Maren thought about that, the way she thought about everything: slowly, every word.

“Maybe,” she said. “You stopped tonight. At three hundred and sixty-one. I watched.” She pulled her sleeves down over her new hands. “I’d like to learn that. Whenever you’ve got time.” And she went up the lake road after the others, alone, with no lantern, because Maren had walked that road in the dark for years and didn’t need one.

Bram went last. He stood in the door a moment, big and grey, and looked at the lamp; and at Alea, who was standing at the end of the jetty beside it with her hand on the post.

“She does it every night,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Like him.”

“Yes.”

Bram nodded. He looked at the reeds, at the north end, white, standing up out of the ice. Then he went up the road.

Thea stayed.

She hadn’t meant to. She sat on the cabin step with the door open behind her and the stove going and her window open in front of her, with the picture in it, and she couldn’t make herself shut it. It was the step. The row of people, fewer now; most of Group 5 had gone in, and it was the king, and two women at the ends, and the man with the rod. Sitting there. In the snow. In the lamplight from the arch. Not doing anything.

Alea came up from the jetty at two and sat down beside her.

“Still there?” said Alea.

“Still there.”

“What’s he doing?”

“Sitting.”

Alea nodded. She pulled her coat round her knees.

They sat there. The lake creaked, out in the dark, the long low sound ice makes when it’s thickening. The lamp burned at the end of the jetty. Thea looked at it, and at the picture, and at the lamp again: a small yellow light at the end of a jetty over the ice, and a small gold square in her window with a man on a step in it a whole kingdom away; and she thought that she’d never in three years asked who the lamp was for, and neither had anybody, and Alea had said on the step the morning he went that she’d never asked either.

She didn’t ask now.

“It’s three-quarters,” said Thea. “The circle. It’ll be three-quarters all winter.”

“You said.”

“I keep thinking that’s bad. That it’s not gone.” She looked at her hands. “And it’s not good. It’s not. But it’s—“ She stopped. She didn’t know what it was.

“It’s waiting,” said Alea. “You said. It’s sat down.” She looked at the lamp. “So’s he.”

They sat there till nearly dawn.

At a little before six, with the sky going grey over the hills at the far end of the lake and the lamp gone pale on its post, the picture in Thea’s window changed.

Not much. Not a heartbeat of something happening. Just: the man on the step, beside the king, stood up. Picked up the rod. Stood there a moment, with it in his hand, looking north. And the picture went out. Gently. All at once. Like a reflection going when somebody opens the window it’s in. Because it wasn’t true any more.

Thea sat very still.

“He stood up,” she said.

Alea didn’t move. She sat with her coat round her knees and her chin on them, looking at the lamp; and then she unfolded, and stood, and went down the jetty in the grey light to the end, and put her hand on the post.

“He’s coming home,” said Alea.

She said it the way she’d said then it’s winter. A fact. Thea didn’t ask her how she knew. She didn’t think Alea would have been able to say. He always stood up when he was going; that was all; you’d know it if you’d lived with him your whole life.

Alea lifted the glass and pinched the wick and put it out, the way he did at dawn. And stood there with her hand on the post a moment longer.

Then she came back up the jetty, past Thea on the step, and into the cabin, and Thea heard her moving round inside: the kettle, the stove, the cups. Two of them. She heard Alea take two cups off their hooks and put them on the table, and stand there, and then put one back.

Not yet, then. Not for a while yet. It was a long way.

She opened her window, because she couldn’t not.

Seven. It had been three since the autumn. Four points, for a door she’d closed on her own at three hundred and sixty-one.

She shut it, and went in, and Alea gave her tea.
