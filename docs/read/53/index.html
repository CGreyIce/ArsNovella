The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 2: Letters

Chapter 53

The Pass

3,806 words · 17 min read

On Earth, Hyvred had seen the world through a hole.

That was how she’d described it, once, in a clip: a circle of light in the middle of a dark screen, moving when you moved your head, everything outside it just gone. Tunnel vision. She’d had it for years. She’d learned to live inside the circle. To turn her whole head to look at things other people could see from the corner of their eye. To walk into rooms and stand very still for a moment while she found the edges.

Ice had seen the clip and called it osu!’s Flashlight mod. Then he’d found out it was real, and apologised so many times she’d laughed until she cried.

She thought about that a lot, lately. Flashlight mod. A circle of light in the dark.

Because here, she could see everything.

The whole room, edges and all. The whole sky. The light in living things: soldiers like lamps, sheep like little candles, grass like a faint green breath over the hills. The wrong light, in people who weren’t people anymore. The absences, where there should have been light and wasn’t. She’d woken up on the floor of the summoning hall a year ago and seen the ceiling, the whole painted ceiling at once, gold suns and all, and cried like a baby, and Bee had sat beside her and said then cry, I’ll watch for both of us.

She could see everything now.

She was about to find out that she didn’t want to.

The passes opened at midsummer.

Fenwick had been watching the snow on the Greyspine every morning since the spring, from the top of the north tower, with Corvin’s brass spyglass, making notes. And on the twentieth day after Pell left, he came down to breakfast with his spectacles pushed up on his forehead and said, “It’s gone. The high snow. The track’s clear to the top.”

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Brannoch put down his spoon, and stood up, and said, “Forty men. Two days to get ready. We leave on the third morning.”

They argued about who was going for most of those two days. Hyvred didn’t. She’d known since the spring.

“You have to,” said Corvin, very quietly, in the long room, the night before. He’d been saying it to himself for months, she thought; she could see it in his light, the way it flickered every time he looked at her. “You’re the only one who can see them. If we go up there without you, we’re blind.”

“I know,” said Hyvred.

“I’m sorry.”

“Don’t be.” She smiled at him, the big warm upbeat smile that she’d been wearing on stream for years and had found, here, that she could still wear when she needed to. “I want to go, Corvin. Honestly. I’ve been wanting to know what’s up there since the first week.” Which was true. That was the worst of it. Some part of her, the part that had always walked into dark rooms first and found the edges, wanted to.

Hild was going, of course. Fenwick. Forty of the Western Watch, the best Brannoch had, under a sergeant called Maddox who’d been on the Redmarch and didn’t talk about it. Kei was going, as medic, with Hisui round his wrist, complaining. Tiger was going, because Drew was going, and Drew was going because Tiger was.

Bee wasn’t.

“Somebody has to stay,” said Hyvred, when Bee came and found her in the stores, packing. “Somebody has to watch the gate. Somebody has to draw the dogs.”

“I can draw dogs up a mountain.”

“I know you can.” Hyvred stopped packing and turned round and took Bee’s hands, charcoal and all. “But I need to know someone’s here. Someone who’ll see it if anything comes back down that track wearing a face. Someone who’ll tell it.” She squeezed. “Please. Bee. Draw a dog on every wall in this fort. And be here when I come back.”

Bee looked at her for a long time, with her antennae drooping and her blunt face very still.

“You’d better come back,” she said at last.

“I will.”

“You’d better. Or I’m coming up there after you. And I’ll fly.” She lifted her chin. “I’ll learn. Out of spite.”

Hyvred laughed, and hugged her, and didn’t let go for a long time.

It took three days to reach the top.

Up past the burned hut above Coldwater, where the black glassy circle still lay in the ash, untouched. Up the old shepherds’ track along the shoulder of the hills. Then higher, into country nobody from Thornwall had walked in living memory: bare grey rock and scree and thin cold air, with patches of old snow still lying in the shadows of boulders and the wind coming down off the peaks like a knife.

Hyvred looked at everything. She couldn’t help it. She’d never been anywhere so high, or so empty. There was almost no light up here. Not living light. A few marmots, little warm sparks among the rocks. A hawk, very high, a dot of brightness against the blue. The forty men of the Watch, strung out along the track ahead and behind her like a necklace of lamps. Tiger, quick and bright, always a few paces ahead of where he should be. Drew, steady, always at his shoulder. Kei, calm. Hild, at the front, a deep slow glow like a banked forge, with her blindfold up and her one eye on the track.

And the tracks.

They were everywhere, up here. In the old snow, in the mud where the snow had melted. Boots, going north. Dozens of pairs, over months. And among them, the long narrow prints with too many joints and no heel. All going one way.

Nothing came back down. Not one print.

“They walked up here,” said Tiger quietly, on the second day, crouching beside a set of boot prints in a patch of snow. Small ones. A child’s. “They just walked up here. Over the mountains. On their own.”

“Not on their own,” said Drew.

Tiger didn’t answer. He looked at the prints for a long time. Then he got up and kept walking, faster than before.

They reached the top of the pass at dusk on the third day.

Hild stopped them a little below it. A hundred paces short, in a fold of rock out of the wind, where they couldn’t be seen from the other side. “We go up after dark,” she said. “Ten of us. No lights. The rest stay here.” She looked at Hyvred with her one eye. “You first. With me.”

They waited for the dark. It came slowly, the way it does in summer in the high places: the sky going from blue to violet to a deep clear black, the stars coming out one by one and then all at once, more stars than Hyvred had ever seen, so many that the sky looked dusty with them. Somewhere below them, in the fold of rock, the forty men of the Watch sat with their backs against the stone and didn’t talk.

Kei came and sat beside her for a while. He didn’t say anything. He just sat there, calm, the way he did, with Hisui on his wrist.

“Hisui says the air’s wrong,” he said eventually, very quietly. “Coming over the top. From the other side.”

“Wrong how?”

Kei listened to his snake for a moment.

“He says it tastes like the bottom of a well,” he said. “Old and cold and deep. And full of something he doesn’t have a word for.” He paused. “He says he doesn’t want to go up there.”

“Neither do I,” said Hyvred.

“You’re going anyway.”

“Yeah.” She smiled at him in the dark. “You too.”

“Yeah,” said Kei.

At midnight, Hild touched her shoulder, and they went up.

Ten of them, in a line, in the dark, with no lights. Hild first. Then Hyvred. Then Fenwick, and Maddox, and four of the Watch, and Tiger and Drew at the back. Up the last hundred paces of the track, over loose stones that rolled and clattered under their boots so that every sound made Hyvred’s heart stop. Up, and up, until the track levelled out between two great shoulders of grey rock, and the wind hit them full in the face, cold and clean and smelling, very faintly, of something like the bottom of a well.

And then the ground fell away in front of them, and she could see the other side.

At first she didn’t understand what she was looking at.

There was a valley. That much was clear. A long deep valley, running away north and west between two lines of black mountains, under the stars. And down into it, from the top of the pass where they stood, there was a road.

A real road. Paved. Old. Very old; older than anything she’d seen in the kingdom, made of huge flat grey stones fitted together without mortar, switchbacking down the mountainside in long smooth curves. Nobody had built that road for shepherds. Somebody had built it for armies.

And at the bottom of the valley, a long way down, there was a town.

That was what she thought, at first. A town. There were buildings: roofs and walls and the dark shapes of streets. There were lights. Hundreds of lights, scattered through it, in every window and every street.

Then her eyes adjusted, and she saw what the lights were.

They weren’t lamps. They weren’t fires. There was no smoke rising from any chimney in that whole town, and no sound, and nothing moving. The lights were people. Hundreds of them. Standing in the streets and the squares and the doorways, perfectly still, in the dark.

And every single one of them was wrong.

She knew that light. She’d seen it at the well in Coldwater, a year ago, in a man who’d looked straight at her crosshair eye. She’d seen it walking north over the ridge on the night Sergeant Dunmore’s ten went missing. She’d seen it at the gate of Thornwall, in a boy with freckles who didn’t blink. A lamp with a hand inside it. Someone wearing someone.

Hundreds of them. A whole town of them.

And they weren’t scattered. That was the next thing she saw. They were standing in rings. Ring inside ring inside ring, all through the streets and out into the fields beyond, all facing inward, toward the centre of the valley floor. Toward a circle.

It was burned into the ground. She could see its lines from here, glowing faintly under the earth the way coals glow under ash: rings and spokes, turning, the same pattern that had been burned into the floor of a shepherd’s hut, the same pattern John had seen coming up under the guild seal in Duskhollow. But this one was the size of a field. The size of the whole of Thornwall. And the people stood on its lines, in their hundreds, perfectly still, waiting.

Waiting for what?

Hyvred stood at the top of the pass in the freezing wind and looked down at it and couldn’t breathe.

Behind her, very quietly, Fenwick said something that might have been a prayer.

“What do you see?” breathed Hild, beside her. Hild could feel things; she could hear things; she’d fought for twenty years with one eye. But she couldn’t see light. Not the way Hyvred could. “Hyvred. Tell me. Plainly.”

Hyvred told her. Plainly, the way Higu’s rules said. A road. A town. Hundreds of them, standing in rings. A circle the size of a fort.

Hild didn’t say anything. But Hyvred saw her light change: the deep slow forge-glow of her tightening, gathering, the way it had gathered in the shepherd’s hut a year ago, right before she’d thrown a wall of light at the thing in the dark.

“Anything else?” said Hild. “Further. Past the town.”

Hyvred looked.

She couldn’t see past the town. Not properly. The valley ran on north and west, into the dark, and her sight thinned out with distance the way it always had, the way everyone’s did. The lights in the town were bright enough to see. Beyond them was only a blur.

But there was something there. At the far end of the valley. She could feel it more than see it: a place where the blur was wrong. Where something that should have been there wasn’t.

“I can’t,” she said. “It’s too far. There’s something. I can’t—“

And then she felt it.

Her left eye. The one with the crosshair in it. The one that had always looked a little different from the other, a red reticle set in a gold iris, the way it had been on her model on Earth, the way it had come through the floor. The eye she used when she really looked at something.

It was aching. A deep ache, behind it, like the ache in a muscle you’ve held too long. And she knew, the way you know where your own hands are, that it wanted to do something. That it had always wanted to do something. That she’d been using it for a year like a magnifying glass when it had been, all along, something else.

On Earth she’d seen the world through a flashlight. Here she saw it through a scope.

You’re an AWPer, she thought, suddenly, out of nowhere, in a voice that wasn’t hers. Scope in.

She felt for the gap. It was right there. Behind her eye, where the ache was. She’d been feeling it for months without knowing what it was.

She didn’t need a long line. She only needed the one thing she wanted most, standing at the top of the world in the freezing dark, looking at something she couldn’t see.

Show me.

The world came toward her.

That was what it felt like. Not like her eye moving. Like everything else moving, rushing in, the whole valley sliding toward her at once as if someone had grabbed the far end of it and pulled. The crosshair in her eye locked: she felt it, a little click, like a lens settling. And suddenly the town wasn’t a mile below her anymore. It was right there. She could see faces. She could see the shape of the lights inside them, every one of them a lamp with a hand inside it, every hand holding very still.

She could see the circle. Every line of it. The way the lines were moving, very slowly, under the earth, like something being drawn.

And she could see past it. To the far end of the valley.

There was a hill there. Or there was a place where a hill should be. A great dark shape, rising up out of the valley floor, as tall as the shoulders of the mountains around it. And it had no light at all.

None. Not dim light. Not wrong light. No light. As if somebody had taken scissors to the world and cut a shape out of it and there was nothing on the other side. She’d seen something like it once before, on the ridge above Coldwater, in the first month: a figure-shaped absence on the skyline, man-sized, standing very still.

This was the same. Except it was the size of a hill.

And as she looked at it, through her locked crosshair, from a mile away and more, it turned.

Slowly. The way something very large turns. The whole vast lightless shape of it, at the far end of the valley, turning, until whatever passed for its front was facing up the long road, toward the pass.

Toward her.

She didn’t scream. She’d think about that afterwards, and be proud of it. She just took a step back, very carefully, and said, very quietly, “Hild. It’s seen us.”

Hild didn’t ask what. Hild didn’t need to.

“Back,” she said. Very low. Very calm. “Everyone. Now. Quietly. Don’t run.”

And then, from down the road, a voice.

“Tiger!”

Everyone froze.

It came up out of the dark, up the long switchbacks of the old road, faint and clear and cheerful in the thin cold air. A young man’s voice. Hyvred knew it. They all knew it.

“Tiger! Oi! Is that you up there?”

Hyvred looked down. She couldn’t help it. Her eye was still locked, still pulling the world close. And on the road below, perhaps a quarter of a mile down, standing on one of the great flat grey stones in the starlight, there was a lanky figure with big feet and freckles, waving both arms over his head.

Ossie. The thing with Ossie’s face. His lamp, with the hand inside it, holding very still.

“Come down!” it called. “It’s warm down here! Everyone’s here! Come and see!”

Tiger made a sound.

Hyvred heard him move. She heard the scuff of his boots on the stones, quick, too quick, the way he moved when he did the thing with the gap, Go. And she turned round, and saw him already a pace past her, his whole body leaning toward the road, toward the voice—

And Drew.

Drew didn’t grab him. That was what Hyvred would remember. Drew didn’t grab him or shout or wrestle him to the ground. He just stepped into the way, very calmly, the way he’d stepped into the way of a hundred things in nine years, and put both his hands flat on Tiger’s chest, and stood there.

“Not here,” said Drew quietly. “Not this one. It’s not him.”

“Drew, please—“

“I know. I know.” Drew didn’t move his hands. “I’ve got you. It’s not him. We’re going home.”

Tiger stood there, shaking, with his friend’s hands on his chest, staring past him down the road at the thing with Ossie’s face, waving.

Then he turned round, and walked back past Hyvred without a word, into the dark, toward the fold of rock where the others were waiting. Drew went with him. Drew’s hand stayed on his shoulder the whole way.

They came down off the Greyspine in the dark, forty-six of them, without lights, as fast as they dared.

All night. Hild at the back now, not the front, walking backwards half the time with her blindfold pulled down over her one good eye so that she could listen better, her light gathered up in her like a fist. Hyvred beside her, looking back. Always looking back. Up at the pass, black against the stars.

Nothing came over it. Nothing followed.

But all the way down, she could feel it. Not see it. Feel it. Like a weight on the back of her neck. The attention of something very large and very patient, from a long way off, watching them go.

“It’s tasting the air,” said Kei once, very low, in the dark, near the top of the shepherds’ track. Hisui was coiled so tight round his wrist that his hand had gone white. “Hisui says. Behind us. Over the top. It’s tasting the air, for us.”

“Can it find us?”

Kei listened. “He says not today,” he said at last. “He says it’s not in a hurry.”

That was worse, somehow.

They reached the burned hut above Coldwater at dawn, and Thornwall a day after that.

Bee was on the wall. Of course she was. She’d been there, the gate sergeant said afterwards, for three days and three nights, coming down only to eat and sleep a few hours at a time. And on every wall of Thornwall, the gatehouse and the towers and the long north wall and the doors of the barracks, there were dogs. Charcoal dogs and chalk dogs, big rough shaggy shepherd’s dogs, hackles up, mouths open, dozens and dozens of them, facing out.

She’d drawn them all. Every single one.

Hyvred came through the inner gate and saw them, and saw Bee coming down the steps from the wall two at a time, and burst into tears.

That night, in the long room, she opened her window.

She looked at it for a long time, with Bee asleep against her shoulder on the bench under Corvin’s maps.

Sixth. After Thea and Maigo and Higu and Salty and Rachael. She should have been happy. She’d been waiting for this all year. She’d imagined it a hundred times: screaming, probably, and jumping up and down, and Bee pretending not to be impressed, and writing to everyone in capitals.

She wasn’t happy. She was just tired. And she couldn’t stop seeing it, every time she closed her eyes: a road for armies, and a town of lamps with hands inside them, standing in rings, waiting. And at the far end of the valley, a hole in the world the size of a hill, turning to look at her.

Scope, she thought. Of course it’s Scope.

On Earth she’d seen the world through a flashlight. Here, she’d see it through a scope. Further than anyone. Closer than anyone. Every single thing, whether she wanted to or not.

Ice would have loved that, she thought. He’d been an AWPer. He’d have laughed and said scope in and finally, a real sniper on the team. And then, maybe, if he’d seen her face, he’d have stopped laughing, and asked if she was okay.

She opened the channel. She didn’t want to. But Higu’s rules were right. Bad news: say it plainly. Don’t make anyone guess.

The answers came slowly, for once. Quietly. Yan first: One shape. East, west, under. And now over. Malcolm: Adding a page. Then a line from Gigichi, sharp: Hyv. You okay? And under it, from all over the kingdom, people asking the same thing, one after another, until the channel was full of it.

Hyvred read them all. Then she wrote one more line, and closed her window, and put her head down on Bee’s shoulder, and slept.
