The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 10: The Sword's Country

Chapter 271

The Hook

6,315 words · 27 min read

It was almost a hook.

It lay on the face of the small anvil at the back of Harl’s forge, on a square of oiled leather so it wouldn’t skate, in the light of the one lamp Gabe had hung low enough to work by: a curve of grey-silver no longer than the last joint of his little finger, with a shank, and a bend, and a point that wasn’t sharp yet, and an eye that wasn’t open yet, and frost in it. Fine branching frost, all the way through, that caught the lamplight and went faintly pink whenever he turned it toward the coals.

It had taken him three months to get it this far, and it had cost Harl eleven files.

Harl kept count. He kept them on a nail by the back door, the dead ones, threaded on a loop of wire like a string of onions: eleven good files from the best maker in Kingsferry, every one of them worn as smooth as a spoon. He’d hung the eleventh up on midwinter morning without saying anything, and then stood in the doorway with his arms folded and looked at Gabe, and at the thumb of grey on the anvil, and back at the nail.

“That’s a year of files,” Harl said.

“I know.”

“For a fish-hook.”

“I know.”

Harl had no eyebrows. He’d burned them off in his twenties showing an apprentice what the heat looked like when it stopped being angry, and they’d never grown back, so that everything he said came out looking faintly surprised, which it almost never was. He’d been at the anvil on the south wharf for forty years. He had hands like spades and a voice like a shovel going into gravel, and he’d taken Gabe in off the street more than three years ago because Gabe had stood outside his door all morning watching him work and he’d got tired of it.

“Good,” Harl said, and went back to the front hearth.

He’d tried heat first. Of course he had. Everybody did.

In the autumn, the night he got back to Kingsferry off the Widow, Harl had lit both hearths for him without being asked and Gabe had put the offcut in the big one, in the heart of it, on a bed of the good Kingsferry coal, and pumped the bellows himself for an hour; and the offcut had gone dull red, very slowly, the way a coal goes when you breathe on it, and stopped there. Dull red. All night. It wouldn’t go to orange. It didn’t care how hard he worked the bellows or how much coal he fed it. It sat in the middle of the best fire on the south wharf with frost in it, glowing like a thing that was thinking about being warm, and thought about it all night, and decided not to.

He’d known it would. He’d seen the star in the king’s fire on the shingle at the lake in the summer: a whole day of feeding it and the far end still cold, until a man in his bare feet put his hand flat on it and the heat came up under his palm, orange, then yellow, the way nothing in the world came up for anybody else. Star-iron didn’t take heat from a fire. It took it from Declan. And Declan was three hundred miles upriver with his hand on a fishing rod, and Gabe wasn’t going to write to him and ask him to come and hold a hook.

So he’d done it cold.

He’d sat at this anvil every night since the autumn, after his afternoons at Harl’s front hearth and his evenings at the chandler’s, with the offcut in the small tongs and a file in his other hand, and taken it down. Not shaped it. You couldn’t shape it. You could only take away what wasn’t the hook, a breath at a time, the way you’d take down a wall with your fingernails. The filings came off it like frost off a windowpane, fine and grey and glittering; he swept them into a twist of paper every night, because they were star, and you didn’t throw star on the floor. The twist of paper was in his shirt now, against his chest, about the weight of a coin.

Eleven files.

The first three he’d ruined in a week, by pressing. That was the thing he’d had to learn first, and it had taken him a month to learn it, because everything Harl had ever taught him was about weight: about getting your shoulder into it, about letting the hammer do the work but making sure the hammer had something to work with. The star didn’t care about weight. If he leaned on the file it skated, and the file went dull, and nothing came off. If he barely touched it, if he drew the file across it the way you’d stroke a cat that didn’t like you, something came off. A breath. A little frost. And the file lasted a week instead of a day.

So he’d learned to barely touch it. A big man with forge hands and a body like a door, sitting on a stool at the back of a forge every night for three months, barely touching something.

Calvin had come in once, in the second week, and watched him for a while, and said, “Bro, are you petting it,” and Gabe had said yes, and Calvin had laughed so hard he’d had to go outside. Then he’d come back in and sat on the bench and watched for another hour, not laughing, and said at the end of it, very quietly, “That’s the most patient thing I’ve ever seen you do,” and Gabe had said, “It’s the only way it comes,” and Calvin had nodded and gone home.

It was the only way it came. That was all. You did it the way it came or you didn’t do it.

Midwinter had been two nights ago.

The market had been on the river, the way it always was, with the candle-boats; Gabe had gone down to the quay with the others and stood with his hands in his coat and watched the children set them on the black water by the customs-house steps, hundreds of them, small and very bright, turning slowly in the current as they went out. Rachael had been at Maud’s booth with the needle case, binding the burns of the chestnut sellers. Dennis had sold something to somebody. Deston had stood beside Gabe on the quay with his eyes half shut, looking north up the river, the way he’d stood every evening since the autumn; and Gabe had known what he was looking at, and hadn’t asked.

And in the morning, at a quarter to eight, the channel had opened in his window while he was standing in the chandler’s kitchen with a cup of tea, and he’d read Winter. freezing fog up the river last night. the black ice. stood on the south rim of the Lists so it froze on me first. And then the rest of it. And he’d put the cup down and read it again, because he’d met Winter properly at the Lists, the summer before last, a girl with silver hair and black horns who stood two paces off from everybody and watched; and she’d stood in front of nine thousand people in the fog all night.

And then, under all of it, an hour later, from Higu: one word.

home.

Nothing else. No name. No Rule three, no capitals, no place. Higu didn’t put places in the channel; that had been one of her rules since the first year. Just the one word, on its own, at nine in the morning on the day after midwinter.

Gabe had sat down on the kitchen bench. Deston had come in a minute later with his window open in front of his face and sat down beside him, and neither of them had said anything for a while.

He ran, Thea had written, a fortnight ago, from the lake. He left twenty-four days ago and he ran. And Hyvred, after: he’s gone. at first light. on foot. east. And Gabe had spent a fortnight not counting days on a road he’d walked himself, in the autumn, with ten people and a cart, and knew to the mile.

home.

He’d got there for the bonfire. He must have. Gabe could see it: the lake road with the forty lanterns on it, and the girls with their tapers, and a man in an old coat with the elbows gone coming over the last hill in the dusk with a rod in his hand. And a fox girl on the end of a jetty with a lamp, who’d lit it every evening for six weeks.

“He made it,” Deston had said, at last, in the chandler’s kitchen.

“Course he made it.” Gabe had picked up the tea again. His hands had been perfectly steady. That was the strange thing; he’d expected them not to be. “Bet he let it go cold.”

“Every night,” said Deston. “Alea’ll have told him.”

Tonight he was doing the barb.

The barb was the hard bit. He’d known it would be since the autumn, since he first saw the hook in the offcut, the way he’d seen Sto in the bar on the first day. The shank was just a line. The bend was just patience. The point was a thing you made by taking away everything that wasn’t sharp. But the barb was a cut that went backwards: a little hooked tooth just behind the point, that let the hook go in and wouldn’t let it come out. You couldn’t file a barb. A file went one way. You had to come at it from inside the bend, with the edge of something very thin, and lift it.

He’d done it on Harl’s iron hooks, for practice, a hundred times. With a cold chisel the width of a fingernail and a tap from the little hammer, and the iron lifted like a wood-shaving and stood up, and you dressed it with a stone. On the star the chisel did nothing at all. He’d tried it once, in the autumn, very gently, and the chisel’s edge had folded over like the corner of a page and the star hadn’t even noticed.

So he was doing it with a stone. A slip of the hard grey Valcrest stone Harl used for the last edge on a razor, worn to the thickness of a coin, that he’d been shaping into a wedge on the side of the wheel for a week. He held the hook in the small tongs, point up, with the leather under it, and laid the stone into the inside of the bend, just behind the point, and drew it toward himself. Barely touching it. A breath.

Nothing.

Again. A breath.

A little frost.

He didn’t count the strokes. You didn’t. You counted strokes, you started wanting to be at the end of them, and then you pressed, and then the stone skated and you were back to the beginning with a scratch on the point. He’d learned that in the second month and it had cost him a week. He just drew the stone, and breathed, and drew it again, and somewhere in the front of the forge Harl banked the big hearth for the night and came back and sat down on the bench by the cold quench trough with a cup of something, and didn’t say anything, and watched.

Somewhere around midnight, there was a barb.

He saw it before he felt it. A little hooked tooth of grey, just behind the point, standing up from the curve no higher than an eyelash, catching the lamp; with frost in it, like everything else. He turned the tongs very slowly in the light and looked at it from every side, the way you’d look at a thing you’d pulled out of the lake that you didn’t know the name of. It was right. It was exactly right. It was the barb he’d seen in the offcut in a posting-house yard at Ashby in the summer with Alea beside him saying make it say something.

He put the tongs down very carefully on the leather. His hands were shaking now. Of course they were. They hadn’t on midwinter morning, and they were now, over a barb.

“Huh,” said Harl, from the bench.

“Yeah.”

“Let’s see it.”

Harl came over and bent down over the anvil with his hands on his knees and looked at it for a long time, not touching it, with his eyebrowless face very close to the lamp. Harl had made ten thousand hooks in forty years. He made them in bundles of twenty, for the chandlers, between anchor chain and cart-tyres; it was the first thing he’d taught Gabe to make, because you made a hook wrong in a lot of small ways before you made it right in one.

“That’ll hold,” Harl said at last.

From Harl, that was a speech.

He needed something to cut the letters with.

That was the next thing, and he’d been putting off thinking about it for three months, the way you put off thinking about the bit of a job you don’t know how to do. The point he could finish with the stone. The eye he could open with the stone, slowly, a week of nights. But the word went on the shank. Eight letters, on a shank no longer than his thumbnail, and you couldn’t put letters on star with a stone.

“Your finest graver,” he said to Harl. “The one you do the guild marks with.”

Harl looked at him. Then at the hook. Then he went to the rack by the back door, under the nail with the eleven dead files on it, and took down the little graver with the boxwood handle that he used for the maker’s mark on a good blade, a tiny hammer-and-wave cut into the tang; and he held it for a moment, and then he didn’t give it to Gabe. He reached up instead, over the back door, to the thing that had hung there on two pegs all the time Gabe had known him.

A sword. Short, heavy, single-edged, black. Gabe had looked at it every day for three and a half years and never once asked. It was just there, the way the anvil was there, the way the nail was there. A Kharvos blade, Harl had said once, in the first month: off the river wall, after the long war, when they were clearing the dead out of the water at the south wharf.

Harl took it down and laid it on the bench beside the anvil, under the lamp.

“Before you ruin my graver,” Harl said, “look at that.”

Gabe looked.

It was good iron. Anybody could see that. Black all through, the way Kharvos iron was black, so that even after twenty years in a forge doorway with the steam off the quench trough going over it every day there was hardly any rust on it. Heavy. Ugly. Brutal. A thing for taking a man’s arm off at the shoulder without having to be clever about it. And down the flat of it, where the black had worn a little from somebody’s thumb, Gabe could see the grain: a faint wavy pattern in the steel, like the grain in an old plank, five or six lines across.

“They fold it,” said Harl. “Kharvos. Not like us. We draw a bar and quench it and that’s the sword. They fold it. Beat it out, fold it over, beat it out again. Four times. Five.” He put his big finger on the grain. “Drives the dirt out. Makes it black. That’s why you can’t break one. I’ve seen the king’s own men hit a Kharvos blade with an axe and the axe come off worse.”

“Five folds,” said Gabe.

“Five’s a master. Six, seven, the good ones.” Harl shrugged. “More than that and you’re a fool. Every fold, you lose iron in the fire, and every fold’s a weld, and every weld’s a chance to get it wrong. One bad weld in a fold and it’s a crack inside the blade you’ll never see till it goes in a man’s hand.” He tapped the flat. “Nobody folds more than seven. I’d bet my anvil on it. Not here. Not there.”

Gabe didn’t say anything.

He was thinking about Astra.

He’d had his hands on it twice. Once at the lake, in the second spring, the afternoon Declan finally slept and the king’s anvil was on the shingle and Gabe had picked up the black half of the old sword because it was lying there, and felt it. And once on the sailcloth on the shingle, a month after, fitting the grip to Alea’s hands with the old saddle leather. Both times he’d felt the same thing, under his palms, the way he felt when a bar was ready: not heat. Grain. Fold on fold on fold, finer than he could count, finer than a hair, all the way through. Thousands, he’d thought, both times. Folded thousands of times. That’s not from here. And it had been waiting. He didn’t know for what.

And the king, who knew every iron in the kingdom by looking, had told him on the shingle in the summer, with the star cooling beside them, that the black sword wasn’t theirs. Not Kharvos work. Not anybody’s he knew. And he’d smiled about it like a boy at a fair.

Thousands.

“You can’t fold a thing a thousand times,” Gabe said slowly.

“That’s what I’m telling you.”

“No.” He was looking at the grain in the Kharvos blade. Five lines. Five folds. “No, you’re right, you can’t. Nobody could. You’d burn it all away. You’d have nothing left.” He reached for the soapstone in his apron pocket without looking, the white stick he marked bars with, and drew on the face of the big anvil next to the small one, the way Malcolm drew in his book. A line. “Fold it once. Two layers.” Another line. “Twice. Four.” He drew it. “Three times, eight. Four, sixteen. Five folds, Harl. Thirty-two layers. That’s your Kharvos blade. Thirty-two.”

Harl looked at the anvil.

“Ten folds,” said Gabe. He wrote it. He’d always been good at this. “A thousand and twenty-four. Fifteen folds.” He wrote the number down and looked at it. “Thirty-two thousand, seven hundred and sixty-eight.”

It sat on the face of the anvil in white soapstone, under the lamp, a number with five figures in it.

“You don’t fold it a thousand times,” said Gabe. “You fold it fifteen. It doubles. Every fold. And at the end you’ve got thirty thousand layers, and they’re so fine you can’t see them, and you’d swear somebody’d folded it till the end of the world.” He put the soapstone down. “That’s what’s in Astra. Not a thousand folds. Fifteen. Maybe more. Done right, every one. Fifteen perfect welds in a row.”

“Nobody could do fifteen,” said Harl.

“Somebody did.”

“Not here,” said Harl. “I’d know. The king’d know.” He was frowning at the number now, the frown he did when a quench went wrong. “Who’d even think of it? Who’d keep going past seven, when every fold you lose half a hand of iron? You’d have to know before you started that it’d come out the other side worth it. You’d have to have seen it.”

Gabe looked at the number.

He knew where you’d have seen it. That was the thing. He’d known for about a minute, since he wrote thirty-two on the anvil and saw the next number coming the way you see the next stair in the dark. He knew it the way everybody knew things on Earth, from somewhere he couldn’t remember: from a programme, or a book, or a teacher, or a video at two in the morning that nobody had asked for. That a long way north of Singapore and a long time ago, there’d been smiths who folded their steel fifteen times, and more. Who’d done it for a thousand years. Who’d made swords so fine that people still went to museums to stand in front of them behind glass, folded so many times the steel looked like water.

On Earth.

Not here.

“Gabe,” said Harl.

He realised he’d stood up. He was standing by the anvil with his hands at his sides and his mouth a little open, looking at a number in soapstone.

“Somebody from home,” he said. “Somebody from home made it.”

Calvin and Deston came to fetch him at half past midnight, the way they did when he forgot to come home, with a pot of soup wrapped in a towel and the kettle.

The kettle was Calvin’s. It went everywhere Calvin went. It was the same kind of kettle Declan had had in Malacca, the worst kettle in Malaysia, which was the whole point of it; he’d bought it from a tinker on Lake Ilse for two coppers and a pike without letting himself notice why, and then he’d held it out to Calvin on Rowan’s terrace and said do you want it and Calvin had held it against his chest like a baby. It made two notes when it boiled, neither of them the right one. Calvin tied it to his belt when he went anywhere further than the end of the street, so it could see.

He put it on the edge of Harl’s banked hearth now without being asked, and Harl looked at it, and at him, and didn’t say anything, which meant Harl liked him.

Deston was looking at the anvil. At the number.

“Fifteen folds,” said Gabe. He told them. All of it: the Kharvos blade, Harl’s five, the doubling, the thirty-two thousand. The swords behind glass on the other side of the world. He told it badly, out of order, with his hands, the way he told everything; and Deston listened the way Deston listened to everything, with his whole face, and didn’t interrupt once.

When he’d finished, Calvin let out a long breath.

“Bro,” he said.

“I know.”

“No, bro. Bro.” Calvin was sitting on the bench beside Harl, with his elbows on his knees, staring at the Kharvos sword. “The hall. The first day. You remember what I said? When the old guy said the windows were the Hero’s words?”

“You said a lot of things.”

“I said there’s always one who came first.” Calvin said it slowly, as if he was reading it off the wall. “And Dest told me to shush. And everybody thought I meant Declan, later, when it turned out Declan was the hero, and it was Declan’s words. And it was. He came first.” He looked up. “But he didn’t, did he. Not first first. He wasn’t the first one here from home. He was just the first one we knew.”

Nobody said anything.

“The register,” said Deston quietly.

He’d sat on the roof of the chandler’s in the autumn with his eyes shut and Hana’s seven cords running through Harmony, holding the other six while Thea read the last frames of it in Solmere. They’d all done it. One Pour. He’d felt the reading go through him like water through a pipe, and come out the other side as words in Thea’s head, and then in the channel: some went home. Fifty-six frames. A ring, and a line out for every person caught, and ticks on the little arc for every one sent back. Thirty-seven frames with ticks. Then nineteen without. Two lines, near the end, unticked: Aldemar’s two. And the last frame, the fifty-sixth, thirty-five lines and nothing on the arc.

“Fifty-five summonings before ours,” said Deston. “Fifty-five frames. Some of them caught one person. Some caught a dozen. More than three hundred years of them, and before.” He was looking at the number on the anvil. “That’s a lot of people from home. Somebody in one of those frames knew how to fold steel.”

“And made a sword,” said Calvin, “and stuck it in a hill in Kharvos, and walked off.” He rubbed his face with both hands. “In the novels, the hero’s sword always has a maker nobody remembers. And then halfway through the book you find out who it was, and it was somebody like you. Somebody who came first. And they left it there for you.” He dropped his hands. “Every single time.”

“Don’t,” said Gabe.

“I’m not saying it’s the novels. I’m saying it’s the shape.”

“I know what you’re saying.”

“Rule four,” said Deston.

They looked at him. He’d taken the folded page out of his shirt, the one he’d carried since the river road more than three years ago, soft as cloth now along the folds; and he opened it on the bench beside the Kharvos sword under the lamp. Five rules, in his small careful English. Be nice. Find out yourself. And under the fourth, small, in a different ink: Except Emrys. Emrys is ours.

“Declan said he never found out who made it,” said Deston. “He told us. On the jetty, the second spring. He never found out who made it, or what it was standing over, and he never went back.” He put his finger on the fourth line. “So we find out.”

“How,” said Gabe. “It’s in Kharvos. On a hill. Past where the maps stop.”

“So’s the answer, probably,” said Deston. “But not all of it. Somebody folded that sword. Somebody from home. Somebody in the register, somebody who came through the same circle we did, and stood in the same hall, and got told the same things by somebody, and didn’t go home, or did, and left a sword behind.” He folded the page up again. “People leave things. They leave stories. In the novels—“

He stopped. Calvin was grinning at him.

“Go on,” said Calvin. “Say it. You never say it.”

“In the novels,” said Deston, with dignity, “somebody always remembers. Not the important people. Somebody on the edge. An old woman. A song.” He put the page back in his shirt. “Cal asks the river. Gabe asks the iron. I’ll ask Emrys what he’s allowed to tell me, and Dennis can tell us which bits are true.”

“And Rae?” said Calvin.

“Rae can tell us we’re ridiculous,” said Gabe, “and patch us up after.”

Calvin laughed. Then he stopped laughing, and looked at the anvil, at the thumb of grey-silver on its square of leather, with its barb.

“We should tell everyone,” he said. “Not the bit about rule four. The folds. It isn’t ours to sit on.”

That was Chris’s line. From the first summer, about the sum. Gabe had read it in the channel at the forge door with sparks going up round him. It isn’t yours to sit on. It isn’t anyone’s.

“Yeah,” said Gabe. “Okay.”

Gabe closed the window.

He knew who else had read it. Everybody did. Somewhere three hundred miles upriver, in a cabin at the end of a lake road with the stove banked low and a lamp lit at the end of the jetty, a man who never once in three and a half years had written a word in the channel would have it open in front of his face by now, reading it on the step, in the cold, the way he read everything. And he wouldn’t write. He never did.

Gabe hoped he’d let his tea go cold. It seemed important, somehow. That it was an ordinary night at the lake, and he’d let his tea go cold.

He cut the first letter after the others had gone.

He hadn’t meant to. He’d meant to cover the hook and walk home with them along the frozen quay, with Calvin’s kettle bumping on Calvin’s hip and the river locked white at the edges and black in the middle where the current still ran. But Deston had looked at him at the door, and at the anvil, and said stay if you want, I’ll leave the window open, the way he’d said it a hundred nights; and Gabe had stayed.

He’d decided about the graver. Harl’s little graver, with the boxwood handle. It would break. It would break on the first stroke, probably, the way the chisel had folded over in the autumn. But he’d thought about it while the others drank the soup, about the man who’d carved twenty letters into the flat of Sto on the shingle at the lake with the king’s fine chisel, all afternoon, without going over a single stroke twice. Declan hadn’t pressed either. He’d sat on the shingle with the blade across his knees and fished through it, Alea said, one stroke and wait, one stroke and wait, as if each letter was a fish that would come when it came.

So Gabe sat on the stool with the hook in the small tongs and the graver in his right hand, and laid the point of it against the shank where the first letter would go, and didn’t push.

He breathed.

He thought about the word. He’d thought about it every night since the summer, since Ashby, since a fox girl said make it say something in a posting-house yard. He didn’t know much Latin. Two words Declan had given him, Sto, and the four on the flat of his blade; the one on the headstone. A few off the old coins at Harl’s that nobody could read.

And one more. He’d seen it on a gravestone when he was eleven, and asked what it meant, and been told, and never forgotten it, and never once in his life had anything to put it on.

Resurgam.

I shall rise again.

He’d thought it was the saddest thing he’d ever read, at eleven. A dead person saying I’ll be back, on a stone, where they very obviously weren’t. He hadn’t understood why anybody would put it there. He’d stood in front of it in the rain for a long time, and somebody had had to come back for him.

He understood it better now. A man had gone to sleep in a room in Malacca with a fan turning, and not woken up; and somewhere else, a long way further off than the other side of the world, a baby had woken up in a cottage with twenty-six years in his head. He’d risen again. Not the way the gravestone meant. Not the way anybody meant. But he had. And now he was a fisherman. And a hook was the thing that brought the fish up out of the dark, every time, onto the boards of the jetty in the cold morning. I shall rise again, on the shank of the thing that did the raising.

It wasn’t a joke. Declan would think it was a joke, at first. He’d look at it and do the thing at the corner of his mouth. And then he’d stop, and look at it properly, and not say anything for a long time.

Gabe drew the graver toward himself. A breath. Barely touching it.

The graver didn’t break.

He looked. There was a line on the shank. A very fine line, finer than a hair, bright in the grey, where the graver had gone; not a cut, quite, more like a scratch on ice. He’d half expected nothing at all. He’d half expected the point of Harl’s best graver lying on the leather in two pieces. Instead there was a line, and the graver was whole, and when he turned the tongs in the lamp the line caught the light and went faintly pink with the frost underneath it, as if the frost had come up to look.

One stroke.

He breathed. He waited. He didn’t know what for; for the line to settle, maybe, or for his hands to. Then he laid the graver back in the line, exactly in it, and drew it again. A breath.

Not the same stroke twice. A different one. The next one. The upright of the R.

It took him until the coals in the back hearth had gone grey and the lamp had burned down a finger’s width and the bells of the river watch had rung three. One letter. An R, no taller than a grain of rice, on the shank of a hook the length of the last joint of his little finger. Seven strokes. He’d counted them afterwards, not during.

He put the graver down and sat back on the stool and looked at it.

Seven more letters. A week each, probably, if he did them the way the R had come: a stroke and a wait, a stroke and a wait, at the back of Harl’s forge after midnight, with the star taking a line when it felt like taking one and not before. Then the eye. Then the point, finished, so it would go into a pike’s jaw like a thought. The thaw, maybe. The thaw, and the Wren breaking up, and the Widow off her winter mooring with Haskett saying easy, easy, and the long brown river north.

He’d take it up himself. Nobody else. He’d put it into Declan’s hand on the end of the jetty and say it’s a perfectly good hook in Declan’s own voice, and watch him look at the shank.

His window was open. He hadn’t opened it. It did that sometimes, at the end of things, as if it wanted to be looked at.

Ninety-seven.

It had said ninety-six since the hail on the downs in the summer, when he’d turned his back to the sky with his arms round Alea and Deston and Petra’s wall over them all, and understood that the thing he did wasn’t about being hard. It was about being in front. Petra had said then you’re nearly there, and he’d been nearly there for half a year. He’d stood in front of nothing since. There hadn’t been anything to stand in front of, in Kingsferry, in the winter; just a hook, and a file, and an anvil, and Harl.

He didn’t know what the one point was for. The barb, maybe. A thing that went in and held, and didn’t let go. Or the R. Or sitting on a stool for three months barely touching something, when everything in him wanted to lean.

He didn’t think it mattered much which.

He wrapped the hook in its square of leather and put it in the little tin box on the shelf over the anvil, beside the twist of paper with the filings in it, and put the graver back on the rack under the eleven dead files, and banked the small hearth, and put out the lamp.

The Kharvos sword was still on the bench where Harl had left it. Black, and heavy, and good, with its five lines of grain under the lamp’s last light. Somebody had folded it five times on the other side of the Redmarch, a long time ago, and been a master, and been proud of it, probably. And somebody else had folded another one fifteen times, longer ago than that, and gone further than any smith in two worlds had any business going, and stuck it in the ground on a hill, and walked away.

Gabe lifted the Kharvos blade back onto its two pegs over the door, carefully, the way you’d put back something that belonged to somebody else.

Then he went home along the frozen quay, with the river black in the middle and white at the edges, and the stars coming out over Kingsferry in all the wrong places, the way they had for more than three years; and climbed in at the window Deston had left open, and slept.
