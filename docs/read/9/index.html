The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 1: Thirty-Four Winters

Chapter 9

Tracks

2,432 words · 11 min read

The first thing Hyvred loved about Thornwall was the sky.

There was so much of it. The fortress sat on a shoulder of rock at the edge of the Greyspine Mountains, with the peaks behind it going up and up into cloud and the valley in front of it falling away for miles, green and grey and dotted with sheep. And above all of it, every morning, the whole sky at once: edge to edge, horizon to horizon, wide and pale and impossibly there.

She’d stand on the eastern wall at dawn and just look at it. Every single day. Bee had stopped teasing her about it by the end of the first week.

The second thing she loved about Thornwall was Hild.

“Again,” said Hild, “with your eyes shut.”

“I can’t see with my eyes shut.”

“That’s the point.”

They were in the fortress yard, in the cold, in the third week. Hyvred was holding a wooden practice sword she had no intention of ever using properly; her path was going to be magic, she was fairly sure, and the sword was just for this. Hild stood a few paces away with a blindfold over her one good eye and a stick in her hand, and every time Hyvred moved, Hild knew.

“How?” said Hyvred, for the fourth time, picking herself up off the flagstones.

“Your boots scrape on the left. You breathe in before you move. Your horns catch the wind.” Hild tilted her head. “You’ve got a lot of tells.”

“Thanks.”

“Everyone has tells. Shut your eyes.”

Hyvred shut her eyes.

And it was, she discovered, almost easy. That was the strange part. The world went dark and small and close, and her body knew exactly what to do with that, because it had lived in a dark, small, close world for twenty-five years before this one. She knew how to listen. She knew how to turn her head toward a sound and hold still and wait for the room to tell her where things were. She’d been doing it her whole life, without ever thinking of it as a skill.

She heard Hild’s foot move on the stone. She stepped left.

Hild’s stick went past her shoulder.

There was a short silence.

“Huh,” said Hild.

Hyvred opened her eyes. Hild had pushed up her blindfold and was looking at her with the closest thing to surprise Hyvred had seen on that weathered face.

“I couldn’t see well,” Hyvred said. “Before. On Earth. Only a little circle in the middle. Everything else was dark.” She shrugged, suddenly shy. “So I listened.”

Hild looked at her for a long time.

“So you’ve been training for this,” she said at last, “your whole life.”

“I guess.”

“Good,” said Hild, and pulled her blindfold back down. “Again.”

Bee’s bird happened at the end of that week.

They were all in the long room at the top of the north tower that Corvin had given them, which had a fireplace and a table and a wall covered entirely in Corvin’s maps, with little pins in them that he wouldn’t explain. Kei was sitting by the window, having a low, ongoing argument with Hisui about mushrooms. Tiger was doing push-ups, because Tiger was always doing push-ups. Drew was asleep in the big chair, smiling, which he could do anywhere. And Bee was drawing.

She’d been drawing every night since they arrived. Her pens hadn’t come through, and neither had her tablet, so she drew with charcoal from the fire, on anything that would hold still: the hearthstone, the backs of Corvin’s discarded maps, the plaster wall by the door. She drew without talking, very fast, with her tongue between her teeth and the two Pollen Puffs perched on her shoulders, watching.

That night she drew a bird. A small one, a sparrow, in about six lines.

And it moved.

Hyvred saw it, because Hyvred saw everything now. The bird on the wall shivered. Its charcoal wings lifted, once, very slightly, as if it were about to take off. For one heartbeat, two, three, there was a faint thread of light running from the tip of Bee’s finger into the lines on the wall.

Then it was just charcoal again.

Bee had gone completely still.

“Did you—“ she said.

“Yes,” said Hyvred.

“Did anyone else—“

“It moved,” said Kei from the window, sounding stunned. “Hisui says it moved. He says he nearly ate it.”

Corvin, who’d been at the table with his maps, had stood up so fast he’d knocked over his chair. “That’s not possible,” he said. “Not at Initiate. Nobody can cast at Initiate. You can sense, you can’t—“

“Then it didn’t happen,” said Bee flatly.

“No, it happened, I just—“

“Then it’s possible.” Bee picked up the charcoal, wiped her fingers, and drew another bird next to the first. It didn’t move. She drew a third. It didn’t move either. She kept drawing them, one after another, until the wall by the door was covered in charcoal sparrows and her hands were black to the wrist, and none of them so much as twitched.

At last she sat back on her heels.

“Six percent,” she said, very quietly, looking at her window. “It was five this morning.”

Nobody said anything.

Then the Pollen Puffs both took off from her shoulders at once and flew in a small, delighted circle around her head, buzzing, and Bee laughed: short and startled and so unlike her that Hyvred, without thinking, went and hugged her, charcoal and all.

After that, the windows became a nightly thing, like it was for everyone, apparently, judging by the letters.

“Four,” said Tiger, sitting up from his push-ups and glaring at his window as if it had insulted him personally. “Four. I’m supposed to be fast. That’s my whole thing. I’m a tiger. And I can’t even feel my aura move yet.”

“It took Alea three years,” said Kei, who had heard that from somewhere. “Somebody’s letter said.”

“I don’t have three years!”

“Three,” said Drew, without opening his eyes. “And I’m fine with it.”

“How are you fine with it?”

“Because it’s going to take as long as it takes,” said Drew peacefully, “and being angry won’t make it take less.”

Tiger lay back down on the floor and stared at the ceiling. “I hate that you’re right.”

“Five,” said Kei. “And Hisui knows every plant in this valley now. He tasted his way round the whole kitchen garden. There’s a mushroom by the north gate that would kill a horse. He’s very proud of it.”

“Seven,” said Hyvred.

They looked at her.

“It’s the sight,” she said. “It’s getting stronger. At first I could only see the light in people. Now I can see it in everything. The sheep. The grass. Hild’s sword, a bit, where she’s held it for years.” She hesitated. “The mountains, when the sun’s on them. They’re so bright.”

“Sounds nice,” said Tiger, a little enviously.

“It is,” said Hyvred. “Mostly.”

She didn’t tell them about the other thing. Not yet. Corvin’s pins.

There were letters, too.

One from Chris, which was mostly about the arena at Valcrest and their guide Lucan, and contained a diagram of something he called “the crowd’s aggro table” that nobody understood. One from Hako, which was short and cheerful and said that everything was going to work out. And at the end of the fifth week, one from Yan to Kei, which Kei read aloud by the fire, and which made the room go very quiet.

The red ground. The rows of markers. A grave with flowers on it, set apart from the others. A long, deep hole in the earth at its head, where a black sword had stood for seven years before the Advisor’s men came with a winch and took it away.

“The sword in the hall,” said Tiger.

“The medium,” said Kei. “Thea wrote to Chris about it, and Chris told me. She says there’s a line in everyone’s window. Medium: one blade.”

“So they took it off a grave,” said Bee.

Nobody said anything for a while.

“Who was buried there?” asked Drew.

“A healer,” said Kei, reading. “That’s all the chaplain knows. Someone the man with the sword cared about.”

Hyvred looked at the fire, and thought about the Advisor, standing on his dais with almost nothing left of his light, because he’d poured it all into a circle in the floor to bring them here. She thought about how sorry he’d looked. She wondered whether he’d been sorry about them, or about the grave.

The pins on Corvin’s wall were tracks.

He told her in the sixth week, when she finally asked him straight out. He’d been waiting for her to ask; she could see it in his light, which jumped and settled like a candle every time she looked at the wall.

“Here,” he said, pointing. “And here, and here. Footprints, mostly, in the high passes. Not animal. Not quite human either. The shepherds find them in the snow.” He moved his finger. “And these are villages. Here, in the valley, where people have started acting… strangely.”

“Strangely how?”

Corvin hesitated. “Small things. A family that moved into an empty farmhouse and doesn’t go to market. A village that stopped keeping dogs. People who don’t go to the shrine any more and don’t say why.” He looked at her, almost pleading. “I know how it sounds. The court thinks I’m mad. I’ve been writing reports for two years. The Advisor is the only one who reads them.”

“Show me,” said Hyvred.

So the next morning they rode down into the valley: Hyvred and Corvin and Hild, with Bee insisting on coming too and nobody quite brave enough to tell her no. They went to a village called Coldwater, a cluster of stone houses around a well, with a little shrine at the end of the lane that had grass growing on its steps.

It was quiet. That was the first thing. A village in the middle of the morning should have been loud, and Coldwater wasn’t. There were people, going about their business, carrying water and mending a wall and hanging out washing. They nodded to Corvin, who they seemed to know. They looked at Hyvred’s horns with the ordinary mild curiosity of country people. Nobody stared.

Nobody had a dog.

Hyvred looked at all of them, the way she looked at everything now, with her whole new sight. And all of them were fine. Each of them carried that faint warm light under the skin, the same as everyone. Ordinary people with ordinary lives.

Until the well.

There was a man drawing water. Middle-aged, broad-shouldered, in a farmer’s smock, with a pleasant weathered face. He looked like everyone else in Coldwater.

But the light under his face was the wrong shape.

It was the only way she would ever be able to describe it. Everybody’s light fitted them: it followed the shape of their bodies, their faces, their hands, like warmth under skin. This man’s didn’t. It was there, but it was bigger than him, or a different size, or a different shape altogether, as if he were a coat that someone else was wearing. As if the face she could see was painted on the outside of something that didn’t have a face at all.

She stopped dead in the lane.

The man at the well turned his head and looked at her.

He didn’t look at her the way the other villagers had, at her horns or her clothes. He looked straight at her eyes, at the left one, the one with the red crosshair in it, as if he knew exactly what it could do. For one long second neither of them moved.

Then he smiled, pleasantly, lifted his bucket and walked away down the lane, and turned the corner, and was gone.

“Hyvred?” said Bee. “What?”

Hyvred found she was holding Bee’s sleeve hard enough to hurt.

“What did you see?” said Hild quietly, beside her. The guard hadn’t moved, but her hand had gone to the hilt of her sword.

“Someone,” said Hyvred, “wearing someone.”

Corvin had gone grey. “I knew,” he whispered. “Two years. I knew.”

That night she couldn’t sleep, so she went up on the wall.

It was very cold. The valley was black, and the mountains were blacker, and above them the stars were out in numbers she still couldn’t believe. The sentries were at the far corners, stamping their feet. Hild was there too, she found, sitting in an embrasure with her blindfold off and her face turned toward the valley, listening.

They didn’t talk. Hyvred sat beside her and looked out.

And on the ridge to the north, above the dark line of the trees, something was looking back.

She didn’t see it at first. That was the strange part; she’d always see light now, everywhere, in everything. The trees on the ridge had it, faintly. The rocks. The night itself, almost. She saw the shape only because it didn’t. On the skyline, where there should have been a faint shimmer of life like everywhere else, there was a figure-shaped absence. A hole in the night. Something standing very still, with no light at all, the way the man at the well had worn the wrong light.

Watching the fortress.

Watching her.

“Hild,” she breathed.

“I hear nothing,” said Hild, very low, without moving. “Only the wind.”

“It’s there. On the ridge. It’s—“ She didn’t have a word for it. “It’s just not there. Like someone cut it out.”

Hild didn’t look at the ridge. She didn’t need to. She shifted in the embrasure, very slightly, so that she was between Hyvred and the dark, and put her hand on her sword, and stayed there.

They waited.

After a long time, the hole on the ridge turned, and was gone.

She wrote to Yan before dawn, by the light of a candle in the long room, with the charcoal sparrows watching her from the wall.

She told him about the man at the well, and the hole on the ridge. She told him that Corvin had been right for two years and nobody had listened. She told him she was frightened, and that she was trying very hard not to be, and that Hild had said they would manage.

Then she wrote one more line, and underlined it twice, and sealed the letter before she could change her mind.

Tell Aldous they’re not only coming from the east.
