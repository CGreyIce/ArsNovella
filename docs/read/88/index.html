The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 3: The Anniversary

Chapter 88

Four Fish

3,702 words · 16 min read

Gigichi had never answered him.

Two words. Lowercase, the way he typed everything late at night. you okay? It had come in on a Tuesday, years ago, in a year when she’d gone quiet: streaming less, answering people less, drifting. Nobody else had noticed. Not Thea, who she’d drifted from. Not Xola, who was busy. Nobody. Just him. A name in her chat now and then, gifting subs to a stream with eleven people in it, and then, one night, two words.

She’d looked at them for a long time. She’d typed yeah lol and deleted it. She’d typed not really and deleted that too. She’d typed a long thing, a real thing, and looked at it, and closed the window without sending anything at all.

She’d meant to answer the next day. And the next. And then it had been a week, and it was too late to answer without explaining why she hadn’t, and she didn’t know how to explain, so she didn’t answer. And then it had been a month. And then he’d died.

She’d answered it, in the end. In the channel, in the summer, in the Ice book, when Hako asked. I never answered. I’m answering now. I’m okay. Mostly. She’d written it to a dead boy, in a place she’d thought only thirty-five people could see.

And three weeks ago, in the upper room, with the fire going, Thea and Higu had told her who else could see it.

She hadn’t slept properly since.

She didn’t sleep the night of the bonfire, either. Nobody did.

They’d sat up at the window until the sky went grey, the five of them, with Thea in the middle and the new word glowing in her window, Candle, and nobody saying very much. Watching the lake. The lamp at the end of the lake road had stayed lit all night. It didn’t usually. Usually it went out around midnight.

Gigichi had watched it, and thought about a man at the edge of the firelight, with snow on his shoulders, looking at each of them. Crying. Not wiping it away.

She’d thought: he saw me.

She’d thought: he read what I wrote.

She’d thought: he knows I never answered.

And she’d sat at the window with her knees drawn up and her arms round them and felt sick.

Maren didn’t make them drill. She came out into the yard at dawn, looked at the five of them standing there grey-faced and hollow-eyed in the snow, and said sit down before you fall down, and went back inside to make tea. Bram brought out a bench. They sat on it. In a row, in their coats, in the cold, the way they’d sat on a bench in the yard the first week, sixteen months ago, waiting to be told what they were.

Gigichi took her watch out.

She did that, when she couldn’t sit still. It was the only thing she’d brought through the floor that wasn’t a body or clothes: a gold pocket watch, old, with a leaf engraved on the lid, that had been in her inventory from the first morning in the castle, ticking. It kept the time of nowhere. It didn’t match the bells in Wrenhaven, or the sun, or anything. It just ticked. She opened it and closed it, and opened it and closed it.

“Chi,” said Higu, beside her. Very quietly. “You’ll break it.”

Gigichi closed it.

And then they heard Alea.

They heard her before they saw her. Feet on the hill road, in the snow, much too fast. Running. Somebody running up a hill in the snow with something heavy bouncing on their back and something in their arms, gasping.

They all stood up at once.

Alea came round the corner of the road and in through the gate at a dead run, with the black sword bouncing in its harness on her back and the basket clutched to her chest with both arms and her coat flying and her face blotched red and white and her ears straight up, and skidded to a stop on the packed snow in the middle of the yard, in front of the bench, and stood there, heaving for breath.

Nobody said anything.

She held out the basket.

Gigichi looked at it. Everyone did. It was the same basket. The one Alea had brought up the hill every morning for a year and more, with the lid on and straw inside and fish in the straw. Two, at first. Then three. Then four. Then five, since the autumn, since PenguChi went up a tier. Five every morning.

Alea took the lid off.

Gigichi counted. She couldn’t help it. She’d counted every morning for a year; they all had, without saying so.

One. Two. Three. Four.

Four.

Beside her, she heard Higu stop breathing.

“There’s four,” said Gigichi.

Her voice came out wrong. High and thin. She hadn’t meant to say it. It just came out of her, the way things did when she was frightened, and she was frightened, she realised. Suddenly and completely. The way you’re frightened when something you’ve been waiting for for a long time is about to happen and you’re not ready.

Alea looked at her. She was still breathing too hard to speak. She nodded.

“Why four?” said Gigichi. “It’s been five. Since the autumn. It’s always five, why is it—“

“Dad says,” said Alea.

She stopped. Took a breath. Started again.

“Dad says,” said Alea, “come for supper.”

Nobody moved.

The yard was completely silent. Gigichi could hear the snow settling on the roof of the hall. She could hear Bram, behind them in the doorway, put something down very slowly. She could hear her own heart.

“Tonight,” said Alea. Her voice was shaking. “At the cabin. All five of you. At dusk.” She looked at the basket in her hands. “The fifth fish is for supper. That’s why it’s four. He said…” She stopped. Her ears had gone flat. “He said to tell you your dad says come for supper. Except he meant me. He meant my dad. He got it wrong, he was nervous, he’s never nervous, he’s—“ She stopped again. “He’s been up all night. He’s cooking a stew that tastes like a fire in a swamp. He’s rehearsing your names to the hens.”

Xola burst into tears.

She did it the way Xola did everything, first, before anyone, all at once, with her hands over her face and Squiddles on her head going every colour Gigichi had ever seen him go, one after another, like a lamp being turned up and down.

PenguChi sat down. Not on the bench. On the edge of the frozen water trough behind it, slowly, as if her legs had decided for her. Waddles, in her arms, made a small surprised sound.

Higu closed her notebook. Very carefully. And put both hands flat on the cover, and held it shut, and didn’t open it again for the rest of the day.

Thea had gone white. Completely white. She was standing very still beside the bench, with her hands at her sides, looking at Alea. Not saying anything. Gigichi had seen her look like that once before. At the market, sixteen months ago, coming back up the hill with a basket of eggs, after she’d walked into a plain man in a plain coat and said trust, and he’d said there’s plenty of fish.

And Gigichi.

Gigichi heard herself say it. She didn’t decide to. It just came out, the way things did, in the voice she used when she was frightened and didn’t want anyone to know.

“Is it—“

She stopped.

She’d never said it out loud. None of them had. Not to Alea. Not in front of her. Rule five. Sixteen months.

Alea looked at her. Straight at her. With her mother’s eyes, that the king had said decided whether you were worth the trouble.

“Yes,” said Alea.

Gigichi sat down on the bench.

She didn’t mean to. Her knees just went, the way PenguChi’s had. She sat on the bench in the snow with her hands in her lap and the gold watch in one of them, and looked at the four fish in the basket, and couldn’t breathe.

Yes.

One word. From Alea. In the yard. In front of all of them. The first time anyone had ever said it out loud.

It didn’t feel the way she’d thought it would. She’d thought, if anyone ever said it, it would feel like the ground moving. Like something enormous. It didn’t. It felt very small and very quiet, like a key going into a lock. Like something that had been true for a long time finally being allowed to be true out loud.

She started laughing. She didn’t know why. It came out of her in a horrible wet hiccup.

“Is that,” said Gigichi, through it, “the dad who’s… or a different dad. Because I need to know. I need to be very sure which dad.”

Alea stared at her.

Then Alea laughed too. The same horrible wet hiccup. And sat down on the bench beside her, basket and sword and all, and put her face in her hands, and laughed, and Gigichi laughed, and Xola was still crying and started laughing as well, and PenguChi on the trough had her hand over her mouth, and Higu was smiling, actually smiling, with her hands still flat on her closed notebook, and Thea was standing in the middle of the yard with her face white and her eyes streaming, saying nothing at all.

“That one,” said Alea, into her hands. “That dad. The only one I’ve got.”

Maren was in the doorway.

Gigichi saw her when she looked up. She didn’t know how long she’d been there. Long enough. She was standing very straight, the way she always stood, in the doorway of the hall, with Bram behind her holding a bucket.

The five of them looked at her.

Gigichi knew what they were all thinking. Sixteen months of it. He is a fisherman, and nothing more. He is not to be disturbed. Maren had kept that order every single day. She’d said a fisherman and nothing else, and watched them work out everything she couldn’t tell them, and never once broken. Last night, at the fire, he’d stopped beside her, and said something, and Gigichi had seen her face afterwards.

Maren looked at the five of them. At Alea on the bench. At the four fish in the basket.

She didn’t say anything for a long time.

Then she said, quietly, the way she said everything:

“Go.”

Behind her, Bram made a sound like a cow in distress, and put his face in his bucket.

The day was the longest of Gigichi’s life.

Longer than the first day in the castle. Longer than the day the floor went soft. Longer than any day of the bad year, when the days had been so long she’d stopped counting them. She opened the gold watch and closed it, and opened it, and it said the same time it had said ten minutes ago, and she was sure it was broken, and it wasn’t.

Alea had gone back down the hill. I’ve got to clean, she’d said, wiping her face. He thinks the house is clean. It’s not clean. It’s fisherman clean. She’d looked at the five of them on the bench. Dusk. Don’t be early. He’ll die. And gone, at a run, down the hill road, with the sword bouncing.

They didn’t do anything. They couldn’t. They went inside and sat in the upper room and looked at each other, and looked out of the window at the lake, and didn’t talk much.

What to wear was a problem. It turned into the problem, around noon, because it was something to have a problem about. They didn’t have much. The clothes they’d come through in, which none of them could wear in this cold. The plain wool things from the castle stores. And the coats.

The coats. Five of them. Heavy wool, lined with fleece, mended in places. Rowan had brought them on a cart in the first winter, a gift from the town, and slipped. I told him—I told them.

Higu had said it at the time. Somebody measured us. With their eyes.

“We wear the coats,” said Thea.

Nobody argued.

What to say was the other problem. It was the problem that mattered.

Nobody talked about that one out loud. But Gigichi could see them all doing it. Xola on her cot with Squiddles, whispering things to him and then shaking her head. PenguChi with her eyes closed, saying something very quietly under her breath, over and over. Thea at the table with her window open and nothing in it, staring at it. Higu at the window, still with her hands on her closed notebook.

Gigichi went and sat on the stairs.

She sat on the third step from the top, the one that creaked, with her back against the wall and her knees up and the gold watch in her hands. And she tried to write it. Her answer. In her head. The way she’d tried to write it a hundred times in three years, at night, to a dead boy.

you okay?

Yeah. No. I’m fine. No. I wasn’t, then. I’m better now. No. I’m sorry I didn’t answer. I didn’t know how. I’d stopped knowing how to answer anyone. No. I’m okay. Mostly. She’d written that one already. He’d read it.

None of them were right. None of them had been right for three years.

She put her face on her knees.

Higu came and sat on the step below her.

Gigichi heard her come. She always did. Six years, she’d been hearing Higu come and sit down near her when she was like this. On Earth, it had been a call that came in at two in the morning with nobody on it, just Higu, not saying anything, letting the call sit there until Gigichi said something first. Here, it was a step.

Higu didn’t say anything. She sat on the step below, with her back against the wall, and looked at the opposite wall, and waited. The way she’d waited for six years.

“I don’t deserve to go,” said Gigichi, into her knees.

Higu didn’t say anything.

“I never answered him,” said Gigichi. “He asked if I was okay. When nobody else noticed. When I’d gone quiet and stopped answering people, he noticed, and he asked, and I never answered. Not once. And then he died, and I still didn’t, until I wrote it in the channel for a dead boy, and it turns out he’s not dead, he’s at the bottom of the hill, and he read it.” Her voice cracked. “And I wasn’t a good friend that year. To anyone. Not to Thea. Not to Xola. Not to him. I let everyone down and I let them think it was their fault, and Thea’s still a bit angry, and she’s right to be.” She took a breath. “And now he’s asked all five of us to supper. And I’m one of the five. And I don’t—“

She stopped.

“You don’t think you should be one of the five,” said Higu.

Gigichi didn’t answer.

Higu was quiet for a long time.

That was the thing about Higu. Six years, and she’d never once rushed. She’d never said the first thing that came into her head. She waited until she knew what was under the thing you’d said, and then she said that.

“You’re not frightened he won’t want you there,” said Higu at last. “You know he does. He sent you subs when you had eleven people in your chat. He asked if you were okay when nobody else noticed. He measured you for a coat with his eyes, on a market road, and sent it up the hill and made sure it was long enough in the arm.” She didn’t look round. “You’re not frightened of that.”

Gigichi lifted her head off her knees.

“You’re frightened,” said Higu, “that he’ll ask again.”

Gigichi went very still.

“That he’ll look at you at the door,” said Higu, “the way he looked at us last night, at the fire. And see you. And ask. You okay? Out loud. In front of everyone.” She was quiet. “And you’ll have to answer. And you still don’t know what to say.”

Gigichi didn’t say anything. She couldn’t.

“That’s what the panic’s covering,” said Higu. “Not I don’t deserve it. You’ve never once in six years thought you didn’t deserve something. You just say it, so you don’t have to say the other thing.” She turned her head, finally, and looked up at Gigichi on the step above her, with her calm half-lidded eyes. “I’ve known you six years, Chi. I know what your face does when you’re lying to yourself.”

Gigichi looked at her for a long time.

Then she put her face back on her knees, and laughed, a little, wetly.

“I hate you,” she said.

“I know.”

“Six years and you’ve never once let me get away with anything.”

“Somebody has to.”

“What do I say?” said Gigichi. “If he asks. What do I say?”

Higu thought about it. Properly. The way she thought about everything.

“The truth,” she said.

“I don’t know the truth. That’s the problem. I’ve been trying to work out the truth for three years. Was I okay? No. Am I okay now? I don’t know. Mostly. Sometimes. Today I feel sick.” Gigichi wiped her face on her sleeve. “That’s not an answer. That’s a mess.”

“Then say the mess,” said Higu. “He asked because he wanted to know. Not because he wanted a nice answer.” She was quiet. “He’s a man who sat on the end of a jetty every night for a year reading thirty-five people write about him, and never once wrote anything back, because he was frightened. He told Alea so. He’s frightened of our faces.” She looked at the wall again. “You think he’s going to mind if your answer’s a mess?”

Gigichi didn’t say anything.

Then, after a long time, she reached down, and put her hand on Higu’s shoulder. And left it there.

“You were,” she said. Very quietly.

Higu looked up.

“A good friend,” said Gigichi. “That year. When I wasn’t anyone’s. You were mine. You called at two in the morning and didn’t say anything.” She swallowed. “I never said thank you for that either.”

Higu looked at her for a long moment. Then she put her own hand up, over Gigichi’s, on her shoulder.

“You’re answering now,” said Higu.

Nobody posted anything in the channel all day.

Gigichi checked. She couldn’t help it. She opened her window every hour and scrolled back through the two hundred lines, and there it all was, the whole kingdom going on: Mashi with four heartbeats and a nosebleed, Maigo in capitals, Rachael’s patients, Chris’s evening numbers, Kei, Tiger, Dennis, Hana, a long argument between Newish and Malcolm about whether IF/THEN counted as a spell. Nobody from Wrenhaven. Not one line. Not since Higu’s three lines and Thea’s one, last night, about the fire.

Gigichi thought about a man at the end of a jetty, reading it. Seeing them say nothing. Again.

He’s waiting too, PenguChi had said. It’s the same wait. From the other side of the door.

Not tonight. Tonight somebody was going to open the door.

At dusk they put on the coats.

They stood in the yard in a row, in the blue light, in the snow, the five of them, in five heavy wool coats lined with fleece, mended in places, each one exactly the right length. Gigichi’s came down to her shins and was long enough in the arm. She’d never once thought about that, in a year of wearing it. She thought about it now. A man on a market road, sixteen months ago, looking at each of them, one after another, as if he were counting them.

Measuring.

Bram gave them lanterns. Five of them, lit, one each. He didn’t say anything when he handed them over. He’d said nothing all day, which was a record. He just gave each of them a lantern and then stood back by the door and blew his nose, enormously, into a handkerchief the size of a tablecloth.

Maren walked them to the gate.

She stopped there. She looked at the five of them, in their coats, with their lanterns. Gigichi had never seen her face like that. Not even this morning.

“Be back by—“ said Maren, and stopped.

They waited.

Maren looked down the hill, at the town, and past the town, at the lake. At the lamp at the end of the lake road, burning in the window, in the dusk, the way it had burned every night for a year.

“Be back whenever,” she said.

They went down the hill.

Five lanterns, in a row, in the snow, in the blue dusk, down the hill road into Wrenhaven. Past the church. Past the square, where the bonfire had been, a great black ring in the snow now, still faintly warm. The town was out. Of course it was. In doorways, at windows, along the street, in the cold, in silence, the way it had been for the king. Watching five girls from another world in five mended coats walk past with their lanterns.

Nobody said anything.

Gigichi walked between Higu and Thea, with her lantern in one hand and the gold watch in the other, in her pocket, ticking. She didn’t look at the townspeople. She looked straight ahead, at the corner at the bottom of the square where the lake road went down past the church.

Where a big grey man in a mayor’s chain had stood every day for eight years, and every day for sixteen months that she’d been here, at the top of the road, in front of it.

She didn’t know, yet, that he wasn’t standing there.

She found out when they came round the corner.
