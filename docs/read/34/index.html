The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 2: Letters

Chapter 34

Spring on the Redmarch

2,424 words · 11 min read

The thaw came to the Redmarch in a week of grey rain, and when it stopped, Salty went out to the smithy and picked up the anvil.

He hadn’t meant to. That was the thing he kept saying afterwards, to everyone, including the smith, who was very upset. He’d gone in to collect a whetstone. The anvil had been in the way, on the floor by the door, where the smith’s boy had left it after moving it to sweep. Salty had bent down without thinking, the way you’d bend down to move a chair, and put his hands under it, and lifted.

It came up off the floor.

He stood there in the doorway of the smithy holding a solid iron anvil at chest height, and the smith turned round from the forge and dropped his tongs.

“That,” said the smith, very quietly, “weighs as much as a horse.”

Salty looked down at it. At his own hands, wrapped round the horn and the heel. At his arms, which were big, they’d always been big, but not that big. He could feel something in them. In his back, in his legs, in the soles of his feet: a heat, steady and deep, like a stove with the door shut.

“Huh,” he said.

He put it down. Gently. It still shook the floor.

“Tempered,” said Rhea, that afternoon, in the practice yard, with her arms folded and a look on her face that Salty had never seen before. It looked suspiciously like envy. “That’s what that is. That’s second-tier. Aura hardening the body. You can’t do that yet. You’re not even Drawn.”

“I did it, though.”

“I know you did it. The whole fort knows you did it. The smith’s telling everyone.” She glared at him. “You’re at what, twenty-six?”

“Twenty-seven.”

“Twenty-seven percent of the first tier, and you lifted an anvil like you were second.” She threw up her hands. “It doesn’t make sense.”

“It’s Thea’s letter,” said Mashi, from the fence.

They both looked at him.

“Thea’s letter. In the winter.” Mashi was sitting on the top rail with his chin in his hand, watching Salty the way he watched everything: carefully, filing it away. “She said you can’t get over the edge by reading. You have to write. Put something back into the world.” He tilted his head. “Salty’s never read anything in his life. He just does things. So when he finally does write, it comes out big and stupid and all at once.”

“Thanks,” said Salty.

“It’s a compliment.”

“It didn’t sound like one.”

“Most of my compliments don’t,” said Mashi cheerfully.

It had been like that since the winter. Since Thea’s letter, which had come to every group at once, four pages long, explaining how she’d crossed the edge at the bonfire in Wrenhaven. They’d read it in the corner of the mess hall, all five of them, passing it round, and then sat in silence for a long time.

You have to write. One small line. In its language, not yours.

Since then, things had been happening.

Maigo had knelt in the yard one morning with his bare wooden toes in the thawing mud and asked it, he said, the way Thea had asked the fire: just asked, politely, for one small thing. And a green shoot had come up out of the mud between his feet, a single blade of grass, bright and new, in the middle of a fort where nothing had grown in seven years. It had lasted an hour before the red ground choked it. But it had come up.

Wendy had stopped aiming. That was how she put it. She’d stand at the line with her bow drawn and her silent headphones on, and she’d stop deciding where the arrow should go, and just agree with it. Let it go where it already wanted to. Her arrows had started doing things arrows shouldn’t do: curving, very slightly, round the edge of a post to hit the target behind it. Rhea had seen it happen once and refused to talk about it for a day.

And Yan had started to feel the living.

Not just the dead, out on the red ground under the markers. The living. The garrison. The cooks and the smiths and the soldiers on the wall. Each of them a small warm presence he could feel if he closed his eyes, the way you can feel the warmth of a fire without looking at it. He’d told them, the night it started, sitting in the corner of the mess hall with Suzu wound loose around his neck. He’d said it slowly, the way Yan said everything, as if he were making sure each word was the right one before he let it go.

“I can feel all of you,” he’d said. “Right now. All five of you. Like candles.”

“What do we feel like?” Maigo had asked at once.

Yan had looked at each of them in turn.

“Maigo’s like a tree in the sun,” he’d said. “Wendy’s like a candle behind glass. Mashi’s two candles that keep almost touching.” He’d looked at Salty last. “Salty’s like a forge.”

Salty had liked that one. He’d thought about it a lot since.

Aldous called them into the chapel at the end of the first week of spring.

He was sitting on the front bench, where he always sat, with a letter in his lap and the long grey light coming in through the windows. He looked older than he had in the winter. Thinner. Salty had noticed that, over the months. The chaplain hadn’t been sleeping well since the scouts.

“The anniversary,” said Aldous, when they’d all sat down. “I’ve had word from the Advisor. It’s set.”

Nobody said anything.

“The end of summer. The last week. Eight years, by then, since the night of the battle.” Aldous turned the letter over in his hands. “The sword will come east with an escort from the capital. Six soldiers and the winch, the same as it went. As far as Cinderhold. And then it’s ours.” He looked up at them. “The last part, from here to the grave, has to be done by hand. There’s no road across the red. The winch can’t come. Somebody has to carry it.”

They all looked at Salty.

They’d been looking at Salty every time the sword came up, since the autumn. He was used to it by now.

“Before,” said Aldous gently, “I hoped one of you could lift it by then. Now I’ve heard about the smith’s anvil.” The faintest smile. “I think I can stop hoping.”

“How heavy is it?” said Salty.

“Heavier than the anvil.”

“How much heavier?”

Aldous thought about it. “I watched six soldiers carry it out of the ground,” he said. “With ropes and poles and a great deal of swearing. Two of them were Gleaming.” He paused. “And the man who planted it there carried it alone, across the whole of the Redmarch, on the morning after the battle. On his back. Like a man carrying firewood.”

Nobody said anything for a while.

“Who was he?” said Wendy. She asked it flatly, the way she asked everything. “The man. The Black Blade.”

“I’ve told you what I know,” said Aldous. “He was a swordsman. He loved the healer. He went into the red on the second night, alone, and the general fell, and in the morning the sword was standing over her grave.”

“And the horns,” said Maigo. “Over the gate. You said only one man alive could have cut those off.”

“Yes.”

“So he’s alive,” said Salty. “Somewhere. And he sent those. And he’s the one who carried the sword.”

“Yes.”

“And we’re carrying it back to her.” Salty was looking at his own hands. “To his… to her grave. The person he loved.”

“Yes,” said Aldous, very quietly.

Salty sat there with that for a while.

He went out to the grave the next day. Alone. He didn’t tell anyone he was going, not even Wendy, which he’d hear about later.

It was an hour’s walk across the red, past the long rows of Aldous’s markers, to the low rise above where the river had once run. The ground was soft with the thaw and the red came up through the mud like rust through paint. Nothing grew. Nothing moved. The sky was grey and very big.

And on the rise, by the cairn, the flowers had come back.

He hadn’t seen them in the autumn. He’d only heard Yan describe them: little white and blue flowers, low to the ground, coming up out of the dead red earth where nothing else would grow. They were just starting now, the first few, tight green buds with a hint of white at the tips. A dozen of them. In a rough ring round the cairn, as if somebody had planted them very carefully a long time ago, and come back every year since to make sure they were still there.

At the head of the cairn, the long narrow hole where the sword had stood was full of rainwater.

Salty stood there for a long time.

He wasn’t good with feelings. Everyone knew that. He said things how they were and let other people worry about how they felt. Wendy said he had the emotional range of a brick, and she said it lovingly, mostly. But standing on the rise in the grey light, looking at the flowers and the hole full of water, he felt something he didn’t have words for. Something big and quiet and heavy, like the anvil.

Somebody had loved her. Enough to plant flowers in ground where nothing grew. Enough to carry a sword across a battlefield on his back like firewood, and leave it standing over her, and come back every year. And then somebody else had come with a winch and six men and pulled the sword out of the ground and carried it off to a castle to pull thirty-five strangers out of the sky.

And now Salty was going to carry it back.

He knelt down beside the cairn. He didn’t know why. He wasn’t religious. He’d never knelt at a grave in his life. He just knelt, in the red mud, and looked at the little stones stacked so carefully on top of each other, and at the buds.

“Who were you?” he said, out loud, to nobody.

The wind came across the red and didn’t answer.

He stayed there until the light started to go. Then he got up, and brushed the mud off his knees, and walked back to Cinderhold the long way, past every one of the markers, reading the names on the ones that had names.

Wendy was waiting for him at the gate, under the horns, with her arms folded.

“You went out there,” she said. “Alone.”

“Yeah.”

“Without telling anyone.”

“Yeah.”

She looked at him for a long moment. He braced himself. Then she unfolded her arms and stepped forward and put her forehead against his chest, which was as high as it reached, and stood there.

“Don’t do that again,” she said, into his coat.

“Okay.”

“I mean it. There are things out there that count forts.”

“I know.”

She didn’t move. He put his arms round her. Above them, the general’s horns hung black and cracked against the grey sky, and somewhere in the fort the evening bell was ringing.

“What was it like?” she said after a while.

Salty thought about it. He wanted to get it right.

“There’s flowers,” he said. “Coming back. Little ones. Somebody planted them.” He was quiet a moment. “I kept thinking: whoever he was, he loved her a lot. To do that. And we’re going to carry his sword back to her.” He shook his head. “I want to know who she was. Before I do it. I don’t know why. It just feels like I should know.”

Wendy pulled back and looked up at him. One amber eye and one violet, the way they always were, and both of them, for once, without any cynicism in them at all.

“Then ask,” she said. “When it’s time. Ask whoever’s there.”

“Who’s going to be there?”

“I don’t know,” said Wendy. “But somebody always is, at a grave. That’s what graves are for.”

That night, in the corner of the mess hall, they did their numbers.

“Thirty-one,” said Maigo. “And the grass came up again this morning. Two blades this time.”

“Twenty-eight,” said Wendy. “The arrow curved round the post twice today.”

“Twenty-nine,” said Yan.

“Twenty-seven,” said Salty. “And the anvil.”

“We know about the anvil,” said everyone.

Mashi was last. As always.

“Nine,” he said. “And nine.”

They all looked at him. Nine and nine. He’d been at four and four in the winter. He’d more than doubled both of them since the thaw.

“Thea’s letter,” said Mashi, before anyone could ask. He opened his window and looked at it with that small private smile he got sometimes, the one that meant he’d figured something out and wasn’t going to tell anyone yet. “You have to write, she said. One line.” He closed it. “I’ve been trying to write two. One in each language. At the same time.” He shrugged. “It’s slower. But it’s working.”

“Does it hurt?” said Yan quietly. “Like before?”

“A bit,” said Mashi. “Less than before.” He looked at Yan. “You said I feel like two candles that keep almost touching.”

“You do.”

“Tell me when they touch,” said Mashi. “I want to know the second it happens.”

Yan looked at him for a long time. Then he nodded, slowly, the way he did when he was making a promise.

“I’ll tell you,” he said.

Outside, the rain had started again, soft and steady on the red ground, and over the gate the horns of a dead general hung in the dark, keeping watch. Salty sat in the warm corner of the mess hall with his friends around him and his hands, which had lifted an anvil, folded on the table. He thought about a ring of white flowers coming up in ground where nothing grew. He thought about the end of summer, and a sword heavier than a horse, and a long walk across the red.

Who were you?

He’d ask, when it was time. Wendy was right. Somebody would be there.

He didn’t know yet that it would be the man who’d planted the flowers.
