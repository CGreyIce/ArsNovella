The third ring came down the valley in the night, and at dawn it was standing at the bridge.

Tiger heard it from Hyvred before he saw it. He was asleep, for once; he’d slept badly since the balance and then well, the way you do after a long time of sleeping badly, all at once and very deep. Drew woke him. A hand on his shoulder in the dark, and Drew’s voice, low and flat in the way it went when he was being very careful.

“Tiger. Get up. Hyvred wants you on the wall.”

“Is it—“

“It’s not the call,” said Drew. “Nobody’s cold. Come and see.”

Hyvred was on the north wall, not the tower. That was the first wrong thing. She was standing at the parapet over the arch beside Hild, in Bee’s coat, with her hands flat on the stone the way Hild’s were, and her face was doing something Tiger had never seen it do. She didn’t look round when he came up the stair. She just put her hand out, sideways, without looking, and he went and stood under it, and she put it on his shoulder.

“Look,” she said.

He looked.

It was grey, just. The first grey, the hour he ran. The valley going north from the wall in the half-light, brown and green, with the swept road up the middle of it, clean, like a line drawn with a ruler. The millstream on his left, loud, white where it went under the bridge. The bridge, a quarter of a mile out. The milestone beside it, grey and leaning.

And on the far side of the bridge, standing across the swept road from one bank of the millstream to the other, people.

Thirty. Forty. He couldn’t count. In a long curved line across the road and into the fields on either side, the way they’d stood in the rings at the top of the valley, eleven miles off, all winter and all spring. Coats. Shawls. A soldier’s grey. Hands open at their sides. Facing the town.

And in the middle of the line, on the road, nearest the bridge, a boy with freckles.

Tiger stopped breathing.

“The third ring,” said Hyvred. Very quietly, beside him, with her hand on his shoulder. “All of it. It came down in the night. It left the others and walked down the road, eleven miles, at a walk, and nobody saw till I came up at the first hour.” She swallowed. “I was asleep. I’m sorry. It was Hild’s watch. Hild heard it. She came and got me.”

“The mountains went quiet,” said Hild. She didn’t look round either. She was looking at the bridge with her one eye. “And then the road. Feet. A lot of them. Not marching. Walking. Like people coming home from market.” She was quiet. “I’ve never heard them walk before. Not since the autumn.”

Tiger looked at Ossie.

A quarter of a mile. He could see him. Not his face; it was too far and too grey. But the shape of him. Big and loose, in the Western Watch grey with the collar up, the way he’d always worn it, Dunmore’s tenth; standing on the road a pace in front of the others, on the far side of the bridge, with his hands open.

Facing the gate.

“Why,” said Tiger. It came out of him like a stone.

Nobody answered.

“There’s more,” said Hyvred. “At the top. One more came down the switchbacks last night, at the same time. A woman. Twenty-seven, in the curve.” She took a breath. “And when she got there, the third ring turned round and came down here. Like they’d been waiting for her. Like that was the signal.”

“Four left,” said Hild. “On the far side.”

Tiger didn’t hear her. He was looking at the bridge.

At the bridge. His bridge. The milestone, and the parapet he’d put his hand flat on every morning since the thaw at the end of the race, wet with the spray of the millstream, shaking very faintly. Where Drew stood in the middle with his hands in his pockets and waited for him to come back.

They were standing on the other side of it. A pace off the end. Ossie nearest.

As if they knew.

“It’s built so you’d go,” said Xav.

He’d come up the stair behind them with the rest of Group 6, and Petra, and Kei with Hisui’s green head up beside his ear tasting the air. They stood along the wall in the grey. Nobody had said anything for a long time.

“I know,” said Tiger.

“Everything it’s done since the spring,” said Xav. “The road. The eight. The balance going over us. And now this.” He looked at the bridge. “It’s brought him to the bridge. Your bridge. The far end of your race. The place you turn round.” He was quiet. “It knows where you turn round, Tiger.”

“I know.”

“So don’t go to the bridge,” said Rui Xiang. Fast and very clear, the way he always said things. He was at Tiger’s other side. He’d been a hand away from Tiger all winter; he was a hand away now. “Not today. Run the old race. The well to the post. Don’t go out of the postern.”

Tiger didn’t say anything.

“Kei,” said Drew. “Hisui.”

Kei listened. The snake’s tongue went in and out.

“He says they don’t taste like anything,” said Kei. “Same as always. The turned. Like somebody wearing somebody.” He paused. “He says there’s no call. Not in the air. It’s quiet.” Another pause. “He says that’s worse.”

“Why is it worse?”

“Because they came down without it,” said Kei. “They walked eleven miles without being called. He says something else is moving them. Not the call. Something underneath it.” He shook his head. “He doesn’t know what. He says it tastes like a hand on a shoulder.”

Tiger looked at the bridge.

The light was coming. He could see more now. The line across the road, forty of them, a little curved. The soldier’s grey. The shawls. And Ossie in the middle, a pace in front of the others, big and loose, with his collar up.

His head was turned. A hair. Toward the gate.

The way it had been turned all winter, eleven miles off, every morning when Tiger’s hand hit the post.

“It’s my race,” said Tiger.

Drew looked at him.

“It’s my race,” said Tiger again. “Every morning. Since before the autumn. He comes to the gate; that was the deal. I said, race you to the gate, and he came to the gate, and I won.” He looked at the bridge. “And then he went up the valley and I said tomorrow every morning, and he turned, and I ran there and back. And now he’s come to the bridge.” He stopped. “He’s come to the end of the race. He’s standing where I turn round.”

“Tiger,” said Drew.

“I’m not going to him,” said Tiger. “I’m not stupid. I know what it is.” He didn’t look away from the bridge. “But if I don’t run, he’s standing there for nothing. And if I run the old race and don’t go out of the postern, it’s not my race. It’s me running away from him round the town.” He swallowed. “I’ve never once run away from him.”

Nobody said anything.

“Drew,” said Tiger.

Drew didn’t answer for a long time.

He looked at the bridge. He looked at Ossie on the far side of it. He looked at Tiger. And Tiger watched him do the thing he did, the counter-fighter, the one who read people: read the whole of it, the bridge and the line and the boy and Tiger, all at once, and come out the other side of it with something decided.

“Okay,” said Drew.

“Drew,” said Rui Xiang.

“I’ll be on the bridge,” said Drew. “In the middle. Where I always am.” He looked at Tiger. “You go to the bridge and you come back to me. Not to them. Not to the post. To me. Same as every morning.” He paused. “And if you don’t, I’m right there. I’ll be a pace from you. I’ll put my hands on you. Counter works through anybody touching me.” He held up his hands. The bladed knuckles, wrapped in cloth, the way he always had them now. “I sent it back down your arm once, in the autumn. Into him and the rings and the thing at the top. I can do it again.”

“You broke three ribs doing it.”

“They knitted,” said Drew. “That’s what ribs are for.”

Tiger looked at him.

“Ninety-two,” said Drew.

“What?”

“My bar. You asked me at the thaw and I wouldn’t say. Ninety-two.” Drew put his hands down. “It’s been going up since the field. Every time I stood somewhere and didn’t move and somebody came back to me. Ninety at the thaw. Ninety-two after the bridge.” He looked at the bridge, at the thing on the far side of it. “So I’m going to go and stand on it. And you’re going to come back. And it’s going to go up again.” He almost smiled. “That’s the race, Tiger. That’s always been the race. You go there. I stand. You come back.”

He ran it at the first grey, the way he always did.

He didn’t change anything. That mattered. He went down to the well, through the guardhouse yard, where Petra was standing with her stick and didn’t say a word, and put his hand on the rim of the well, flat, cold and wet. And waited. The bit before. The held breath. He could feel the whole town holding it with him; he could feel them on the wall, all of them, Group 5 and Group 6 and Hild and Hyvred and the Watch, and the lamps going out one by one down the long street the way they did every morning at the first grey, and a lot of faces in a lot of windows.

He could feel the bridge. A quarter of a mile north, past the wall. Ossie on the far side of it.

He could feel Drew walking out to it. Through the postern, which Old Pruett had unbarred for him with his face saying everything, on to the swept road, a quarter of a mile at a walk, not hurrying. On to the bridge. To the middle. Stopping there, with the millstream roaring under him and forty people standing a pace off the far end, and turning round, with his back to them, to face the town.

Drew had his back to them. Tiger felt it. The one who stayed, standing in the middle of the bridge with his back to the thing that had called the whole valley, facing the way Tiger would come.

Okay, thought Tiger.

He ran.

Well. Guardhouse lane. The corner by the Bell, where the new slates were. Up the long street with the lamps going out, past the farrier’s, past Joss with his broom by the long-room door, who didn’t say anything and didn’t sweep. Through the arch. To the post.

Flat hand on the wood. The old black oak inside the arch, wet, with two marks on it: his, worn deep, and a fainter one beside it from a man with a rod who’d put his hand there once in the winter and said nothing.

“Tomorrow,” said Tiger.

He didn’t need Hyvred. He felt it. A quarter of a mile off, on the far side of the bridge, a boy with freckles turned his head toward the gate.

Not a hair. The whole way.

And then the postern. Out, on to the swept road, clean and hard under his feet, wrong, the way it always felt. The bridge ahead. Drew in the middle of it with his back to the line. The milestone. The millstream white.

And Bolt came up in his legs.

He’d felt it every morning. The spring wound tight, the held breath, the thing that had always been in him, and had had a name for a long time now. Go. He let it come, a little. Enough to run. He’d always let it come a little; that was the race, from the bridge to the post and back to the bridge, at a run, not full. Never full. Not since the field.

He was halfway to the bridge when Ossie said his name.

Not the call. Tiger knew the call; he’d heard it say his name in the lanes every step of the battle, sweet, you came all the way, you were always going to come. It wasn’t that. It was hoarse and stupid and too loud, the way it had always been, across a yard, across a field, at the end of a race.

Tiger.

His own voice. Ossie’s.

Race you.

And Bolt went.

All of it. He didn’t decide. He hadn’t decided anything; it just went, the whole of it, out of the bottom of him into his legs, the way it had gone on the night he’d gone over the north wall in the dark, the way it had gone in the field. Full. He’d never once been able to hold it at full. Nobody could. He was the fastest of the thirty-five and at full Bolt the world went past him like water past a stone and there was nothing in it but the place he was going.

The bridge came at him.

Drew in the middle of it. He saw Drew’s face. Drew had turned round. Not to the line; to him. Drew was standing in the middle of the bridge with his feet planted and his hands up and open, the bladed knuckles, and his face doing nothing, nothing at all, the way it did when he’d read the whole of a thing and decided.

And past Drew, a pace off the far end of the bridge, Ossie, with his hand out.

Open. Palm up. The way it had been in the field, in the third ring, in the autumn, when Tiger had put his hand flat on it and said I won. Waiting.

It was downhill. That was what it felt like. Like the field. Like running downhill, faster and faster, so that stopping was a thing your legs had forgotten how to do. You came all the way. Not the call. Something under it. A hand on a shoulder. You were always going to come.

He was ten paces from the bridge. Five.

Going’s easy, said Drew. Not out loud. On the bridge, in the winter, with the millstream going under. You just have to be brave.

Coming back’s the hard bit.

He turned.

There wasn’t any way to do it. He knew that, afterwards. Rui Xiang told him, and Xav, and Petra, who’d watched it from the wall and gone white. There wasn’t any way for a body going at full Bolt down a road to stop and turn round. Not in the space he had. Not in a pace. A horse couldn’t. A hare couldn’t. You’d break. Everything in your legs would go, the knees and the ankles and the long bones, all at once, like a cart hitting a wall.

He didn’t think about it. He didn’t think about anything. He was going at full Bolt toward a boy with his hand out, and he wanted to be somewhere else more than he wanted to be brave.

He put his foot down. And turned.

All of it went into him.

The whole of the run, the whole of Bolt, the quarter of a mile at full and everything behind it since the field, every morning, every race, every tomorrow; it went into the foot he’d put down, and up the leg, into the knee and the hip and the spine. All the speed he had, stopped in a pace. He felt it. He felt it go in like a hammer into iron.

And it didn’t break him.

It went in and rang. That was all. A deep ring, all through him, from the sole of his boot to the tips of his ears, like a bell when the hammer hits it; and then it was gone, and he was standing on the swept road three paces from the end of the bridge, turned round, facing the town, with his tail out straight behind him and both legs whole.

He’d stopped.

He’d stopped at full Bolt. In a pace. He’d turned round.

And he ran back.

Not at full. Not at anything. He didn’t know how fast. He ran back down the swept road toward the town, toward the postern, with the millstream roaring on his left; and behind him he felt Drew on the bridge, Drew who’d had his hands up to catch him and hadn’t needed to, turning round too.

And behind Drew, a pace off the end of the bridge, he felt Ossie.

Turn.

After him. The way he had in the thaw, the first time, a hair. Not a hair now. The whole of him. A boy with freckles turning on the swept road to watch Tiger go, with his hand still out, open, and his face, for one heartbeat, his own.

Big, and loose, and a bit stupid. The grin of a boy who’s lost a race.

He put his hand flat on the post.

Wet. Two marks. His own, worn deep, and a fainter one beside it.

He stood there with his palm on the oak and his breath going and the whole of the north wall above him silent. And he didn’t say anything. He didn’t need to. He’d said tomorrow already, on the way out. This was the other half.

Drew came through the postern a long time after, at a walk, with his hands in his pockets.

He didn’t say anything either. He came and stood beside Tiger in the arch, a pace off, on his left, the side Tiger always forgot, and looked at the post.

“You came back,” he said at last.

“I came back.”

“At full.”

“I didn’t decide it,” said Tiger. “He said my name. His voice. Not the other one. Race you. And it just went.” He looked at his hand on the post. “All of it. I’ve never been able to hold it at full. Nobody can.”

“I know,” said Drew.

“And I turned round.” Tiger’s voice did something he didn’t expect. “At full. In a pace. You can’t. Rui Xiang’s going to tell me you can’t. It should’ve broken everything.”

“It didn’t.”

“It rang,” said Tiger. “Like a bell. All through me. And then I was standing there.”

Drew looked at him. At his legs, which were whole. At his tail, which was still out straight behind him and hadn’t come down yet.

“Open your window,” he said.

He looked at it for a long time, standing in the arch with his hand on the post.

Back.

He didn’t need anybody to tell him what it meant. He’d done it every morning all winter and all spring. Since the field, since Drew on his knees with his ribs in pieces saying there and back, you’ve done there, now you have to do back, that’s the half nobody ever does. Every morning, on his own, in the dark, with nobody watching. The well to the post and back to the well. The bridge to the post and back to the bridge. And his bar had moved only for back. Never for there.

Tempered. Aura hardens the body.

He’d always thought it meant harder. For hitting things. For being hit. That was what it was, for Gabe, and Jared, and Milli off his roof, and Hako under his hammer.

It wasn’t, for him. For him it was the thing you needed to turn round. You couldn’t stop at full speed with legs made of bone. Nobody could. The faster you went, the harder it was to come back, until at full there wasn’t any way at all. You just went, and went, and the thing you were running at got you.

Unless the body could take it. All of it. The whole run, stopped in a pace.

He’d been building it all winter without knowing. Every morning. Every back. The body learning, a hair at a time, to take the turn.

“Back,” he said. To Drew.

“Yeah,” said Drew. “That’d be it.”

Up on the wall, somebody started to laugh. Tiger looked up. It was Hyvred. Standing at the parapet over the arch in Bee’s coat with her hands over her face, laughing, the way she’d laughed at the king and the shield boss, with tears coming out between her fingers. And Bee beside her, both wings half up, the way they went when she forgot she couldn’t fly.

The third ring stood at the bridge all day.

Nobody went out to them. They didn’t come any closer. Forty people in a curved line a pace off the far end of the bridge, facing the town, with their hands open, and a boy with freckles in the middle with his face turned, the whole way, toward the gate.

Tiger sat on the gate step with Drew and didn’t go to them.

He wanted to. All day. He’d always want to; he knew that now. There’d never be a morning when he didn’t. But he sat on the step with Drew on his left and Rui Xiang a hand away on his right and his legs out in front of him, whole, still ringing a little if he pressed on the knee, and let himself want to, and didn’t go.

At dusk, the third ring turned round.

All of them. At once. The way they’d turned at the top of the valley every time something finished. And walked back up the swept road, north, at a walk, like people going home from market. Eleven miles. Tiger watched them go till he couldn’t see them, and then Hyvred told him. Past the milestone. Past the fields. Up the valley in the dark. Back into the rings, into the third one, on the north side, every one in their place.

And Ossie last.

Tiger read it on Ossie’s shed roof. The third shed, the one that had stood two years, with the roof Ossie’s father had come all the way from Harrowford to put on. The moss was green under him. The valley went north in the starlight, and the swept road, and the bridge, empty now, and past it all the dark at the top of the valley where the pass was.

Drew was lying on his back beside him with his hands behind his head.

“It brought him to the end of the race,” said Tiger. “To where I turn round.”

“Yeah.”

“To see if I would.”

“Yeah,” said Drew. “I think so.”

“And I did.” Tiger looked north. “And it let go. Hisui said. Of the shoulder.”

Drew didn’t answer for a long time.

“You know what I think,” he said at last.

“What.”

“I think it’s never seen anybody come back.” Drew was looking at the stars. “Not once. Everybody it’s ever called went. The whole valley. Four hundred people. The miller’s widow and the boy soldier and Elin and the Valcrest thirty-nine and the eight. They all went up the road and none of them came back. Except two. Rhys and Olwen. And they didn’t come back by themselves; Kei’s doses did it.” He was quiet. “It doesn’t know what back is. It doesn’t have the word. It’s all come.” He turned his head. “And then you, at full, three paces from the bridge. It’s never seen anything do that.”

Tiger didn’t say anything.

“It let go because it didn’t know what else to do,” said Drew. “That’s what I think. It doesn’t mean anything. I’m just saying it.”

“You say where everybody’s going.”

“I say where everybody’s going,” said Drew. “And you came back.” He shut his eyes. “Ninety-three, by the way. Since about seven this morning. I didn’t have to touch you. First time.” He was quiet. “I think that’s what it’s measuring, for me. Not whether I can catch you. Whether I can stand on a bridge and not have to.”

Tiger lay back on the moss beside him.

Above them the stars. North of them the valley, the rings, the curve, twenty-seven holes in a line and four more on the far side of the pass, waiting. And in the third ring, on the north side, eleven miles off, a boy with freckles in a soldier’s grey coat with the collar up, standing with his hands open, with his face turned toward a gate he’d walked eleven miles to stand near and eleven miles back from.

“Tomorrow,” said Tiger. To the dark.

He knew it was a promise. He’d always known it was a promise. Tonight, for the first time, he thought it might be one he could keep.

At the first grey he got up and went down to the well and put his hand flat on the rim, cold and wet, and ran.

There. And back.
