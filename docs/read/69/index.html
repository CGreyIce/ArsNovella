The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 3: The Anniversary

Chapter 69

Stones

4,463 words · 19 min read

Rowan Hale had been standing in front of things for most of his life.

That was what the job was, mostly. People thought being mayor meant chains and speeches and arguing about the price of barley, and it did, some of it. But mostly it meant standing in front of things. In front of the council when it wanted something stupid. In front of the tax men from the capital. In front of the town, when the town wanted to go somewhere it shouldn’t. You stood there, big and slow and grey, and you were too much trouble to go round, and after a while they stopped trying.

He’d learned it before he was mayor. He’d learned it at a cabin door by a lake, eight years ago, with a sword in his hand and a little fox girl asleep upstairs, while a war happened two hundred miles away. He’d stood in that door every night for a season. Nothing had come. He’d stood there anyway.

And then one morning in the last week of summer, the girl’s father had come home alone, across the fields, with nothing on his shoulder and his face like a house with the fire gone out. And Rowan had looked at him, and moved out of the doorway, and never really stopped standing in front of things since.

Nobody follows us down. He’d said it a thousand times. To curious traders. To pilgrims who’d heard a song. To a bard from the south who’d come asking, with a lute, and gone home without his lute, though that hadn’t been Rowan’s doing exactly. To the whole of Wrenhaven, without ever saying it out loud, until it was just a thing the town knew, the way it knew which end of the lake froze first. The fisherman was a fisherman. You said good morning to him at the market, and you let him buy his eggs, and you didn’t ask. And you didn’t go down the lake road.

He’d made it stick for eight years. He was proud of that, in a quiet way. It was the only thing he’d ever been able to give the man.

The morning after the king came, the whole town was out of its mind.

Rowan had expected that. He’d been expecting it since the seventh, when he’d gone and stood at the south gate. He’d known Wrenhaven would lose its head, and it had: there were people in the market square at dawn who had no business in the market square at dawn, standing about in knots, talking in low voices, all of them with their faces turned toward the lake road. Edric the baker had burned two trays. The miller’s wife had come in from the mill for no reason at all. Three separate people had already been to the town hall to ask Odette if it was true about the lace.

Odette was not taking it well.

“One blanket,” she said. She was at her desk with her keys and her ledgers and her face very tight. “One. The plainest. He sent the rest back with the boy. With his thanks.”

“He’s a plain man, Odette.”

“He’s the king.”

“He was a smith first,” said Rowan. “Ars said.”

Odette looked at him over the ledger. Then she put her pen down very carefully and said, in a different voice, “Is he all right? Ars?”

Rowan thought about it.

“I think so,” he said. “I think he might be.”

Odette nodded, once, and picked her pen back up, and Rowan saw her blink rather hard at the ledger, and pretended he hadn’t.

Ars came into the square at the third hour.

Rowan saw it from the town hall steps. He’d come out to tell a knot of carters to go and do some work, and he was halfway through telling them when he saw the square change. The way it had changed for the king, yesterday, and the way it changed every evening at dusk, when a plain man walked up from the lake to buy eggs: people stopping, one by one, outward from the lake road, like a ripple.

But it was the third hour. Ars never came up at the third hour. He came at dusk, or not at all. And he wasn’t carrying anything. No basket. No rod.

He walked into the middle of the square, by the well, and stopped.

Rowan stood very still on the steps of the town hall.

The square went quiet around the man by the well. Not all at once. In pieces. The carters stopped talking. The miller’s wife stopped. Edric came to the door of his bakery with flour to his elbows. The women at the well stepped back, without meaning to, a pace. Children stopped, and were pulled in by their mothers. And in a minute, perhaps less, there were two hundred people in Wrenhaven’s market square, and every one of them was silent, and every one of them was looking at the fisherman.

He stood there and looked back at them. All of them. Slowly. The way Rowan had seen him look at people only a few times. The way he’d looked at the five girls on the market road, a year ago, the day they arrived.

Then he said, quite clearly, so that everyone could hear:

“I need help.”

Rowan had known Ars for ten years. He’d stood in his door through a war. He’d carried messages for him, and made up a code for him, and put his own body between him and the curious for eight summers. He’d been there the morning he came home alone.

He’d never once heard him say those words.

Not during the war. Not after. Not when the girl was sick with the fever, the second winter, and Ars had sat up with her for nine nights and not slept and not let anyone else sit with her. Not when the roof went in the big storm. Not when anything.

The square was so quiet that Rowan could hear the lake.

“We’re building a forge,” said Ars. “By the water. Below the cabin.” He said it plainly, the way he said everything. “There’s a sword that has to be cut, and worked, and it won’t go in any fire in Wrenhaven. We need a long hearth. Stone.” He paused. “I can’t build it on my own. Not in time. I need stones. A great many. Flat ones, big ones, the kind in the old field walls on the north road, and the fallen quarry wall past the mill.” He looked across the square. “And the anvil. Garrow. If you’ll lend it.”

Garrow the smith was standing in the door of his forge on the far side of the square, in his leather apron, with his hammer still in his hand. He was a big soot-faced man of forty who’d shod every horse in Wrenhaven for twenty years and never been anywhere. He went red to the roots of his hair.

“And I need people,” said Ars. “To carry. Today, if you can. Down the lake road.”

He stopped.

Rowan felt the whole square take that in. Down the lake road. Eight years of nobody. Eight years of a big grey man at the top of the road, standing in front of it.

“Anyone who wants to,” said Ars, more quietly. “Anyone at all.”

Nobody moved for a long moment.

Then Garrow came across the square.

He came slowly, in his apron, with his hammer still in his hand because he’d forgotten he was holding it, through the crowd, which parted for him. He stopped in front of Ars at the well. He was a head taller and twice as wide, and he looked, standing there, like a boy called up in front of the schoolroom.

“You can have the anvil,” said Garrow. His voice came out hoarse. He cleared his throat. “You can have it. Only…” He swallowed. “Only, the king. Is it true he’s going to work it? Himself?”

“It’s true.”

“Then I’d like to watch,” said Garrow. “If he wouldn’t mind. I’d stand at the back. I wouldn’t say anything.” He went redder still. “I’ve wanted to see a real smith work, my whole life. Just once.”

Ars looked at him.

“Don’t stand at the back,” he said. “Work the bellows. He’ll want someone who knows how.”

Garrow’s face did something Rowan wouldn’t forget.

And then Edric the baker said, from his doorway, “I’ve got a cart.”

And the miller’s wife said, “There’s the old quarry wall. The boys can bring the barrow.”

And somebody else said something, and then somebody else, and then everyone was talking at once, and the square wasn’t silent anymore at all.

It was Odette who made it work.

Rowan should have known it would be. She came out of the town hall at a walk that was very nearly a run, with her keys at her belt and a slate under her arm, and took one look at the square, two hundred people all shouting at once about carts and walls and barrows, and went up the town hall steps past Rowan, and turned round, and clapped her hands. Twice. Very sharply.

The square went quiet again.

Odette had been a maid once. At a cabin by the lake, eight years ago, in a war, with a little fox girl to keep fed and warm and a protector in the doorway who couldn’t boil an egg. Rowan had watched her, that season, run a household of three like an army on the march. He’d forgotten. He remembered now.

“The north road walls,” said Odette. “Edric’s cart and the Hollin brothers’. Big flat stones only. Nothing you can’t lift with two people.” She pointed. “The quarry wall. Every barrow in town, and the miller’s boys. Children carry the small ones, the size of your fist, no bigger, and they carry them in pairs, and they stay on the road.” She wrote on the slate. “Garrow. Your anvil, on the brewer’s dray. You’ll need eight men. Take the Tallow boys; they’re too big for anything else.” She looked out across the square. “And somebody fetch water and bread down to the shore at midday, because nobody’s going to stop to eat if they’re not made to, and I’ll not have people fainting on the lake road in front of the king.”

Nobody moved.

“Now,” said Odette.

They moved.

By the fourth hour, there was a line.

Rowan walked it from end to end, twice, because it was the sort of thing a mayor should do and because he couldn’t quite believe it. It started at the field walls on the north road and the fallen quarry wall past the mill, and it came down through the market square and past the church and onto the lake road, and it went all the way down the lake road, past the fields, past the long stone wall, to the shore below the cabin. A line of people. Carts and barrows and handcarts and people carrying stones in their arms. Old men with a stone each, carried slowly. Young men with two. The Hollin brothers with a slab between them that should have taken four. And children, everywhere, in pairs, each pair with a stone the size of a fist, walking very carefully down the lake road in the middle of the line, as if they’d been given something precious, which they had.

Nobody followed them down, Rowan thought. Eight years. And now the whole of Wrenhaven was walking down the lake road, and Rowan was walking down it with them, and not one bit of him wanted to stand in front of it.

Ars was at the bottom.

He’d set himself at the place where the lake road ran out onto the shingle below the cabin, and he was taking the stones. That was all. Each one, from each person. A cart came down, and he took the stones off it himself, one by one, and passed them to the men building the hearth. A child came down with a stone the size of a fist, holding it in both hands, and he went down on one knee and took it from her, and looked at it, and said thank you. And she went red and ran back up the road to get another.

He said thank you to every single one of them. Rowan stood a little way off and listened to him do it, for an hour. Thank you. Thank you. Thank you. To the Hollin brothers and the miller’s boys and Edric and old Mother Breck, who was ninety and had brought one stone, very slowly, all the way down from the square on her own. To the children, every time. Every stone.

Rowan had to go and stand by the reeds for a while.

The king was building the hearth.

In his shirtsleeves, with his coat off and folded on the jetty and his sleeves rolled to the elbow. He’d marked it out on the shingle at dawn with a stick, a long low shape like a trough, three paces long, with a gap at each end for the bellows, and now he was laying the stones himself. Bedding each one in clay from the lake shore. Turning it, and looking at it, and turning it again, until it sat right. The masons from the town had come down to help, and they’d stopped helping after the first quarter of an hour and started watching instead, because he was better than them, and they knew it.

The big red hen sat on the jetty beside his coat and watched him too. She hadn’t left his side all morning.

And the king’s guard, the tall dark woman with the sword, who’d sat on the cabin step all night, Rowan had seen her from the road at dawn: she’d taken her coat off too. She was carrying stones. From the carts to the hearth, the big ones, one at a time, her face perfectly still. Rowan saw Edric try to take one from her and get a look that made him put his hands in his pockets.

“She’s Reaching,” said Garrow, beside him, low and awed. He’d come down with the anvil. “The fifth tier. The fifth. And she’s carrying stones.”

“So’s the king,” said Rowan.

Garrow looked at the king, laying stones in clay on the shingle, and opened his mouth, and found he had nothing to say.

Maren came at midday.

Rowan saw them on the lake road: the guide from the militia hall, straight-backed as a fence post, at the front. The big border veteran, Bram, behind her with a barrow. And the five girls.

They’d come down the road in the line like everybody else. Each of them carrying a stone. Rowan watched them come and felt something tighten in his chest that he couldn’t have named.

He knew them, by now. Everybody did. A year in a small town and you knew everyone. The one with the pink and violet in her hair, who’d lit the bonfire from the inside, so the story went, last midwinter. The one with the white bow and the notebook, who wrote everything down. The silver-haired one, sharp-tongued, who’d made a hawthorn hedge do something in the spring that the hedge-layers still argued about. The little lavender one with the purple creature on her head, who could make the water in a bucket turn round, the children said. And the quiet one with the yellow heart in her hair, the only swordswoman among them, who’d been carrying the heaviest stone on the whole lake road and was barely out of breath.

Alea was with them. She’d come down from the cabin to meet them at the top of the road, and she was walking in the middle of the five, with a stone in her arms the size of a small dog, and she and the quiet one with the yellow heart kept looking at each other’s stones, and then at each other, and then walking slightly faster.

“Militia duty,” said Maren, to Rowan, as she passed him. Very stiffly. “The town asked for help. The militia helps the town.”

“Of course,” said Rowan.

She didn’t look at him. She was looking at the bottom of the lake road. At the man taking the stones.

Rowan watched them reach him.

He’d tell Odette about it afterwards, that night, at the kitchen table, and he wouldn’t be able to explain it, not properly. He’d say you had to be there, and she’d say I was there, Rowan, I was at the top of the road with the slate, and he’d say no, at the bottom, and they’d both go quiet.

The first one was the one with the pink and violet hair. She came down off the road onto the shingle with her stone held out in front of her, and stopped in front of Ars, and held it out. Rowan saw her hands. They weren’t quite steady.

Ars took the stone from her.

He didn’t say anything for a moment. He just held the stone, and looked at her. The way he’d looked at all five of them on the market road on the first day. The way he’d looked at them a week ago, going past the militia hall gate on his way to the Redmarch. As if he were reading something written on her face and wanted to get every word.

“Thank you,” he said.

Just that. The same as he’d said to the Hollin brothers, and to Mother Breck, and to every child on the road.

It wasn’t the same. Rowan didn’t know how he knew that. It just wasn’t.

The girl looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, very small, and turned, and walked back up the shingle. She didn’t look back.

The silver-haired one was next. Then the one with the notebook. Then the little lavender one, who’d brought a stone and a pebble, the pebble in the purple creature’s tentacle, held out separately; and Ars took the stone, and then, gravely, the pebble, and said thank you twice, once to her and once to the creature, and the creature turned a faint pink. Then the quiet one with the yellow heart, last, with the enormous stone, and Ars took it from her one-handed and didn’t seem to notice the weight, and said thank you. And she smiled at him. Slowly. Like the sun coming out.

Five stones. Five thank yous. No names.

He didn’t know their names. Rowan was almost sure of that. He’d never been introduced; he’d never said a word to any of them except, once, you’re blocking the road. He couldn’t know their names.

But Rowan had stood in front of that man for eight years, and he’d learned to read his back, the way you learn to read weather. And he watched Ars stand there on the shingle after the fifth girl had walked away, with her stone in his hands, very still. And he watched his shoulders.

He’d seen them like that once before. On the morning he came home. Holding something too heavy to put down.

Only it wasn’t grief, this time. Rowan didn’t know what it was. It looked, if anything, like a man holding something too good to put down.

By the middle of the afternoon the hearth was built.

Long and low and grey, three paces of stone bedded in lake clay, on the shingle just above the waterline, beside the jetty. Two great leather bellows, one at each end: Garrow’s from the town forge, and the brewer’s, which was older and bigger and wheezed. Over the top of it, the masons had put up a roof of poles and old sailcloth, open on all sides, so the smoke could get out and the rain couldn’t get in. And beside it, on a stump of oak that the Tallow boys had rolled down the lake road, the anvil. Garrow’s anvil. Polished, Rowan noticed, by Garrow, that morning, before it left the forge. Polished till it shone.

A little way off, on the shingle, in a neat row, the tools from the king’s leather roll. Hammers, a dozen of them, every size. Tongs. Chisels. Punches. Things Rowan didn’t know the names of. Old, all of them, the handles dark and smooth with use, and the iron of them clean and bright. Garrow kept going and looking at them and coming back, the way a boy goes and looks at a horse he’s been promised.

The town didn’t go home.

Rowan had thought they would. They’d done what they were asked. But they didn’t. They stood about on the shingle and the lake road and the field above it, in their hundreds, the whole of Wrenhaven, eating Odette’s bread and talking in low voices and watching the forge by the water. Nobody went near the cabin. Nobody went past the hen-yard gate. Rowan didn’t have to tell them. They just knew.

They lit it at dusk.

To cure the clay, the king said. A small fire first, then a bigger one, slowly, so the stones didn’t crack. He knelt at the mouth of the hearth and built it himself, kindling and then charcoal, Garrow’s charcoal, and lit it with a coal from the cabin fire that Alea carried down in an iron pot. The flames came up small and yellow among the black. The smoke went up through the sailcloth roof into the evening.

“Bellows,” said the king.

Garrow went to one end. Ars went to the other.

Rowan watched them work. The two bellows, slow, in turn, one and then the other. Garrow’s face, rapt. And the fire coming up, yellow and then orange and then, at the heart of it, the first deep red glow of the charcoal catching properly, the colour iron goes before it’s ready.

“Slower,” said the king. To Ars.

“I’m going slowly.”

“You’re going like a man pumping a well. Slower. Even. Like breathing.” The king watched the fire. “You were always terrible at this. At Cinderhold, you nearly put the whole forge out.”

“That was one time.”

“It was every time. The garrison smith used to hide the bellows when he saw you coming.”

And Ars laughed.

Rowan heard it from the shingle, twenty paces off, with the town around him.

It wasn’t loud. It came out of him short and surprised, as if it had been startled out of him, and then it didn’t stop. He stood there at the end of the hearth with his hands on the bellows handle and his head back, in the orange light of the fire, and laughed. Properly. The way a man laughs when something is actually funny, and he’s forgotten for a moment to be anything else.

Rowan hadn’t heard it in eight years.

He’d heard it before the war. Often. Back then it had been easy to make him laugh; he’d laughed at everything, at Rowan’s bad jokes and the girl’s tumbles and his own clumsiness with the bellows at the garrison, and the woman with him had laughed at him laughing, and the whole cabin had been full of it.

And then he’d come home alone, across the fields, and Rowan had never heard it again. Not once. He’d smiled, sometimes, over the years. At the girl, mostly. Small smiles, quick ones, there and gone. He’d never laughed.

He was laughing now. At a forge, by the lake, with a king and a smith and the whole town watching.

Rowan turned away, and looked very hard at the reeds.

Odette was beside him. He didn’t know when she’d come down. She had the slate under her arm still, and her keys, and she was crying, without any fuss, the way she did everything, with her face perfectly composed and the tears just running down it.

“I’d forgotten,” she said. “Rowan. I’d forgotten what it sounded like.”

“So had I,” said Rowan.

He put his big arm round her thin shoulders, and she let him, which she never did, and they stood there on the shingle together in the dusk, the protector and the maid, and listened to it.

The town went home at dark. Slowly. Up the lake road in a long line, the way it had come down, talking quietly, the children asleep on shoulders. Nobody looked back at the cabin. Everybody looked back at the forge.

Rowan went last. He always went last.

Ars was at the bottom of the road, by the hearth, where the fire had been banked down to a red glow under its sailcloth roof. He was standing with his hands in his coat pockets, looking up the lake road at the line of people going home. When Rowan came past, he turned.

“Rowan.”

“Aye?”

“Thank you.”

Rowan stopped. He looked at him.

“For the stones?” he said. “That was the town. That was Odette.”

“Not for the stones.” Ars looked at him for a long moment, in the red light. “For eight years.”

Rowan didn’t say anything. He found he couldn’t.

“You stood at the top of that road,” said Ars, “for eight years. I never asked you to. You just did it.” He looked away, up the lake road, at the last of the lanterns going round the bend toward the town. “I don’t think I ever said thank you. For that. Or for the door.” He was quiet a moment. “I’m saying it now.”

Rowan stood there on the shingle, big and slow and grey, with his chain forgotten at home on the bedpost, and found that his eyes had gone hot.

“Pike running?” he said, roughly. It was all he could think of.

Ars almost smiled. “No,” he said. “Just thank you.”

“Aye,” said Rowan. “Well.” He cleared his throat. “You’re welcome, then.”

He went up the lake road in the dark. Halfway up, he stopped, and turned round, the way Odette would tell him off for doing, and looked back.

The forge was glowing by the water. A small red light under a sailcloth roof, on the shingle by the jetty, with the lake black and still beyond it and the stars coming out. Two figures beside it, one big and one not, sitting on the stones with their backs to the warmth, talking. And a hen.

It was the first new thing anybody had built at that cabin in eight years.

Rowan stood on the lake road and looked at it for a long time. Then he went home, and didn’t stand in front of anything at all.
