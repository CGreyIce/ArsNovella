The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 9: The Other Front

Chapter 270

Home for Midwinter

5,523 words · 24 min read

He stood up a little before dawn.

He hadn’t decided to. He’d sat on the step under the arch for four nights and most of four days, with the rod across his knees and the king beside him and the valley in front of him under the snow; and then, in the dark before the fifth dawn, with the snow stopped and the stars out hard and small over the pass, he found that he’d stood up. The way you find you’ve stood up from a table when the meal’s done.

The king didn’t look round.

“Going,” said Severin.

“Yes.”

The old man nodded, slowly, with his big hands in his lap. His face was grey in the light from the gate lamp, and the beard was dark down the front, and he’d slept, a little, in the last two nights, sitting up against the black oak post with his chin on his chest. His door was shut now. All the way. Ars could feel it, the way you feel a forge that’s been banked: the heat still in it, right through, under the ash. It would be a long while before he opened it again. It would be a long while before he needed to.

“I’ll not write,” said the king.

“No.”

“You’d only not read it.”

“I’d read it.”

“You’d read it,” agreed the king, “and not answer, and that’s worse.” He looked north, at the valley, white, going up into the dark. “Go home. Your lamp’s lit.”

“She’s lighting it.”

“Then go home to her lighting it,” said Severin, and didn’t say anything else; and Ars stood there a moment longer beside him in the cold, an old man on a step and a man with a rod, the way they’d stood beside each other on a wall on the Redmarch once, in the war; and then he turned round.

Hild was at the end of the step. She’d been there all night; she always was. She stood up when he turned, with her sword in her hand point-down, and her one eye on his face.

She could see it. She and Petra were the only ones in Thornwall who could, besides the old man. She’d looked at it from the arch on the last morning, with the snow coming down, and put her hand flat on her chest; and she hadn’t said a word about it since, to him or anybody. She didn’t now. She looked at him, the way you’d look at a road you’d heard about for twenty years and finally walked down, and put her hand flat on her chest again; and kept it there.

He nodded to her. She nodded back.

Petra was at the other end. She didn’t stand up. She’d never needed to stand up for him; she’d stood once, at a ditch, at twenty-four, with her unit going down round her, and he’d come down the line and stood in front of her, and that was enough standing for a lifetime. She looked up at him from the step, slate-grey, all one colour, and said:

“The king asked. We’re staying the winter. Group 6 and me.”

“Good.”

“There’s a lot of shields need hanging.” She looked at the valley. “And a girl on a tower who sleeps sitting up.”

“Yes.”

“Sir,” said Petra. And looked away north again, and that was all.

Group 6 were in the long room door, when he came up the long street. All five, in a knot, with Azac at the front filling the frame and the other four round him: Xav, and RX, and Malcolm with his book, and John with no motes out, for once, and his hands in his pockets.

“He’s going,” said Azac. To the street. To nobody. The way he said everything. “He’s coming up the street. He’s got his roll on. Okay.”

“You’re staying,” said Ars.

“The king asked,” said Xav.

“Good.”

Xav looked at him for a long time. Xav, who fought the way he thought; who’d sat beside Drew on a roof in the snow every night since and not said a word, because there wasn’t one. Ars had watched him do it from the step. He’d have done the same.

“Okay,” said Xav.

Malcolm held something out. Bread, and cheese, in a cloth. He held it out and stood there, the way he stood with the bowls, until Ars took it.

“IF he doesn’t eat on the road,” said Malcolm, “THEN Alea will know. Backup: she’ll know anyway.” He didn’t smile. He put the book under his arm. “Eat it.”

“I’ll eat it.”

RX didn’t say anything. He was standing at the back of the five, against the door frame, and he was very nearly not there at all; the way he’d learned to be, in the yard, with Hild. Furniture. Exactly what he was and nothing extra. Ars looked at him, and RX looked back, and for a moment neither of them was anything but two people who’d been in a valley at night, at different times, close enough to touch a thing that had no name.

“Nice work,” said Ars. “In the gap.”

RX went red to the ears.

John didn’t show him anything. He just stood there with his hands in his pockets, and when Ars went past him to the street he said, very quietly, “Yes,” as if somebody had asked him something; and Ars knew what he meant, and didn’t say anything back, because John didn’t need him to.

Tiger was at the post when he came back down to the arch.

It was first light. Grey, cold, the stars going. The boy was standing in the arch with his hand flat on the black oak, the way he stood every morning, the way the rule said; with his ears flat and his tail wrapped round his leg and his breath going up white. RX had come down behind him from the long room, a few paces back, the way he always did.

“Tomorrow,” said Tiger. To the valley.

Eleven miles away, in the third ring, in the snow, something leaned.

Ars didn’t feel it lean. He didn’t need to. He felt the boy, and the post, and the line that went out from the boy’s hand along the road and up the valley and into a field; he felt that the way you feel a line in the water. Taut. Something on the end of it. Not ready to come in. Not gone, either.

He walked past Tiger to the post.

And laid his own hand on it, flat, beside the boy’s. On the wood. On the worn place at the height of a boy’s shoulder where two boys had slapped it every morning for years, coming in from a race up the road and back.

He didn’t say anything. He kept it there for the count of a breath. Then he took it away, and walked out through the arch, and turned along the outside of the wall toward the east lane and the ridge road; and didn’t look back, because you didn’t, on a road, if you meant to get anywhere.

Behind him, he heard Tiger breathe in. And out.

On the north tower, a hundred and twelve steps up, a girl with a red mark in one eye watched him go along the wall, and he felt her watch, and let her.

It was faster, going home.

He’d known it would be. The road west had been autumn: rain on the downs and mud to the knee on the clay, and the rivers up, and every ford a swim. He’d run what he could and walked the rest, and it had taken twenty-four days, and he’d been afraid every one of them. The road east was frozen. Hard as a floor under the snow, the ruts set like iron, the fords iced from bank to bank; you could run on a road like that all day if you didn’t mind your feet, and he didn’t. And there was nothing at the end of this one that he had to get there with something left for. Only a lamp.

He ran most of it.

Not hard. Not the way he’d run west. A long slow lope, the way soldiers on the Redmarch had moved between towers when there were no horses: run a mile, walk a mile, run a mile, all day, with the roll on your back and your breath going up white and the sky very big. Over the east ridge and down. Across the downs, white to the edges of the world, with nothing taller than a sheep for ten miles and the sheep all in. Through villages he’d run through on the way west in the dark without seeing, and saw now: a forge with its door open and a boy at the bellows; a woman at a well who looked at him as he went by and couldn’t hold his face, and put her hand on her chest, and didn’t know why.

He slept in barns. He ate Malcolm’s bread and cheese on the second day, as promised, sitting on a milestone; and was glad of it.

He read the channel at night. He didn’t write in it. He never had.

Hyvred, at dawn and dusk, every day: 23. same. Then, on the third morning: 23. same. I slept again. Group 3, every night at the same hour, one line each, always the same; Wendy counting them, eleven, and the king, and then, after the first night, eleven. and the king. and him, on the road. He read that one twice. Thea’s, from the last night on the step, a long careful post that he read back three times by the light of a barn lantern with snow sifting in through the boards: the last eight are cut off clean. I’ve never seen a cut like it. And under it: three-quarters. waiting under the snow for spring. Kei, after: a hair quieter. the seven.

He didn’t think about the valley much. He’d thought he would. He thought about it once, properly, on the sixth night, in a hayloft above a dairy with the cows breathing under him; let himself remember it, all the way, the way you’d let yourself feel a bad tooth with your tongue to see if it was still there.

The ridge road at first light. The town below him, with its lamps going out down the long street, one after another, and a girl on a tower going to her knees; and another girl on the parapet in front of her drawing a dog in the air. And the line going out from the rod, a mile over the roofs, and the hook coming down tink on the cobbles at the edge of the dark. And the cut, going on past the hook, out through the arch, across the fields, along the end of the curve. A dog from Kharvos in two halves. Eight empty coats. Nothing in them. Nothing came out.

And then the thing in the long street, turning round. And facing him.

He’d let it. That was all he’d done. He hadn’t knelt and he hadn’t drawn and he hadn’t done anything at all; he’d stood on the ridge road with the rod in his hand and let it face him, the way you’d stand at the end of a jetty at night with no moon and let the lake be the lake. It had faced him for a long time. He didn’t know how long. He hadn’t counted; Bee had, until she lost count. And then it had turned round and gone home.

He didn’t know why. He didn’t pretend to himself that he did. It might have been the cut. It might have been the snow. It might have been something it saw in him, facing it, that he didn’t know was there. He lay in the hay above the cows and thought about that for a while, honestly, the way you’d look at a thing you’d pulled out of the lake that you didn’t have a name for.

He’d taken the rod. He’d left the sword on the wall. Not that day.

It hadn’t been. He was sure of that, lying in the dark. The turned were neighbours, and you didn’t take a sword to your neighbours; the hunter was a dog, and you didn’t take a sword to a dog. The sword was for the day something had to be finished. That thing at the top of the valley wasn’t finished. It was three-quarters, and waiting, facing a town, under the snow.

Not yet, he thought.

He went to sleep.

On the twelfth night he read the channel in the porch of a chapel on the edge of the chalk country, with the frost so hard that the stones of the porch rang under his boots.

Winter. Three lines, from that morning, in her flat plain way:

black ice coming to valcrest. midwinter night. freezing fog up the river off the sea.

if you know anyone with old people in the river quarter, tell them. stoves if they’ve got them.

I’m telling everybody.

He read it twice.

He sat in the chapel porch with the frost ringing in the stones and thought about a girl with silver hair and two black horns, barefoot on the cobbles of a yard in Valcrest at dawn, the summer before last, with a face she couldn’t see two paces off. Who’d been the last to know. Who’d told him she was going to watch, and see it first, and tell him. And he’d said: tell everyone. It’s no good unless you make sure nobody else is last.

I’m telling everybody.

He laughed. Out loud, in a chapel porch on the edge of the chalk, so that a fox going along the top of the churchyard wall stopped dead and looked at him.

Then he put the window away, and lay down in his blanket, and slept better than he had since the lake.

He came over the last hill at dusk on the day of the bonfire.

He hadn’t planned it. He’d known, on the road, roughly, how many days; he’d known the bonfire was on midwinter night, the way it had been every year since before Alea was born. He hadn’t tried to make it. You didn’t, on a road; you went as fast as the road let you and got there when you got there. But on the fourteenth day, in the afternoon, he came up out of the woods on the far side of the river onto the long rise above the lake, and the light was going, and he knew; and he stopped at the top.

The lake was there.

White. Ice under snow, from the reeds at the north end to the far shore, with the hills going up behind it into a sky going green at the edges. The town on its slope, grey and white, with the smoke going straight up. The square at the top of it, where something tall and dark was standing in the middle, waiting to be lit. And below the town, going down to the shingle between its two field walls, the lake road.

Lit.

Forty lanterns. Small ones, paper, red and yellow and white, on every second stone of the walls on both sides, going down from the square to the water; and some of them not lit yet, at the bottom, where small figures with tapers were going along the walls one at a time. A road of light going down to the lake. To a cabin with one window lit, and smoke going straight up.

To a jetty. Thirty-four planks, going out over the ice. And at the end of it, on its post, a small yellow light. And somebody beside it with her hand on the post.

He stood on the top of the hill and looked at it.

And in the town, a mile off and below, in the upstairs window of the house that looked down the lake road, something stood up.

He couldn’t see it from there. Nobody could have. He felt it the way you feel somebody look at you across a room. A hen. On a windowsill. Who’d been put there, every evening, by a woman who’d kept a straight face at him for ten years, so that she could see him come over the hill before anybody said it was him.

She’d seen him. Before anybody.

He laughed. Out loud, at the top of the hill, in the cold.

He tapped his fingertips together. Three times.

Well, then.

He went down.

Gigichi was on her bench.

The one in the hedge, on the shelf above the shingle, grown into the gap where the gap had been: woven green and white, facing up the lake road toward the hill. The hedge was bare now, with frost on it, and the bench was bare too, the green gone grey-brown for the winter; but she was on it, in her coat, with her knees up and her hands round a cup that had stopped steaming a long time ago. She’d been there, he thought, a while. Most evenings, probably. Since he went.

She stood up when he came round the corner of the hedge.

She didn’t say anything for a moment. She looked at him the way she’d looked at him on the last plank in the autumn, with her head on one side: at his shoulders, and his coat with the elbows gone, and the rod, and the roll on his back, and his boots, which were finished.

“You okay?” said Gigichi.

He thought about it. Properly. The way she deserved, the way he’d thought about it the first time she asked him, at the end of a jetty, in the autumn.

“Yes,” he said.

She looked at him for a long time. Then she nodded, once, and sat back down on her bench with her cold cup, and looked up the road the way she’d been looking up it; as if she was going to sit there a bit longer anyway, now she’d started.

“Good,” said Gigichi. “Go on, then. She’s on the end.”

Group 1 were on the lake road, halfway down, with the tapers.

They saw him come along the shingle. He saw them see him. Xola, nearest, on her toes on a wall with a taper in her hand and a lantern half lit, who made a sound like a kettle and nearly fell off. PenguChi below her, enormous and slow, who put out one hand without looking and steadied her. Thea, further up, very still, with her taper burning down toward her fingers. And Higu, at the top of the lit part, with her notebook under her arm, who looked at him for a long moment and then said something, quietly, that he couldn’t hear and didn’t need to.

Rule three.

They didn’t come down. That was the thing. He saw Xola start to, and Higu put a hand on her arm; and the four of them stood on the lake road between the lanterns with their tapers in their hands and didn’t come down to him. Thea looked at him. Then she looked past him. At the jetty.

She nodded at it. Once. Go on.

He went on.

Alea didn’t turn round.

She was standing at the end of the jetty with her hand on the post, the way he stood every night, with the lamp lit beside her and the glass down; looking at it, for as long as it took to know it would stay lit. Her coat. Her good knife on her hip. Astra on her back, black, in its sheath. Her ears, up, and very still.

She knew. He could tell from the ears. She’d known since he came over the hill; she always did, she’d always been a hill ahead. She didn’t turn round.

He walked down the jetty. Thirty-four planks. The boards were white with frost and they sounded under his boots the way they always sounded in the winter, hollow and hard, with the ice under them creaking a little where it was thickest. He’d walked it every day he’d been home, all her life.

He stopped on the last plank.

“Thea says,” said Alea, to the lamp, “you let your tea go cold.”

“Yes.”

“Every night?”

“Most nights.”

She thought about that. Her ears moved. Then she took her hand off the post, and turned round.

He couldn’t see what her face was doing. He never could, quite; she’d always had her mother’s way of keeping it, and she’d been keeping it for forty-one days, every dusk, on the end of a jetty. He could see her ears. He could see her hands, which had come out of her coat pockets and didn’t know where to go.

He put the rod down on the boards.

She came the last pace, the one between the last plank and the post, and put her forehead against his chest, and stood there. He put his arms round her. She was taller than she’d been at eight. She was taller than she’d been in the spring. Her ears were cold against his chin.

“You ran,” said Alea. Into his coat.

“Some of it.”

“Higu said you ran.”

“Some of it,” he said again.

“Did you take the sword?”

She knew he hadn’t. She’d seen it on the wall every evening for forty-one days, over the drawing and the blank side of the list. She was asking anyway, because you asked.

“No.”

“Good,” said Alea. And stayed where she was, with her forehead against his chest, on the end of the jetty, in the cold, with the lamp beside them and forty lanterns going up the hill behind; until the last of the light had gone out of the sky over the far shore and the first star was out.

The cabin was warm.

She’d kept it the way he kept it. The stove going, banked low, and the kettle on the side of it. The two stools at the table. The cups on their hooks. And over the hearth, on Garrow’s two pegs, the sword: long and grey-silver and patient, in the light from the stove, exactly where he’d put it back on the morning he went. Under it, the drawing, in its frame. And beside the drawing, on its nail, the list. Blank side out.

He stood in the doorway and looked at the sword for a long moment.

Not that day. It hadn’t been. It still wasn’t. It lay on its pegs the way the thing at the top of the valley lay under the snow, waiting for a day; and he didn’t know which of them would come to that day first, or if either of them would. He looked at it, and it didn’t ask him anything, and he didn’t ask it anything back.

Alea had gone past him to the wall.

She took the list off its nail. Higu’s list, every word on the front of it crossed through but one, in his own pencil, over the summer; and the back of it blank, the way it had been blank at Oakes’s table on the edge of the red, when she’d pulled it toward her across the table and turned it over and sat with the pencil over it, not writing anything. I’ll wait, she’d said, then.

She sat down at the table with it. Blank side up.

She had the pencil. Higu’s. He hadn’t known she’d kept it; he’d thought it was in his coat, with the drawing and Halvard’s letter. It wasn’t. She’d had it all along. She held it over the paper the way she’d held it at Oakes’s table, and he stood by the stove and didn’t say anything, because it wasn’t his to say.

She wrote.

One line. Slowly, in her careful square hand, the one Diwa had taught her at the slate, the other way round, love, until it came out right.

Then she looked at it for a moment. And drew a line through it. Straight, from one end to the other, the way he’d drawn them through Higu’s list over the summer, one at a time.

She turned it round on the table, and pushed it across to him.

Come home.

With a line through it.

He looked at it for a long time.

“That’s one,” said Alea.

He didn’t say anything. He couldn’t, for a while. He stood by the stove with the kettle beginning to tick behind him and looked at a scrap of paper on a scarred oak table, with his daughter’s hand on it, one line, already done.

“What’s the next one?” he said, at last.

Alea thought about it. Seriously, the way she thought about everything; with her chin on her fist and her ears forward.

“I’ll think of one,” she said. “There’s a winter.”

“Take your time.”

“I will.” She stood up, and took the list, and hung it back on its nail beside the drawing, under the sword. Her side out. One line on it, crossed through. And a lot of room underneath.

Then she took two cups off their hooks and put them on the table. And didn’t put one back.

They went up to the bonfire at full dark.

Together, up the lake road, between the forty lanterns, the way they’d gone up it last year. Group 1 were waiting at the bottom by the cabin gate, all five of them, in their coats; Gigichi had come along the shingle from her bench with her cold cup. They didn’t say anything. They fell in round him, the way they had last year, and Xola took his hand without asking, which nobody in Wrenhaven had ever seen anybody do until last year, and which he let her; and they went up.

The square was full. It always was, on midwinter night. Trestle tables round the edges with roast pork and bread and honey cakes and the great steaming vat of something hot and spiced. A fiddler. A man with a drum. And in the middle, the bonfire, as tall as a house, built over three days by every man and boy in the town; and Rowan Hale in his chain of office at the foot of it, bellowing at the Hollin brothers about a lean on the east side that was entirely their fault.

He stopped bellowing when he saw them come up out of the lake road into the square.

He stood there in his chain with his mouth open. Then he shut it. He looked at Ars across the square for a long moment, the way he’d looked at him on the shingle in the autumn, with a cup in each hand.

“Good,” said Rowan. Gruffly.

And went back to bellowing at the Hollin brothers.

Maren was at the edge of the light, in her good grey cloak, with her new hands folded in front of her. She didn’t say anything; she nodded, once, the way she nodded at a line of the language when it came out right. Bram was beside her, big and grey, with his boy’s rod over his shoulder for no reason anybody could tell, and his sword on his hip. He looked at Ars, and at the rod in Ars’s hand, and lifted his own, a little. The way you’d lift a cup across a room.

Ars lifted his back.

They lit it at full dark.

He’d seen it from the jetty, the first winter, alone. He’d sat at the end of the boards in the dark with a torn net in his lap and watched the sparks go up over the town on the hill, and felt a small candle catch, up there in the square, among the lanterns; and gone in, and set out two cups, and not said a word about it when she came home. He’d stood at the back, the year after, behind his daughter, at the very edge of the light. He’d come up the lake road last year between forty lanterns with five girls round him and stood in the middle, and it had been the hardest thing he’d done in nine years.

It wasn’t hard now.

The fire went up. All at once, roaring, the way it always did, with a sound the whole square felt in its chest; and the sparks went up out of the top of it into the black sky. Thousands of them. Swirling, spiralling, gold against the dark, rising and rising until they went out among the stars. The crowd went ahhh. Children shrieked and pointed. The fiddler struck up.

He stood in the middle of the square with his daughter on one side of him and a girl with a squid on her head on the other, holding his hand; and Thea beside Alea, with her face lit gold, watching the sparks go up the way she’d watched them the night she found her first word in them. Three years ago. Three bonfires ago. Parse.

She looked round at him. She didn’t say anything. She didn’t need to.

Above the town, the sparks went up and up, and went out, each one, among the stars.

He opened his window on the step, much later.

Alea had gone up to bed. The lanterns on the lake road had burned down one by one and gone out, and the bonfire on the hill was a red glow under a column of smoke, and the town had gone quiet. The lamp on the end of the jetty was the only light on the shore. She’d trim it at dawn. She’d said so. I’ll do it till the thaw, she’d said, at the top of the ladder, with her ears in the firelight. You can have it back in the spring.

Sixty-seven.

He looked at it for a long time. It had said sixty-six on this step in the autumn. It had said sixty-six for longer than that. A percent a year, he’d told himself, a long time ago, on this step; that was what it was worth, and it was all right, because the bar wasn’t the point. It just told you, afterwards, which bits had been.

It had moved. He didn’t know for what. For the road, maybe; for running, which he hadn’t done since the war, and had done twice in one winter. For the line over the roofs, and the cut, and the eight empty coats. For standing on a ridge road and letting a thing with no name face him, and not kneeling, and not drawing. For a hand on a post. For four nights on a step beside an old man, not fixing anybody.

For Come home, with a line through it.

He didn’t know. He thought it was probably all of it. He thought it didn’t matter much which.

He closed it.

Behind him, through the open door, in the dark, a long grey-silver blade lay on its pegs over a drawing and a list with one line on it, and was patient. To the south and west, a long way off, past the hills, past the downs, a grey stone town at the foot of the Greyspine was asleep under the snow, with a girl on a tower asleep sitting up and somebody beside her; and a king who’d been a smith asleep in a chair by a banked forge; and a black oak post inside an arch with a cap of new snow on it, and two hand-marks worn into the wood at the height of a boy’s shoulder. And past that, past the town, eleven miles up a white valley, thirteen rings of people stood in a field in the snow, and a curve of twenty-three holes stood on the left of them, three-quarters of a sentence; and at the top of everything, at the foot of a pass that was shut, a round place where the snow didn’t lie sat facing the town. Waiting for the thaw.

And a long way south of all of it, further than he’d ever walked in a winter, a city on a river that didn’t freeze would be going white about now, from the water up, the way a girl with two black horns had said it would; and somewhere at the front of it, standing where it came first so that it came to her and not to anyone else, she’d be there. He’d read how it went in the morning. He didn’t need to.

He sat on the step and looked at the lake.

It was winter. He knew what you did with a winter. You kept the lamp lit. You fished through the ice. You let your tea go cold, most nights, and somebody told you about it.

And you waited for the thaw.
