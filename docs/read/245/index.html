The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 9

Chapter 245

The Fourteenth Place

4,246 words · 18 min read

Severin came into Thornwall on the nineteenth day from the lake, on the big grey, an hour ahead of the dray, because he’d felt something on the road that morning and couldn’t make himself walk any more.

He didn’t know what it was. That was the trouble with being what he was. You felt things. A ninth tier didn’t sit in you the way a sword sits on a hip; it went out from you, all the time, a little, whether you wanted it to or not, and things came back along it. Weather. Animals. The mood of a crowd. Once, in the war, the moment a man forty paces behind him decided to put a knife in his back; he’d turned round and taken the knife off him before the man had finished deciding. You got used to it. You learned not to listen to most of it, the way a smith learns not to hear the hammers in the next forge.

He’d heard this one.

A pull. Very faint. In the west and north, where the mountains were. Not toward him. Not at anything. Just a pull in the shape of things, the way you feel a draught in a room before you see which door’s open. He’d felt it once before, in the spring, on the tower at Thornwall, when the absence turned and looked at a cow girl with a gold eye and he’d stepped in front of her.

He hadn’t liked it then. He liked it less now.

So he’d left the oxen and the empty dray and the carter Kestra had hired at Pellow’s Cross, and ridden. Kestra came with him without being asked, on her bay, a length behind, saying nothing, the way she’d ridden behind him for twenty-two years.

Thornwall was the same. That was the first thing. A grey stone town on a hill at the foot of the Greyspine, with walls on three sides and the mountains on the fourth, and the old north tower going up out of the middle of it like a thumb. Smoke going straight up from the chimneys in the still evening. The harvest in, mostly; the fields round the town cut to stubble and the stooks gone in. A woman at the south gate selling the last of the plums. Brannoch’s soldiers on the walls in their grey, looking north, the way they always looked north.

The second thing was that it was quiet.

He’d heard about the quiet in letters. Fenwick’s, mostly; Fenwick wrote letters the way other men breathed, eleven pages at a time, with drawings in the margins. The town has gone quiet since the spring, Majesty. Not afraid. Quiet. As if it is listening for something in the next room. He’d thought Fenwick was being Fenwick. He wasn’t. Severin rode up the long street from the south gate in the evening, and people looked up from their doors as he passed and put their hands on their chests, the way they’d learned to on this border, and didn’t say anything, and went back to what they were doing. Quietly. Listening.

He knew why. He’d felt the pull on the road. They’d been living with it all summer.

Corvin met him at the guardhouse.

The surveyor looked older. Severin had last seen him in the spring, on the tower, a thin earnest man of thirty-six with ink on his fingers and a voice that went up at the ends of things, desperate to be believed. He still had the ink. The voice had gone down.

“Majesty,” said Corvin.

“Where is she?”

Corvin didn’t ask who.

“On the tower,” he said. “She’s been going up again. Since the sixth night.” He hesitated, and Severin saw him decide to say it. “She doesn’t look north any more. She faces south, toward the town, with her eyes shut. Hild taught her. She says she can feel it from there.”

“Feel what?”

“The valley,” said Corvin. “The rings. All of it. With her back to it.” He looked at the king. “And something else, Majesty. Further. She hasn’t told you. She hasn’t told anyone but me and the girl at the lake. She’ll tell you. She’s been waiting for you.”

A hundred and twelve steps.

Kestra counted them under her breath behind him, in case he wanted to know. He didn’t. He’d climbed them a dozen times in the spring and the number didn’t help; they were steep and worn hollow in the middle, and a man of sixty-six with a ninth tier in him still had knees. He came up through the trapdoor onto the leads with his breath going, and stood.

She was there.

Sitting on the leads in a blanket with her back against the north parapet, facing south, toward the town. Her golden horns catching the last of the light. Her glasses in her hair. Her eyes shut.

She didn’t open them.

“Majesty,” said Hyvred.

“How did you know?”

“You’re very loud,” said Hyvred. “When you’re worried. Everyone is, a bit, but you’re loud. You came up the hill like a bonfire.” She opened her eyes then, and looked at him, with the gold one and the other gold one, the left with its little red mark in it like a fine-drawn sight. “Sorry. That was rude.”

“It’s true,” said Severin. “My mother said the same thing. Without the bonfire.” He came across the leads and lowered himself down beside her against the parapet, the way he’d sat beside his father against the forge wall when he was eight, with his knees up and his back to the stone. Facing south too. The town below them in the dusk, going blue, with its lamps coming on.

He didn’t ask. He’d learned that from a man on a jetty. You sat down, and you didn’t ask, and if they wanted to tell you, they did.

She told him.

She told it plainly. He was grateful for that. People told kings things the way they’d tell a priest, all dressed up, with the important part at the bottom where you had to dig for it. She didn’t. She told him about the night of the tenth: the seven of them, and the reading, and some went home, two hundred and nine of them. And then about Deston letting go, and Range staying open, and her turning her head the wrong way.

West. Past the valley. Past the mountains. Past the end of Corvin’s maps.

“There’s a shape,” said Hyvred. “In the dark. Bigger than the valley. Still. With parts. It’s not a light and it’s not a hole. It’s a thing.” She was quiet. “And it looked back. At me. On purpose.”

Severin didn’t say anything.

He sat on the leads with his back against the north parapet and the whole of the Greyspine behind him going up into the dark, and felt it. Not the shape. He couldn’t feel that; it was too far, past anywhere his ninth tier had ever gone. But the pull. The one he’d felt on the road that morning. The draught in the room. Coming from the north and west, over his shoulder, very faint.

He’d thought it was coming from the valley. The absence, at the foot of the pass. The rings.

It wasn’t. It was coming from further.

“It’s been pulling all summer,” said Hyvred. “Kei says. Hisui tastes it. The whole town feels it a bit, he says, like the air before rain. I didn’t know what it was.” She looked at her hands. “I think it’s that. I think the call isn’t the valley. I think the valley’s just where it comes out.”

“Like a spring,” said Severin slowly. “The water comes up in one place. It doesn’t start there.”

“Yes.”

He thought about that for a long time.

“And the fourteenth place,” he said at last.

She shut her eyes.

He watched her do it. It was a strange thing to watch. She didn’t turn round. She didn’t move at all. She sat with her back to the mountains and her face to the town and her eyes shut, and something in her went out, behind her, over her shoulder, the way a man reaches behind him in the dark for a door he knows is there. He felt it go. A thin line of something, very fine, going north and up and away, eleven miles, into the valley. And stopping there, at the edge of it, held, the way a dog’s held on a short lead.

“Eleven,” said Hyvred.

Severin closed his eyes too.

“There were nine on the sixth night,” said Hyvred. “There were seven all spring and summer. Since the elves left. Seven, standing outside the thirteenth ring where a fourteenth would go. Not in a ring. In a little curve. Like the start of one.” She breathed. “Nine on the sixth night. Ten on the ninth. Eleven now. A boy and a woman, the last two. I can’t see faces like this. I can feel there’s more of them. And the curve’s longer.”

“Going round.”

“Going round,” said Hyvred. “The outside of everything. The way the thirteenth went round the twelfth.” She opened her eyes. “They’re building another ring, Majesty. Out of threadless. One at a time. Like the thirty-nine built the thirteenth.”

He went down to the guardhouse at dark and they told him all of it again, round a table, the way soldiers do: Brannoch with his ploughed-field face and his arms folded, Fenwick with eleven pages under his hand and his spectacles on his forehead, Corvin with a map. Hild at the end of the table, sitting very still with her head a little on one side, the side with the eye, listening the way she listened. And the five of them from another world, along the wall on a bench, because Brannoch had wanted them out and Corvin had wanted them in and Severin had said in.

The cow girl. The bee girl, with her wings folded flat and her chalk-white fingers in her lap and two round blue bees asleep on her shoulder. The tiger boy, who couldn’t sit still and was trying to. The calm one with the green snake. And the brawler, the one who’d stood in the valley road in the spring with his hands open at his sides and said not here to a wind that was taking his town, and been the anvil. Drew. He sat at the end of the bench with his elbows on his knees and his big quiet face turned toward the table, and watched everyone, and didn’t say anything, which Severin had learned meant he was listening harder than any of them.

Fenwick did the numbers. He always did the numbers.

“Thirteen rings, Majesty,” said Fenwick. “Full since the spring. Four hundred and some of the turned, by the girl’s count, with the thirty-nine that came from Valcrest to fill the last ring. The absence at the foot of the pass since the spring, facing the pass.” He turned a page. “The road swept clear a furlong past the bottom of the pass, toward us; the sweeping hasn’t moved since midsummer. And the fourteenth place. Seven since the spring. Eleven tonight.” He looked up. “If it’s like the thirteenth, Majesty, it’ll be about forty when it’s full. At the rate it’s going since the tenth, that’s—“

“Don’t,” said Severin.

Fenwick stopped.

“I don’t want the date,” said Severin. “Not tonight. You’ll work it out, and you’ll be right, and then everybody at this table will be counting days.” He looked round them. “We’ll count them when we have to.”

Brannoch unfolded his arms.

“With respect, Majesty,” he said. “I’ve been counting days since the spring.” He leaned forward. He’d been on the Redmarch; he’d been on the Redmarch the year Severin’s ninth came, and a great many years before, and he had the face for it. “We know where it is. We know what it’s doing. It’s building something, eleven miles up a road, and every week it’s a little bit more built. Give me the Watch and your own guard and the girl to see for us, and I’ll go up the pass in daylight and burn it.”

“Burn what?” said Severin.

Brannoch looked at him.

“The rings,” said Severin. “Four hundred people standing in a valley, Captain. You’ve seen them. Through the glass, on a clear day. You’ve seen their coats. Some of them are your coats.” He didn’t raise his voice. He’d never needed to. “Sergeant Dunmore’s ten are in the third ring. Trooper Pell. The villages up the western valleys that went visiting the first winter and didn’t come back. Half the people in those rings had names here once.” He looked at the bench along the wall. At the tiger boy, who had gone very still. “You’ll burn them?”

Brannoch didn’t say anything for a long time.

“They’re gone, Majesty,” he said at last. Quietly. “Whatever’s standing up there wearing their faces. They’re gone.”

“Maybe,” said Severin.

He looked at the cow girl.

She’d gone white. She had her hands together in her lap, very tight. She didn’t look at him; she looked at the tiger boy, beside her, and then at the table.

“He’s turning,” said Hyvred. Very low. “Ossie. Trooper Pell. Since the spring. Every morning Tiger runs to the gate and back, and every morning he’s turned a bit further. Toward the town. Toward the gate.” She swallowed. “I don’t know if it’s him. Or the thing. I don’t know if anybody’s in there.” She looked up at Brannoch. “But I don’t know that nobody is, either.”

The room was very quiet.

“Not yet,” said Severin.

He’d said it before. He’d said it from the gate of this town in the spring, standing in the road with Hild beside him, to a council in Solmere that wanted an army up the pass and a town emptied and the summoned sent somewhere safe, wherever that was. Not yet. He’d learned it from a man who said it to everyone, about everything, and meant something different by it every time, and always meant one thing underneath: I know what I’m going to do. I’m not going to do it until it’s true.

“Not yet,” he said again. “Nobody goes up the pass. Nobody burns anything. Nobody empties this town.” He looked round the table. “The thing in the valley is building a ring. We don’t know what it’s for. When it’s finished, something will happen, and we don’t know what, and we’ll be here. That’s what we’re for.” He paused. “Until then, we watch. We get ready. And we get the people here who’ll be needed.”

“Who?” said Corvin.

Severin thought about it. He’d been thinking about it since the road.

“Walls,” he said.

Hild turned her head.

“You,” said Severin to her. “And another one. There’s a girl in Duskhollow.” He’d heard it from a cord, a strip written out in Corvin’s hand and brought to him on the road by a rider, three days out of Pellow’s Cross: a strip from Cinderhold, through a cat girl in Solmere, in a chaplain’s careful words. A guard of Duskhollow stood in front of him on the red and made a wall. Rhea says Shaped. She stood in front of him. “Petra. Of Duskhollow. She was in a ditch on the Redmarch ten years ago and he held it over her all night. Now she’s Shaped. She stood in front of him and made a wall a hundred paces long.” He looked at Hild. “There aren’t many of you in the whole kingdom. I want two of them here.”

“She’s a guard,” said Fenwick. “Duskhollow’s guard. The Advisor’s posting.”

“Then I’ll ask the Advisor,” said Severin. “And I’ll ask her. By name.” He looked at Corvin. “Have Garrick read it to her himself. Not a rider. Him. Tell him the king asks for the girl from the ditch.”

Corvin wrote it down.

“And eyes,” said Severin.

He looked at the cow girl. She looked back at him, with her gold eye.

“You see further than anybody alive,” said Severin. “You see things I can’t feel. But you see them alone, and when you look too far, something looks back.” He turned his hands over on the table, the big burnt smith’s hands, palms up, the way he held them over a hearth. “There’s a boy at Duskhollow who put a red plain into thirty-five windows at sunset three weeks ago. For two seconds. Corvin told me. He wanted them to see it, and they did.” He paused. “And there’s a girl at the lake who read three hundred years of a register through seven people’s eyes on the night of the tenth, and came up breathing.”

“John,” said Hyvred. “And Thea.”

“I want the girl at the lake to look through you,” said Severin. “At the valley. Through the cords, the way she did on the tenth. So that it isn’t only your eyes on it. So that you’re not alone with it.” He looked at her. “And I want the boy here. With his lights. So that when the time comes, the rest of them see what you see. All of them. At once. The way they saw the red.”

Hyvred didn’t say anything for a long moment.

Then she put her hand over her face, and kept it there, and nodded.

“You’ll write to him,” said Kestra, on the stair, at midnight.

She’d come up behind him from the guardhouse with a lamp. She’d said nothing all evening; she never said anything at a table where he was the one deciding. She said it now, on the hundred-and-twelve-step stair of the north tower, halfway up, in the dark, with the lamp held low so he could see the hollows in the steps.

He knew who she meant.

“No,” said Severin.

“Majesty.”

“No.” He stopped, on the stair, with his hand on the cold stone of the wall, and turned round and looked down at her. She’d been his guard half her life and was the only person alive who argued with him on stairs. “I told him on the shingle. I’ll feel it if you need me, and you won’t, and I’ll feel it anyway.” He breathed. “It goes both ways, Kestra. He’ll feel it if he needs to. He’s got a ninth tier’s worth of feeling in him and more. He’ll know before any letter could get there.”

“And if he doesn’t come?”

“Then it’ll be because we didn’t need him,” said Severin. “And that’ll be the best thing that’s happened on this border in thirty years.”

Kestra didn’t say anything.

“He’s ploughing,” said Severin. More quietly. “That’s what I said in the spring. Not the lake. He’s ploughing. He’s got a sword on his wall that he made with his own hand on my anvil, for the day he leaves the lake, and he didn’t take it east. He took a rod.” He looked up the dark stair. “If I write to him, he’ll take it down. For me. Because I asked. And it’ll be my day, not his. I’m not going to steal that from him.” He was quiet. “My father made twelve plough blades in his forge on leave before I was king, one a day, and on the twelfth night I became what I am. Nobody asked him to. Nobody sent for him.”

“That was you,” said Kestra. “Not your father.”

“I know,” said Severin. “That’s what I mean.”

He went to the forge the next morning.

Thornwall had one. Of course it had; every town on every border had one, and this one was good, a long low stone building behind the guardhouse with two hearths and three anvils and a smith called Agnes who was sixty and had hands like his and had looked at him when he came in the door with an expression he’d last seen on Harl at Kingsferry, which was: oh, it’s you, then.

“Majesty,” said Agnes.

“I want to work,” said Severin.

She looked at him for a long moment. At his hands. At the little cross-peen hammer in his belt, his father’s, with its honey-coloured handle worn to the shape of a thumb, that he’d carried home from the lake.

“What on?” said Agnes.

He told her.

Not swords. He’d thought about swords on the road, the whole nineteen days, the way a smith thinks about the next job while he’s walking. Brannoch had swords. The Watch had swords, a hundred and twenty of them, good ones, Valcrest steel. If it came down the pass, the swords would come out, and he’d let them, because men had to defend themselves.

But he’d sat at a table last night and said some of them are your coats. And a girl with a gold eye had said I don’t know that nobody is.

“Shields,” said Severin. “Bosses and rims. Big ones. For a line. And staves.” He looked at the rack of bar on the wall. “Ash staves with iron heels. Long enough to keep a man off without opening him. For holding, not for killing.”

Agnes looked at him.

“Holding what?”

“Neighbours,” said Severin.

She didn’t say anything for a while. Then she went to the rack, and took down a bar of good iron, and put it on the near anvil, and handed him the tongs.

He worked all day.

He hadn’t worked all day since the lake. He’d worked five days there, at a star, with his palms up over a hearth feeding the fire with everything he had while a fisherman struck and a boy from another world tapped where. That had been the biggest work of his life. This was the smallest. A shield boss. A dome of iron the size of two hands, drawn out from a bar, raised over the horn of the anvil, a hundred blows, a hundred and fifty, the way his father had taught him at nine. Keep your thumb out of the way. Don’t hurry the iron. It tells you when it’s ready.

He made eleven.

He didn’t decide to make eleven. He found, at the end of the day, with the light going and Agnes banking the second hearth, that there were eleven of them on the bench, in a row, cooling, grey-black, with the hammer-marks on them still bright; and he looked at them, and counted them, and thought about a curve of threadless standing outside a thirteenth ring eleven miles away.

He made one more before he stopped. Twelve. He didn’t know why. He wanted it to be more than them.

He went up the tower that night with his hands still black.

She was there, in her blanket, facing south. She opened her eyes when he came up through the trapdoor and looked at his hands and almost smiled.

“You’ve been at the forge,” said Hyvred.

“Shields. For holding.”

“Holding who?”

“Whoever comes down the pass wearing a face we know,” said Severin. He sat down beside her against the north parapet with his back to the mountains, the way he had the night before, and his knees complained, and he let them. “I’d rather hold them than open them. Until somebody tells me there’s nobody inside.”

She didn’t say anything for a while.

“Thank you,” she said at last.

“Don’t thank me. It’s iron.” He looked at the town below them, the lamps, the long street, the north gate, a small dark shed against the inside of the north wall with a shape on its roof that was the tiger boy, sitting, looking north. “How many?”

She shut her eyes.

He felt her go. Over her shoulder, north, eleven miles, into the valley, held on its short lead. He sat beside her very still and didn’t let any of his own go with it; he didn’t want to be loud, out there. He wanted her to be able to feel.

“Eleven,” said Hyvred. And then, after a moment, more quietly: “Twelve. There’s one more. Just now. At the end of the curve.” She opened her eyes. “It’s a man in a farmer’s coat. I can’t see his face. I can feel his coat.”

Severin didn’t say anything.

He looked at his black hands in his lap. He’d made twelve.

“Not yet,” he said. To her. To the valley behind them. To the shape in the dark past the end of the maps that she’d felt turn and look at her and that he couldn’t feel at all. To a man on a jetty a long way east and north, who was probably sitting on the last plank right now with a rod across his knees and a red hen against his hip, not fishing, mostly; who’d feel it, if he needed to; who didn’t need to yet.

“Not yet,” he said again. Quietly. The way you’d say it over a hearth. Feed it. Don’t drown it.

Beside him, the cow girl leaned her head back against the stone, and shut her eyes, and didn’t look north; and behind them both, eleven miles off, in a valley at the foot of a pass, twelve people with nothing inside them stood in a short curved line outside thirteen rings of people who’d been called, and waited, and were patient, and were not in a hurry at all.
