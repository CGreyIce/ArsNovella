Chapter 327

Home Before the Anniversary

There was a crier in every town on the north road, and Alea heard the same thing in all of them.

In the square at Fennick, at noon, from a man on a mounting block with a bell. In the market at Pellow’s Cross, from a woman with a voice like a gull. At the Sallow ferry, from the ferryman himself, who’d been told to say it to everybody he took across and said it to her twice, because she had a sword on her back and he thought soldiers didn’t listen. By order of the Crown. Don’t pick up hooks. If you’ve a cut that’s gone grey and cold, go to a healer. Don’t close it. Go to a healer.

And on the road between, soldiers.

She’d passed the first of them three days out of Valcrest, going the other way: a column of the Marshal’s foot, two hundred, in grey and blue, with their pikes on their shoulders and their faces set east. Not marching to anything; there was no war, the criers said, and no army into the black. They were going to Cinderhold to stand on a line in the dirt. All night, every night. A man’s width at a time.

She’d stood at the side of the road with her pack and Astra and watched them go past, and one of them, a boy no older than Tamlin, had looked at the black hilt over her shoulder and then at her face and touched his cap.

She’d thought about Salty sitting on the band in the old west fort with blood coming out of his ears. It’s a front if somebody’s coming at it. And two hundred men walking east to do the same thing, because a king had said I want it full of holes.

She didn’t know why it made her want to cry. It did, though. She let it, at the side of the road, and then walked on.

She’d been on the roof of the Lance the night it happened.

Not on purpose. She hadn’t been able to sleep, the ninth night after the final, and she’d gone up the ladder from the attic room Lucan had given her, with the paper rod still on the chest by the bed, and sat on the leads with her knees up and looked north. The way she looked north every night. And at a quarter past one Chris had come up the ladder after her in his shirt with his window open in front of him and his face doing something she’d never seen a face do.

He’d sat down beside her.

“Something’s happening,” he’d said. “At Kingsferry.”

She couldn’t read the channel. None of the natives could. She’d known for four years it was there, the way you know there’s weather in the next valley. And Chris had sat beside her on the roof of the Broken Lance in the dark and read it to her. Line by line. As it came. In his flat careful voice that never went up or down.

Rope walk’s on fire.

Maud’s gone down to the fire.

There’s a ring on the customs-house steps.

It’s the rise. It’s for me.

She’d known, then. What her father was doing. Where he was. She’d sat on the roof a long way south and known exactly where he was: on the end of a jetty in the dark, a hundred miles up the river from the steps, reading the same lines; and that the old thing in him, the thing that went over walls, would be standing up.

She’s going in.

Chris read that one, and stopped, and they’d sat there.

He’d read her the rest. All of it. Three hours of nothing and then Calvin, and then Deston, and then Maud’s gone. And after it, in the flood of lines at dawn when everybody wrote at once, that Chris didn’t read all of, one from Gigi that he read out and then went quiet over, and then read again because Alea asked him to:

he’s at the near end. he sat down. the ash made him a seat.

Alea had put her face in her knees on the roof of the Lance.

“Did he—“ she’d said, when she could.

“He didn’t go,” said Chris. “Gigi says. He walked down the jetty. He stopped three planks short. He sat down.”

She’d stayed there till dawn with Chris beside her not saying anything, which was the kindest thing Chris had ever done for anybody, and when the sun came up over the bowl of the Lists, gold, on the great west wall with its forty lamps going out one by one, she’d gone down the ladder and packed her bag.

She hadn’t gone, in the end.

The letter came eight days later. To the Broken Lance, by the post-rider from the north, in a hand she knew better than her own because it was the hand she’d learned her letters beside: the native hand, careful and plain, not her mother’s slant. Her father’s. On the outside, her name. And Lucan’s inn. And nothing else.

He’d never written to her. Not once. Not in nineteen years. She’d never been anywhere long enough to need it, and when she had been, at the Lists, he’d come.

She opened it in the yard of the Lance standing up.

Alea,

I’m sat down. At the near end. The ash grew me a seat; it faces south. Gigi’s on the stones. The lamp’s lit. It’s yours till you’re home.

Sorrel asked you to the bar at Gullhaven. Chris says. Go.

Don’t come home because of me. Come home because you’re done.

And under it, where his name would go, a fish.

Not a very good one. He’d never been able to draw. She stood in the yard of the Broken Lance and looked at a fish with no fin drawn by a man who’d answered every letter he’d ever had and written first three times in his life, all of them this year, and she sat down on the edge of the horse trough and laughed until Lucan came out in his goose gown to see what was wrong.

She went to Gullhaven.

With Sorrel, on the coast road, four days, the two of them with their packs and Sorrel’s hooks over her shoulder in their sailcloth roll and Astra over Alea’s. Sorrel talked the whole way. About Tamlin, mostly, with a great deal of outrage, and about her mother’s boat, and the herring, and the bar.

The bar was where Sorrel’s father had gone over. Alea had known that since the first summer; her own father had told her, on the quay, the morning the Widow went, watch the bar at Gullhaven. A long sand bar across the harbour mouth where the river met the sea, and a sea that broke on it in white lines all day, and in a storm eight years ago a boat coming in too late on the wrong side of the tide.

Sorrel took her out to it on the third morning, in her mother’s boat, at slack water. They sat in the boat on the still grey sea a hundred yards off the bar and looked at it.

“I used to come out here every day,” said Sorrel. “After. When I was ten. With his hooks. I’d sit here and look at it and think I’d hook him back.” She was quiet. “You can’t, though. Hook a thing that’s gone. It doesn’t pull back.” She looked at Alea. “Your dad’s like that. Isn’t he. You can’t hook him. You’ve got to let him swim at you.”

Alea looked at the bar for a long time.

“He swam at a seat,” she said.

Sorrel laughed. Then she didn’t. “That’s good, though,” she said. “Isn’t it.”

“Yes,” said Alea. “It’s the best thing he’s ever done.”

She stayed eleven days. She hadn’t meant to. She learned to gut herring faster than Sorrel’s mother, which Sorrel’s mother said was impossible and then had to admit. She learned the knots the Gullhaven boats used, which were different from the lake’s. She stood on the quay at dusk and watched the boats come in over the bar on the tide, one after another, the way they’d come in for a hundred years, and Sorrel’s mother told her the name of every one and who’d been lost off it and when.

Watch properly, Maren had said. All of it. Not only him. The ones who lose.

She’d watched. In the Lists. She’d sat on the fourth tier of the east stand for four days and watched every bout of the Fledgling, the way Maren asked, the winners and the losers both; and she’d seen what the ones who lost did with their feet after. Most of them stood still. Some of them walked off fast. A few of them, the ones she’d remember, turned round in the chalk and looked at the one who’d beaten them, the whole of them, the way Tamlin had looked at her two summers in a row. And then came back next year.

She thought about the bar, and the boat that came in too late on the wrong side of the tide. And her father, who’d come too late to a rise on the wrong side of a host, and spent ten years after on the end of a jetty with his back to everybody but her.

And who’d sat down.

On the eleventh night in Gullhaven she opened her window in Sorrel’s mother’s loft, which smelled of tar and herring, and looked at it.

Forty-four. It had been thirty-eight since the spring.

She didn’t know what had moved it. She hadn’t fought anybody. She hadn’t held Astra in her hands since the road south except to wipe it. She’d sat in the stands and watched people lose, and sat in a boat and looked at a bar, and gutted herring.

She thought it might be the watching. Gleaming: the edge that throws light. You didn’t throw light by looking at the light. You threw it by seeing everything else.

She shut the window, and in the morning she went home.

The north road again. The criers. The soldiers going east.

At Caddow she met a carter coming down from Wrenhaven who knew her, the way everybody on the road north of Pellow’s Cross knew her now, the fox girl with the black sword, and he told her the news from the lake as if she’d asked, which she hadn’t. That the lamp was lit every night. That Odette had lost a goose and found it in the militia hall. That the Fowler girl’s arm had a pink spiral on it from wrist to elbow that she showed everybody. That Maren and the summoned girl with the black hair had burned the grey out of a boy from the weir in forty heartbeats and the boy had run home.

That the fisherman sat at the other end of his jetty now, the near end, by the ash, in a seat that had grown out of the ground, and nobody in Wrenhaven knew what to make of it.

“Folk walk down the lake road special,” said the carter, “to look. Like it’s a fair.” He shook his head. “Never seen him sit anywhere but the end in my life. Thirty years I’ve been on this road.”

“Nineteen,” said Alea.

“What?”

“He’s been at the lake nineteen years,” said Alea. “Not thirty.”

The carter looked at her for a long time. Then he laughed. “Feels like thirty,” he said. “Feels like he’s always been there.”

“I know,” said Alea.

At Wrenhaven she didn’t stop. She went through it at a walk at four in the afternoon, past the militia hall where Higu was at the loft window and saw her and didn’t wave but put her hand flat on the glass; past Odile’s infirmary with its four beds and the elder by the gate, with a new seat grown in the roots of it by the door that Gigi had made in the spring, and a woman sitting in it with a baby; past the post-house, where Mistress Hennet was in the yard and looked up at her and said, “He’s been down every day asking if there’s anything from the south. He doesn’t say it’s for you. He asks if there’s anything,” and went back inside.

And up the lake road.

She stopped at the top of the last hill.

She always did. It was the only road into the lake that wasn’t water, and from the top of it you could see the whole end of the lake laid out like a dish: the shingle and the alders and the cabin, and the jetty going out into the water, and the far shore. The sun was low. She took her pack off and sat on it at the side of the road with Astra across her knees, the way she’d sat on it in the spring going the other way, and looked.

The light went off the water. Then off the far hills, from the bottom up. The lake went grey, and silver.

The lamp came on.

She saw it catch. A small yellow point in the window of the cabin, very small from up here; and it wavered, and steadied, and stood.

She looked for the jetty.

He wasn’t on the end of it.

The last plank was empty. She’d never once in her life seen it empty at dusk. Thirty planks of jetty going out into the silver lake, and the end of them bare, and the water past it.

She looked at the near end.

At the landward end, where the planks met the shingle, by the first post and the ash. A dark shape, sitting low, against the post, in something that wasn’t the stones. A seat. And beside it on the shingle, smaller, with its knees up, another.

He was facing south. Down the lake. Toward the place where the Wren went out at the bottom, past the point.

And then he turned his head. Up the hill. As if he’d known.

He’d known. Of course he had. She’d have known his candle blind and deaf and a hundred miles off; she always had. He’d know hers.

He lifted his hand.

She lifted hers.

And down on the shingle at the near end of the jetty, Gigi got up off the stones, and stood, and brushed herself off, and went up the shingle to her bench, two paces nearer the water than it had been in the spring and three now, and sat on that instead. And left the stones beside the seat empty.

For her.

Alea put her pack on and went down the hill.

The hen saw her first.

The brown one, the one with opinions about the yard. It came round the side of the cabin at a run with its wings out, shrieking, and went straight past her to the step and stood on it and shrieked at the door, as if it were announcing her to the house. Which it was.

Her father didn’t get up.

She came down the shingle past the bench, and Gigi on it, who said “Oh, there you are,” and nothing else, and down to the near end of the jetty, to the first post and the ash and the seat. He was in it. With the rod across his knees, not casting. Facing south.

The seat was smooth and dark and warm-looking, grown up out of the ash’s roots against the post, with a back that came up exactly where his back went and an arm on the right side where his hand was. She’d never seen anything like it. She’d seen Gigi’s bench grow a back at midwinter and leaves in the spring; she’d seen the seats at Odile’s door. This was different. This one fitted. It looked as if it had always been there and the jetty had been built next to it.

She sat down on the stones beside it. Where Gigi had been. Where it was warm still.

He didn’t say anything.

She didn’t either.

They sat at the near end of the jetty in the dusk with the lamp lit behind them in the window, and the lake going dark in front, and the stars coming out in it one at a time, upside down. The hen gave up shrieking and went to bed. Up on the bench, Gigi started humming something with a tune.

After a long time he put his hand out, sideways, without looking. And put it on her head. Between her ears.

She leaned into it.

The list was on its nail by the hearth.

She looked at it when she went in to take her pack off. Higu’s list, his side to the wall. Her side out. Her mother’s slant.

Come home. A line through it.

Tell me where you found it. A line through it.

Write first. To Halvard. Not an answer. A line through both.

Let somebody sit with you. While I’m gone. Gigi.

There wasn’t a line through it.

She looked at it for a long time. Then she took Higu’s pencil off the shelf, where she’d left it in the spring, and stood at the hearth with the list on its nail in front of her, and drew a line through the fourth.

Then she stood there a bit longer, and looked at the room under it. A lot of room. More than half the sheet.

She didn’t write anything. She didn’t know what the fifth one was yet. She’d know. She always had.

She put the pencil back.

There was a letter on the table.

Under the salt jar, where Lucan’s had been in the spring. Cream paper. The king’s, she thought, or Halvard’s, which was the same paper. Folded once. Unsealed. Opened. On the outside, in the kingdom’s hand, small and very neat, one word. Ars.

She didn’t read it.

She knew whose it was. She knew it had come nine days on a good horse from the west tower, because Mistress Hennet’s riders were the only ones who came all the way to the door. She could see, without opening it, that it was short, because the paper was only folded once and there was nothing showing through. And she could see from the corner, where the fold had gone soft, that he’d opened it and shut it and opened it again a great many times.

He hadn’t answered it. There was no sheet beside it. No ink on the table. The pen was in its jar.

He came in behind her with the rod and put it against the wall. He saw her looking at it.

He didn’t say anything.

She didn’t ask.

That was the rule. It had always been the rule, at the lake, with him; you didn’t ask. You sat on the step and waited and one day, if you waited long enough, he’d tell you, or he wouldn’t, and either way you’d sat. She’d learned it before she could walk. Everybody who came to the lake learned it. Gigi had learned it so well she’d grown a bench out of it.

She went and sat on the step.

He came after a while, with two cups. He put one in her hands. He sat down beside her, on the step, in the door, with the lamp lit behind them and the lake dark in front; not at the near end of the jetty, in his seat. Here. With her.

They drank the tea. It was hot.

“He asked what I need,” said her father.

She didn’t move.

“Halvard,” he said. “In the letter. How are the hens. And then: What do you need.” He looked at his cup. “Nobody’s ever asked me that.”

She didn’t say anything. She held her cup in both hands and looked at the lake.

“I don’t know,” he said. “I’ve been sitting in that seat for six weeks thinking about it. I don’t know.” He was quiet. “I’ve never needed anything. That’s what I’d have said, a year ago. It’s what I’ve said to everybody. I’m fine. It’s fine. I’ve got the lake.” He turned the cup in his hands. “And then I sat down at the near end of my own jetty because a girl was sitting there, and a tree grew me a chair. And I think that means I was wrong. About the needing.” He was quiet a long time. “I just don’t know what it is yet.”

Alea looked at the lake.

She didn’t ask. She didn’t say you’ll find out. She didn’t say tell me when you do. She sat on the step beside her father, home, eleven days before the eleventh anniversary of the night her mother died on a rise, and let him not know, and held her tea.

“You’re back,” he said, after a long time. As if he’d only just noticed.

“I’m back,” said Alea.

“Did you hold it up?”

“The rod?” She smiled into her cup. “I held it up. Forty thousand of them went up round it. Lucan had them under the benches.”

He nearly smiled. She saw it. In the dark, in the light from the lamp behind them, the corner of his mouth.

“Good,” he said.

“It’s in my pack. It came home.”

“Good,” he said again. And then, after a moment: “Put it back in the box. With hers.”

She looked at him.

“It’s been to two finals now,” he said. “That’s enough for anybody.”

She laughed. It came out of her before she could stop it, the way it had in the spring, wet, the way Gabe’s did. And her father sat on the step beside her with his tea and the lamp lit behind him, and didn’t put it out; and they sat there till the stars were all the way out in the lake, and Gigi went home humming up the lake road, and the hen muttered in its sleep in the yard, and she was home.
