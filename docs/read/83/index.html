The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 3: The Anniversary

Chapter 83

Ad Astra

4,337 words · 19 min read

Wendy believed it now.

That was the strange thing. She kept waiting for it to feel different. She’d spent her whole life not believing things, carefully, on purpose, the way you’d keep a hand on the rail on a ship. Not luck, not fate, not horoscopes, not the Advisor, not the way home. Not the Hero of the Redmarch. She’d said it in the chapel, two months ago, in front of the empty trestles, with Yan looking at her: I believe it. And she’d waited to feel the ground move under her.

It hadn’t. Nothing had moved. She’d just gone on believing it, quietly, every day since, the way she went on wearing her headphones. Lavender over-ears, round her neck on their band, with nothing to plug them into. She didn’t believe in much. But she believed in keeping things that had come through with you.

And now she believed in a man with a fishing rod who’d walked across the red and carried all five of them, and looked at each of their faces, and not said anything. And sent them a stone.

She’d read it in the courtyard yesterday, with her fingertips. To Diwa, my love. In English. In letters nobody else in this world could read.

She’d touched the letters one by one, and then put her hand over the little star on her necklace, and kept it there for a long time.

They took it out at dawn.

There was frost on the red. The first proper frost of the year, a white skin on everything: the markers, the grey grass, the old cart track between the rows. The red ground showed through it in patches, the colour of rust under salt. The sky was very clear and very pale and the mountains of Kharvos stood up black against it in the east, with snow on them already, high up.

It was cold. Wendy had her navy cardigan buttoned up to the neck and her hands in the pockets, and the pockets were heavy.

Stones. Children’s stones. A handful each, out of the sack: they’d shared them out in the chapel at first light, the four of them who weren’t carrying the big one, counting them into each other’s hands. Maigo had put his in the front pocket of his hoodie, the way he’d carried the sapling in the spring. Yan had tucked his into the fold of his robes, against his chest. Mashi had held his in his two hands, cupped, and looked at them for a long time before putting them away. Wendy had eleven. She’d counted. Small grey ones, and brown ones, and one white one from the lake shore, smooth as an egg.

Some child in a town she’d never seen had carried each of these down a road to a forge. And a man had gone down on one knee, and taken each one, and said thank you.

She kept her hands round them, in her pockets, the whole way.

Salty carried the stone.

Nobody had argued. Aldous had looked at him in the courtyard, the night before, and Salty had looked back, and Aldous had nodded. Salty’s ribs had knitted. They’d knitted crooked, a little, Aldous said, the way bones do when the man they belong to won’t lie still; he’d carry them crooked for the rest of his life. He didn’t care. He’d said so. They’ll match the sword.

The stone was lighter than the sword. Not light. It was iron, and it was that iron, the iron nobody could name. But it was half. And Salty had been Drawn since the summer, and he’d carried the whole sword half a day across the red with the forge in him running on nothing, and he got the stone up onto the folded blanket on his shoulder in the courtyard at dawn without a sound, and stood there under it with his blunt face set, and said:

“Don’t count.”

Wendy looked at him.

“Last time you counted,” said Salty. “Three hundred, then down. Every stretch.” He settled the weight on his shoulder. “Don’t count this time.”

“Why?”

“Because I’m not putting it down,” said Salty. “Not till it’s there.”

Wendy looked at him for a long moment, under the stone, with the frost on his hair.

“Idiot,” she said.

“Yeah,” said Salty.

She didn’t count.

They went out through the east gate under the horns.

Not so many of them, this time. Aldous, in front, with his book. Rhea, with her sword on her shoulder and her new pink scar across her scalp and a look on her face as if she were daring the red to try something. Four soldiers. Sergeant Teague, with his arm still in a sling, who’d come anyway, because, he said, he’d been there for the first half and he was damned if he’d miss the second. And Garrow the smith, from the north, in his leather apron under his coat, with the king’s father’s hammer in his belt, walking beside Salty with one big hand held out flat under the end of the stone’s long black spike, not touching it, just there, in case.

And the five of them.

The winch cart took them as far as the last markers, the way it had in the summer. Then they went on by hand. Into the gullies. Across the old riverbed. Out onto the open red beyond.

Nothing came.

Wendy had been waiting for them. All of them had. They walked through the gullies with their eyes on the clay banks, the way they’d walked in the summer, waiting for the long grey shapes to come up over the lip, fifty of them, a hundred, still-then-somewhere-else. Wendy had an arrow on the string the whole way. She didn’t draw. She just held it there, the way she always did, feeling for where it wanted to go.

It didn’t want to go anywhere. There was nothing to go to.

“They’re not here,” said Yan, after the first hour. His eyes were half shut. Suzu was wound round his neck, scarf-sized, with her green head up, tasting the air. “Not in the gullies. Not on the red. Not anywhere I can feel.” He was quiet. “There’s nothing cold for miles.”

“Since the summer,” said Aldous, from the front. “Not one. Not at the edge of the red. Not counting the towers. Not anywhere.” He looked back at them. “They haven’t come within sight of Cinderhold since the day he came across the red. Three months. Not once.”

Nobody said anything.

“It’s not flinching,” said Maigo.

They all looked at him. He was walking barefoot on the frost, with his hands in the front pocket of his hoodie round his stones, and his sunglasses pushed up on his antlers. His face had a strange look on it.

“The ground,” said Maigo. “It’s not flinching. It always flinches. Every time something walks on it. It flinched at us, for months.” He put his root-toed foot down on the frost, flat, and held it there. “It’s quiet. The way it went quiet under him. In the summer, when he walked across it. It’s still quiet.” He swallowed. “It’s been quiet for three months. Where he walked. Like it remembers.”

The grave came up out of the red at noon.

The low rise above where the river had once run. The line of dead trees on the far side of it, grey and bare. The cairn of grey stones. And beside the cairn, the tree.

It had no leaves.

Wendy’s heart went, for a moment, before she understood. It was autumn. Nearly winter. The young tree, Maigo’s tree, the one he’d carried out across the red in his hoodie pocket in the spring, stood beside the cairn with its pale bark and its slim branches bare against the sky, every leaf gone.

“It’s asleep,” said Maigo, before anyone could ask. Very quietly. “It’s fine. It’s just asleep. Trees do that.” He was looking at it the way you’d look at someone you love who’s sleeping. “It’ll wake up in the spring.”

And the flowers.

They hadn’t gone. That was what made Wendy stop walking. In the spring, Aldous had said, they came up every year and flowered for a few weeks and went over, gone till next year. This year they’d flowered all summer. Maigo’s tree. Now it was nearly winter, and the flowers were gone over, the white and blue petals long since blown away across the red. But the plants were still there. Low and green. A ring of green, in the frost, round the cairn, round the tree, round the hole at the head of the grave. Green in winter, on the Redmarch, where nothing grew.

“They’re staying,” said Maigo. “This year. I think they’re going to stay all winter.”

Salty walked up the rise with the stone on his shoulder, and into the ring of green, and stopped at the head of the cairn, by the hole.

The hole where the sword had stood. Eight years. A long, deep, narrow wound in the red ground, with crumbling edges, that the rain had filled in the spring and the summer had dried. It was full of frost now, white at the bottom.

Salty stood over it, under the stone, breathing hard.

Garrow went to him. And Aldous, and Rhea, and Yan. Between them, very slowly, they took the weight off Salty’s shoulder, off the blanket, and turned the stone in their hands, upright, the way it was meant to stand. The broad black face with its rounded top, and the long black spike below.

They held it over the hole.

“Let it go,” said Garrow. “Gently. Let it find its own way.”

They let it go.

It slid in.

Wendy would never forget the sound. There wasn’t one, really. The long black spike went down into the hole in the red ground the way a key goes into a lock it was made for. Smoothly. All the way. Deeper and deeper, the whole length of it, without catching once, without anyone pushing. As if the ground remembered. As if the hole had been waiting eight years for exactly this shape to come back to it.

It stopped when the bottom of the face touched the earth.

And stood.

Black, in the ring of green, in the frost, at the head of the cairn, beside the sleeping tree. Upright. Steady. The light lying on its face soft and still, like light on water. Facing west.

And across it, near the top, the letters. In English. That nobody born in this world could read.

TO DIWA, MY LOVE

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then Garrow, the smith from the north, who’d carried it a month across the kingdom behind two oxen, went down on one knee on the frost in front of it. Slowly, the way a big man kneels. He didn’t say anything. He just knelt there, with his big soot-marked hands on his thighs, and looked at it. The way you’d look at a piece of work you’d helped make that was better than anything you’d ever make yourself.

“It’s right,” he said at last. Very quietly. To nobody. “It’s standing right.” He swallowed. “He’d want to know it’s standing right.”

They laid the stones.

Maigo did it first. He took the children’s stones out of the front pocket of his hoodie, one at a time, and knelt in the ring of green, inside the flowers, at the foot of the cairn, and set them down. Carefully. In a curve. Each one touching the next. Talking to them as he did it, under his breath, the way he talked to the tree.

Then the rest of them. One after another. Yan, from the fold of his robes, with Suzu’s head lowered over his hands, watching. Mashi, slowly, from his cupped hands, looking at each stone before he set it down, as if he were learning it. Salty, who hadn’t been given any to carry, and took a handful from Maigo’s pile and set them down with his big square hands, very gently, the way he’d set down the sword on the cobbles in the summer.

Wendy laid hers last.

Eleven. She’d counted. She knelt in the frost inside the ring of green and took them out of her cardigan pockets one at a time, and set them down at the end of the curve, closing it. Grey ones, and brown ones. And the last one, the white one from the lake shore, smooth as an egg. She held that one a moment longer than the others. Then she set it down, right at the foot of the black stone, in the middle, where the curve began and ended.

A ring. Small stones, the size of a child’s fist, all different, inside a ring of green, round a grave with a black iron headstone, on the Redmarch, in the frost.

So she knows a whole town carried her stone.

Garrow, still on his knees, reached into his coat. He took out something small. A stone, dark and smooth and slightly scorched, that had been part of the hearth of a forge on a shingle shore. He held it up, so they could see. Then he leaned forward and set it in the ring beside Wendy’s white one.

“From the forge,” he said. “Where they made her.”

Aldous prayed.

He stood at the foot of the cairn with his book open in his hand, an old man in a black coat gone grey at the elbows, and prayed in the church tongue nobody spoke anymore, slowly, the way he’d prayed over a thousand graves on this ground. Wendy didn’t understand the words. She didn’t need to. She stood with her hands in her empty pockets and her silent headphones round her neck, and listened to the sound of it, and the wind.

Yan stood beside her with his eyes closed.

“It’s still asleep,” he said quietly, when Aldous had finished. “The cold. Under her.” He opened his eyes. “It hasn’t woken once. Not since he sat here in the summer. Three months. It’s like…” He stopped. “It’s like somebody put a hand on it. And it’s been asleep under the hand ever since.”

Maigo, on his knees by the stones, laid his palm flat on the green earth inside the ring, and was quiet for a long time.

“It’s still there,” he said. “The hand. His. Holding her.” His voice cracked. “And there’s more now. All the stones. All those kids. I can feel them. Little warm things. Holding on too.”

They stayed until dusk. Nobody said they should. Nobody said they should go. They just stayed, the way he’d stayed in the summer, sitting on the frost on the low rise above where the river had once run, round a grave on the Redmarch, not saying much.

Salty sat with his back against the fallen grey tree at the edge of the rise, where they’d waited for the man in the summer. Wendy sat beside him. She put her head on his shoulder. He let her.

“Eleven,” said Salty, after a long time.

Wendy looked up at him.

“The list,” said Salty. “In Malacca. The things to eat. He sent me eleven.” He was looking at the black stone. “I told you about it. On the wall, in the summer. We never booked the flights.”

“I know.”

“When we find him,” said Salty, “I’m making him take us. All eleven. In one day. He said we could do it in one day if we were serious about it.” His blunt face didn’t change. “I’m serious about it.”

“There’s no Malaysian food here,” said Wendy. “There’s no Malaysia here.”

“Then he’ll have to make it,” said Salty. “He’s a fisherman now. He can catch something and make it into eleven things. He’s got thirty-four years. He’s had time to learn.”

Wendy laughed. She didn’t mean to. It came out of her short and wet and startled, the way it had in the chapel in the summer, the first time Yan told her.

“Trust,” she said.

Salty looked down at her.

“That’s what he’d say,” said Wendy. “If you asked him. He’d say yeah, I’ll cook you eleven Malaysian dishes on a lake in another world. Trust.” She wiped her eyes. “And it’d be a lie. And it’d be true. Both. At the same time.”

“Most good things are,” said Salty. It was what Yan had said to her, in the chapel. She’d told him.

The sun went down behind them, in the west, over the red, enormous and orange. The whole Redmarch went the colour of a coal for a few minutes, and the black stone at the head of the cairn stood up against it, dark, with the light lying across its face, and the letters on it.

And then the light went, and the sky went grey and then blue and then dark, very clear and very cold, and the first star came out.

Right over the stone.

Wendy saw it first. She always saw things first. A single star, low in the east, over the mountains of Kharvos, very bright, exactly above the rounded top of the black stone, as if somebody had hung it there.

She thought about four words scratched into black iron with the point of a knife. To the stars, through hardships. One letter gone over twice. And four more, cut clean into this iron, with one gone over twice too.

She stood up.

She didn’t decide to. She just found she was on her feet, with her bow in her hand, and an arrow on the string. The others looked at her. She didn’t look at them.

She’d always been good with the bow. From the second day, three arrows in the centre at fifty paces. Her arrows curved round things, all year, because she could feel where they wanted to go. That was a gift, Aldous said. A sense. Not aura. Never aura. She’d tried, all year, the way Salty had tried, the way Rhea had shown them. Let it come up. Put it into the thing you trust. And it had never once come.

Aura goes into whatever you trust, Aldous had said, the first week, in the yard. Wendy had listened to that and thought: well, that’s me finished, then.

She’d never trusted anything enough. Not an arrow. Not a bow. Not herself. She’d trusted Salty, and you couldn’t shoot Salty at things.

She drew.

Slowly. All the way back, to her ear, the string cold against her cheek, the arrow pointing up. Not at anything on the red. Up. At the star over the stone.

She didn’t feel for where it wanted to go. She knew where it wanted to go. It wanted to go where the letters said.

She thought about a man with a fishing rod who’d walked across the red and carried all five of them. Who’d looked at each of their faces and not said anything, and then sent them a stone with my love on it in a language only they could read. Who’d done everything, all year, quietly, from somewhere none of them could see, without once asking to be believed.

She believed him.

She let it go.

It went up.

Not the way an arrow goes up. An arrow goes up and slows and turns and comes down; Wendy had shot a thousand of them and she knew the shape of it in her bones. This one didn’t slow. It went up off the string with a sound like a breath let out, and up, and up, past the height an arrow should go, past the height anything should go, and as it went, it shone.

Faintly. A thin silver line, rising, the colour of the star it was going toward. Wendy felt it go out of her. Down her arm, along the bow, into the arrow, the way Salty said it went into a sword. Like water filling a jar. Like warmth leaving a room when a door opens. Out of her, all of it, everything she’d never been able to put anywhere, into a single arrow going up off the Redmarch at the first star.

It went up until she couldn’t see it anymore.

It didn’t come down.

She stood there with the bow still raised and her arm shaking and her face wet, looking at the star.

Then she opened her window, because her hands were doing it before she told them to.

Thirteen. Of thirty-five.

She stood there and looked at it for a long time. The word. Drawn. Hers. Out of all the things she’d never believed.

Behind her, nobody said anything. She heard Maigo make a small sound, very quietly, and stop himself, which was the most extraordinary thing that had happened all day. She heard Rhea let out a breath. She heard Aldous close his book.

And she heard Salty get up off the frost, slowly, with his crooked ribs, and come and stand beside her. Not in front. Beside. With his big square hand, very carefully, taking the bow out of her shaking one.

“If you go,” said Salty quietly, “I go.”

Wendy looked at him.

They’d been passing it between them for a year. She’d said it in the summoning hall, the first day. He’d said it on the wall in the winter, in his coat, looking out at the dark: if you go, I go. If you stay, I stay. And she’d said that one I believe. And on the wall in the summer she’d said it to him, and he’d said it back. Back and forth, like a ball between two people who never once dropped it.

She said it back now.

“That one,” said Wendy, “I believe.”

It started to snow.

Just a few flakes, at first. Coming down out of the clear dark sky from nowhere, slowly, white, the way the first snow always comes, so that you’re never quite sure it’s snowing until it is. One landed on the black stone, on the rounded top, and sat there, and didn’t melt. Another landed on the tree. On Maigo’s antlers. On Suzu’s head, and she hissed at it.

Then more. Then a great many, all at once, soft and slow and silent, out of the dark. Snow on the Redmarch. White on the red. On the frost, and the ring of green, and the small stones, and the cairn, and the face of the black iron, settling in the letters, filling them, so that for a moment, in the starlight, TO DIWA, MY LOVE stood out white on black, clear enough for anyone to read.

“Second winter,” said Yan quietly.

“She’s got a roof now,” said Maigo. He was looking at the snow settling on the top of the stone, on the sleeping branches of the tree. His voice was very small. “Like Ossie’s shed. She’s got a roof.”

They stood there, all of them, in the falling snow, round the grave, until it was too dark to see. Then Aldous lit a lantern. And they walked home across the red together, slowly, at Salty’s pace, toward the lights of Cinderhold and the horns over the gate, with the snow coming down round them, and the ground quiet under their feet.

Nobody counted.

Wendy wrote it in the channel that night, in the barracks, sitting on her bunk with her headphones round her neck and the snow still falling outside the window.

The channel exploded. She’d known it would. Two hundred lines now, and times on all of them, so she could scroll back up and watch it happen, line by line, minute by minute: Maigo in capitals again, finally, SHE BELIEVES IT NOW, and Kei, and Rachael, and Tiger, and Hana, and a long one from Chris that was mostly numbers, and Dennis saying thirteen, that’s lucky somewhere, and Lucan’s group all at once.

And after it, a minute later, from somewhere else, a line Wendy read three times:

That was all. Just the number. Wendy didn’t know why it made her cry. She thought, maybe, that Xav was counting too. That somewhere upriver, somewhere north, somewhere a long way off where it was cold and the fish were good, somebody else might be counting with him.

She closed her window. She lay down on her bunk with her silent headphones round her neck and the little star on her necklace in her hand.

Outside, the snow kept falling on the Redmarch. On a fort with horns over its gate. On a grave a long walk out on the red, with a black iron stone at its head and a sleeping tree beside it and a ring of children’s stones inside a ring of green. On the letters, filling them.

And a long way north-west, past the edge of the red, past the hills and the forests and the long grey grassland, past the crossroads at Hethe, on a lake that had just begun to freeze from the north shore, the same snow was falling on a jetty, where a man sat with a fishing rod in the dark, and a window open on his knees, and read the channel for a long time.

Then he closed it.

And sat there in the snow, not fishing, with his face turned east, toward a grave he couldn’t see.

He was smiling.
