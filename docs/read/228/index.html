The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 8: The Third Seal

Chapter 228

The Third Move

7,523 words · 33 min read

Xav woke before the light, the way he had every morning at the lake, and lay on his bedroll at the edge of Gigichi’s hedge and listened to everybody breathing.

Six mornings. He’d counted. Six mornings he’d woken in the dark and lain here with the briar over him and the stars going out one at a time beyond the top of it, and got up, and gone down the shingle, and out along the jetty, thirty-four planks, and sat down on the last one beside Declan. And not done it.

Declan had never asked. He’d been on the end of the jetty every morning before Xav got there, with the rod and the lamp and the hens, and he’d moved over a little on the plank when Xav sat down, and that was all. They’d sat. They’d watched the mist come off the water. Declan had fished. On the third morning a heron had come down in the shallows by the shingle and stood there on one leg for an hour, and they’d watched that too. On the sixth, yesterday, Declan had said, without turning his head, “Good popiah,” meaning the night before, meaning Rui Xiang’s shirt, and Xav had laughed so hard the hens got up.

That was all. Six mornings. Not a word about the door.

Xav lay on his bedroll and thought about it. The way he thought about everything: slowly, from every side, until he could see the whole shape.

He’d known it would be here. He’d known on the quay at Duskhollow last summer, standing alone at dawn with an ash stick, looking at the place where the third move should have been. Next year. On the jetty. For the tenth. He’d known it on the sand at the Lists with his hand on the handle and Rui Xiang coming from nowhere. He’d known it with Declan at the back of his neck behind the rail, saying save one for the jetty, and himself saying I am without turning round.

He’d saved it. He’d brought it here, carefully, the whole year, the way you’d carry a full cup.

And for six mornings he’d sat beside the one person in two worlds who could catch it and not opened the door, because he was afraid.

Not of losing. He’d lost to Declan more times than he could count, in two worlds. He’d lost to him in a server at two in the morning and on a shingle beach at dawn and in a computer lab at seventeen, at a game neither of them was supposed to be playing in class. Losing was fine. Losing was the most ordinary thing in the world.

He was afraid of the other thing. The thing he’d worked out on the quay at Duskhollow and never said out loud to anyone except Petra, once, on the gravel bar, with the river going past.

The last time anything had made Declan move without deciding, a plain had gone red.

Xav lay on his bedroll in the dark and listened to the lake.

Then he got up.

Declan was on the end of the jetty with the rod.

Xav walked out to him. Thirty-four planks. He’d counted them every morning; he counted them now. The mist was lying on the water flat and white, a few feet thick, so that the jetty went out into it like a road into a cloud, and the lamp on the last plank was a smudge of yellow in the grey. The hens were in their row. The big red one looked at him and didn’t get up.

He didn’t sit down.

He stood on the twenty-ninth plank, five from the end, with his practice sword on his back, and waited.

Declan reeled in. Slowly. He laid the rod down along the planks beside the lamp, carefully, the way he always laid it down. Then he stood up, and turned round, and looked at Xav standing on the twenty-ninth plank in the mist with his sword on his back and his feet set.

He didn’t say anything for a while.

“Now?” he said.

“Now,” said Xav.

Declan looked at him a moment longer. Then he nodded, once, and came back along the jetty past him without a word, and down onto the shingle, and walked along the waterline in his bare feet until he found a piece of driftwood the length of his arm, grey and smooth and a little crooked. He picked it up. He weighed it in his hand.

The same stick. Or one just like it. Xav knew it the way you’d know a voice.

“On the shingle,” said Declan. “Not the jetty. You’ll want room.”

They didn’t wake anybody. They didn’t have to.

Petra was already on the stones. Xav didn’t know when she’d come down. She was sitting on the big flat rock at the top of the shingle where Alea had sat the spring before last, the morning Xav made him move twice, with her driftwood stick across her knees and her face doing nothing; and she didn’t get up, and she didn’t say anything, and she didn’t look away.

Then the hedge woke.

Xav felt it before he heard it. Deston first, probably; Deston would have heard two threads go down to the shingle in the dark and known exactly which two. Then the others. Shapes at the lake-side gap of the hedge, in the grey, one and two at a time, coming out onto the slope and stopping there, and then coming down. Gabe, enormous, in his shirt. Alea, with Astra in her hand, not on her back, as if she’d grabbed it without thinking. Thea, reading something nobody else could see. Higu with the book. Rui Xiang, very quiet for once, and Malcolm, and Azac standing between all of them and the slope without noticing. John, with his light already up over the water, gold, a square of it the size of a door, hanging in the mist.

Nobody said anything. They came down to the stones at the top of the shingle and sat down on them in a row, the way people sit at the edge of a ring, and waited.

Two of the town’s children were on the wall at the bottom of the lake road, in their nightshirts, swinging their legs. Xav didn’t know when they’d come. They always came. They’d come every morning since the spring before last, to the dawn class, to watch the fisherman stand on the shingle and not move.

Declan stood at the waterline with the stick hanging loose in his hand.

He was in his bare feet. He always was. His feet in the wet shingle, his shoulders easy, his face doing whatever it did, which Xav had never once been able to see; and the mist behind him, and the lake under the mist, and the far shore nowhere.

Xav drew his practice sword.

He knew the first move. It was his.

You moved while the other man was still deciding. That was the whole of it. That was Now, the thing he’d found in the first summer at Duskhollow on the gravel bar: the moment before the other man’s body made up its mind, when there was a lean starting and a weight shifting and a decision coming, and you could feel it coming, and you went in before it got there.

He’d done it on Declan the spring before last. On this shingle. It had worked once. Declan had moved.

He did it now.

He felt for the lean. The weight. The deciding.

There wasn’t one.

There never was, with Declan. That was the first thing anybody learned on this shingle. The man didn’t decide; or he decided so far down, so long before, that there was nothing left on top to feel. He just stood there, the way a post stands in a lake. Now reached for what was coming, and found the lake.

So Xav went anyway.

Not on the lean. On the breath. On the out-breath, at the bottom of it, the place Declan had taught them all on this shingle, where you were emptiest. He went in low, from the left, the way he always came, the way Declan knew he always came; and the practice sword went for the ribs, fast, flat, at the place where a man standing still in bare feet has to do something or be touched.

Declan moved.

One step. To the right. Unhurried. The practice sword went through the place he’d been.

One, said Petra, very quietly, from her rock.

The second move was Declan’s. The answer.

Xav knew what it would be, roughly. Not exactly; nobody ever knew exactly. But he knew it would come round from somewhere it shouldn’t have been possible to come round from, short and flat and light, toward his neck, and that it would come before he’d finished the first move. It always did. That was what the answer was.

The spring before last he’d made Declan move twice by being there before the answer. By going on.

He couldn’t do that now. Declan knew he could do that now. Declan had watched him do it, and had stood on the end of a jetty for more than a year since, thinking about it, the way he thought about fish.

So Xav did the other thing. The thing he’d found on the sand at the Lists with his hand on the door. Third.

He decided late.

So late it was the same moment as moving. So late there was no lean, no weight, no crosshair drifting a hair to the left before the shot. He didn’t decide what to do next. He let the stick come. He felt it come, the way you feel a draught; and at the very last instant, when it was a hand from his neck, he decided and moved in the same breath, both at once, nothing between them for anybody to feel.

Down. Under the stick. And up, inside it, on Declan’s other side, where the answer had left him open.

Declan moved again.

A half-step back. The stick came round behind him and the practice sword went past his chest a finger off; and he was somewhere else, and Xav was somewhere else, and the shingle went crunch under four feet at once.

Two, said Petra.

On the stones, Alea had both hands over her mouth. The way she had the spring before last. She’d been trying since she was thirteen, Xav knew. She’d never once made him move twice.

Declan stood at the waterline with the stick in his hand.

He was looking at Xav. Xav couldn’t see his face. He could see the rest of him. And the rest of him had gone very still, and very quiet, and very ready, in a way Xav had seen exactly once before: the spring before last, on this shingle, in the half-second after the second move, when he’d looked at Xav and said once more than anyone, ever.

He was waiting.

Not for a move. For a door.

Xav stood on the wet shingle with the practice sword low and the mist round him and thought: now or never.

Then he stopped thinking.

That was the third move. That was the whole of it, the thing he’d worked out on the quay at Duskhollow with an ash stick and nobody across from him. You couldn’t decide it. If you decided it, it was yours, and Declan would catch it, because Declan caught everything you decided. And you couldn’t let the other man decide it either, because that was the answer, and the answer was his. The third had to be neither.

So you opened the door, and let whatever was behind it come through.

He opened it.

He felt it open. The way you feel a door open behind you in a warm room, by the cold coming in. He’d stood with his hand on it at the Lists and felt something come through the gap and take his sword behind his back, without asking him, and he’d slammed it shut again, frightened. He didn’t slam it now. He took his hand off the handle. He stopped holding anything at all; not his breath, not his sword, not the thought of what he was going to do. He let go of all of it at once, the way Alea said you let go of a line when the fish runs, and stood there on the shingle with the door open and nothing in his hands.

And something came through.

He didn’t decide it. He’d remember that for the rest of his life. He watched it happen from somewhere a little behind himself, the way you watch your own hand reach for a falling cup before you know it’s falling. His body moved. All of it. Not left, not right, not low; up. Off the shingle. Turning in the air, the practice sword going from his right hand to his left behind his back without him, the way it had at the Lists, and coming round from above and behind his own shoulder in a line that he had never trained, never seen, never once in his life imagined. A line that came from nowhere, toward a man who’d never been anywhere it could reach.

And Declan had to move.

Xav saw it.

He was in the air, turning. He saw it the way he’d see it in John’s light afterwards, a hundred times, from above, slowed down, and never quite believe it.

Declan didn’t decide either.

He didn’t have time. Nothing had come at him from there in thirty-six years. Not the general on the rise, not a thousand things over the lip of a ditch. There was no answer ready, so far down, so long before, for something that came through a door neither of them had opened on purpose. There was nothing to do it with but the thing under the deciding. The thing that had moved him, once, on a rise, against a general, with everything he had, and turned a plain red.

And it came up.

Xav felt it. Everybody on the shingle felt it. For one heartbeat, something came up out of the bottom of the man at the waterline the way the lake comes up through a hole in the ice: huge, cold, dark, enormous, from very far down. The mist round him jumped. The shingle under his feet hissed, every stone at once. The hens on the jetty went flat on the planks. On the stones, somebody made a sound. Xav, in the air, with his sword coming round from nowhere, felt it reach him, and knew, with total clarity, the way you know a thing in a dream, that this was what the general had felt. On the rise. In the last second.

He wasn’t afraid.

He’d thought he would be. He’d lain awake on a quay for a year thinking he would be. He wasn’t. Because it came up out of Declan, all of it, the whole dark lake of it, for one heartbeat; and then Xav felt it turn.

Not stop. Turn. The way a big fish turns in deep water, at the end of a line, when it’s decided not to run.

And the driftwood stick came up, slowly, from under, and met the practice sword in the air above Declan’s head, at the end of a line that had come from nowhere; and caught it. Gently. The way a jetty post catches the lake.

And Declan stepped back a third time, onto the wet stones at the very edge of the water, with the lake round his ankles; and Xav landed on the shingle in front of him, on his feet, and stood there, with his sword resting on the stick, a finger from Declan’s face, and his own heart going like a drum.

Three, said Petra.

For a moment nothing happened.

The mist settled. The shingle went quiet. The hens on the jetty lifted their heads. Xav stood there with his sword on the stick and his breath going in and out and the door still open behind him, a cold draught down his back; and Declan stood with the lake round his ankles, holding the stick up, holding the practice sword on it, not moving.

Then he laughed.

Not the short one. Not the one that went up at the end, that he did when somebody said something stupid in a call. The other one. The one Xav hadn’t heard since a polytechnic canteen, ten years ago, in another world, when Rui Xiang had tried to eat a whole cendol in one go and it had come out of his nose. The one that started somewhere in the middle of him and couldn’t stop.

It came up out of him. Out of the same place. Out of the bottom of the lake, where the dark thing had come up from a heartbeat ago, and turned, and gone back down; and what came up after it was this. Helpless. Huge. A great shaking gusting laugh, with his head back and the stick still up in one hand and Xav’s practice sword still resting on it, so that it shook too.

He couldn’t stop.

He sat down. In the water. In the shallows at the edge of the lake, with the mist round him and his bare feet in front of him and the stick across his knees, and laughed until he couldn’t breathe; with his free hand over his face, and his shoulders going, and the lake lapping at his coat.

Xav stood on the shingle with the practice sword in his hand and looked down at him.

He’d heard that laugh once in this world before, he knew. Salty had told him, at the Lists, in a corner of the Lance, very seriously. On a forge floor, at Cinderhold, in the dark, the night Salty said twelve. He laughed till he couldn’t breathe. I didn’t know he still could.

He still could.

It had come up out of the same place as the red. The same place. And it had come up laughing.

Xav found that he was laughing too. He didn’t decide to. It came through the door with everything else.

“Now you’re dangerous,” said Petra.

She’d stood up off her rock. She was standing at the top of the shingle with her driftwood stick under her arm, looking at Xav, not at the man laughing in the lake.

“Not to him,” said Petra. “To everyone else.” She came down the shingle a few steps. “You opened it. On purpose. And something came through that neither of you chose, and he caught it.” She looked at Declan, in the water, with his hand over his face. “Nobody else alive can catch that. I couldn’t. I was ready and I couldn’t.” She looked back at Xav. “So you don’t open it again. Not on anybody. Not in the Lists, not on a road, not in a ditch. Not unless it’s him, or it’s the end.”

“I know,” said Xav.

“Say it.”

“Not unless it’s him,” said Xav, “or it’s the end.”

Petra nodded. Once. And then she did something Xav had never seen her do in two years of dawns. She put her hand flat on her chest. The way the old soldiers did at Cinderhold, on the wall, when Declan came in under the gate; the twenty-five of them, in a row, without a word.

She held it there for as long as it takes to count to three. Then she turned round and went back up to her rock and sat down.

In the shallows, Declan had stopped laughing. Nearly. He was sitting in the lake with the stick across his knees and his shoulders still going a little, now and then, like the last of a storm going off over the hills. He looked up at Xav.

“Three,” he said.

“Three.”

“Three.” He put his head back and looked at the sky, where the mist was thinning and the first of the blue was coming through. “Thirty-six years.” He was quiet. “Thank you, Xav.”

Xav didn’t know what to say to that. So he held out his hand.

Declan took it, and Xav pulled him up out of the lake, dripping.

Then Alea came down the shingle.

She’d been sitting on the stones at the top with her hands over her mouth through all of it. She came down now with Astra in one hand, slowly, the way you’d walk up to a horse that had just done something extraordinary, and stopped in front of her father where he stood dripping at the waterline, and looked at him.

“Dad,” she said.

He looked at her. Xav couldn’t see his face. He saw him know what was coming.

“You said you’d never ask,” said Declan.

“I know.”

“In the spring. On the last plank. I’m never going to ask.”

“And then I asked you to come to the closing,” said Alea. “In the painters’ yard. That was one.” She held Astra in both hands in front of her, point down on the shingle. “This is two.”

Declan was very still.

“I’m Honed now,” said Alea. “I know what Tempered is. I know what Honed is. I felt it go into the steel. I know what Petra is, Reaching, because I’ve seen it go past the edge. I know what the king is, because I’ve stood next to him at an anvil.” Her ears were flat. Then they came up. “I don’t know what you are. I’ve never seen it. Nobody here has. Not really. Not all of it.” She looked at the lake. “Show us. What’s at the top.”

Nobody on the shingle said anything.

Xav looked at Gabe, and Gabe looked at Deston, and Deston, who was crying a little already, from the laugh, and smiling, looked back at both of them; and they knew. All three of them. Without saying it. They’d said it on the end of the jetty, at midnight, in the second spring, the night he told them everything: we’re not going to ask. And they hadn’t. Not once. Not about any of it.

“We said we wouldn’t ask,” said Gabe. His voice came out rough. He stood up off the stones. “We’re not.”

“She is,” said Deston.

“So are we,” said Xav. “Now.”

Declan looked at them for a long time.

At Alea in front of him with her sword point down on the shingle. At the three of them standing behind her. At the rest of them on the stones, Group 1 and Group 6 and Petra, and the two children on the wall in their nightshirts, swinging their legs, who’d stopped swinging them. At the lake, with the mist going off it. At the hills round it, all the way round, green and high and quiet in the first of the sun.

He looked at the hills longest.

“Not past the hills,” he said. Half to himself. “I’ll keep it in.”

Then he looked at Alea.

“Up the shingle,” he said. “All of you. To the stones. Not past.” He turned to the two children on the wall. “You two. Sit on your hands.”

The children sat on their hands.

Alea went back up the shingle, slowly, and sat down on the stones between Gabe and Thea with Astra across her knees; and Xav went with her, and Deston, and they sat in the row with the others, fourteen of them and two children on a wall, and watched him walk out along the jetty.

Thirty-four planks. He picked up the rod on the way. He stood on the last plank, beside the lamp, with the hens round his feet and the rod in his hand and the lake in front of him, the mist nearly gone off it now, flat and grey and steaming a little in the first sun, all the way to the far shore. He stood there a moment with his back to them.

Then he put his hand in the air beside him, the way you did, and opened his window.

And turned it round.

Xav had seen windows for two years and a half. He’d seen thirty-five of them. He’d seen his own every morning. He’d seen the long progress lines and the short skill lines and Chris’s logs and the one line of Declan’s that everybody had seen by now, Progress to Ascendant, a percent at a time, over two years.

He’d never seen this.

It hung in the air over the end of the jetty, turned so they could all read it from the stones, and nobody said anything for a long time.

Eight. One for every tier. Every one he’d found, one at a time, in a field behind his father’s house at fourteen and a garrison at sixteen and a war and a ditch and a rise; every one a word, plain, short, the kind of word you’d use for a knife. Slice. Cut. Maim. And on, and on, getting worse, until the last.

“Bro,” said Rui Xiang. Very quietly. “Bro. Annihilate.”

“That’s not a skill,” said Malcolm. “That’s a sentence.”

Nobody laughed. Declan closed the window.

Then he stopped holding it in.

Xav would try for years to say what that was like, and never manage it. He’d try in the channel that night and delete it four times. He’d try to Rui Xiang, who’d been there, and Rui Xiang would say bro, I know, I was there, stop. He’d try to his own window, alone, on the end of the jetty, and the window would say nothing back.

The nearest he’d get was this: that for two years and a half, without knowing it, he’d been standing next to a man who was holding something in. All the time. Every minute. On the end of the jetty, at the long table, in the north stand, in a polytechnic in another world before he died. Holding it in so carefully, so completely, so far down, that it had never once occurred to anybody that anything was being held.

And then he let go.

The lake stopped.

Not froze. Stopped. The last of the mist, lifting off the water in thin white threads, stopped lifting and hung where it was, in the air, a hand above the surface, all the way to the far shore. The water under it, which had been moving the way water always moves, little ripples, little catspaws of wind, went flat. Not calm; flat. Flat as glass poured into a mould and left. Every ripple on Lake Ilse, all at once, lay down, as far as Xav could see, a mile of it and more, a sheet of grey-silver glass with the sky in it.

A gull that had been crossing the bay stopped calling.

The hens on the jetty lay down. All of them. The big red one too, flat on the planks at his feet with her wings spread, the way hens lie down in thunder.

On the stones, Xav felt it reach him.

It didn’t push. That was what he’d remember. He’d thought, if he’d ever thought about it, that Dominion would push, the way a big man’s presence pushes you back in a room, the way the general must have felt it on the rise. It didn’t. It held. It came across the water and up the shingle and over all of them, fourteen people on the stones and two children on a wall, and held them. Every candle. Every one. The way you’d cup your hands round a flame on a windy night to keep it lit; not crushing it, not putting it out. Holding it. Making it still, so the wind couldn’t get it.

Beside him, Deston made a sound. Xav looked round. Deston’s eyes were shut and his face was wet and he was smiling, the whole of him, the way he’d smiled at the Lists when Rachael’s hand went down on his.

“They’ve all gone to one note,” Deston whispered. “All of them. Every thread in the valley. Ours. The town’s. The fish. The grass. One note.” He was shaking. “It’s his. It’s the low one. It’s the one I’ve been hearing under everything since the first night.” He opened his eyes. “It’s not under everything. It is everything. He’s been humming it with his mouth shut the whole time.”

Xav reached for Now. He couldn’t help it. It was what he did; it was what he’d always done, in every fight, in every room. Reach for what was coming. Feel the lean.

He felt the whole lake decide.

All of it, at once. The water and the air above it and the stones under it and the hills round it, everything in the bowl of the valley, leaning the same way, at the same moment, in the same direction, like one body; and the direction was the man on the end of the jetty, and the decision was his.

He let go of Now. He had to. It was too big to hold.

On his other side, Thea had her eyes wide open and her lips moving, reading. She read magic like code; she’d read the circle and the leaves and the frames. She was reading this. Xav watched her read the first line of it, whatever it was, written in the air over the whole lake. And stop. And go back to the start, and read the first line again. And stop.

“I can’t get past the first line,” she said. Very quietly. To nobody. “It’s one line. I can read the first word of it.” Her voice shook. “It goes on for miles.”

Xola had both hands flat on the stones, as if the shingle might tip. “The water won’t talk to me,” she said. “It always talks to me. It’s not talking. It’s listening. To him.”

On Alea’s knees, Astra was humming. Xav could hear it from where he sat. A low steady note in the black steel, like a bell somebody had struck a long time ago and was only now beginning to ring.

And on the end of the jetty, in the middle of all of it, Declan stood with the rod in his hand and his back to them.

He lifted the rod.

Not fast. Xav would see it in John’s light afterwards, slowed down, and it wasn’t fast even then. It was the slowest thing he’d ever seen a swordsman do. He lifted the rod in his right hand the way he’d lift it any morning to cast: back, over his shoulder, easy, the line hanging loose.

And brought it forward.

There was no flash. That was the strangest thing. Xav had thought there’d be light, a burst of it, the way there was light when the threadless came, or when Ina lit the forty lamps, or when the summoning took them out of their rooms in a single white instant. There wasn’t. The rod came forward and down, and something came off the end of it, past the end of it, the way Petra’s Reaching came off a stick: the edge folded into the rod, the one you’d never see unless he let you.

He let them.

It went out across the lake.

Xav didn’t see the edge. Nobody could have. He saw what it did. A line. That was all. A line going out from the end of the jetty across Lake Ilse, ruler-straight, as if somebody had laid a rule on the morning and drawn a blade along it. And where the line went, the morning was cut.

The mist went first. The threads of mist hanging a hand above the flat water parted along the line, left and right, cleanly, like hair under a comb, all the way out; a straight bare lane opening across the lake through the white, faster than a bird could fly, toward the far shore.

Then the water.

The lake opened.

Xav saw it and didn’t believe it and saw it again in John’s light a hundred times afterwards and still didn’t. The flat glass surface of Lake Ilse split along the line. Not splashed; split. Opened, the way a seam opens, cleanly, two walls of grey-green water standing up on either side of a gap a few feet wide, and the gap going down: down past the weed, past the dark green water where the pike lived, down to the bottom of the lake. To the bed. To the stones. Xav saw them. Round grey lake stones, dry, in the morning light, at the bottom of a lane cut through the water, a mile long, running out from the end of the jetty toward the far shore with the sun coming down into it.

And the line didn’t stop at the far shore.

It lifted. The way a cast lifts. Out over the far shore, clear of the trees on the far hills by the height of a tower, up, and on, into the sky; and the low grey clouds lying along the far hills in the morning, the last of the night’s weather, split.

Split in a straight line. From the hills to the top of the sky. A long clean cut through the cloud, edge to edge, as if somebody had drawn a blade across a sheet of grey wool; and through the gap, the sun.

It came down through the cut in the cloud in a single bar of gold. Straight down the line. Across the far hills, across the far shore, along the lane cut through the lake to its stony bottom, all the way back to the end of the jetty, where it lay at the feet of the man with the rod and lit up the hens.

For one heartbeat it was all there. The cloud cut. The light coming down. The lake standing open to its bed. The mist parted. Everything held, still, perfectly still, in the bowl of the valley, by a man on the end of a jetty who wasn’t moving.

Then the sound came.

It came late, the way thunder comes late from a long way off. Not a crack. Not a bang. One note. Very low, lower than anything Xav had ever heard, so low he felt it in his chest and his teeth before he heard it with his ears, rolling back off the far hills and across the water and up the shingle and through all of them, like a bell the size of a mountain struck once, very gently, a long way down.

And the lake closed.

The two walls of water came together over the lane with a sound like a door slamming in a house the size of the sky, and a wave ran out from the seam, both ways, and came across the flat water toward the shore, and up the shingle, fast, white-edged, all the way up, over the wet stones and the dry ones and over everybody’s boots, to the bottom of the stones where they sat, and stopped there, and slid back down.

He let go.

Xav felt it go. All of it, back in, back down, the way it had come up after the third move, into the bottom of him, under the deciding, where it lived. Gone. As if it had never been there. The mist began to lift again off the water, thin white threads, going up. The ripples came back. A catspaw of wind went across the bay.

The gull called.

The hens on the jetty got up, one after another, shaking out their feathers, and looked round at the man on the end of the jetty with the expression of hens who have been made to lie down for no reason by someone who should know better, and the big red one pecked him on the ankle.

Nobody moved.

Xav sat on the stones with the lake water draining out of his boots and couldn’t move. He couldn’t think. He couldn’t do anything at all but look at the far hills, where the cut in the cloud was still there, a long straight blue gap in the grey with the sun coming down through it.

Beside him, Gabe sat down.

He’d stood up, Xav realised, at some point; he didn’t know when. He sat down now, on the wet shingle, not on the stones, just down, where he was, all of him, the way a tree goes down; and sat there with his enormous black hands loose in his lap and his mouth open.

Deston was crying. Openly, with the tears running down, and smiling, and not wiping them.

Petra hadn’t moved from her rock. She had her hand flat on her chest again. She didn’t say anything. She’d been in the ditch. Xav understood, looking at her, that she’d seen something like this before, once, on the Redmarch, ten years ago, from the bottom of a ditch with her sword shaking in her hands; and that it hadn’t been like this. That it had been this turned the other way.

Higu was writing. Her pencil was shaking so badly she had to hold her wrist with her other hand. Xav could see the page. 06:12. And nothing else. She was trying to write something after it and couldn’t.

Thea had her hands over her face.

On the wall, the two children were still sitting on their hands, exactly as they’d been told, with their mouths open, and their eyes enormous, and their nightshirts wet to the knee from the wave.

And John, at the end of the row, was sitting very still with his light hanging over the water, gold, the size of a door, and his eyes shut, and his face white.

“Did you get it?” said Rui Xiang. His voice cracked.

“I got it,” said John. He didn’t open his eyes. “I got all of it.” He swallowed. “I don’t think I can watch it.”

He couldn’t, as it turned out. None of them could. He played it back that night, in the hedge, on the wall of briar, for the ones who asked; and fourteen people sat in the dark and watched a man on the end of a jetty lift a rod, and nobody said anything, and halfway through, at the place where the lake opens, John stopped it. Every time. For years. Nobody ever saw the end of it twice.

Declan came back along the jetty.

Thirty-four planks. Slowly. With the rod over his shoulder and the hens behind him in a row, and his coat still wet from the lake. He stepped off the land end onto the shingle by Gigichi’s little tree and came up the stones to where they sat; and stopped in front of them; and stood there, in the first sun, dripping, in his bare feet, a plain man with a fishing rod.

Nobody could say anything.

Malcolm tried. Xav watched him. Malcolm opened his mouth and shut it and opened it again, and what came out, in the end, very small, was the one thing Malcolm could always say, the thing he’d say in a fight or a fire or a flood, the thing that was a question with an answer:

“Which one,” said Malcolm, “was that?”

Declan looked at him.

“Slice,” he said.

Nobody breathed.

“Slice,” said Malcolm.

“The first one,” said Declan. He shifted the rod on his shoulder. “From the field. At fourteen. It’s the only one I needed.” He looked out at the lake, where the mist was lifting again, ordinary, as if nothing had happened; and at the far hills, where the cut in the cloud lay across the sky like a road. “They don’t go away when you get the next one. They get bigger. All of them. Everything you’ve got, the first thing you ever found, it comes up with you.” He was quiet. “That was the first thing I ever found.”

He looked at them. At all of them, along the row. At Alea with Astra across her knees and her ears all the way up and her face wet. At Gabe sitting on the shingle. At Deston crying. At Xav. At Thea with her hands over her face and Higu with her shaking pencil and PenguChi with the hen that had followed her up the stones and Xola and Gigichi and Rui Xiang and Malcolm and Azac and John and Petra on her rock with her hand on her chest; and at the two children on the wall, sitting on their hands.

“One day,” said Declan.

He said it quietly. He didn’t have to say it loudly.

“One day. One day you’ll reach these heights.” He looked at each of them in turn. “Never stop pushing.”

Then he turned and went up the shingle toward the cabin, with the rod on his shoulder and the hens behind him.

“Breakfast,” he said, over his shoulder.

Nobody moved for a long time after he’d gone.

Then Gabe got up off the shingle. He stood there a moment, enormous, with his hands hanging. Then he held one of them down to Xav, who was still sitting on the stones, and Xav took it, and Gabe pulled him up.

“You made him move three times,” said Gabe.

“I know.”

“And then he did that.” Gabe looked at the cut in the cloud. “With the first one.” He was quiet. “With Slice.”

“I know.”

Gabe was silent for a long moment. Then he laughed. A big helpless one, the kind that came out of Gabe about once a year; and Deston started too, still crying; and then Rui Xiang, and then all of them, the whole row on the stones, fourteen people laughing on a lake shore in the morning with their boots full of lake water, for no reason, because there wasn’t anything else to do with it.

Breakfast was nasi lemak.

Nine, on Wendy’s list. For breakfast. Yes, breakfast. Trust. Coconut rice in a heap, and a fried egg, and little crisp fish, and peanuts, and cucumber, and the red paste, Salty’s, hot and sweet and dark. He’d had it ready. He must have made the rice in the night. He’d known.

Higu didn’t tick it on the list on the wall.

“It’s breakfast,” she said, when Gigichi asked. Her pencil had stopped shaking. “It doesn’t count.”

The cut in the cloud stayed over the far hills until noon.

The whole of Wrenhaven came out to look at it. Rowan stood at the top of the lake road in his chain with his hand on his chest. Garrow came out of the forge on the square with his apron on and his hammer in his hand and looked at it for a long time, and said, to nobody, with deep professional respect, that’s a clean cut. Odette came to the door of the lace room. On the mill quay the miller’s boys stopped loading flour and stood with a sack between them, looking.

And on the stones at the top of the shingle, all morning, two children in wet nightshirts sat on the wall with their hands under them, because nobody had told them they could stop.
