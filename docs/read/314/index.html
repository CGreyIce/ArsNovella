The tally was on the inside of the back cover of the fifth book, where nobody looked.

Malcolm had kept it since the frozen gravel bar. Since the morning on the Wren, two winters ago, when Petra leaned on Azac’s post with a third of her fifth and he’d written Hold and then stopped where the backup went and written Then instead, and it had held, and the window had come up. He’d started counting that day. Every Then he’d ever written: on a ward, a blade, a strap, a bar, a boot. A stroke for every one he wrote. A ring round the stroke for every one that held.

Four hundred and twelve strokes. Two hundred and seventy-one rings.

Sixty-six in a hundred.

It wasn’t bad. He’d have been pleased with it, on Earth, at anything. Most things in his life had gone about sixty-six in a hundred, and he’d been the one at the back of the class with a list for the other thirty-four. But Maren had said, in the first winter, to Thea, and Thea had said it in the channel, and it had gone into Malcolm’s second book in capitals and never come out: Candle is when it works every time. Ten out of ten. Not most. Every.

Sixty-six wasn’t ten out of ten. Sixty-six was a Spark who’d been a Spark for a year and seven months and was ninety-seven per cent of the way to something he couldn’t do.

He’d written a great deal. He did it every night: round the town after the lamps were lit, the way Azac did the long-room door and Hild did the wall. The postern bar. The north-gate hinges, which Agnes said were the worst in the kingdom and she should know, she’d made them. The rope on the tower stair that Hyvred held going up in the dark. Bee’s chalk box, once, as a joke, and she’d drawn the dog with it and the dog had stood on the slates so hard the slate cracked, and Bee had looked at him and said don’t do that again in the voice she used for Tiger.

Sixty-six in a hundred. Every night. And the bar sat at ninety-seven like a stone in a shoe.

He wrote it down, on the mounting block, on the fourth morning, with the race going on at the bottom of the road and nobody coming down out of the pass to stand at the end of it.

IF it moves when I watch THEN it’s not about me.

He looked at that for a long time.

Backup: it’s about me.

“Why do you write a backup?” said Petra.

They were on the wall walk above the postern after the dawn class, the two of them, which was rare. Petra didn’t talk to him much. She talked to Xav, and to Hild, and to Rui Xiang in a sort of sign language of half-sentences that Malcolm had never learned; and she watched the rest of them the way she watched the ditch, from a little way off, as if she were counting. She’d never thanked him for the sixth fish. She’d never thanked anybody for anything; she’d told him once it was a thing she was working on, and he’d written it down.

“Because the first thing fails,” said Malcolm.

“Does it?”

“Thirty-four in a hundred.” He’d brought the book. He showed her the back cover. She looked at the strokes and the rings for a long time with her head on one side.

“Then the backup’s for the thirty-four,” said Petra.

“No,” said Malcolm. “It’s for me.”

She looked at him.

“I don’t write them any more,” he said. “Not on the thing. Not since the bar on the Wren. I write Then and stop where the backup would go and leave it empty, the way Pryce said. A backup is something you do because you don’t believe the first thing.” He shut the book. “So I stopped. On paper. It still fails thirty-four in a hundred. So it’s not the paper.”

Petra was quiet a long time.

“When I was twenty-two,” she said, “I stood in a ditch on the Redmarch for a day and a night, and a man I’d never met stood in front of me, and it held.” She looked up the pass. “Afterwards, every time I stood in front of anything, I stood in front of it as if I’d be the one who wasn’t enough. Ten years. I was very good. People said so. It held most of the time.” She turned her head. “It’s in your feet. Not your hands. The not believing. You can stop saying it and your feet still know.”

“Then it’s in your hands,” said Petra. “Ask somebody who reads.”

Then he took the shield off the wall where it hung at night and turned it over on the table, face-down, so the real one lay under the picture of itself, and put his hand flat on the rim. “Doesn’t matter,” said Azac. “It’s held me up the Wren and in the Hollow and on the downs in the rain and on the wall all winter. It’s fine.”

“Yeah,” said Azac. “And I’m here.” He took his hand off it. “So that’s a hundred in a hundred, from where I’m sitting.”

Malcolm wrote that down too. He didn’t know what to do with it.

IF there’s an unless in every hold THEN every hold fails when the unless happens.

IF the unless doesn’t say what THEN it’s anything.

He wrote it on the mounting block in the morning with his breakfast going cold on the stone beside him.

That was it. That was the thirty-four. He’d never once written a backup on Azac’s shield since the Wren; he’d been very proud of that. And all the time, under every word, in a hand he didn’t know he had, he’d been writing unless and not finishing it. Hold, unless. Unless anything. Unless the rain. Unless the dark. Unless he’d got it wrong, which he always assumed he had. A hold that would give way for anything at all wasn’t a hold. It was a door with no latch. It held till the first draught.

So: don’t write it.

IF I don’t write the unless THEN it holds every time.

He didn’t write a backup under that one. He was very careful not to.

He tried it on the postern bar.

It was the obvious one. He did it every night; Old Pruett let him, the way Pruett let the Watch lean on his wall and Bee draw on his door, with a long-suffering grunt. A great square bar of black oak with iron at the ends, older than Pruett, which made it older than most things in Thornwall. He went down after the lamps were lit, at eleven, with the ink and the pen, and knelt on the cobbles, and wrote Hold on it.

And while he wrote it, he thought, as hard as he’d ever thought anything: not unless. Not unless anything. Just hold.

He felt it fight him.

That was the strange thing. He’d never felt a word fight him before. The Hold went on to the wood the way it always did, and he felt the little catch of it taking, the thing every enchanter felt, like a hook setting; and then under it, in his own hand, he felt the other word try to come. Small. Patient. The way it must have come every time, four hundred and twelve times, without him noticing. Unless. And he pushed it down. He held the pen hard and thought no and pushed.

The bar split.

It went with a noise like a musket shot in the dark: the whole length of it, end to end, straight down the grain, and the two halves fell off their irons on to the cobbles, one either side of him, and lay there.

His nose was bleeding. He hadn’t noticed it start.

Lamps came on up and down the street. A window went up. Somebody shouted for the Watch. “Who broke my bar?” said Old Pruett.

He’d written it eleven times. He counted.

He sat on the ridge of the Bell’s roof with the two books open on his knees and the town going on below him, the smoke from Agnes’s forge, and Bee on the gatehouse roof, and Tiger’s feet on the swept road, and Hyvred on the tower with the coat round her shoulders, and he understood something that he would have understood a year ago if he’d been anybody else.

He’d never been able to finish the unless. Because the unless was everything. Hold, unless anything. The rain, the dark, being wrong. You couldn’t build on that. It was a whole world of worst cases, a hundred pages of them, and it was true, all of it could happen, and his hand knew it and wrote it under every word.

But there was one he knew wouldn’t.

Not hoped. He didn’t hope; he wasn’t built for it. Knew. The way he knew the porridge took twenty minutes and Agnes’s hinges were the worst in the kingdom. They weren’t going. Xav had said it on this roof. I’m not doing it twice. They’d drifted from Declan once, all of them, from nobody’s fault, from there always being another morning; and Xav had looked down at Group 5 on a shed roof and said not twice, and Malcolm had written it down by feel in the dark, and they’d stayed. All winter. All spring. Every one of them. Every night in the long room, every dawn on the wall. That wasn’t a hope. It was a fact. He’d counted it. He counted it every hour.

Backup: we’re here. He’d been writing it for a year. He’d thought it was a joke.

It was the end of the sentence.

HOLD. UNLESS WE’VE GONE.

We haven’t.

He felt it take. Not like a hook. Like a rope going tight. Like something at the other end of a line that had been slack for two years, and now wasn’t; and the weight on the other end of it was all of them. Old Pruett in his nightshirt. Agnes asleep over the forge. Azac in the long-room door. Xav on the wall. The whole town with its lamps lit in every window, every night, since the autumn, for nobody coming.

Pruett looked at him for a long time. Then he grunted, and went in.

Not fast. They didn’t hurry; they never had. Not hard. They never pushed hard. Malcolm had been pushed once, in the long room, by Fenna who did the shirts, smiling, and he’d gone backwards three paces on his heels and sat down. Drew set.

Post. The line in his hand with a pike on the other end of it, a big one, the biggest he’d ever had on anything, forty people’s weight coming on to two rings of letters he’d written kneeling on the cobbles ten minutes before. They hadn’t. They were all up here. Every one of them. On the wall.

Then Tiger came.

He’d seen. Somebody on the wall had shouted at last, and he’d gone. Bolt, the sound of it, the slap of a man’s feet on cobbles going faster than a man’s feet should, and then the arch, and then the swept road, and Tiger going down it at full stretch with his head down and his arms going, faster than Malcolm had ever seen him go, all of himself in it, toward a bridge with forty people on it and Drew in the middle.

Three paces off the ring, at full Bolt, he turned.

In a pace. The way he’d turned the first morning the ring came down, the thing nobody’s knees could do. The whole run went into him and rang; Malcolm heard it from the wall, a note like a struck bell, low; and Tiger was going back up the road the way he’d come, at full stretch, with his face over his shoulder.

The hands came off Drew.

All of them. At once. Malcolm had read it in Kei’s post in the spring and never quite believed it: the thing that moved them tasted Tiger turn and let go of the shoulder. It let go. The forty stood on the bridge with their hands up and open in the air where Drew had been, as if they’d forgotten why they’d lifted them. And then, together, the way they’d come, they took one step back. And another. Off the stone. On to the road. A pace off the far end of the millstream bridge, where they always stood.

Facing the town.

Except one.

Ossie had turned his head. Not toward the town. Toward the boy, in Drew’s arms, with his face in Drew’s shoulder. Hyvred said afterwards, in the channel, that she saw it go along the seam: under the face, the other face, pressed flat, pushing up against the top of the join where it was loose, the way it had pushed since the race. Ossie looking at a boy he’d probably seen every day of his life in Thornwall, at the mill, at the well. Looking at him. And then the seam held, and the face was the face, and Ossie looked at the town again.

Drew came down the bridge. He didn’t run. He carried the boy back up the swept road on his hip at a walk, and Tiger came back down it at a walk to meet him, and turned, and walked beside them, not touching, and the forty stood at the end of the bridge and watched them go and didn’t move at all.

He didn’t open the window till they were in.

He stood on the wall walk with his book shut in his hand and watched Drew carry the boy in at the postern, and Pruett step back to let them through, and put his hand on the new bar that was green and would warp and was the worst bar he’d ever had. He watched Tiger come in after them and stop inside the arch and put his flat hand on the oak post, and stand there with his hand on it, and not say Tomorrow. Not today.

Then he looked.

He knew what it would say. He’d felt it on the millstream bridge, in Drew’s boots, in the place under the word. He’d known it before it said anything at all. The window only ever told you what you’d done.

He turned round.

Xav was on the wall walk behind him, three paces off, with his arms folded and the driftwood stick under one of them, doing the face. The one Malcolm had known since they were seventeen, from the back of the poly lab. The face that meant I’m very pleased and I’m not going to do anything about it.

Malcolm looked at him.

Then he opened his book. The fifth one. At the very back, past the tally, past the channel, to the last page, where he’d written, in the first weeks at the Hollow, nearly four years ago, in capitals, the heading he’d written at the back of every book since. And never anything under it.

WORST CASE.

He wrote under it, in his small square hand:

We’ve gone.

And then under that, because he was who he was:

We haven’t.

The boy didn’t speak all morning.

He sat on the long-room bench at the Bell with a bowl of porridge on his knees that he didn’t eat, and Drew beside him, and the women from the kitchen coming out every half hour to look at him and go back in. Nobody asked him anything. His aunt came, and sat on his other side, and cried, and he let her. He looked at the door.

Malcolm sat at the end of the bench.

Not near. He didn’t touch people. He’d put his hands on somebody once, in the autumn, in the long room, because Drew had shouted his name: Fenna who did the shirts, leaning on Azac’s ward, and she’d turned round in his arms smiling and pushed him over. He hadn’t since. He sat at the end of the bench with his book shut on his knee and the boy two places along, the way Gabe sat with Rachael, which he’d watched Gabe do on the Widow for a hundred miles and never understood: near enough that you knew he was there, and not so near you had to do anything about it.

About noon the boy said, “She didn’t look.”

Nobody said anything.

“She didn’t look at me,” said the boy.

“No,” said Malcolm.

He hadn’t meant to. It came out. He was the one with the worst cases; he couldn’t say she will. He didn’t know that. He’d never known anything that wasn’t true, and he couldn’t start now, on a bench, to a seven-year-old.

The boy looked at him.

“Ossie did,” said Malcolm. “A hair. Hyvred saw. He looked at you.” He was quiet. “So somebody in there knows you’re out here. And somebody out here—“ He looked at the door. At the street. The boy looked at the door for a long time.

Then he ate the porridge. All of it. Cold.

Malcolm wrote it down. Not in the tally. On a new page, at the front of the sixth book, which he’d started that morning, and which had nothing in it yet but that.

IF she didn’t look THEN she can’t yet.

And under it, where the backup went:

Unless we’ve gone.
