The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 7: Myths

Chapter 194

There was a map on the wall of Halvard’s study, and he had made it smaller every year for twenty years.

Not the map itself. The map was the same size it had always been: six feet by four, on oiled linen, nailed to the plaster between the two windows of the west tower, where the light was good in the afternoons. The kingdom of Aurelan, from the sea to the Greyspine, from the downs to the Redmarch, drawn by the royal surveyors in the old king’s time and corrected since by Halvard’s own hand, in his own small ink, every time a report came in that said a river had moved or a village had burned. He knew every inch of it. He could have drawn it with his eyes shut.

What he’d made smaller was the world.

That was the trick of it. You couldn’t plan for everything. Nobody could. So you drew a line round the things you could plan for, and you called that the world, and you made it as small as you could bear, so you could hold it. The kingdom. The Redmarch. Kharvos past it, a grey wash with nothing written in it, which was the enemy. The Greyspine to the north-west, where Corvin’s reports came from, and the pass. The sea to the south-west. And the south, past the downs, past the last market town, where the surveyors had stopped, and the linen just went on being linen for another inch, blank, to the edge of the frame.

Nothing there. That was the point. You didn’t plan for nothing.

He’d been looking at that inch of blank linen for about an hour when the girl knocked.

It was very early. Not yet the grey. He hadn’t been asleep; he rarely was, at this hour, and the lamp was lit. He said come in without getting up, because nobody climbed a hundred and four stairs in the dark to his door unless they had to, and the people who had to were few.

It was the cat girl. Hanarisu. Hana, the others called her. In a coat over her nightgown, with her tail wrapped round her legs for the cold, and her hair everywhere, and seven cords on her left wrist, knotted and beaded, one for each of his guides. One of them was a plain brown knot with no bead at all. Aldous’s. Aldous had never wanted a bead.

She held a strip of paper in her hand.

“It came at midnight,” she said. “I’ve been sitting up with it. I didn’t know if I should wake you.” She paused. “Then I thought you’d be awake.”

“I am,” said Halvard.

“It’s Aldous,” said Hana.

He held out his hand. She didn’t give it to him. She couldn’t; he couldn’t read it. The cords carried what you meant, not what you wrote, and set it down in the script of whoever held the other half, and the other half was on her wrist, so it had come out in hers. The square, tidy, impossible letters of the summoned. He’d seen enough of them now to recognise the shape and not one word.

So she read it to him. Standing in the doorway of his study with the lamp behind him and the strip held up in the light.

“Three came out of the south,” Hana read. “Elves. Real. They knelt to the snake. The rite is theirs. Aldous.”

Halvard didn’t say anything.

He found, after a while, that he was looking at the map again. At the inch of blank linen at the bottom, past the downs, past where the surveyors had stopped.

“Say it again,” he said.

She said it again.

“The rite is theirs,” said Halvard. “What does that mean?”

“I don’t know,” said Hana. “Yan’s been writing all night. In the channel. They came to the west gate at dusk. Rhea lost an arm-wrestle. They knelt in the snow. There’s a word, Vael, they say it means the one who carries. Yan says Suzu shouted at one of them.” She stopped. “Maigo’s written ELVES in capitals forty times. Wendy keeps telling him to stop and he keeps not stopping.”

“The rite,” said Halvard.

“I think it’s the white cloth,” said Hana. “Aldous’s. Over the dead. Yan says they looked at the white cloths in the chapel like they knew them.” She lowered the strip. “I didn’t know if I should tell you what the channel said. Before he did. Aldous is your guide. It felt like his to tell you.”

Halvard looked at her.

Two years and a half, and he still didn’t know what to do with them. Thirty-five people from another world who’d been told let them and been let, and who’d turned out, nearly every one, to have manners nobody had taught them. This one had sat up all night on the end of her bed with a strip of paper because it felt like somebody else’s news.

“Thank you,” said Halvard.

He went down to the forge at the grey.

He didn’t decide to. He found he was doing it. Down the hundred and four stairs, and across the courtyard, and through the arch into the old stable yard where the castle forge was, with its chimney already smoking, because the king didn’t sleep either, these days. Kestra was in the door. Of course she was. Leaning on the frame with her book in her coat, watching the yard. She saw him coming across it and straightened, very slightly, and looked down.

At his feet. She always looked at his feet now. He’d stopped minding.

He stopped at the edge of the heat. Then he remembered that he’d stepped over it once, in the autumn, and nothing had happened, and he stepped over it again.

The king was at the anvil in his shirtsleeves, drawing out a bar. For nothing. He did that. He’d done it since his father’s last winter: stood at an anvil and made a bar longer, and then shorter, and then put it back in the fire, because his hands needed something to do while his head went round. He didn’t look up. He’d heard the step.

“You’re in my forge,” said the king.

“Elves,” said Halvard.

The hammer stopped.

The king stood there with it up, over the bar, for a long moment. The bar went from orange to red to dull. He didn’t put it back in the fire. He just stood there, a big man in a leather apron, the ninth tier held in all round him the way he held it all day for the sake of everyone else in the room, and looked at Halvard over the anvil.

“Say that again,” said the king.

So Halvard told him. The strip. The west gate. The snake. The rite. All of it, the way he’d tell it in council, in order, with nothing added. It took about as long as a bar takes to go cold.

When he’d finished, the king put the hammer down on the anvil. Very carefully. The way you’d put down something you were afraid of dropping.

And then he laughed.

Not the way he laughed at the lake, at the hens. The other way. The way he’d laughed once, Halvard remembered, when they were both young, in a tent before a battle, at something nobody else in the tent had found funny. Helplessly. With his head back. Until he had to sit down on the quench-tub lid with his big burned hands on his knees.

“Elves,” said the king, when he could.

“Yes, Majesty.”

“Elves, Halvard.” He wiped his eyes on his apron. “My father told me about elves. When I was five and wouldn’t go to sleep. He said they lived past the end of the world and they’d come and take my ears if I didn’t shut my eyes.” He laughed again, quieter. “I shut my eyes. I’ve been shutting them ever since.”

“Aldous says they ask nothing,” said Halvard.

The king stopped laughing.

“Everyone asks something,” he said.

“So I’d have said.”

“All the years I’ve worn the hat,” said the king. He looked at the cold bar on the anvil. “Every single person who’s ever come to that castle has asked me for something. The ones who say they don’t, ask for the most.” He was quiet. “What do they want?”

“I don’t know,” said Halvard. “Aldous’s strip says nothing. I’ve known Aldous a long time. He doesn’t use words he doesn’t mean. He doesn’t use words at all, if he can help it.”

The king looked at him for a long moment.

“Does he know?” he said.

Halvard knew who he meant. There was only ever one he, between the two of them, when the king said it like that.

“I don’t know,” said Halvard. “I don’t know what he knows. I never have.”

“Write to him.”

“The ridge past Hethe is shut, Majesty. Until the thaw.”

“The cat girl’s got a string,” said the king. “To the militia woman. Hasn’t she.” He picked the bar up with the tongs and put it back in the fire, and pumped the bellows, once, and the coals went white. “Write to him, Halvard. Tell him about the elves. And ask him about the hens.”

In the door, Kestra took her book out of her coat and wrote something in it.

The council met on the third day.

By then the whole castle knew. The whole city, very nearly. It had gone out of the castle the way everything went out of the castle: through the kitchens. A summoned boy had told a summoned girl over breakfast, in their own tongue, and a scullion who’d learned a few of their words had heard elves, which was one of them, and by noon there was a ballad-singer on the steps of the Lantern Quarter making up verses. Two of them rhymed ears with spears. One rhymed Vael with ale. Halvard had sent a page down to find out which, and then wished he hadn’t.

So the council came in already talking.

The Lord Chancellor first, with his papers. Then the Lord Treasurer, soft and careful, who’d been the colour of milk for a week after the king’s two words in the autumn and had got most of his colour back. The Marshal. The lords of the provinces. Warden Aubrecht of the Summoners’ Hall, very old, very upright, with his hands folded on his staff. And in the gallery, at the king’s invitation, which nobody on the council had yet found a way to argue with, the five summoned from the castle. The cat girl. The tall one with the tail. The quiet one who heard things. The big one who stood where he stood. And the one with the burned hand and the blue ink, who’d walked into a fire in the autumn, writing in a notebook with his left.

“An embassy,” said the Lord Chancellor.

He’d said it before he sat down. He said it again when he had.

“An embassy, Majesty. At once. With respect. If these people are real, and the reports from Cinderhold say they are, then they are the first contact this crown has had with another people in four hundred years, other than the one we are at war with.” He laid his hand flat on his papers. “They must be received. Properly. Brought to Solmere, to your presence, with an escort fit for—“

“They don’t want to come to Solmere,” said the king.

“With respect, Majesty, we have not asked them.”

“What do they trade?” said the Lord Treasurer. Softly. Carefully. “Forgive me. It is my office to ask. Timber? Furs? There is a great deal of country south of the downs that nobody has—“

“Nobody’s there,” said the Marshal. “That’s why nobody’s mapped it.”

“Somebody is now,” said the Lord Treasurer.

Halvard sat at the king’s right hand and said nothing, and watched it go round the table. He’d watched a great many things go round this table. He could have told them, before they began, the order in which they would each say the thing they were going to say. The Chancellor: protocol. The Treasurer: trade. The Marshal: are they a threat. The lords of the provinces: whatever their wives had told them that morning. He’d built the council in his head as carefully as he’d built the map, and it was the same size, and it did what it was built to do.

Then the Warden spoke.

He didn’t speak often. He hadn’t spoken in council since the autumn, since he’d stood in this room and asked the king to burn the leaves for everyone’s sake and been told no in two words. He was very old. He was sincere; that was the worst and best of him. He’d stood on the third ring at the Great Summoning and believed, every moment of it, that he was doing a good and necessary thing.

“Majesty,” said the Warden. “If they are real—“

Everyone stopped.

“If they are real,” said the old man, “then the songs are real.” He looked round the table. Slowly. At each of them. “Do you understand me? Not one song. The songs. We have told our children for four hundred years that the elves are a story, and the dwarves are a story, and the halflings and the orcs and all the rest, and we have told ourselves that the kingdom is the world, and past it there is the enemy and then there is nothing.” He was quiet. “And three of them have walked out of nothing to a fort on the Redmarch. In the snow. And knelt.” His old hands tightened on his staff. “I am not frightened of three people, Majesty. I am frightened of what else we have been calling a story.”

Nobody said anything for a while.

Halvard found that he was thinking about the map. About the inch of blank linen at the bottom.

He’d thought he was afraid of it too. On the stairs, at the grey, with the strip in Hana’s hand; he’d thought that was what the feeling was. The world getting bigger. Everything he couldn’t plan for, pouring in at the edges. He’d spent twenty years making it small enough to hold. It was the only way he’d ever known how to keep anyone safe.

He looked at the feeling again, now, with the Warden’s words in the room.

It wasn’t fear.

He didn’t know what it was. He hadn’t felt it in a long time. He thought, possibly, that the last time had been on a wall at Cinderhold, twenty years ago, when a garrison boy of fifteen had looked at a court mage’s careful plan for the night watch and said it was stupid, and been right, and the whole world had turned out to be a little larger than Halvard had drawn it.

The second strip came in the middle of the afternoon.

He saw the cat girl feel it. She was at the front of the gallery, leaning on the rail, and her hand went to her left wrist, to the brown knot with no bead, and her face changed. She unwound it. She read it. Her ears went flat and then straight up.

Then she came down.

Nobody came down from the gallery. It wasn’t done. The gallery was for watching. But the king had said in the autumn, in front of all of them, only the strong speak to the king, and I’ve decided you’re strong, and nobody had yet found a way to argue with that either. So she came down the little stair at the side, and across the floor of the council chamber, with every lord in Aurelan watching her tail, and stopped beside Halvard’s chair.

“Aldous,” she said. Quietly. “Again. You’ll want it now.”

“Read it,” said the king. Before Halvard could say anything. “Out loud, girl. For the room.”

Hana looked at Halvard. He nodded.

She held the strip up in the light from the high windows.

“They ask nothing of the king,” she read. “Their eldest says: tell your king we came. Tell him we went. Kings are short. We have never asked one for anything. We will not start with a good one.”

The council chamber went perfectly silent.

Halvard looked at the Lord Chancellor’s face. He would remember it for some time. He looked at the Treasurer’s, which had gone the colour of milk again. He looked at the Marshal, who had his mouth slightly open, and at the Warden, who had closed his eyes.

And then he looked at the king.

The king had put his elbows on the council table, which was not done, and his face in his big burned hands, which was not done either. His shoulders were going. He wasn’t making any noise. Kestra, behind his chair, had taken out her book, and was writing in it, and her face was doing the thing it did when she was trying very hard not to let it do anything.

Kings are short.

The king took his hands down. His eyes were wet.

“Well,” he said. To the table. To all of them. “There it is.”

“Majesty,” said the Lord Chancellor. Faintly.

“Nobody goes,” said the king. “Nobody sends. No embassy. No escort fit for anything.” He looked at the Treasurer. “No commission formed to consider it.” He looked round the whole table, one face at a time, the way the Warden had. “Nobody writes them a letter with a seal on it. Nobody rides south of the downs to see what’s there. Nobody asks them for timber, or furs, or a song, or the time of day.” He laid his hand flat on the table. “They asked nothing. We give them nothing back. That’s manners. My father would’ve known that. He was a smith in Brakewater and he knew everything worth knowing.” He was quiet. “We will not start with a good one.” He shook his head. “I’ve been called a great many things since I put the hat on. Nobody’s ever said it like that.”

“Majesty, they are in a royal fort,” said the Chancellor. “On the frontier. Under the crown’s—“

“So is a tree,” said the king. “And a snake. And a man with silver hair who talks to dead people. And you’ve never once sent a commission to any of them, Rodric, and they’ve done very well.” He stood up. The whole table stood up with him; it had to. “Council’s done. Go home. Tell your wives the elves are real and they don’t want anything.” He paused at the end of the table. “That’ll frighten ‘em more than spears.”

Behind him, Kestra underlined something. Twice.

He wrote it that evening. Or tried to.

He sat at his desk in the west tower with the lamp lit and the map behind him and a sheet of good paper in front of him, and wrote Ars, and stopped.

He’d written thirty-eight letters at this desk that he’d never sent. Eleven more he’d never finished. He’d burned the eleven in a lamp on the end of a jetty in the autumn, with the man sitting beside him on the boards, and the man had said you don’t have to keep the wall, and he’d stopped keeping it. Mostly. He’d written four letters since, all through the winter, that had gone by rider as far as Hethe and sat there, he assumed, in a postmaster’s drawer, waiting for the ridge to open. How are the hens. That was what he’d been told to write. Anything but is he well. He’d written how are the hens four times, and a good deal else, and none of it had got there.

This one had to get there.

He looked at the paper for a long time. Then he folded it, and put it in his coat, and went down the hundred and four stairs again, and across the courtyard to the king’s house, where the summoned lived, and knocked.

The cat girl opened the door. She looked at him. She looked at the folded paper in his hand.

“Maren’s,” she said. Before he’d said anything. She was already unwinding a cord from her wrist: a blue one, with a white bead, the plainest of the seven except Aldous’s. “Only the one. Not all at once. I learned.”

He knew what she’d learned. She’d written The door is open on all seven halves at once in the autumn and broken the channel for a night. He’d never seen her look so frightened, before or since.

“Only the one,” said Halvard.

She gave him a strip, and a pen with the blue ink in it, the boy’s ink, the burned one’s. And he sat down at the end of their long table, with five people from another world pretending not to watch him, and the big one stirring something on the stove, and the tall one with the tail pretending to read, and found that the cords didn’t want a letter.

They wanted a few words. That was all they’d carry. A few words, and what you meant by them.

He’d never in his life written a few words to the man. He’d written thirty-eight letters and eleven drafts and a report a week for nine years about him. He sat with the blue pen over the strip and thought about everything he could say, the way he thought about everything, in order, the way he’d compose it for council. The west gate. The rite. The Warden. Kings are short. The world, past the inch of blank linen, getting bigger. What it might mean, for the war, for the binding, for the way home.

He wrote:

How are the hens. Also, elves.

He looked at it.

“That’s it?” said Hana. Over his shoulder. She couldn’t help it; he’d have looked too.

“He’ll understand,” said Halvard.

She tied it to the cord. She held the cord in her hand for a moment, with her eyes shut, the way they all did. And then she opened them and said, “It’s gone,” and Halvard, who had commanded the movement of armies, felt something go out of him down a piece of blue string with a white bead on it to a militia hall on a lake a week’s ride away, and sat very still.

“Does he know?” said Halvard. “About them. The elves.” He didn’t know why he asked her. “Would he?”

The cat girl looked at him oddly.

“He reads the channel,” she said.

Halvard didn’t say anything.

“Every night,” said Hana. “On the jetty. All of it. Group 1 told him we knew, the midwinter before last. He’s read every line we’ve written for two years.” She was quiet. “He’ll have read Yan’s the night they came. And Maigo’s. All forty of Maigo’s.” She paused. “You didn’t know?”

“No,” said Halvard.

He sat at the end of their table for a while.

Two years. Every night. On the end of a jetty, in the dark. Reading thirty-five people write to each other about him, and never once writing back. And Halvard in a tower a week’s ride away, building a lake out of reports, never once knowing.

He’d known before Halvard did. Of course he had. He always had.

Halvard put his hand over his eyes. And laughed. Quietly, at the end of a long table in a house full of people from another world, the way he’d laughed once in the summer at a hawk, and before the hawk not since a wall at Cinderhold. And the big one at the stove, who stood where he stood, turned round and looked at him with a ladle in his hand and said, very gently, in his own tongue, something Halvard didn’t understand and the cat girl didn’t translate.

The reply came in the morning.

He was at the map when she brought it. He hadn’t slept much. He’d stood in front of the map most of the night, in his shirtsleeves, with the lamp, looking at it. Not at the kingdom. At the edges. At the grey wash of Kharvos with nothing written in it, and the Greyspine with the pass, and the sea, and the inch of blank linen at the bottom.

She knocked. He said come in. She came in with the strip and stood in the doorway and read it to him, the way she had three days before, with the grey coming up in the window behind her.

“They hate me,” Hana read.

Halvard closed his eyes.

“There’s more,” said Hana. “Underneath. Like he stopped and then wrote it.” She read it. “Elves: I know. Good.”

He stood there in front of the map with his eyes closed for a long time.

Good. That was his word. That was what he’d written back in the autumn, every time, to they hate me. Good. And the man had taken it, and turned it round, and sent it back up a piece of string from the end of a jetty: the world has got bigger, and I know, and it’s good. In one word. The way he’d always said things. With fish, or a horseshoe, or a pair of black horns in a box. Never more than he had to. Never less.

“Will you answer?” said Hana. Quietly.

“Yes,” said Halvard.

She held out the pen.

He wrote Good. On the strip, in blue, in the summoned’s ink. And she tied it to the cord, and shut her eyes, and it went.

When she’d gone, he took his own pen. The black one, his, the one he’d corrected the map with for twenty years.

He went to the map. To the bottom of it. Past the downs, past the last market town, past where the surveyors had stopped. To the inch of blank linen where there was nothing, because you didn’t plan for nothing.

He’d spent twenty years making the world small enough to hold. It was the only way he’d known how to keep anyone safe. He’d thought, on the stairs three days ago, that he was frightened of it getting bigger.

He looked at the blank linen.

Then he wrote, very small, in his own hand, in the corner, where the frame began:

Further.

And stood back, and looked at it, and found that he didn’t mind at all.
