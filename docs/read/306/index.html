The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 11

Chapter 306

Who's the Cup

3,996 words · 17 min read

Calvin had got into the habit, since the spring, of listening to the whole of Kingsferry at three in the morning.

He didn’t sleep much. He never had, really; on Earth he’d been the one still awake at the end of every call, asking one more question. Here it was worse, because here there was Earshot, and Earshot didn’t sleep at all. It had come to him at midwinter, Tailwind turned round, the wind that used to push him going the other way and bringing things back to him instead: sound. From a long way off. Everything the wind had touched, on its way to his ears.

So at three in the morning, most nights, he went down to the end of the north quay, the soldiers’ end, where nobody went at that hour except the Watch, and sat on a bollard with his coat round him and the river going past black and fast, and turned his face into the wind, and listened.

It was the best thing he’d ever done. He’d tried to tell the others and failed. It was like having the whole city in his lap. The night boats knocking at the customs-house steps. A baby, up in the Tanners’ Row, and its mother singing to it under her breath. Two men arguing in a cellar about a horse. The bells on the river marks going tonk, tonk as the current pushed them. Dogs. A cat on a roof by the ropewalk, very offended about something. Harl at the forge, who started before anybody, the first slow chink of a hammer finding the iron in the dark. Snoring, everywhere, thousands of people, like the sea.

He loved it. He was a little ashamed of how much he loved it. It felt like reading over somebody’s shoulder.

And on the twenty-third night after they came home from the lake, at a quarter to three, with the wind off the river and the moon down, he heard somebody singing in Kharvos.

He knew the voice. He’d heard it first in the winter, the night Earshot came: an old woman’s voice on the far wharf, singing a song in a language nobody in Kingsferry was supposed to speak, and the window turning it into words for him a breath behind, the way it turned everything. He’d followed the voice across the river the next day and found Yeva, mending nets, with her walnut face and her dark sharp eyes and four shawls, and asked her straight out what the song was. She’d looked at him for a long time and then told him. Six verses. A nail on a hill, and what it held, and a garrison boy with a red temper who’d pulled it out, and the priests weeping.

That was all of it, she’d said. Six verses. She’d sung them back to the boy who pulled it out, she said, by way of the net she’d sent upriver; she’d stood on the north quay in the spring for the first time in forty years and held out a net over the water to a barge, and said tell him an old woman at the last well said a song back to him.

This wasn’t one of the six.

It was quicker. Lighter. It had the bounce in it that songs have when children use them for something: counting, or skipping, or deciding who’s it. Yeva was singing it very quietly, alone, in the dark, the way you’d hum something to yourself while you worked with your hands. He could hear the net needle going in the pauses. He could hear her breath.

The window turned it into words for him, a breath behind.

Empty hands from the cold side,

came to the priests and stayed a night,

taught them how to pour.

Pour it out and pour it in,

what you were won’t be again.

Who’s the cup? Who’s the cup?

Mender, mender, sit you up.

No one mends in all the black;

fetch a mender, bring her back.

And then the needle again, going in and out, and the old woman’s breath. And then the first line again, from the beginning, the way children start a skipping rhyme again when the rope comes round.

Calvin sat on the bollard on the north quay with the wind in his face and didn’t move for a long time.

He didn’t go across in the dark. He thought about it. He sat on the bollard and thought about it very hard, the rhyme going round in his head like the rope, who’s the cup, who’s the cup, and he thought that an old woman alone at three in the morning on a wharf singing to herself might not want a man she’d met twice coming at her out of the dark asking what it meant.

He went home instead and woke Dennis.

Dennis didn’t like being woken. Dennis didn’t like anything before the sixth hour; he said the market didn’t open till then and neither did he. He sat up on his cot in the rooms over the chandler’s with his hair on end and listened to Calvin say the rhyme, twice, all of it, the way the window had given it; and by the end of the second time he’d stopped looking cross.

“Sing it,” said Dennis.

“I can’t sing.”

“I know you can’t sing. Sing it anyway.”

Calvin sang it. Badly. The tune was simple and he couldn’t hold it. Dennis listened to it with his head on one side and his eyes half shut, the way he listened to a man telling him what a bolt of cloth was worth.

“No crack,” said Dennis, when he’d finished.

That was the thing Dennis could do now. Since True, since the Candle that had come to him last autumn on the docks with the glamour going the wrong way round, making the thing that was really there easier to see instead of something that wasn’t. He’d found out afterwards it worked on words too. Not well. Not always. But if you said a thing to him that wasn’t so, he heard it, he said, like a crack in a cup when you flick it with your nail.

“It’s not a lie,” said Calvin. “It’s a song.”

“Songs can lie. Songs lie all the time. Half the songs on the river are lies about women.” Dennis rubbed his face. “That one isn’t. Not a crack in it. Whoever made that up wasn’t making it up.” He looked at Calvin over his hands. “Fetch a mender.”

“Yeah.”

“Bring her back.”

“Yeah,” said Calvin. “I heard.”

Yeva was on the far wharf at the sixth hour, the way she was every morning, on her upturned crate in the lee of the fish sheds with a net across her knees and her needle going. She looked up when they came along the planks: Calvin first, then Dennis, then Sabine, because Sabine had heard them getting up and said that if there was a reason to be up at this hour she wanted a cut of it.

“The boy with the wind,” said Yeva. “And the boy with the cheese.”

“It was very good cheese,” said Dennis.

“It was very expensive cheese.” Yeva’s needle didn’t stop. “Mira at the fish sheds bought a slice and has told everyone. You’ve made enemies.” She looked at Sabine. “And the woman with the sword.”

“Morning,” said Sabine.

Calvin crouched down on the planks in front of the crate, close enough to talk and not close enough to crowd, the way Rachael had shown him once with frightened patients. Yeva watched him do it with her dark sharp eyes.

“I heard you singing,” he said. “Last night. Late.”

The needle stopped.

“I’ve got a thing,” said Calvin. “In my ears. Since the winter. I can hear a long way. I didn’t mean to listen; I just do, at night, all of it. And I heard you.” He didn’t look away. “It wasn’t one of the six.”

Yeva looked at him for a long time. The fish sheds clattered behind her, and the river went past, and somewhere a gull said something rude to another gull.

“No,” she said at last. “It wasn’t.”

“What was it?”

She put the needle down. She did it carefully, sticking it into the cork on her knee so it wouldn’t roll, the way she must have done ten thousand times. Then she folded her hands on top of the net.

“A skipping song,” said Yeva. “That’s all. When I was small. The girls in my village skipped to it. You’ll have them here. You stand in a ring, with the rope going round, and you sing, and when the song stops, whoever’s standing in the middle is the cup.” She looked at the river. “And the cup has to sit down in the middle of the ring and not move, while the rest of you go round. Till the next song.”

“In the middle of a ring,” said Calvin.

“In the middle.” She looked at him. “I didn’t know what it meant. None of us did. It was older than my grandmother; she’d skipped to it too, and her grandmother. Children’s songs are like that. Nobody knows what they mean. You sing them because the rope’s going round.” She was quiet. “I thought the cup was just a cup. I thought it was about a kitchen.”

“When did you find out it wasn’t?”

“Ten years ago,” said Yeva.

Nobody said anything.

“The soldiers came back down the river,” said Yeva. “From the Redmarch. After the battle. The big one, when the black came across the red harder than it had in my whole life, and the hero turned it back. And they were on the north quay drinking, the way the river wall men do, and I was there with my nets, and nobody looks at an old woman mending nets.” She turned her hands over on the net, and looked at them. “And they said that the hero’s woman had died. The healer. On a rise. In a ring. They said the black had done something to her, in the middle of a ring, in front of the whole wall, something nobody had ever seen them do. And then she died.”

She stopped.

“And I went home,” said Yeva, “and sat in my kitchen, and I sang that song to myself, the whole of it, for the first time in fifty years. And I knew what it was about.”

Sabine had gone very still.

Calvin hadn’t seen her go still like that before. Sabine was all movement, usually: shifting her weight, turning her sword-hilt in her hand, looking at the exits. Their guard, since the first day. A Reaching swordswoman who’d guarded merchant caravans for fifteen years before the crown hired her, and said so, and had opinions about everything and wanted a cut of all of it. She was standing on the planks behind Dennis with her hand on her sword-hilt, not turning it, and her face had gone the colour of the river.

“Say the end again,” said Sabine.

“No one mends in all the black,” said Yeva. “Fetch a mender, bring her back.”

“No one mends,” said Sabine.

“There are no menders in the black.” Yeva looked up at her. “You’ve fought them. You know. They don’t heal. Not their own, not anything. A hunter loses an arm, it goes on with one arm. A thing in the black gets cut, it stays cut, or it dies.” She shrugged. “When I was a girl we said that was why they hated us. Because we mend and they can’t.”

“So they’d have to bring one,” said Dennis. Very quietly. “From over here.”

“Fetch a mender,” said Yeva.

“And bring her back,” said Calvin. “Back where?”

Yeva looked at him. It was a long look, and there was something in it he didn’t have a word for. Pity, maybe. Or the look you give a child who’s asked a question you’d hoped they wouldn’t ask until they were older.

“Across the red,” she said. “Where else would the cup go?”

Dennis said no crack, afterwards. Walking back along the planks with the sun coming up over the fish sheds. Not a crack in any of it. She believes every word. And I think she’s right to.

Sabine didn’t say anything at all, the whole way back across the bridge. At the top of the Tanners’ Row she stopped, and looked down the hill at the river, at the customs-house steps going down pale and long into the water with the morning boats tying up along them; and then up the hill, toward the infirmary.

“I’m going up there,” said Sabine.

“Rae’ll hate it,” said Dennis.

“Rae can hate it.” Sabine turned her sword-hilt in her hand, once, the way she did. “I’ve stood behind that girl for four years, since the first day, while she healed every idiot on this river with a cut, and I’ve never once stood in front of her. That’s a guard’s job. I’ve been doing it wrong.” She looked at Calvin. “Tell the others. Gabe and Dest. Tell them about the song.”

“What about the channel?”

Sabine looked at him.

“I don’t know your channel,” she said. “I don’t read it. I can’t. But I know what you lot put in it, and I know you don’t put everything.” She jerked her chin at the river. “That song’s about her. The healer on the rise. And that’s not yours to put anywhere. That’s his.” She turned away up the hill. “Ask Dest.”

Deston said the same thing. At the forge, at midday, with Gabe at the anvil and the hammer going, standing in the yard with Calvin and Dennis in the heat coming off the hearth.

“Not yet,” said Deston. “Not in the channel. Not till he says.”

“He doesn’t even know we know about the rise,” said Calvin. “Not properly. Rae knows. You know. You three heard it at the lake, the second spring, all of it. The rest of us—“ He stopped. “The rest of us put it together. From bits. From your faces.”

“I know.” Deston looked at the hammer coming down. “Rae’s written to him.”

Calvin looked at him.

“Two words,” said Deston. “By the post road. Last week. She told me after. She didn’t tell me what.” He was quiet. “I’ve got a guess.”

Gabe put the hammer down. He’d been listening the whole time; Gabe listened with his back as well as anything. He came over to them wiping his hands on his apron, red to the eyebrows from the heat, and stood with them.

“Say it,” said Gabe. “The song. All of it.”

Calvin said it.

Gabe stood in the yard with his black hands hanging and listened to all nine lines, and his face, which did everything, wet and loud and too much, did nothing at all. Calvin had seen that once before. On the barge at the Narrows, the morning after, when Gabe had looked up at a cliff.

“Mender, mender, sit you up,” said Gabe.

“Yeah.”

Gabe looked up the hill toward the infirmary. Then he went back to the anvil and picked up the hammer and hit the iron, once, very hard, so the whole yard rang.

“They’ll have to come through me,” said Gabe, to the iron. “That’s all.”

Rachael was at the window at the end of the long ward when he went up at dusk. She had the boy’s notebook on her knee, the one she’d started for Ned Webb, and Ned himself was asleep on the end bed with his mother in a chair beside him, because the grey had reached his wrist the night before, and Maud had said he wasn’t going home again till they knew.

Sabine was on a stool by the door of the ward. Facing the door. With her sword across her knees. Rachael had obviously had words with her about it, and obviously lost, and was obviously pretending Sabine wasn’t there.

“Cal,” said Rachael. “If you’re going to tell me something awful, sit down first. I’ve had enough people standing over me today.”

He sat down on the bench opposite her. Near enough. Not too near. He told her.

She listened to all of it without moving. The three in the morning, and the voice, and the nine lines. Yeva on the wharf. The skipping. The cup in the middle of the ring. The soldiers on the north quay ten years ago. No one mends in all the black.

When he got to the end, she looked down at the notebook on her knee for a long time.

“Fetch a mender,” she said. “Bring her back.”

“Yeah.”

“Back across the red.” She turned a page in the notebook, and turned it back. “I’d wondered. All the times. The hat man in the first autumn, saying a position in a household in the hills. In the west. I thought, why the west, if it’s the black that wants me? Why not just—“ She moved her hand, take. “But they don’t want me to have. They want me to carry. Somewhere else. Across. To wherever the ring is.” She closed the notebook. “And the west’s the long way round, but it’s the way round the wall.”

“Rae—“

“I know,” said Rachael. “I know. Don’t.” She looked out of the window, at the customs-house steps going down into the dusk. “What’s it like? The song. The tune.”

He hummed it. Badly. She listened with her head against the window glass.

“That’s a nice tune,” she said. “That’s the worst part.”

He didn’t know what to say to that. He sat there, on the bench, near enough. And after a while she reached across without looking at him and took hold of his sleeve, at the cuff, between her finger and thumb, the way she did in a crisis. He’d seen her do it twice before, in the worst moments, and never to him.

She held on to his sleeve for about a breath.

Then she let go, and put her hand back on the notebook, and said, “Don’t make it a thing.”

“I’m not making it a thing.”

“You’re making a face like it’s a thing.”

“I’m making a face,” Calvin agreed, “because it’s a thing. Rae. You held my sleeve.”

“I’ll hold your neck in a minute,” said Rachael, and nearly smiled.

He didn’t go down to the north quay that night. He thought he wouldn’t be able to bear it, the whole city in his lap, with the rhyme going round in it like the rope. He lay on his cot in the rooms over the chandler’s and tried to sleep, and listened to Dennis snore, and to Deston not sleep either, on the other side of the room, breathing too evenly.

At a quarter to three he got up anyway.

He didn’t go to the quay. He went to the window, and opened it, and leaned out into the night with his face in the wind off the river, and let Earshot go.

The city came. All of it. The night boats knocking. The bells on the marks, tonk, tonk. The baby in the Tanners’ Row, asleep at last; he could hear its mother not singing, the small careful breathing of somebody lying very still so as not to wake it. Dogs. Harl’s first chink, early, in the dark. Thousands of people sleeping, like the sea. And up the hill, faint, through the open shutters of the long ward, a woman turning a page, and a boy breathing in his sleep, and the scrape of a stool as Sabine shifted her weight by the door.

He listened to all of it. He listened for the old woman on the far wharf, and she wasn’t singing tonight; he could hear her, just, an old woman asleep in a room over the fish sheds, breathing slow.

And he heard the hole.

He almost didn’t. It was the kind of thing you don’t hear; it was the absence of a thing. He was listening along the waterfront, the way he did, the whole line of it from the north quay round to the fish sheds, every sound the river and the boats and the sleeping city made; and at the customs-house steps, at the bottom, by the water, where the night boats tied up, there was a place where the sound went round.

Like water round a stone.

The boats knocked on either side of it. The river slapped the bottom step on either side of it. The wind went past it on both sides. And in the middle of the steps, about the size of a man standing, there was nothing. No breath. No shift of weight. No heartbeat, which he could hear, at this hour, in a quiet street, if he leaned. Not the silence of an empty step. The other kind. The kind where something is standing in the middle of the noise, and the noise doesn’t touch it, because there’s nothing in it for the noise to touch.

He stood at the window with his heart going.

It didn’t move. He listened to it for a long time. A quarter of an hour, maybe more. A place on the steps where the world went round, standing very still at the edge of the river, facing up the hill.

Facing the infirmary.

Then, between one breath and the next, it wasn’t there. The sound closed over the place like water over a dropped stone, and the boats knocked, and the river slapped the step, and there was nothing on the customs-house steps but the night.

He went down at first light, with Dennis, who’d woken when he went out and come without asking.

The steps were wet with the river mist and empty. A few early boats tying up. A woman with a basket of eels. Nobody standing anywhere.

On the bottom step, at the water’s edge, exactly where the hole had been, there was a fish-hook.

It was lying on the stone by itself, in the wet. A big bright one, the kind you’d use for pike, with a barb like a cat’s claw. Brand new. Not a speck of rust on it, though it had been lying on a wet step all night by a river.

Dennis crouched down and looked at it without touching it. He looked at it for a long time.

“Glamour,” he said at last, very quietly. “I’m going to look at it with True. I’m going to see what’s really there.” He held his hand out over the hook, flat, not touching. “Hang on.”

Calvin waited.

Dennis’s hand stayed there a long moment. Then he took it away, and stood up, and wiped it on his coat, slowly, the way he’d wiped his hands on his trousers in the first winter after he’d picked up an empty hat at the night market.

“It’s a hook,” said Dennis. “That’s what’s really there. It’s just a hook.” He was quiet. “There’s nothing else on it. Nothing at all. It’s the most nothing thing I’ve ever looked at.”

He didn’t pick it up. Neither did Calvin. They stood on the bottom step of the customs-house stair in the mist and looked at it, and the river went past.

“It’s a message,” said Calvin.

“Who to?”

Calvin looked up the hill. At the long low roof of the infirmary on the rise above the Tanners’ Row, with smoke coming out of the chimney at each end, where Maud was up, and the fires were going, and a girl with cold hands was sitting at a window with a notebook on her knee, watching a boy’s wrist.

He didn’t need to answer. Dennis had looked too.

“Bring Gabe,” said Dennis. “He’ll want to pick it up with tongs.”
