The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 1: Thirty-Four Winters

Chapter 19

Respawn

2,444 words · 11 min read

The worst part of being nearly killed, Azac decided in the second week, was the ceiling.

Mother Annick’s spare room had a ceiling of rough plaster with a long crack running from the corner by the window to a water stain above the door, and by the second week he knew every inch of it. He knew the place where the crack forked. He knew the patch of plaster that looked like a horse if you squinted, and the other patch that looked like Garrick if you squinted harder. He knew it the way he’d known the loading screens of games he’d played too much: so well that he’d stopped seeing it, and then so well that he started seeing it again.

“You’re staring at the ceiling,” said Mother Annick, coming in with a bowl of something brown.

“There’s nothing else to stare at.”

“There’s the window.”

“The window’s on the other side of the stitches.”

She looked at him over her spectacles, set the bowl down on the table beside the bed, and went to the window and opened the shutters all the way, so that the cold autumn light came in across the floor and the bed and his hands.

“Then stare at that,” she said, “and eat your soup.”

He ate his soup. He stared at the light. It didn’t help as much as she seemed to think it would.

They came every day. That was the other thing about being nearly killed: you found out who came.

Xav came every evening, after training, and sat in the chair by the bed and told him what they’d done that day. Not the way you’d tell an invalid, softened and careful. He told it the way he’d always told it after a match, in the post-game call, with the mistakes and the good calls and the bits that had gone sideways. It was the most normal thing in the world, and it was the thing Azac looked forward to most.

John came in the mornings, and talked about light. He’d started being able to see it moving in the Hollow, he said, before anyone else could see anything: a faint change in the dark, half a second before something came round a corner. “It’s like the game’s giving me a sound cue,” he said. “Except it’s not a sound. And it’s not a game.”

RX came at odd hours, always without warning, and sat on the end of the bed and talked too fast about everything and nothing, and never once mentioned the thing in the wall. Azac knew why. RX had been going in. He’d had his knives out and he’d been going in, and if Petra had been one second slower, there’d have been two of them in Mother Annick’s spare rooms, or one of them in the ground. RX didn’t talk about that. He talked about everything else, very fast, as if he could build a wall out of words between himself and the gap in the stone.

Malcolm came every day too, and sat in the corner, and wrote in a notebook, and didn’t say much at all.

“What’s that?” Azac asked him, in the third week.

Malcolm didn’t look up. “Plans.”

“For what?”

“For when it goes wrong.” He turned the notebook round. The page was covered in his neat English handwriting, in columns, like a spreadsheet. IF Azac down → Mal front with spare shield, RX pull aggro left, John light-flash, Xav call fall back to last corner. IF John blind → RX eyes, Xav count steps from last junction. IF Xav down → Mal calls. Mal hates this. Mal will do it anyway. There were dozens of them. “Every way I can think of that it goes wrong. And what we do.”

Azac read the page twice.

“That’s really depressing,” he said.

“Yes,” Malcolm agreed. “That’s why it works.”

He turned the notebook back round and kept writing. After a while Azac said, “Put one in for if I do it again. Step in front when I shouldn’t.”

Malcolm’s pen stopped.

“I already did,” he said quietly. “It’s the first one.”

Nobody asked him the question. That was the strange part. Not Xav, not RX, not John or Malcolm or Garrick or even Mother Annick, who asked him everything else, including some very personal questions about his bowels. Nobody asked him why.

Why he’d gone forward when Garrick said back. Why he’d planted his feet in front of a thing that none of them could see properly, with an outfitter’s shield and an Initiate’s nothing, when every instinct he had should have been screaming at him to move.

He knew the answer. That was the problem. He’d known it lying on the stone with the blood coming out of him, and he’d known it every day since, staring at the crack in the ceiling.

He’d gone forward because that was the spot.

That was all. That was the whole of it. There was a spot, in every game, in every raid, in every party they’d ever been in: the front, the place where the first hit landed. For years, somebody else had stood in it. Somebody who’d joke the whole time he did it, who’d say don’t worry, I’ve got this, trust right before something completely untrustworthy happened, who’d taken every hit that was meant for the rest of them and laughed about it in the post-game call.

And then he’d died in his sleep in Malaysia. And the spot had been empty.

They’d tried to play without anyone in it, the first few weeks. It hadn’t worked. It had felt wrong, like a missing tooth, like a gap on a team sheet that everybody kept looking at and nobody mentioned. So Azac had stepped into it. Not because he was good at it. Because somebody had to, and he was the biggest, and he couldn’t stand looking at the gap anymore.

And somewhere in three years of standing there, he’d stopped thinking of it as Declan’s spot that I’m standing in and started thinking of it as the spot. As if it were a law. As if, whenever something came at them, the spot had to have someone in it, and that someone had to be him, no matter what.

He’d planted his feet in front of a thing that split shields like paper because three years ago, a friend of his had died, and he’d never quite stopped trying to fill the hole.

He didn’t say any of this to anyone. He just stared at the ceiling and thought it, over and over, until it wore a groove.

In the fourth week, he could sit in a chair by the window. In the fifth, he could stand, if he held the bedpost. And from the window, he could see the yard behind Mother Annick’s house, where Petra was teaching Xav to wait.

Azac had never seen anything like it.

She’d put Xav in the middle of the yard with a practice sword and stood in front of him with her own, and then she’d just… stood there. Not moving. Not attacking. Not doing anything at all. She stood perfectly still, with her blade low and her eyes on him, and waited.

And Xav waited too. For a minute. Two. Five. Azac could see him trying not to move: his weight shifting, his hand flexing on the grip, the tiny restless movements of someone who’d been told to be still and couldn’t quite.

After ten minutes, Xav moved first. Just slightly. Just a shift of the back foot.

Petra’s blade was at his throat before Azac saw her move.

“Again,” she said. Azac could hear it through the glass.

They did it again. And again. All afternoon. Xav never once got his blade anywhere near her. But by the end of the day he’d stopped shifting his feet, and the last time, he stood there for twenty minutes without moving at all, and it was Petra who finally lowered her sword.

“Better,” she said.

Xav was soaked with sweat and looked as if he’d run a mile. He also looked happier than Azac had seen him since they arrived.

When he came up to the room that evening, Azac said, “What was that?”

“Waiting,” said Xav, sitting down heavily in the chair. “She says I’m good at it. She says I’m not good enough.”

“Good at what, exactly? You just stood there.”

“That’s the whole thing.” Xav rubbed his face. “She says the man she saw on the Redmarch could stand at the top of a ditch for an hour with demons coming over the bank and not move until the exact moment he needed to. She says that’s what aura’s for, at the top. Not hitting harder. Knowing when.” He was quiet for a moment. “She says I do it already, a bit. Without aura. She says somebody must have taught me.”

Azac looked at him.

“Somebody did,” he said.

Xav didn’t answer. But he didn’t look away either. They’d both been in the same calls, years ago, listening to the same person sit on one angle for a full minute without blinking and then take one shot and win the round. They both knew who’d taught him to wait. It hadn’t been on purpose. It had just been from playing next to him for a thousand games.

“He’d have hated Petra,” said Azac, after a while.

“He’d have loved Petra,” said Xav. “He’d have spent the first hour trying to make her laugh, and the second hour asking her to teach him that thing with the sword, and then he’d have said he could already do it, and then he’d have fallen over.”

Azac laughed. It hurt his side, badly. He laughed anyway.

The windows, that night, were as slow as they always were. They did them round his bed now, because he couldn’t get downstairs.

“Ten,” said John. “The light’s getting easier to read.”

“Nine,” said Malcolm. “I expected eight.”

“Eleven,” said Xav. “It went up two today. Standing still.”

“Ten,” said RX, and then he stopped. He was looking at his window with a strange expression, his mouth slightly open, the way he looked at a map he hadn’t seen before. “Wait. Wait, hold on.”

“What?”

“The slot. The grey one. On my skill page.” RX turned his window toward them, as if they could see it, which they couldn’t. “It’s been a blur for weeks, right? A shape, like a word through fogged glass. I told you.” He swallowed. “I can read it now.”

They all leaned in, uselessly.

“What does it say?” said Azac.

RX read it out slowly, as if he were afraid it would vanish.

[ Unseen · locked · requires Tier 1 ]

Nobody said anything.

“Unseen,” said John. “That’s… yeah. That’s an assassin skill. That’s you.”

“It’s locked,” said RX. “I can’t use it. I can’t do anything with it. It’s just there, waiting.” He stared at it. “But it’s mine. It’s got my name on it. It’s the first thing in this whole world that’s been… mine.”

He didn’t say anything else. He didn’t need to. He just sat on the end of Azac’s bed with his window open in front of him, reading one word over and over, and for the first time in five weeks he wasn’t talking fast at all.

“Eight,” said Azac, when they looked at him. “Same as before. You don’t level much in bed.”

Garrick came up last, late, with his hat in his hand and a face like weather.

“Guild’s sealed the Hollow,” he said, without sitting down. “Below the first ring. Nobody goes deeper until they know what’s coming up.” He looked at Azac. “Three more parties ran into things in the last month that shouldn’t have been that shallow. One of them didn’t come back.”

The room was very quiet.

“There was a letter, too,” said Garrick. “From your people in the west. Thornwall.” He held it out to Xav. “The courier gave it to me at the gate. It’s in your script. I can’t read a word of it.”

Xav read it aloud.

Coldwater. Someone wearing someone. The old woman’s peas. Tracks going up the hill and never coming down. And from the east, eleven scouts counting a fort.

Two sides.

When he finished, nobody said anything for a long time.

“And underneath,” said Malcolm finally. His voice was very flat. “Don’t forget underneath. The things coming up out of the Hollow. That’s three.” He looked at his notebook. “Three sides. I’m going to need more pages.”

He walked for the first time on the last day of the fifth week.

Not far. Down the stairs, holding the rail, with Xav on one side and RX on the other, and Mother Annick at the bottom with her arms folded, ready to say I told you so. Across the kitchen. Out the back door into the yard, into the cold bright afternoon, where Petra was standing by the fence as if she’d been waiting for him.

He stood in the middle of the yard on his own two feet, breathing hard, his side aching, and looked at the sky.

“Well,” said Petra. “You’re up.”

“I’m up.”

She studied him for a moment, with that faraway look she had sometimes, the one that seemed to be seeing something behind you.

“You’ll go in front again,” she said. It wasn’t a question.

“Yeah,” said Azac. “Probably.”

“Why?”

He thought about it. He’d been thinking about it for five weeks, staring at the crack in the ceiling. And he found, standing in the yard with the light on his face, that the answer had changed while he wasn’t looking.

“Because somebody’s got to,” he said. “But not because the spot has to be full.” He looked at Xav, and RX, and John at the window, and Malcolm with his terrible notebook. “He didn’t stand in front because he had to. He stood in front because he trusted us behind him. I’ve been standing there because I didn’t trust anyone else to.” He took a breath. It hurt. “Next time I’ll stand there because I trust them. And I’ll move when Xav says move.”

Petra looked at him for a long moment.

Then she nodded, once, the way she’d nodded at Xav after twenty minutes of standing still.

“Better,” she said.

And she went back inside, and Azac stood in the yard on his own two feet with his friends around him, and for the first time in five weeks, he didn’t think about the ceiling at all.
