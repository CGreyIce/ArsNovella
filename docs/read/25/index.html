The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 1: Thirty-Four Winters

Chapter 25

First Snow

2,733 words · 12 min read

The first snow came to Wrenhaven on a night with no moon, and Higu felt it before she saw it.

She’d been lying awake in the long room above the militia hall, the way she often did now. Not because she couldn’t sleep. Because the night was when her path was loudest, and she’d learned to lie still and listen to it. Her window climbed faster at night; it always had. Something in her was tied to the dark, and to the moon, and to the slow wheel of the stars, and on clear nights she could feel it pulling at her like a tide.

But tonight there was no moon. And at some point after midnight the pull changed. It went soft, and muffled, and very quiet, the way a room goes quiet when someone draws a curtain. She got up and went to the window.

Snow. Falling straight down out of the dark, thick and slow and silent, onto the yard and the roofs and the old grumpy tree by the gate. Already the cobbles were gone. Already the world was white.

She stood there for a long time with her forehead against the cold glass. Behind her, the others were asleep: Xola curled around Squiddles, PenguChi on her back with Waddles on her chest, Gigichi with one arm flung over her face. Thea was at the far end, by the other window, and Higu knew without looking that she wasn’t asleep either. She’d be lying there with her window open, reading the lattice, the way she did every night now. Eighty-eight percent. So close she could probably see the edge of it.

Higu looked at the snow and thought about lists.

She had been keeping one since the second month. She’d never told anyone, except Thea, once, and only because Thea had caught her writing in it.

Things Alea says that aren’t answers.

It was long now. Two pages, in her small neat hand, in English, in the back of the notebook she used for training.

”Everyone in Wrenhaven buys fish from him.”

”I just don’t eat them all.”

”Someone told me. Someone who could see it.”

”It’s a fish. It doesn’t need an answer. It needs a pan.”

”My dad says a road’s only blocked if you stop on it.” That one had a little star next to it, because it was the only time Alea had ever mentioned her father, and she’d looked, for a heartbeat afterwards, as if she wished she hadn’t.

”Some of mine aren’t mine to give away yet.” That one Thea had told her, after the water butt.

Higu wasn’t keeping the list to catch Alea out. She wanted to be very clear with herself about that, because she liked Alea, a great deal. They all did. Alea was clever and funny and kind, and she’d made PenguChi’s aura come up when nothing else could, and she’d sat with Gigichi by the gate when Gigichi wouldn’t talk to anyone. She brought them fish. She brought them three fish, lately, which nobody had remarked on and everybody had noticed.

Higu was keeping the list because she was a strategist, and a strategist pays attention to the shape of things. And the shape of Alea, the more you looked at it, was the shape of someone standing in front of something, very carefully, so that you wouldn’t see it.

The snow kept falling for two days. On the third morning it stopped, and the sky came out clear and hard and blue, and the whole of Wrenhaven was buried to the knee.

Maren cancelled training. That had never happened before. She stood in the doorway of the militia hall looking out at the drifts in the yard with an expression of deep personal offence, and then turned round and said, “Today you will dig.”

So they dug. All of them, Maren included, with shovels borrowed from the stables: clearing the yard, and the path to the gate, and the steps of the hall. Bram dug for about ten minutes, then claimed his knee had gone and sat on an upturned bucket directing everyone. Xola discovered that she could make the snow on her shovel go slushy if she concentrated hard enough, and was so delighted that she spent the next hour melting things on purpose and getting everyone’s boots wet. PenguChi dug steadily and slowly and moved more snow than anyone. Gigichi and Thea dug side by side, at the far end of the path, not talking, but not not talking either. Higu watched them from across the yard and thought that was the best thing she’d seen all winter.

And Alea arrived in the middle of it, with snow up to her waist and the basket over her arm held high out of the drifts, and her tail fluffed out to twice its size with cold.

“You walked here in this?” said Xola.

“It’s only snow,” said Alea. “The lake road’s not that bad. Dad says—“ She stopped.

There was a tiny pause.

“The fisherman says it’ll freeze tonight,” said Alea, very smoothly, and held up the basket. “Three trout. And he— and I thought you might want them before it’s too cold to get to market.”

Higu didn’t write that one down. She didn’t need to. She’d remember it.

The letter from the capital came on the fourth day, when the roads cleared enough for the courier.

It was addressed to Group 1, Wrenhaven, the militia hall, in a round, careful English hand that none of them recognised. Maren handed it over with the faint distaste she reserved for things she couldn’t read. Thea opened it at the long table, with everyone crowded round.

Hello. You don’t know me. My name is Aleaka; I’m in Group 2, in the capital, with Hanarisu and Jared and Milli and Cursiffe. Thea, I think you write to Cursiffe? He says you’re brilliant.

Oswin and I are trying to work out how we’re all connected. Not just who’s friends with who now. Who you knew BEFORE. On Earth.

Who brought you to who? Who did you meet first? If you had to draw a line back from everyone you know here, where would it go?

Please be honest. Please be specific. I think it matters. I don’t know why yet.

There was a short silence round the table.

“That’s a weird question,” said Gigichi.

“It’s a good question,” said Thea slowly. “Oswin’s the archivist. Cursiffe told me. He says no summoning in four hundred years brought more than three people, and never people who knew each other.”

“So why us?” said Xola.

Nobody had an answer.

“Let’s do it,” said Higu. “Let’s answer it properly. All of us.”

So they did. That evening, round the fire, with a sheet of paper in the middle of the table and Higu holding the pen, because her handwriting was neatest and because she wanted to.

It was easy at first. They all knew how they’d met.

“Xola,” said Thea, at once. “I met Xola first. Through streams, years ago. Everyone met Xola first. That’s sort of the point of Xola.”

“I am the point,” Xola agreed.

“I met Xola through Thea,” said Higu. “In a collab.”

“Xola,” said PenguChi. “Through Higu.”

“Xola,” said Gigichi. “Ages ago. Before any of you.”

Higu drew the lines. They all ran into Xola, in the middle of the page, like spokes into a hub. Xola beamed at it.

Then Higu drew one more, without saying anything. A short one, across the rim of the wheel, that didn’t go through the middle at all: from her own name to Gigichi’s. Six years, that line. Longer than most of the spokes.

Gigichi looked at it, and looked away, and didn’t say anything either. But she didn’t look annoyed.

“Okay,” said Higu. “That’s us. Now: anyone outside the group. Anyone in the other groups, or anyone who isn’t here, who connects to more than one of us. Anyone we all knew.”

And that was where it got strange.

Because they started telling stories. Not on purpose. Just the way you do, when someone asks who did you know: one name leads to another, one memory to the next. And every few minutes, without anyone meaning it to, the same name came up.

“He modded my chat,” said Higu. “Two years. That’s how I met half the people in the other groups, honestly. Through his friends.”

“He was in my chat all the time,” said Xola. “Every stream. Being so annoying. And gifting subs like it was nothing, every single time I went live.”

“He was in mine,” said PenguChi. “Word games. He always won.”

“He used to raid me,” said Thea quietly. “When I was small. Before anyone else did. And gift subs, when nobody else was.” She looked round the table. “He raided all of us. Didn’t he? Every one of us, at some point.”

One by one, they nodded.

Gigichi didn’t say anything for a while. Then she said, “He gifted me subs. Even when I was barely streaming anymore. Like he was keeping a seat warm for me.” She pulled at a loose thread on her sleeve. “He sent me a message once. When I’d gone quiet for a month and nobody else noticed. He just said you okay? I never answered.” She looked at the table. “I should have answered.”

The fire popped. Nobody said anything for a moment.

Higu looked down at the paper. At the five lines running into Xola. And then at the other line she’d started drawing, without quite realising it, off to one side: one line out of each of them, running toward a name she hadn’t written yet, at the edge of the page.

She didn’t write it.

She just looked at the five lines, all pointing the same way, and felt something cold and careful settle in her chest, the way it did on a clear night when the stars came out and she could suddenly see the shape of a constellation that had been there all along.

“What?” said Xola, watching her face.

“Nothing,” said Higu. “I’ll write it up properly tomorrow.”

She did write it up, the next morning, in her small neat hand, and she was honest and specific, the way Aleaka had asked. She told the story of five girls who’d all met through Xola. She mentioned, among a dozen other names, the friend who’d modded her chat and raided Thea and played word games with PenguChi and sent Gigichi a message nobody else had thought to send.

She didn’t draw the line. She didn’t say he’s the one we all knew. Not yet. She didn’t know what it meant, and she was a strategist, and a strategist doesn’t move a piece until she knows what the board looks like.

But she kept the paper from the night before, the one with the line she hadn’t finished. She folded it very small and put it in the back of her notebook, next to the list of things Alea said that weren’t answers.

Maren was in the kitchen when Higu came down for water, late, on the night they’d written the letter. She was sitting at the long table alone, with a single candle and a cup of something gone cold, and a folded paper in front of her that she put away into her sleeve, very smoothly, the moment Higu came in.

Higu pretended not to see. She filled her cup at the barrel. Then, instead of going back upstairs, she sat down across the table.

Maren looked at her. Higu looked back.

“You bowed to him,” said Higu. “The fisherman. On the road, the first week. All the way down.”

“I did.”

“You don’t bow to anyone. You didn’t bow to the mayor. You barely nodded at the Advisor in the courtyard.”

“No.”

Higu turned her cup round on the table. “I’m not going to ask who he is,” she said. “I know you won’t tell me. I just want to ask one thing, and you can not answer it if you like.”

Maren waited.

“Is he dangerous?” said Higu. “To Alea. To us.”

Something moved across the guide’s precise, composed face. Higu had been watching it for six months and she’d never seen it do that. It was almost like being startled into honesty.

“No,” said Maren. “He is the least dangerous man I know.” She paused. “To you.”

Higu sat with that for a moment. To you. She thought about Bram’s whispered stories round the fire, the eight-foot hero who’d killed a general with his bare hands. She thought about three fish in a basket, every morning, in the snow.

“Thank you,” she said.

Maren looked at her a long moment longer. Then, unexpectedly, she reached across the table and touched the back of Higu’s hand, very lightly, with two fingers, the way you’d touch something to check it was real.

“You notice a great deal,” she said. “That’s a good thing, in a strategist. It’s a heavy thing, too.” She took her hand back. “Be careful what you carry.”

She got up and blew out her candle and went upstairs, and Higu sat alone in the dark kitchen with her water, and thought that Maren had just told her more in four words than in six months: the least dangerous man, which meant he was dangerous to someone, and to you, which meant Maren knew exactly who you were to him.

That night the moon came back.

A thin one, a fingernail sliver, low over the lake. Higu felt it before she saw it: the pull returning, the tide coming in. She went out into the yard in her coat, into the cleared space where they’d dug, and stood in the snow and looked up.

Her window opened on its own. She let it.

Twenty-two. Up three, just from standing in the moonlight for a minute. It always went up faster at night.

Behind her, the door of the hall opened, and Thea came out, wrapped in two blankets, and stood beside her.

“Couldn’t sleep?” said Higu.

“I never sleep anymore.” Thea looked up at the sliver of moon. “Eighty-nine.”

“That’s one more than yesterday.”

“I know.” Thea’s breath smoked in the cold. “I can see it, Gu. The edge. It’s right there. Like the end of a line of code where you know what the next character has to be and you just can’t make your fingers type it.” She laughed, a little shakily. “It’s driving me insane.”

“It’ll come.”

“Everyone keeps saying that.”

“Because it will.” Higu was quiet for a moment. “Thea. When Alea’s near you. Your window. The sealed thing.”

Thea glanced at her sharply.

“It still does it?” said Higu.

“Every time.” Thea pulled the blankets tighter. “It rattles. Like a door when there’s a draught on the other side. It doesn’t do it for anyone else. Not even Maren.” She hesitated. “And you know what’s strange? It did it on the road, too. That first week. When the fisherman walked past.”

Higu didn’t say anything for a long moment.

“The fisherman,” she said finally.

“Yeah.”

“And Alea, who walked beside him. Who brings his fish. Who says Dad says and then stops.” Higu looked out across the white yard, toward the gate, and the lake road beyond it, buried in snow, going down through the dark toward the water. “Who says some of her secrets aren’t hers to give away.”

Thea was very still beside her.

“You think they’re…” Thea started.

“I don’t think anything yet,” said Higu. “I’ve just got a list.” She smiled faintly. “And a line I haven’t finished drawing.”

They stood in the snow together for a while, under the thin moon, not saying anything else. Down by the lake, very far off, there was a single light: a lamp in a cabin window, small and yellow, the only light for a mile.

Higu looked at it for a long time.

Then she went back inside, and lay down, and didn’t sleep, and listened to the tide of the moon pulling at her through the dark, and thought about constellations: how they’re always there, all along, waiting for someone to notice that the stars make a shape.
