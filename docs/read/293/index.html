Chapter 293

The Balance

6,033 words · 26 min read

The day and the night were the same length, and everybody in Thornwall knew what that meant.

Hyvred woke before dawn on the morning of the balance with Bee asleep across her shins and knew it before she’d opened her eyes. She’d known it for a week. The whole town had. You could see it in the lamps. Every window had been lit every night since the autumn, whether anything was coming or not, the way you’d keep a hand on a dog that had bitten once; but this week they’d been lit earlier, and trimmed higher, and on the last two nights people had put a second lamp in the window beside the first, as if one might not be enough.

She lay there and looked at the ceiling. The beams, the smoke-stain, the crack, the bent nail. She’d cried at it, in the winter, the morning the sight went down. She didn’t now. She just looked at it, on purpose, for a count of ten, the way Hild had taught her, the way you’d look at a friend’s face before a long day. Then she let it go out.

Out. Over her shoulder, north, eleven miles, on its short lead.

The valley came up the way it always came up now: not over everything, the way it had in the autumn, like a reflection on a window you couldn’t see past. Just there. Where it was. A long white-and-brown floor going up between dark hills, with the swept road up the middle of it, clean, and the rings standing on it in their rings, thirteen of them, facing the town. Four hundred and some people who’d been called, in their coats, in the thaw, with their hands open at their sides. The third ring, on the north side, a boy with freckles, turned. And outside the rings, the curve. Holes in the light, in a line, like teeth missing from a mouth, except it was the other way round: the holes were the teeth.

Twenty-four.

And at the top, the round dark place. Facing her.

It always faced her. It had faced her since the autumn, since the first time she’d looked at it with the red mark open on purpose; it had faced the king, and the town, and then a man with a rod on the ridge road in the autumn for longer than Bee could count. But in the mornings, when she went out to it on the short lead, it was always facing her. As if it had been waiting all night for her to look.

She looked past it. Up the switchbacks. Over the notch.

Seven. On the far side, at the top of the far road, in a line in the last of the snow. Facing this way. They hadn’t moved since the man in the brown coat came down.

Twenty-four. And seven.

She let it go. Down. The way a fire goes down when you bank it. It was still there; she could have reached it again in a breath. But it was eleven miles off, at the top of a valley at the foot of the Greyspine, and she was here, in the long room, with Bee asleep on her legs and the smoke-stain on the ceiling and a crack she knew the shape of, and that was where she was.

She got up without waking Bee, which she’d got good at, and went up the tower.

A hundred and twelve steps. She counted them every time. The leads at the top were wet with dew, not frost; it was spring. The sky in the east over the ridge road was going grey, and the town under her was lamps, all of them, two in every window, like a field of something that had come up in the night.

She looked at the town first.

She’d started doing that at the count, in the autumn, when she couldn’t stop seeing the valley and had needed something else to look at. She’d found she could see the town the way she saw the valley, every light in it, and that the lights told you things if you watched them long enough. A light that leaned meant grief: leaning toward a place where somebody had been, the same shape as love, pointing at something that wasn’t there. A light that flickered meant somebody dreaming. And a light that went cold, at the edges, a blue thinness creeping in like frost on a window, meant the call had found it.

She’d learned to see that a day or two early, by the autumn. Thirteen named in a week. Corvin had written every one in the book, and Drew had put them in the long room before the call came, and it had saved most of them.

She looked at every light in Thornwall. All of them. She went down the long street and the lanes and the Watch house and the Bell, one at a time, the way she’d done every dawn for a week.

Nothing. No cold. Not one.

That was the thing that frightened her.

“Nothing,” she said to the king.

He was at the forge. He was always at the forge. He’d been there since before dawn, by the look of the fire; Agnes wasn’t up yet, and he’d lit it himself and was sitting in her big chair beside it with a pair of tongs across his knees that he wasn’t using. Kestra was in the side door with her arms folded. The light was coming grey through the open front of the forge on to the anvil.

Hyvred sat on the bench. She always sat on the bench. She’d sat on it every evening of the winter while he made hinges, and laughed at him when Azac asked him what his shield was held up by.

“Nothing,” said the king.

“Not one light in the town.” She looked at her hands. “Last spring there were nineteen by now. Joss, and the others. In the autumn I had thirteen the week before. This morning, the morning of the balance, there’s nothing. Not a hair. Every light in Thornwall is just—“ She didn’t have the word. “Itself.”

The king turned the tongs over in his hands.

“That should be good news,” he said.

“I know.”

“It isn’t.”

“No.”

He looked at the fire for a long time. His light was the way it always was, to her: a forge with both doors open, red and gold and huge, so much of it that the valley couldn’t lie over it. The one thing in Thornwall the valley couldn’t make into a reflection. She’d sat beside it every night of the worst week of her life, and slept on his arm under his coat, and it had never once gone down. It was a little narrower than it had been in the summer. She’d noticed that at midwinter and not said. He was sixty-six. He’d held the tower for fifteen nights.

“Hisui,” he said. “What does the snake say?”

“Kei says he’s tasted it since yesterday. The call. It’s in the air, the way it always is at the balance.” She stopped. “He says it tastes the wrong way round.”

“The wrong way round.”

“Facing the other way. That’s how Kei put it. Hisui said it like a smell coming out of a door instead of going in.” She looked at the king. “I don’t know what that means.”

“No,” said the king. “Nor do I.” He put the tongs down on the anvil, very carefully, as if they might break. “Tonight, then. We’ll do it the way we did it in the autumn. All of it. The long room, Drew’s chain, the boy’s doses, Azac on the door. Corvin’s book. Hild on the gate.” He looked at her. “And you on the tower. Watching every light in the town. If one goes cold, you shout.”

“And if none do?”

“Then you watch the valley,” said the king. “And tell me what it’s doing instead.”

They did it the way they’d done it in the autumn.

All day the town got ready, and it was a strange kind of ready, because nobody was frightened of the thing they were getting ready for any more. They were frightened of not knowing what it was. That was worse. Hyvred watched it from the tower all afternoon, the lights going about the town: busy, tight, pulled in at the edges the way a light goes when somebody’s holding their breath.

The long room filled at dusk. Forty-one. Everybody who’d ever been called and come back, or been called and not gone, or been named in Corvin’s book: Annet, and Ivo, and Old Linnet in her chair, and Mother Ivey, and Iolo the cooper’s boy, and Corporal Dunn, who still minded not being on the wall. Joss, with his broom, which he wouldn’t put down. Rhys Hywel in his grey shield. And Olwen of Coldwater, who’d come back at the battle and whose son was still in the second ring, by the window.

Drew went down the room and said every name, the way he always did, and they took hands, and he stood at the head of the chain with his open hands and his bound ribs that weren’t bound any more and his feet planted. Counter. Kei went down the room after him with a tray of cups, a barley-grain of knitwort in each, cut to the size of each person’s crack the way Hisui tasted it, and asked each of them, one at a time, out loud, because that was the rule. Will you. And each of them said yes.

Azac stood in the door with his shield and the pale blue showing at the edges of him and said, low, to the room, “Drew’s at the top. Kei’s coming round. Hyvred’s on the tower. The king’s on the gatehouse. Hild’s on the arch. Petra’s in the square.”

Petra was in the square. Hyvred could see her from the tower: grey, like slate, all one colour, at the well, with the children of Thornwall sitting on the cobbles behind her. Nobody had asked them to. They’d just come, at dusk, the ones who’d sat in the granary in the autumn, and sat down behind her with their backs to the well-house wall, because that was where you went now when something was coming. Petra hadn’t put her wall up. She didn’t need to yet. She stood there with her stick under her arm and let them sit behind her.

Hild was on the arch. Pale silver, water in a bucket in moonlight, darker where the eye had been. Like a tree grown round a nail.

The king was on the gatehouse. Red and gold. Both doors open.

And Group 6 in the gaps between, without anybody saying where. Malcolm at the long-room bowls. John on the tower stair below her, with a new picture of the valley he’d made at noon in all ten windows, sitting still. Rui Xiang in the long room, a hand from Tiger, who was on the end of the chain beside Drew. And Xav on the north wall, by himself, with his sword across his knees, doing nothing.

Bee came up the tower at dusk and sat against the parapet beside her, and didn’t say anything, and took her hand.

The sun went down behind the Greyspine.

The day and the night were the same length.

It came at full dark.

Hyvred felt it before Kei posted. The way you feel a door open somewhere in a house: not a sound, a change in the air. It came down off the Greyspine over the notch and the switchbacks and the round dark place at the top of the valley, and it was the call. She knew it the way you’d know a voice you’d heard every night for two years. Come. The one word, the way it had been since the autumn. Not names. Not the soft sweet voice it had used on the three who walked out of the gate. Just come, like a tide, all at once, everywhere.

She opened the sight all the way. The red mark, the whole town.

Every light in Thornwall braced.

She watched it happen. All of them, all at once, the whole town pulling in at the edges the way you’d pull your coat round you in the rain. In the long room, forty-one lights in a chain, held together by Drew at the head of them, gold and steady and too thin. The knitwort in each of them, Kei’s doses, sitting in the cracks like moss, waiting. Azac in the door, a blue ring round all of it. In the square, the children behind Petra, small and bright and very still. Hild on the arch. The king.

She watched for the cold. At the edges. In every one of them.

It came over the town like wind over a roof.

That was the only way she ever found to say it, afterwards. It came down the valley and over the north wall and over the long room and the square and the Bell and every roof in Thornwall, the whole call, come, and it didn’t stop. It didn’t press. It didn’t look for the edges, the cracks, the places where somebody had lost somebody and not mentioned it, the way it had in the autumn, so that Kei’s doses bit and forty doors shut at once. It didn’t touch the doors at all.

It went over.

She felt it go. All of it, over the town, over her on the tower with her hand in Bee’s, over the long room and the chain and Drew’s open hands, so close she could feel the wind of it in her hair, and past. Down the far side of the town. Out of the south gate. Away down the valley road toward the east.

And then it came back.

Not to them. Past them, again, the other way. Like a wave going out. Back up the valley, over the rings, which didn’t move, over the curve, which didn’t move, over the round dark place at the top, which was facing her. Up the switchbacks. Over the notch.

West.

She sat on the leads of the north tower of Thornwall with the whole call of the spring balance going away from her over the Greyspine into the dark, and understood what Hisui had meant.

It tastes the wrong way round.

It wasn’t calling Thornwall.

It was calling them.

She read it with Bee’s hand in hers and didn’t know what she felt.

Relief. That was the first thing. Not one light in the town. Not Joss, not Iolo, not Annet, not Olwen at her window, not a single child in the square. Nobody was going to walk out of the north gate tonight in their stockings with their hands open. She felt the relief go through her like warm water, and sat in it, and let it.

And under it, cold, like a stone at the bottom of the water: it doesn’t need us any more.

The rings were full. Thirteen of them. Full since last spring; full of the people of Thornwall and Coldwater and the hill farms and the Western Watch and the Valcrest thirty-nine. It had called all it needed from here. What it needed now wasn’t in Thornwall. It was on the far side of the Greyspine, standing in the snow at the top of a road, with nothing inside it.

She looked up the valley. Past the rings. Up the switchbacks.

On the far side, at the top of the far road, in the line, two of the seven had moved.

They came down in the dark.

Not slowly, this time. Not like the man in the brown coat, walking all afternoon with his sack, like somebody going home for supper. They came over the notch together, two holes in the night side by side, and down the near switchbacks at a walk that was a little too fast for a person on a mountain road in the dark, and didn’t stop, and didn’t stumble, and didn’t pause at the bends.

Hyvred watched them all the way down. She couldn’t not.

And turned round. And faced the town.

Nobody in the long room moved. Drew posted it. Nobody in the chain had felt anything since the call went over; it had gone over and away and nothing had come back for them. But nobody let go. They sat in the long room with their hands joined till dawn, forty-one of them and Drew at the top, the way they had in the autumn, because nobody knew what else to do.

And at some point in the middle of the night, Olwen of Coldwater got up from her place by the window and walked out of the door.

Hyvred saw her go. She’d been watching every light in the town all night, out of habit, out of the fear that had nowhere to go now; and she saw one light in the long room get up out of the chain, and go past Azac in the door, and out into the long street. And go north. Toward the gate.

Her heart stopped.

She looked. With everything. The red mark open, all of it on one light, the way she’d looked at the absence in the spring with the whole of the thirty-five behind her. She looked at the edges of Olwen of Coldwater for the cold, the blue creeping thinness that meant the call had found her, and—

There wasn’t any.

Olwen’s light wasn’t cold. It wasn’t called. It was the same colour it had always been, a tired warm brown like an old loaf. It was just leaning. All of it. Out of the long room, up the long street, toward the north gate and the swept road and the valley and the second ring eleven miles off, where her son was standing in a field in the dark facing the town with his hands open. Leaning so far it was nearly lying down.

The same shape as love, pointing at something that isn’t there.

It hadn’t called her. She was going because her son was up there.

Olwen stopped at the arch.

Hild was on the walk above it. Hyvred saw her light turn, pale and silver, and look down. Hild didn’t move. She didn’t come down. She stood on the walk over the arch with her hands on the stone and watched.

Olwen stood in front of the gate. It was barred. It had been barred every night since the autumn. She put her hand on it, flat, on the old black oak, the way Tiger put his hand on the post in the mornings.

Azac stopped behind her. Not close. Two paces.

The way he’d narrated in the long-room door every night since the count. Telling Olwen where everybody was.

She found out later, from Azac, in the morning, what he’d said. He told it to her on the tower with his hands round a cup.

Dafydd’s in the second ring, he’d said. On the north side. Eleven miles up. He’s standing still, the way they stand. It’s not snowing on him tonight. It’s spring. It’s quite warm. Hyvred watched her light. Leaning, up the swept road, toward the valley, as far as a light could lean without going over. And Azac behind her, a blue ring, two paces off, not touching, just there, narrating, low, all of it, where everybody was.

Not all the way. It would never go all the way back; Hyvred knew that now, she’d learned it at the count. A light that’s lost somebody always leans a little. But it came back. Off the gate. Back toward the long street, and the long room, and the blue ring two paces behind it.

Olwen took her hand off the oak.

On the walk over the arch, Hild took her hands off the stone, and turned, and went back to watching the valley.

Fifty-one. It had said forty-seven in the winter, the morning she put the sight down on purpose for the first time and told Corvin she was on watch.

She didn’t know what had moved it. She’d stopped trying to know, mostly. She thought it might have been last night: watching Olwen’s light at the gate, and knowing the difference. Knowing it so fast she’d had it typed to Azac before Olwen got to the farrier’s. Not cold. Leaning. Not the call. Love, pointing at something that isn’t there. In the autumn she’d have seen a light walking toward the north gate in the dark and shouted for the whole town. You stand a watch, Hild had said to her once, on the well. The ones who never go down aren’t watching any more. They’re just awake.

She’d been on watch last night. All of it. She thought she might have been, for the first time, all the way.

The king left two days later.

He’d said a week after the pass opened, and it had been a week and a day; and the road east was dry enough now, mostly, that a man on a big grey horse could get down off the Greyspine and across the downs to the river in a fortnight if he didn’t stop to look at things. He wasn’t going to stop to look at things, he said. He’d a Council to see, and two motions waiting on him that he was going to say no to in person, and a lord called Vane who was going to remember it, and he wanted to get it done before the Council found something else to want.

He didn’t make a speech. He’d never made one in Thornwall. He had his horse brought round to the south gate at first light, and Kestra’s, and that was all, and the people who ought to be there came.

Hyvred went up the tower.

She didn’t decide to. Her feet did it. A hundred and twelve steps, and the leads at the top, and the sun well up now over the ridge road, and the road going east from the south gate across the downs toward the river, a pale line in the green. And on it, getting smaller, a big grey horse with a big grey man on it, and a smaller horse a length behind.

She watched his light go.

It took a long time. She stood at the parapet with her hands on the cold stone and watched the forge with both doors open go away east down the road, getting smaller, the way a fire looks when you walk away from it at night: still huge, still red and gold, still more light than anything else on the downs, but further, and further, until it was a spark, a coal, a point on the green going over the first rise.

And then it was over the rise, and gone.

She stood there.

Behind her, eleven miles north, at the top of a valley at the foot of the Greyspine, the round dark place faced her. It had faced her all morning. She could feel it. She didn’t look.

The king’s light is the one thing the valley can’t lie over.

She’d said that to herself all winter. Every night. It had been the thing she held. When the valley came up over everything, when she couldn’t put the sight down and it lay over the ceiling and the beams and Bee’s face like a reflection on a window, she’d look for the king’s light, the forge at the bottom of the long street, and it would be there, and the valley couldn’t lie over it, and she’d know where she was.

It was gone.

She turned round.

And Olwen, at a window, leaning a little, the way she always would; and in the door beside her, a blue ring, standing, not going in, just there.

In the square: the children, going home. And at the well, Petra. Grey, like slate, all one colour, with her stick under her arm.

On the arch: Hild. Pale silver. Water in a bucket in moonlight.

On the north wall: Xav, by himself, with his sword across his knees. Malcolm, coming up the stair to him with a bowl. John on the tower stair below her, making a new picture of the valley, very carefully, for ten windows. Rui Xiang at the well with Tiger, a hand away.

None of them was a forge. None of them was a thing the valley couldn’t lie over.

But there were a great many of them. And they were all here.

She looked at them for a long time.

Bee came up out of the stair hatch and stood beside her, very close, shoulder to shoulder, the way they’d stood a hundred times on the other side with nobody able to see them, and didn’t say anything.

“He’s gone,” said Hyvred.

“I know.”

“The valley can lie over all of them. Every light in the town. It could if it wanted.”

“I know,” said Bee.

“It’s not,” said Hyvred. She looked at the town. “It’s not even trying. It’s looking west.” She was quiet. “And they’re all here. And none of them’s going anywhere.”

Bee took her hand.

[ 07:44 · Hyvred: the king’s gone east. Kestra with him. ]
[ 07:44 · Hyvred: Petra’s staying. she told him. he didn’t ask. ]
[ 07:45 · Hyvred: twenty-six in the curve. five on the far side. ]
[ 07:45 · Hyvred: and everybody else is here. ]
[ 07:46 · Hyvred: I’m on watch. ]
[ 07:46 · Hyvred: Hild’s on at noon. ]

She shut the window.

Down in the town, in the forge, nobody was making hinges. Agnes was back at her own anvil, the big chair empty beside it, and the sound came up the tower on the morning air the way it had every morning of the winter. Tink. Tink. Tink. Not the king’s hammer. Hers. A little quicker. A little lighter.

It still sounded like somebody was there.

End of Part Three.
