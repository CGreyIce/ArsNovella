When I Get Back from the Grave

6,181 words · 27 min read

He read it on the east wall at dawn, in his merlon, with his back against the cracked coping and the rod across his knees.

He’d read it at midnight too. He’d been in the barracks, on a bunk by the door, not asleep, the way he was never asleep on the night of the tenth; and the whole fort had shivered, a little after a quarter past six, when two halves of a black sword rang an hour east of it on a knoll, and he’d felt the binding go round under everything like a struck bell, and lain there with his eyes open. And then, a long time after, much later, at a little past eleven, he’d felt something else.

He hadn’t known what it was. That was rare. He’d lain on the bunk and felt it go through the binding, all fourteen rings, the way you feel a cart go over a bridge you’re standing under; seven things, all at once, going into one place, and holding. And under them, so faint he’d nearly missed it, a girl at a table two hundred miles west, breathing.

He’d opened his window at eleven minutes past midnight and read three words.

He’d read them a great many times since.

Now it was dawn, and the red going from grey to red to gold all the way to the mountains, and the knoll an hour out with its little green flame on top, and the channel open in front of his face in plain white letters, the same as it had been every morning for three years.

He read all of it. Every line. The way he’d read every line of it for three years from the end of a jetty, at night, not answering.

He didn’t answer.

He sat there in his merlon with the sun coming up over Kharvos and his window open in front of him and read 209 home and frame 56: not yet until the letters stopped meaning anything and were just shapes; and then put his hand over his face, for a while, and didn’t take it away.

He’d known. That was the thing.

Not known. He’d never known; he’d never been able to read a tick any more than Thea could, before last night. He’d never looked. He’d made a rule, in the first winter, sitting on the end of the jetty with the channel scrolling past in a window nobody knew he could see: not mine. The binding wasn’t his. The circle wasn’t his. The way home wasn’t his. He’d come here a different way, thirty-seven years ago, not through any circle; he’d died in his sleep in another world at twenty-six and opened his eyes as a baby in a house behind a field, and nobody had drawn a line in for him, and nobody was going to draw a tick out.

So he hadn’t looked.

But he’d known what Edwy said. He’d read it, in Hana’s lines, a month ago: they’re people going home. And he’d read Thea’s Not yet, and her Some frames are empty, and he’d thought, every night since, sitting on the end of a jetty and then by fires on the road east: please. Not to anybody. Not to God; he’d stopped that at nineteen on a hill in Kharvos with his hand on a black sword. Just: please. Let it be home. Let her be right, the boy with ink on his hands. Let some of them have got there.

And they had. Two hundred and nine of them. Gold going red at the edges, Yan said. Frightened and glad.

And then forty-seven who hadn’t. And thirty-five who hadn’t yet.

He sat with his hand over his face on the east wall at dawn and thought about two hundred and nine people he’d never met, three hundred years and more ago, standing on an arc that was the blank half of a ring and choosing. And going. Out along the line they’d come in by. And arriving, somewhere, in a different key.

Frightened and glad.

He knew exactly what that felt like. He’d felt it at the door of a cabin the winter before last, with five girls from another world coming down a lake road in the snow, and his own voice saying it’s me.

He took his hand away.

The red was gold now, all the way to the mountains. The knoll was green. And at the bottom of the east stair, in the yard, five people were coming in through the east gate from the burial track, slowly, in the early sun: an old man in black with a lantern gone out; two tall men, one of them much taller, walking with their shoulders nearly touching; a boy with silver hair and a white snake round his neck; and in front of them, setting the pace, his daughter, with a black sword on her back.

He watched them cross the yard.

He watched the boy with the silver hair come to the bottom of the east stair, where he’d stopped every morning for a year with his hand on the wall, breathing; and go up it. Thirty-nine steps. To the top. And not stop.

“You sat up,” he said, when Alea came along the wall.

“With her,” said Alea. “And Yan. And Aldous.” She sat down on the walkway beside his merlon with her back to the parapet and Astra across her knees, the way she’d sat beside him on the jetty all her life. “Deston and Xav were on the slope. They didn’t say why. They just stayed.”

“They promised.”

“I know.” She leaned her head back against the stone. Her ears were down. She looked, he thought, the way she’d looked at nine, coming down the lake road in the dawn after a night in Odette’s spare bed, with her face set and her eyes dry, when he’d come home: like somebody who’d done a thing all the way to the end, and was very tired, and would be all right. “Something happened. On the knoll. In the night. Two things.”

He waited.

“Yan held my colour,” said Alea. “I put my hand on his. On the stone. He was grey. And something went out of my hand into his and came back, and he said he’d held it. For nine heartbeats.” She was quiet. “He’s not grey this morning.”

“I saw. On the stair.”

“And then Aldous’s cord went warm. Thea needs your eyes. And Deston stood up.” She turned her head and looked at him. “I couldn’t see anything. I sat there by Mum with Astra and watched Yan and Deston and Xav go very still for a long time, with their eyes shut. And Aldous holding his cord. And then Deston started crying. And then Yan said a lot of colours.” She stopped. “I don’t know what happened.”

“Some went home,” he said.

Alea didn’t say anything for a long time.

“The ones in the frames,” he said. “The ticks. Thea read them. With seven of them. Two hundred and nine people, in the first thirty-seven frames, went home. Along the line they came in by. They chose it, and they went, and they got there.” He looked at the red. “Then nobody. For three hundred years. And then them.”

“Not yet,” said Alea.

He looked at her.

“That’s what Xav said,” said Alea. “On the slope. After. When they opened their eyes. He said not yet to Deston, and Deston said not yet back, and they both laughed, and then Deston was crying again.” She put her hand on Astra’s grip. “Not yet. That’s yours. You say it to everybody.”

“I do.”

“So it’s a door,” said Alea. “Isn’t it. Not yet. You said, the autumn before last, to the king. Severin said you said it. It’s a door left open.”

He didn’t answer that. He didn’t have to. She knew what it was; she’d known what his words meant since she was five, better than he did, mostly.

She leaned against his arm. He let her.

They sat on the east wall in the sun and didn’t say anything, and below them the fort woke up, the way it always woke up, with Pim at the pump and the cook swearing and Hesper’s stove ticking in the infirmary; and in the yard, by the old oak post, a woman with a long plain sword stood still for an hour, and brought it across, and nothing happened, and she looked at the place where nothing had happened with patient attention, like somebody watching a pot.

He went down to Oakes’s forge when the sun was well up.

He always did, the morning after. He’d done it last year, and the year before that, every year since the first: gone down to the forge in the corner of the yard where an old grey smith who’d been at Cinderhold since before the war kept a fire banked day and night and a kettle on the side of it, and sat at the table by the door, the scarred oak one where he’d arm-wrestled Rhea two nights ago and a king had split a sword in his head ten times over, and had a cup of tea.

Oakes’s tea was terrible. It always had been. It was the worst tea in the kingdom; it was made from something the old smith bought by the sack from a carter who came through from Ashby twice a year and swore it was tea, and it wasn’t, and Oakes boiled it to death on the side of the forge in a black iron pot that had never once been washed, on principle, because it’s seasoned. Diwa had drunk it every morning for three years. She’d said it was the only honest thing in the fort.

Oakes put a cup down in front of him without being asked, and went back to the forge, and didn’t say anything. He never did, the morning after.

Ars put both hands round the cup. Close. Against his chest.

Then he took the letter out of his coat.

It was the first.

He’d taken it out of the bundle in the dark at the lake, twenty-five days ago, and wrapped it in oilcloth and carried it east against his ribs with a drawing and a list, and not opened it. He’d said he wouldn’t. One a year, he’d said to a man on the end of a jetty in the autumn, with thirty-eight letters on the boards between them and eleven drafts burning in the lamp. When I get back from the grave.

You’ll be seventy-four, Halvard had said.

Then I’ll have something to do at seventy-four.

He unwrapped it now. Thin rough paper, the kind the Advisor’s office had used ten years ago, before the king’s cream paper came in. His own name on the front, Ars, in a hand he knew: small and very careful, the Advisor’s hand, the hand that had written orders to him for twelve years on the border; except that the pen had been held too tight, and the letters were pressed so hard into the paper he could feel them through the back.

He broke the seal. It was plain wax. No crest. Halvard hadn’t sealed it as the Advisor.

He read it.

> Ars. > > It is the fourth day. > > I have the record of the second night in front of me. I have read it eleven times. The south line was not the attack. I read it as the attack and I sent you to it. I want you to know that I believed it. I want you to know also that it does not matter that I believed it, and that I know that. > > On the third day I barred the east gate for an hour. You know why. I will not write it down here because if I write it down I will have to read it. > > I stood beside you on the wall for the hour. At the eleventh merlon. You did not look at me. I looked at you. I could see your face. I have always been able to; it is the one thing my tier has ever been good for. I have never once wished, until that hour, that I could not. I will not forget it while I live. > > I am not asking you to forgive me. There is nothing in this letter that is asking for anything. I am writing it because if I do not write it to you I will say it to the walls of this room, and the clerks will hear. > > Rowan Hale has written from Wrenhaven by rider. Your daughter is with him and his wife. She is well. She has asked for you every day. He says she sits at the window that looks down the lake road. > > I will not send this. > > H.

He read it twice.

Then he put it down on Oakes’s table, flat, beside the cup, and put his hand on it, and sat there.

He’d known all of it. That was the strange thing.

He’d known the order, and the hour. He’d stood beside the man on the wall for the hour and not looked at him, the way the letter said, and known exactly what the hour was for: that if the gate had been open he’d have gone through it running, across the red, to a rise with fires round it, and arrived running, and spent the whole of whatever there was left of her trying to get her back. And the hour had made him walk. And because he’d walked he’d arrived knowing. And because he’d arrived knowing, she’d got to say goodbye.

He’d told the man that. In the autumn before last, at Diwa’s own table in the infirmary across the yard, in a letter that went west on a rider: I didn’t obey you. I agreed with you. He’d thought that was the end of it. He’d thought, when the man came to the lake in the frost and sat on the end of the jetty with thirty-eight letters and said I have missed you every day for nine years, and he’d said me too, that there was nothing left in the drawer he didn’t know.

There wasn’t. He’d known every word of this.

He just hadn’t known the man had written it on the fourth day.

On the fourth day. With his daughter at a window two hundred miles away asking for him, and his wife’s things still on the shelves of the infirmary with her labels on, and the infirmary wall still open to the red with sacking hung over it; and Halvard, the Advisor to the King, the highest mage in the kingdom, a Comet, sitting at a desk in this fort with a record of the second night that he’d read eleven times, and a pen held so tight he’d nearly gone through the paper. Writing a letter he’d never send. I could see your face.

Rowan Hale has written from Wrenhaven by rider.

He hadn’t known that either. That the man had written to Rowan. That Rowan had written back. That Halvard, on the fourth day, with everything else, had found out where his daughter was and whether she was well.

She sits at the window that looks down the lake road.

He sat at Oakes’s table with his hand flat on the letter and his other hand round the cup, close, against his chest.

Across the yard, through the open forge door, he could see Alea. She’d come down off the wall. She was sitting on the barracks step with Astra across her knees beside Xav, watching Petra in the corner by the post; and Xav said something, and she laughed, and put her hand over her mouth.

She’d sat at the window that looked down the lake road for a month, ten years ago, Odette said. And then he’d come over the hill.

“Bad?” said Oakes.

He hadn’t heard the old man come back. He looked up. Oakes was standing on the other side of the table in his leather apron, with the black iron pot of terrible tea in one hand and his face the way it always was, like an anvil that had been hit a great many times and didn’t mind.

“No,” said Ars.

“You’ve got a face.”

“It’s a good letter,” said Ars. “He wrote it on the fourth day.”

Oakes thought about that. He’d been at Cinderhold on the fourth day. He’d been at the forge, Ars knew, all through the fourth day, and the fifth, and the sixth, mending the hinges on the infirmary door because he couldn’t think what else to do with his hands.

“Who,” said Oakes.

“Halvard.”

Oakes looked at the letter on the table for a while. He couldn’t read it; he’d never learned. He looked at it anyway, at the small careful hand and the letters pressed too hard into the paper.

“He was on the wall,” said Oakes. “For the hour. Old Ansel said. Beside you.” He filled Ars’s cup again without asking, though it wasn’t empty. “I never liked him.”

“I know.”

“Still don’t.” Oakes put the pot down on the side of the forge. “But he stood there.”

“He stood there,” said Ars.

Oakes nodded, once, and went back to his fire.

Ars folded the letter along its old folds. He put it back in its oilcloth. He put it inside his coat, in the inside pocket on the left, against his ribs, where the drawing was, and the list. Three things. They didn’t take up any room.

Thirty-seven left, in a chest under a window at the lake. One a year. He’d be seventy-four.

He thought, sitting there, that he’d like to be. He hadn’t thought that before. He’d thought about it as a thing that would happen or wouldn’t, the way you think about weather. He’d never once, in thirty-seven years in this world, sat at a table and thought I’d like to be seventy-four.

He drank Oakes’s terrible tea. It was the only honest thing in the fort.

Then he took the stub of pencil Higu had given him at the top of the lake road out of his coat, and a scrap of the king’s cream paper from the six he’d kept back from Alea’s, and wrote a note to go west by the commander’s rider in the morning. Not long. He’d never once written a long letter to that man in his life.

Read the first. On the fourth day. Thank you for asking after her. The hens are with Odette. A.

The twelfth dinner was at dusk, in Oakes’s forge, and he wasn’t allowed near the fire.

Salty had been cooking since noon. Ars knew, because he’d tried to go into the forge at noon to put the cup back and been stopped at the door by Wendy, who’d stood in it with her arms folded and her silent headphones round her neck and said no. And at two, by Niko, who’d stood in it with his short sword in his hand, not drawn, just held, and said no very politely. And at four, by Maigo, who hadn’t said anything at all, just stood in the doorway with his hood up and his sunglasses on and his root foot flat on the step and looked at him until he went away.

He’d sat on the barracks step instead, with Alea and Gabe and Deston and Xav, and smelled it.

The whole fort smelled it. The cook complained twice. It came out of the forge door all afternoon and across the yard and up the walls, a smell he hadn’t smelled in thirty-seven years and knew at once, the way you know a song from the first two notes. Hot oil. Garlic. And the peppers. Not the wrong ones. The right ones. Salty’s own, from the plants by the forge wall that had grown from a handful of dried peppers Ars had left behind in a twist of paper in the autumn before last; red and small and wicked, cracking in hot oil, so that everybody in the yard sneezed at once, at about three, and the dogs left.

“It’s oil,” said Gabe, sniffing. “Chilli oil. He’s making chilli oil.”

“He’s been making it all year,” said Yan, who’d come down to sit with them. He wasn’t grey. “He makes it every night. In the skillet. On the second anvil. He’s very particular about the temperature. He drops a bit of bread in to see if it’s hot enough.” He thought about it. “He’s burned four skillets.”

“Five,” said Wendy, from the door.

They ate at Oakes’s table and two more trestles the garrison carried in from the mess, and the bench along the wall, and the anvil, which Niko sat on.

Everyone. Salty had said everyone, in the channel, and he’d meant it. The ten from the lake. Group 3. Aldous, in his chaplain’s black, with his book shut beside his bowl. Rhea, at the end, with her stiff arm on the table. Oakes in his apron, because it was his forge. Hesper, enormous and soft-handed and very old, who’d made up Alea’s bed in the infirmary two years running and had been in the ditch at dawn ten years ago with the stretchers. Pim, with the blue bucket under the bench. Nell from the laundry, who sat very straight and said nothing and ate everything. Jory Platt and Jessop. Teague. The commander, who’d come to the forge door at dusk with his brass glass at his belt and stood there not knowing whether he was invited, until Salty saw him and said sit down, sir, there’s enough, and he sat.

And him.

At the table. On the bench. In a place in the middle, between Alea and Deston, where Salty had put him, physically, with both hands on his shoulders, pushing him down onto the bench as if he were a sack of meal.

“Sit,” said Salty.

“I’m sitting.”

“Stay sitting. You’re not getting up. You’re not standing at the end. You’re not eating out of the pot after.” Salty’s face was red, all of it, from the fire and the afternoon and the rest. “PenguChi said. I read it. In the channel. Every night on the road. I read every night.” He stood over Ars with his big hands still on his shoulders. “This one’s mine. You sit.”

Ars sat.

It was noodles.

That was all it was. Noodles. Salty had made them himself, in the afternoon, on Oakes’s floured table, from flour and water and a pinch of salt and nothing else, and pulled them, badly, so that some of them were as thick as a finger and some as thin as thread and most of them somewhere between, and nobody minded. Boiled in the big pot. Drained. Into the bowls, every bowl, two dozen of them, the fort’s own, tin, dented.

And on top, from the skillet, a spoonful of the oil. Red. Dark red, almost black at the bottom where the peppers had sunk, with the garlic gone gold in it and a sort of shine on the top that caught the forge light. And a fried egg, from the fort’s hens, each one, done in the skillet after, crisp at the edges, the yolk still soft.

That was it. Noodles and chilli oil and an egg.

Salty put the bowl down in front of him himself. Last. After everyone else’s. And stood there.

“It’s what I make at two in the morning,” said Salty. “At home. When I’m up late. On a call.” He wasn’t looking at Ars. He was looking at the bowl. “You used to say. On calls. At two in the morning. Every time. Hold on. Noodles. And you’d go off for ten minutes and come back eating and you’d be eating the whole rest of the call, and we’d all hear it, and Wendy said it was disgusting.”

“It was disgusting,” said Wendy.

“So,” said Salty. “Noodles. At two in the morning. That’s the twelfth.” He was going redder. “I know it’s not two in the morning. I know it’s not a call. But it’s—“ He stopped. “It’s what we’d have eaten. If we’d come. If Wendy and me had come to Malacca, like we said, and you’d taken us to the eleven, all of them, in order, like the list. At the end. At yours. At two in the morning. After the eleventh. You’d have been hungry again.” He breathed out. “I’d have made this. In your kitchen. With the wrong peppers, probably. And you’d have eaten it.”

Nobody said anything.

The whole forge was quiet. Two dozen people round two trestles and a scarred oak table and an anvil, with their bowls in front of them and the steam going up, and the fire banked down in the hearth, and the door open on the yard, and the first stars coming out over the east wall.

“So eat it,” said Salty.

He picked up the spoon.

It was too hot.

He’d known it would be. It was always going to be. Salty didn’t know any other way; Salty had never in his life done anything halfway, not a swing, not a carry, not a pepper. The oil was too hot and the peppers were too many and the garlic was very slightly burnt at the edges, the way it goes when you’ve done it on a forge and not a stove, and the noodles were all different thicknesses so that some were soft and some weren’t. And the egg.

It was perfect.

He ate it. Sitting down. At a table. With a spoon. With two dozen people round him who were all, very carefully, not watching, and all watching; and Alea on one side of him and Deston on the other; and Salty standing at the end of the table, by the place nobody sat in, with his big arms folded and his face red, watching him eat. The way he’d stood at the end of eleven tables. Watching.

He understood, eating it, what that had been like. From the other side.

His eyes were running. It was the peppers. Gabe said so, across the table, loudly, to everybody, it’s the steam, the way he’d said it at the second dinner when the asam pedas made everyone cry; and Deston laughed, and Xav put his hand over his eyes, and RX said a great many things in Malay that the commander was very glad he couldn’t understand. It was the peppers. He ate the whole bowl.

When he’d finished, he put the spoon down in it, and looked up at Salty.

“It’s good,” he said.

“It’s mostly good,” said Salty.

“It’s mostly good,” said Wendy. “The garlic’s burnt.”

“The garlic’s always burnt,” said Ars. “That’s how you know it’s at two in the morning.”

Salty stood at the end of the table and looked at him for a long time.

Then he unfolded his arms, and sat down. In the place at the end. The one nobody sat in. And Wendy pushed a bowl across to him that she’d been keeping, with an egg on it, and he picked up a spoon and ate, very fast, with his head down, the way he always ate, and didn’t look up again for a long time, and his ears were red.

He opened his window under the table.

He didn’t decide to. It was there.

Sixty-five, on the hill above the lake, the morning they left. Sixty-five on the red, at the ditch, with a woman standing in front of him. Sixty-five on the knoll, last night, with four men humming a song in a gap he’d filled without deciding to. Sixty-five on the east wall this morning, reading 209 home.

Sixty-six now. At a table in a forge, on a bench, after a bowl of noodles at two in the morning that wasn’t two in the morning, that a man he’d never met in person in one life had made for him in another with the right peppers and burnt garlic.

He closed it.

Under the table, Alea’s hand found his wrist. She didn’t look at him. She was talking to Deston, across him, about the sea. She just put her hand on his wrist, and kept it there, the way her mother used to under the table at this fort ten years ago when the infirmary ate in the mess, and the cook complained, and it was the only honest thing.

He took the list out at the end.

When the bowls were stacked and the trestles carried back and the commander gone, with a very formal bow to Salty that made Salty go red all over again; when the garrison had gone to their bunks and Oakes had banked the fire for the night and the forge was down to the ten from the lake and Group 3 and Aldous, round Oakes’s table in the last of the light, with the door open on the stars.

He took it out of the inside pocket on the left, from between the drawing and the letter. Higu’s small neat pencil, folded in half along the old crease. He opened it on the scarred oak.

Fish. A tree. Twelve dinners. Gabe. East. Answer them.

Most of it crossed through.

Twelve dinners with eleven ticks after it, neat, like a builder’s marks on a beam, and a space, and a very small drawing of a briar.

He took out Higu’s pencil.

Everybody was watching. He didn’t mind. He’d stopped minding, a while ago, somewhere between a hall in Valcrest with forty thousand paper fishing rods and a knoll with a girl’s hand over a boy’s on a stone.

He made a twelfth tick. In the space. Neat. After the eleven, before the briar.

Then he drew a line, very carefully, through Twelve dinners.

Salty made a sound at the end of the table, very small, that he pretended was a cough.

And then East.

He drew the line through it slowly. All the way. The way he’d drawn it through Gabe on the wall of the cabin with Higu’s pencil through the window, two days before they left; the way he’d drawn it through Answer them on a barge in the autumn, and A tree at the land end of a jetty, at ice-out. East. Twenty-two days. A ditch. A grave. A wall. Seven people reading one line. Two hundred and nine of them, home.

He put the pencil down.

The list lay on Oakes’s table in the lamplight, Higu’s neat pencil and his own lines through it. Every word on it crossed out, now, except one.

At the bottom. In his own hand. Where he’d written it again in the spring, after crossing it off, because he’d thought about it on the end of a jetty and decided it wasn’t the kind of thing you could cross off and be done with.

Fish.

“That one doesn’t come off,” said Alea.

He looked at her.

“Fish,” said Alea. She was looking at the list, with her chin on her fist, the way she’d sat at the end of the forge bench every night of the eleven dinners. “You crossed it off in the spring. And wrote it again. I saw.” She thought about it. “It doesn’t come off. It’s not a thing you finish.”

“No,” he said.

“So there’s always one left.”

“There’s always one left,” he said.

She nodded. As if that settled something. Then she reached across him for the pencil, Higu’s, which she’d never once touched, and pulled the list toward her across the table, and turned it over to the back, where there was nothing; and sat there with the pencil over the blank side of it, not writing anything.

“Next year,” said Alea.

Nobody said anything.

“You need a list,” said Alea. “For next year. Higu’s not here. So.” She didn’t look up. “I can’t write in your letters. I know that. I’ll do it in mine and you can copy it.” She thought for a long time with the pencil over the paper. “What’s on it?”

He looked at the back of the list. At her hand with the pencil in it. At the lamplight on the scarred oak, and the door open on the stars, and twelve people from another world and a chaplain round a forge table on the edge of the red, all of them watching his daughter not write anything on a blank piece of paper.

“I don’t know yet,” he said.

“That’s all right,” said Alea. “I’ll wait.” And she put the pencil down on the paper, very carefully, on the blank back of the list, the way you’d lay a rod across your knees; and left it there.

He sat on the east wall that night, in his merlon, the last time before they went west.

He didn’t read the channel. He’d read it. It would be there in the morning. He sat with his back against the cracked coping and the rod across his knees and three things against his ribs, a letter and a drawing and a list with every word crossed out but one and nothing on the back yet, and looked out at the red under the stars.

The red sang. He couldn’t hear it. Deston said it did. He’d taken Deston’s word for it, a long time ago, on the end of a jetty.

But round the knoll, an hour out, it didn’t. He could feel that, the way he’d always been able to feel the knoll: the green, going down into the riverbeds; and the ash, with every leaf turned in; and beside her, a hand from her foot, a small thing with seven leaves, that she could hear now. And under them, a long way down, under a black stone with his wife’s name on it in letters only he could read and a fox girl knew by their shape, something cold and small and very still, that had lain down further last night than it had ever lain in ten years, and was asleep.

Two hundred miles west, on a lake, a jetty with thirty-four planks, and a lamp on the end of it that somebody lit every night and nobody sat by. And a cabin. And on the wall of the cabin, over the table, over a drawing of a jetty with forty-one planks and an empty nail where a list had been, a long grey-silver blade on two iron pegs, laced with frost, waiting. Very patient about it.

And in a window in the mayor’s house, looking down the lake road, a big red hen on a cushion. Watching the hill.

She’ll see you first, Odette had said. Before anybody.

He thought she probably would.

He sat there until the moon came up, late, over Kharvos, a little more lopsided than last night; and then he got up, and put the rod on his shoulder, and went down the thirty-nine steps, minding the twenty-third, to the barracks, to sleep.

In the morning, they’d go west. Alea would set the pace. He’d carry the water.

And at the end of the road there’d be a lake, and a jetty, and a hen in a window, and two cups on a mayor’s table facing the door, and a list with one word on it that didn’t come off.

Fish.

End of Volume Eight.
