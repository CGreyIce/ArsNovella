Chapter 152

Grey Grass

6,704 words · 29 min read

The green gave out on the fifteenth day, and Wendy watched the girl watch it go.

It didn’t go all at once. Wendy had known it wouldn’t; she’d come this way twice before, once going out in the first autumn and once going the other way at the start of this summer, south to the Lists, and both times she’d watched it happen and hated it. It went the way a person goes grey. A field here and there where the grass was a little thinner than it should be. A hedge with dead wood in it. A farm with its wall rebuilt in two colours of stone, the bottom half black. Then a farm with nobody in it. Then a village where all the men in the inn were old, and missing things, and looked at the summoned on the cart for a long time without saying anything, and looked at the girl with the black sword for longer.

And the grass going. Day by day. From green to tired green, to yellow, to the colour of straw left out in the rain. And then, on the fifteenth morning, the first of it. A single tuft at the side of the road, standing up on its own in a patch of bare earth, a pale dead grey, the colour of ash, the colour of an old man’s beard.

Alea stopped.

Wendy, on the corner of the cart with her bow across her knees, watched her do it. The fox girl stopped in the road and looked at the grey tuft for a long moment, with her ears forward. Then she went down on one knee in the dust with her mother’s half of a black sword on her back, and touched it.

“It’s not dead,” said Alea.

“No,” said Wendy.

“It looks dead.”

“It doesn’t die,” said Wendy. “It doesn’t grow, either. It just stands there. Two years, I’ve been looking at it. The same tufts. In the same places.” She shifted the bow on her knees. “Aldous says it was like this the spring after. And it’s been like this ever since.”

Alea looked at the grass a while longer. She didn’t pull it up. Wendy had half thought she would; it was what you’d do, with a weed. She just touched it, with two fingers, the way you’d touch something to see if it was warm.

Then she stood up and walked on beside her father and didn’t say anything for an hour.

Wendy watched her the whole hour. She couldn’t help it. It was what she did.

Wendy didn’t believe in things.

She’d been like that on Earth. She’d been like it for as long as she could remember. Not in a sad way, she’d always thought, or not mostly. Just in a careful way. You didn’t believe the weather. You didn’t believe the horoscope or the gacha rates or the man in the shop who said it would last a lifetime. You didn’t believe Salty when he said it’s fine, because Salty said it’s fine about everything, including a broken wrist once and including, she was fairly sure, a house fire, if he ever had one. You believed what you could see. And you kept a little bit back, always, just in case, so that when it all went wrong you’d have something left.

It had worked. Mostly. It had worked right up until a phone call in the night, three years ago, about a man she’d known for three years and liked a lot and never once met, and Yan’s voice on the call afterwards, very calm, too calm, and Salty sitting on the edge of their bed in the dark with the phone in his hand not saying anything for an hour.

She’d kept something back from that too. She’d been sad. Of course she had. But mostly she’d been sad for them. For Salty, and for Yan, who’d lost something she’d never had: someone they’d talked to every night for years, who’d been going to meet them at an airport. She’d been sad the way you’re sad at a funeral for somebody else’s father. Properly. But from the next room.

And then a hall in Solmere, and a window in front of her face, and two years on the edge of the red.

And then a man walking across the red toward them with a fishing rod over his shoulder.

And then a star.

She’d believed him. That was the thing. On the red, on the night they set the stone, at the first star, with an arrow on the string, she’d believed him. All the way. Without keeping anything back. And it had come out of her, all of it, everything she’d never once been able to put anywhere, down her arm and into an arrow going up off the Redmarch toward a star, and it had never come down.

She’d been Drawn ever since. That was what the window said. Drawn.

And she’d been finding out, for most of a year now, that you couldn’t put it back. Once it was out it was out. Aura didn’t go back into you once it had come up; Rhea said that, in the yard, about the first tier. It’s out. It’s yours. You’ll never not have it again. Wendy had thought she was talking about aura.

She’d thought about it a great deal since.

She was a cynic who believed in things now. It was the most annoying thing that had ever happened to her.

“What’s it like?” said Alea.

It was the afternoon of the fifteenth day. The cart was going slowly, because the road had got bad, the way roads got bad out here where nobody mended them; and Wendy had got down to walk, because her back hurt from the boards, and found she was walking next to the girl. Her father was a little way ahead, with the buckets, beside Salty. Talking about fish, as far as Wendy could tell. Salty was listening the way he listened to everything, with his whole square face, as if fish were a thing that might be on an exam.

“The red,” said Alea. “Cinderhold. Where you live.” She was looking ahead, up the road. “Maigo says it hurts. Dad doesn’t say anything. Yan says it’s quiet.” She looked at Wendy. “What’s it like?”

Wendy thought about lying.

Not lying. Just doing the thing people did. The soft version. It’s beautiful in its own way. You’ll see. She’d heard people do it her whole life, about hospitals and funerals and countries they’d moved away from. She could feel the shape of the soft version in her mouth, ready to go.

She didn’t. She never had. It was her one thing.

“It’s ugly,” said Wendy.

Alea’s ears went still.

“I’m not going to tell you it’s beautiful,” said Wendy. “It isn’t. It’s flat and red and dead, as far as you can see, all the way to the mountains. Nothing grows on it but this.” She kicked at a grey tuft by the road. “No trees, except the dead ones. No birds. Not even crows. Nothing comes out there, ever, except scouts at night and us in the day. It smells of iron when it rains. And there are markers.”

“Markers?”

“Aldous’s. For the dead.” Wendy looked up the road. “Wooden posts. With names burned into them. In rows. And then the names stop, and it’s just posts. A thousand of them. More. He put up every one. Two years, every day, with a shovel and a book.” She was quiet. “You walk through them to get to the grave. There’s no other way. It takes an hour, and the first half of the hour is markers.”

Alea didn’t say anything.

“And the fort’s grey,” said Wendy. “And it’s cold in the winter and hot in the summer. And the food is terrible. And everybody on the wall is old, or very young, and the old ones don’t talk about it, and the young ones talk about it all the time and don’t know anything.” She breathed. “That’s what it’s like.”

She waited for the girl’s face to do something.

It didn’t. Alea walked beside her up the bad road with her ears forward and her mother’s sword on her back and looked at Wendy for a long time, the way her father looked at things: all of it, the whole thing, at once.

“Thank you,” said Alea.

Wendy hadn’t expected that.

“For what?”

“For not lying.” Alea looked ahead, at her father and Salty and the buckets. “Everyone lies to me about it. Not lying. They make it softer. Rowan used to. Odette. People in town. Even him, sometimes, when I was small. It’s a quiet place. It’s peaceful now.” She was quiet. “I knew it wasn’t. Nobody gets that sad about a peaceful place.”

“No,” said Wendy.

“So thank you.”

Wendy walked for a while.

“Except once,” she said.

Alea looked at her.

“The first winter,” said Wendy. She hadn’t meant to tell her this. “The first frost. It came down in the night, and in the morning the whole of it was white. The red, the grass, the markers. All of it. White, all the way to the mountains, flat and perfect, with the posts standing up out of it like the masts of ships. And the sun came up over Kharvos and turned it pink. And gold.” She looked at the road. “For about a quarter of an hour, it was the most beautiful thing I’ve ever seen. Niko was on the wall. He said the same. And then the red came back through, and it was ugly again.” She shrugged. “So. Once.”

Alea walked beside her for a long time, with her ears very still.

“Once is something,” she said.

“Yeah,” said Wendy. “That’s what I thought.”

She told him that night, at the stream.

She’d gone down for the water because it was her turn for supper and she wanted it for the pot, and he was there already, of course, filling the buckets, because Aldous had said water every morning for fifteen days and he’d taken it as a sacred trust. He was crouched on a flat stone at the edge of a brown little stream with a bucket in the current and his rod lying on the bank behind him.

She crouched on the next stone and put her pot in.

“I told her about the markers,” said Wendy.

He didn’t look up. “I know. She told me.”

“Somebody had to.”

“Yes.”

“You weren’t going to.”

He was quiet for a moment. The water ran into the bucket.

“No,” he said.

“Why?”

He lifted the bucket out of the stream and set it on the stone beside him, very carefully, so it didn’t slop. He looked at it.

“Because I didn’t know how,” he said. “I’ve tried. For a month. On the riverbank in Valcrest and every night since.” He was quiet. “Every time, I’d get to the markers, and I’d see her face, and I’d make it softer. I couldn’t help it. I’ve been making it softer for her since she was nine.” He put the second bucket in the stream. “You didn’t.”

“I never do.”

“I know,” he said. “I remember.”

Wendy looked at him sideways.

“You were in the call,” he said. “Most nights. You’d come in late, after Salty, and say hi and then nothing for an hour. And then somebody would say something stupid, usually me, and you’d say exactly what everyone was thinking and nobody would say.” The bucket filled. “And then go back to not saying anything. I used to wait for it. The whole squad did.” He lifted the bucket out. “It was the best part of the call.”

Wendy found she didn’t have anything to say to that.

She hadn’t known he’d noticed. She’d been the one in the call who wasn’t one of the four. Who’d come in through Yan and stayed for Salty and liked the man with the stupid jokes from the next room, properly, but from the next room. She hadn’t thought he’d noticed she was there at all, most nights. She’d been fairly sure he hadn’t.

“Thank you,” he said. “For telling her.”

“Somebody had to,” said Wendy again, but it came out different this time, and she picked up her pot and went back to the fire before he could see her face do anything.

She couldn’t sleep, that night.

She lay under the blanket with Salty, at the edge of the firelight, with his arm under her neck and his crooked ribs against her back and his breathing very slow and very loud in her ear, the way it had been every night for years, in two worlds, and looked at the dark.

There wasn’t any moon. It had been going down for a week, past full and then lopsided and then thinner, a little less every night, and tonight it wasn’t there at all. Just stars. More stars than Wendy had ever seen on Earth, in a city, under the orange sky. She’d seen this many every night for two years and she still wasn’t used to it.

She thought about the trip.

She’d had a spreadsheet. That was the stupid thing. On Earth. She’d made a spreadsheet, the way she made spreadsheets for everything, because if you wrote a thing down in boxes it couldn’t go wrong without you seeing it go wrong. Flights. Dates. Things he said he’d take us to. There’d been a column for the eleven dinners. Salty had found it and laughed for ten minutes and then put in three more restaurants he wanted, in his own column, in capitals.

They’d been looking at dates. Real ones. They’d been a month off booking.

And then the phone call, and she’d closed the spreadsheet and never opened it again. She hadn’t deleted it. She wasn’t sure why. It was probably still there, on a laptop on a desk in a flat in another world, three years later, with Salty’s three restaurants in capitals at the bottom of the column. The trip that never happened.

She looked across the fire.

He was sitting up with the first watch, where he always sat, at the edge of the light, with the rod across his knees, looking east. Alea was asleep beside him with her head on his coat. The fire had burned down and lit him from below, the way fires do.

She couldn’t see his face. Nobody could. Your eyes went to it and slid off, like rain off a window, and came back and slid off again; she’d stopped trying on a road outside Valcrest, the day he said yes. It wasn’t the face from the camera anyway. It never would be. It was a stranger’s face in a body born in another world.

But she didn’t need the face. He was sitting there with the rod across his knees, and every so often he’d turn his head and look down at his daughter asleep on his coat. And the turn of his head was exactly the way he’d turn it on the camera, when somebody in the call said something that made him laugh and he was trying not to.

Wendy lay very still under the blanket.

Oh, she thought.

It was happening. The trip. It was happening right now. She was on it. She’d been on it for fifteen days.

They’d been going to fly out and see him. Salty and Yan and her. They’d been going to sleep on his floor and eat eleven dinners and go to the places in the column. And they never had, and he’d died, and it had been three years for them and thirty-four for him, and they’d ended up in another world on the edge of a red plain. And now they were walking home across a kingdom with him. To their place. To the fort. To show him where they lived.

They’d been supposed to visit him.

He was visiting them.

She turned over, very carefully, under Salty’s arm, so that she was facing him. He was asleep. He was always asleep before she was. She put her hand flat on his chest, over the crooked ribs, and felt it go up and down.

“Salty,” she said, very quietly.

“Mm.”

“It’s the trip.”

He didn’t open his eyes. He was quiet for so long she thought he’d gone back to sleep.

“Yeah,” said Salty.

“You knew.”

“Since Valcrest.” His voice was thick with sleep. “The road outside. He said yes. I thought, oh, it’s the trip.” He was quiet again. “Didn’t say. Thought you’d get there.”

“It’s thirty-four years late.”

“For him.”

“For him,” Wendy agreed.

Salty’s arm tightened, very slightly, round her. He still didn’t open his eyes.

“Eleven dinners,” he said. “At the fort.” And then, after a while, so quietly she nearly didn’t hear it: “He’s cooking.”

Wendy put her face against his shoulder and laughed, without any sound at all, for a long time, until she wasn’t sure any more that it was laughing.

She read the channel on the seventeenth night, by the fire, because Yan was asleep and somebody ought to.

Somewhere a long way north-west, near a lake, five girls in a militia hall at the top of a road read that, Wendy supposed. And somewhere north, a big man with a sword called Sto on his back. And somewhere south, in a city full of paper fishing rods, a woman with a sword who’d been in a ditch.

Nobody answered. Nobody needed to.

They came to the old line on the twentieth day.

Wendy knew it before she saw it. You couldn’t come to Cinderhold from the west without crossing it; they’d crossed it going out, and they’d crossed it coming back, and every time Aldous had gone quiet on the box and made his two-fingered sign under his sleeve and not said anything. It was where the red came round.

Most of the red was east of the fort. That was where the battle had been, from the ridge to the far trees, the whole long rust-coloured plain going away to the mountains. But not all of it. On the south side, below the fort’s hill, a long arm of it came round, as if the plain had reached out one hand in the night, nine years ago, and laid it across the land to the west of the walls and never taken it back. A band of red earth a few hundred paces wide, going away south and west across the green, with grey grass standing up out of it. And the road went across it. There was no other way.

And in the middle of the band, running across the road from south to north, a long low trench, grassed over with grey. Overgrown. Half filled in. You’d have taken it for an old field drain, if you didn’t know.

The horse stopped at the edge.

It always did. Two years, and it had never once walked onto the red without stopping first. It stood at the edge of the green with its ears back and its nostrils going, and its front feet planted, and wouldn’t move. Rhea swore at it. Maigo leaned forward from the baggage and talked to it, very softly, the way he did. It didn’t help. It never did.

And Maigo stopped talking.

That was the other thing. Every time. Wendy looked up at him, on top of the baggage, and saw it happen. His face changing under the pixel glasses. The leaves on his antlers going still. His bare root-toed foot, hanging over the side of the cart, drawing up, away from the ground, the way you’d draw your foot back out of cold water.

“It hurts,” said Maigo. Very quietly. Not to anyone.

He always said it. And then he’d say nothing at all, all the way across, the only time in the day he ever stopped talking, and Wendy hated it more than she hated anything else about the red. Not the markers. Not the no birds. That. Maigo, quiet.

Alea had stopped too. She was standing beside the cart at the edge of the green, looking at the red. At the long low trench across the road. At the grey grass standing up out of the rust-coloured earth, rattling.

It always rattled. That was the sound of the red. Wendy had never told anyone, because it sounded stupid, but it was the thing she’d hated first and hated longest. The grey grass didn’t move like grass. It didn’t bend in the wind and come back. It was too stiff for that. It shivered, all of it, all the time, even when there was no wind at all that you could feel, and it made a sound like whispering. A long dry endless hush, going out across the red in every direction as far as you could hear. Like somebody in another room telling somebody else to be quiet, over and over, forever.

Alea stood at the edge and listened to it with her ears, and Wendy watched her listen to it.

And then her father put the buckets down.

He didn’t say anything. He set the two buckets down very carefully on the green at the edge of the road, so they didn’t slop, and slid the yoke off his shoulders and laid it beside them. Then he walked past the horse.

Onto the red.

Wendy saw it. She’d seen it once before, a year ago, from the middle of a broken ring of fire with fifty grey shapes round her and an arrow on the string, and she hadn’t known what she was seeing then. She’d been too frightened. She’d thought it was the fear. Things go quiet when you’re frightened; everyone knows that.

She wasn’t frightened now.

He walked onto the red, and the grass went still.

Not all of it. Not the whole plain. Just round him. Round his feet, as he walked, in a ring a few paces across: the grey grass stopped shivering and stood still, the way grass stands still on a day with no wind. And the sound stopped. The hush. She heard it stop. In a ring round a man walking across the red with no rod and no buckets and his hands at his sides, the whispering stopped, and there was nothing. Just the ordinary quiet of a road. His boots on the earth.

He walked to the middle of the road. To the edge of the long low trench. And stopped there, and looked down at it.

“Oh,” said Maigo.

Wendy turned her head. Maigo was sitting up on the baggage very straight, with his sunglasses pushed up on his antlers and his white eyes wide.

“It stopped,” said Maigo.

“What stopped?”

“The hurting.” His voice was very small. “Where he’s standing. It’s—“ He put his bare foot down. Over the side of the cart, onto the edge of the red, very slowly, the root-toes spreading out on the earth. “It’s quiet. Round him. It’s like—“ He swallowed. “It’s like when you’ve been crying for ages and somebody you know comes in. And you stop. Not because you’re better. Because they’re there.”

Nobody said anything.

The horse put its ears forward.

It looked at the man standing in the middle of the road on the red, by the trench, in his ring of still grass. It looked at him for a long moment. Then it walked onto the red. On its own. Without anybody pushing. Slowly, the way horses walk into water, and the cart came after it, and Rhea, behind the cart, stopped pushing and just walked, and stared.

Maigo started talking again. To the horse. Very quietly. Good boy. Good boy. That’s it. You see. You see.

Alea walked onto the red after them.

She did it without stopping. Wendy watched her feet. One step off the green, onto the rust-coloured earth, and then another. Toward her father. Into the ring of quiet. And Wendy saw her ears go back, just slightly, at the first step, when the grass outside the ring was still whispering all round her; and then come forward again when she reached him, and the whispering stopped.

She stood beside him at the edge of the trench and looked down at it.

“What is it?” said Alea.

“A ditch,” said her father.

He didn’t say anything else for a while. Wendy, at the side of the cart, didn’t move. Didn’t breathe, very nearly.

“There was a line here,” he said. “The south line. The second night. They came at it in the dark, and the line held, mostly, except here. Here it broke. And there was a soldier in this ditch, a girl of twenty-three, with her arm broken and a sword in her other hand, holding it. On her own. With the line gone on both sides of her.” He looked at the trench. “I came across that way. From the fort. And got to her.”

“Petra,” said Alea.

“Petra.”

Wendy knew the name. Everyone did. The woman with the sword who’d come up the Wren with Group 6, who’d stood on the jetty with them, who’d been at the Lists. Hello, girl from the ditch. She’d never known it was a real ditch. She’d thought it was a way of talking.

It was this one.

Alea looked at the trench for a long time. Then at her father. She didn’t ask anything else. Wendy saw her not ask. She saw the question go across the girl’s face and stop there, and saw her decide, and close it, the way you’d close a door on a room you weren’t ready to go into.

Her father picked a single tuft of grey grass from the edge of the ditch, very carefully, roots and all, and looked at it in his hand. Still. Quiet. Not shivering. Then he put it back where it had been, and pressed the red earth round it with his thumb.

“Come on,” he said. “Fort’s tonight.”

He walked back across the red to the buckets. The ring of quiet went with him. And behind him, as he went, Wendy heard the whispering come back. Not all at once. Slowly. The way the sound comes back into a room after somebody important has left it.

“It does that,” said Wendy.

She didn’t know why she said it. They were walking again, on the green, on the far side of the band of red, with the fort somewhere ahead over the last long rise and the sun going down behind them. Alea was beside her. Her father was ahead with the buckets, the yoke across his shoulders, and the water in them, Wendy noticed, very steady.

Alea looked at her.

“The red,” said Wendy. “It does that. Wherever he walks. It goes quiet.” She looked ahead at his back. “It did it last year, when he came across to us. I thought it was me. Being frightened. I thought I’d imagined it.” She shook her head. “Maigo says the ground knows him. Yan says he doesn’t know what it is. Aldous won’t say anything, which means he’s got an idea and it frightens him.” She was quiet. “The red goes quiet wherever he walks.”

She heard herself say it. She heard how it sounded.

On Earth she’d have laughed at anybody who said a thing like that. She’d have laughed at it in the call. The ground knows him. The red goes quiet. She’d have said something dry and quick, the thing everyone was thinking and nobody would say, and the call would have laughed, and he’d have laughed loudest.

She didn’t laugh. She’d seen it. Twice now. With her own eyes, which were the only things she’d ever believed.

She believed it.

Oh, for God’s sake, thought Wendy.

“Why?” said Alea.

“I don’t know,” said Wendy. “Nobody knows.” She looked at the girl beside her. “Maybe you’ll find out. You’re the one who’s going to stand on it with him.”

They saw Cinderhold at dusk.

They came up the last long rise in the road, the one Wendy had been waiting for since noon, with the sun going down red behind them and the shadows going out long in front of them across the grey grass. The cart first, slowly, with Rhea pushing and Maigo talking and Niko on the tailboard with his eyes shut, at eleven again, and Wendy counting without thinking about it. Then Salty and Wendy. Then Yan, with the scythe. And at the back, a man with two buckets and a girl with a sword.

And at the top of the rise, there it was.

A fort on a low hill, a mile off, on the edge of the world. Grey stone. Square, squat, ugly, with a square tower at each corner and a long wall between them, the stone gone dark with age. Lamps lit along the top of the west wall, very small, like a row of sparks. Smoke going straight up from the kitchen chimney into the still evening air.

And beyond it, on the far side, the red.

You couldn’t see it from the west, not really. Just the edge of it, going away past the fort on both sides, very flat, toward the mountains. The mountains of Kharvos, black against the last of the light, a long ragged line all along the edge of the sky. And between the fort and the mountains, the whole of it, all the way, going dark. Rust and grey and dark, under the first stars.

Somewhere out there, an hour’s walk past the east wall, was a cairn of children’s stones under half of a black sword. And a young ash tree in a ring of flowers. And a white stone at the head, that Wendy had put there herself, in the snow.

Alea stopped at the top of the rise.

Wendy stopped beside her. She didn’t mean to. She just found she had.

The girl stood on the road in the dusk with her mother’s sword on her back and looked at the fort. Her ears went flat. All the way flat, against her red-gold hair, the way Wendy had seen them go once before, at the edge of a ring in the Lists, in round five, before she’d gone in.

And her hands came together in front of her. Palm to palm, at the level of her chest, the way you’d hold them to pray.

Wendy didn’t know what that meant. She didn’t need to. She saw the girl’s lips move, just slightly, three times. And then stop.

And then Alea let her hands fall, and her ears came up, slowly, and she looked at the fort, all of it, the whole thing, at once.

“That’s where she worked,” she said.

“Yes,” said her father, behind her.

“Which wall?”

He was quiet for a moment.

“The east one,” he said. “You can’t see it from here.”

Alea nodded. She didn’t ask anything else.

“We’ll stop here,” said Aldous.

He’d stopped the cart at the top of the rise. He sat on the box with his book shut on his knee, looking at the fort, with his old chaplain’s coat buttoned to the neck.

Rhea stared at him.

“Here?” she said. “It’s a mile. I can see the kitchen chimney. I can see my bed.”

“We’ll go in in the morning,” said Aldous.

“Why?”

Aldous didn’t answer. He looked past her, at the back of the cart. At the man with the buckets, who’d stopped at the top of the rise with his daughter and was standing there very still, looking at the fort.

Wendy thought it was superstition. That was Aldous all over. Two fingers under the sleeve; white cloth over the dead; don’t go into a fort at night. She opened her mouth to say something dry about it.

“He’s right,” said the man with the buckets, quietly.

Wendy shut her mouth.

He didn’t explain. He set the buckets down at the side of the road, very carefully, and slid the yoke off, and stood looking at the fort with the lamps along its wall. And Wendy, who saw things first, who’d always seen things first, saw that he wasn’t looking at the west wall, with its lamps. He was looking past it. At where the east wall would be, on the far side, where you couldn’t see.

The morning after, she thought, and didn’t know where the thought came from. He came back in the morning.

She didn’t say anything. For once in her life she didn’t say the thing everyone was thinking. She went to help Salty with the fire.

The patrol came out at full dark.

Wendy heard the gate first. A long way off, the creak of it, carrying in the still air. Then hooves. Then a lantern, coming up the road from the fort toward their fire, swinging, with two more behind it. Three riders. The fort had seen a fire on the rise and sent somebody to look at it, the way it always did. Two years, and Wendy had been on that patrol herself more times than she could count.

Rhea stood up and went out to meet them, shouting, because Rhea always shouted. And the lantern stopped, and somebody on the front horse shouted back, Rhea? RHEA?, and laughed, and then there was a lot of noise.

Wendy knew the voice. Everyone at Cinderhold knew it.

Sergeant Teague rode up into the firelight with the lantern in one hand and his reins in the other. Old and grey and lean as a post, with the long white scar down the inside of his right arm from the elbow to the wrist, where a thing had opened it on the red a year ago. He looked round the fire. At Aldous, getting up. At Salty and Wendy and Maigo, waving, and Niko and Yan. At the cart.

At the man at the edge of the firelight, sitting on the grass with a rod across his knees.

Teague went very still on his horse.

He didn’t say anything. He got down. Slowly, the way an old man gets down off a horse, with his bad arm held against his side. He gave the reins to the boy behind him without looking at the boy. And he stood there, at the edge of the light, in his old patched coat, looking at the man on the grass.

The man looked up at him.

“Sergeant,” he said. “How’s the arm?”

Teague’s face did something.

“Holding, sir,” he said.

“Good.”

Teague didn’t move. He stood with his lantern hanging from his good hand, and Wendy saw his eyes go to the man’s face, and slide off, the way everybody’s did, and come back, and slide off again. And then stop trying. And stay on the rest of him instead: the shoulders, the hands, the rod. As if he didn’t need the face. As if he’d known the man by his shoulders for a long time, from a long way off, from the top of a wall.

Then they moved. To the girl sitting on the grass beside the man. With a black sword lying in the grass beside her, and her red-gold hair tied up, and her ears up, looking at him.

Teague went white.

Wendy saw it happen, in the firelight. All the colour going out of his old lined face at once, the way it goes out of a man who’s seen something he wasn’t ready for. He looked at Alea’s face. At her ears. At her eyes. For a long, long moment.

“She’s got her face,” said Teague.

It came out of him very quietly. Not to anyone. The way Maigo had said it hurts.

“She has,” said the man on the grass.

Teague stood there a while longer. Then he did a thing Wendy had never seen him do, in two years. He took his cap off. An old soldier’s cap, the cloth gone shiny with wear. He held it in his good hand, against his chest, and looked at the girl on the grass.

“She stitched me up,” he said. “Twice. The first year. Your mother.” His voice wasn’t steady. “She had cold hands. She’d warm them on the lamp before she touched you. Every time. Even with a line of men out the tent and the drums going.” He swallowed. “I never thanked her. You didn’t, in them days. You just went back out.” He looked at Alea. “So. Thank you, miss.”

Alea didn’t say anything. She couldn’t, Wendy thought. She sat on the grass and looked up at the old sergeant with his cap against his chest, and her ears had gone very still, and she nodded. Once.

Teague put his cap back on. He looked at the man one more time.

“Morning?” he said.

“Morning,” said the man.

Teague nodded, slowly, as if that were the answer to something he’d been asking for nine years. Then he got back on his horse, with the boy holding it, and turned it, and rode back down the road toward the fort with the lantern swinging and the other two behind him. Slowly. Not looking back.

The lamps came on along the wall an hour later.

Wendy saw it from the fire. She was lying under the blanket with Salty, not sleeping, looking at the fort a mile off in the dark. The lamps along the west wall had been lit since dusk, a row of small sparks. And then, at some point, more. One at a time. All along the top of the wall, and then on the towers, and then, faintly, round the far side, where the east wall would be. Lamp after lamp after lamp, going on in the dark, the way lamps go on in a house when somebody’s come back late and the word’s gone round.

The whole of Cinderhold, waking up.

She didn’t know what it meant. She thought she might know in the morning.

She looked across the fire. The man was sitting up with the first watch, at the edge of the light, with his rod across his knees. He wasn’t looking at the lamps. He was looking at the ground in front of him. The way, Wendy thought, he must have looked at the ground the last time he’d walked toward that fort. As if there were nothing else to look at.

And beside him, wide awake, sitting up with her knees pulled up and her chin on them and her mother’s sword in the grass at her side, his daughter was looking at the lamps. All of them. One after another. As they came on.

She didn’t look away all night. Wendy knew, because Wendy didn’t either.
