The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 3: The Anniversary

Chapter 66

No Charm

4,645 words · 20 min read

Hanarisu learned names.

It was the first thing she’d done in the summoning hall, before she’d even properly stood up: looked round at thirty-four frightened faces and started learning which ones went with which voices. It was the first thing she’d done in the castle, too. Every guard on every door. Every clerk in the archive. Every lord on the Council, and his steward, and his steward’s boy. The woman who brought up the hot water in the mornings, whose name was Ama, and who had a grandson in the river watch. The old man who swept the long gallery, whose name was Pellam, and who’d been sweeping it since before the king was king.

She knew all of them now. Hundreds. She’d written them down at first, in a little book, the way she’d once kept a list of regulars on stream. Then she’d stopped needing the book.

A name was a door. That was what she’d learned, in a year at court. You said someone’s name, the right one, the one they used, and something in their face opened, just a crack. Ama’s did, every morning. Pellam’s did. Even Lord Vane’s had, once, at his own banquet, before he remembered who she was.

And her charm went through doors. That was the trouble. That was the thing she’d learned about herself in a year at court, and didn’t like. She’d say someone’s name, and their face would open, just a crack, and she’d feel it rise in her, warm and sweet and easy, the charm, wanting to go through.

She never let it. Not since the Keeper of the archive, in the third week, when she hadn’t known what she was doing. Not once.

There was one man in the castle whose name she’d never learned. He’d never given it. Nobody used it. In a year, she’d never heard anybody call him anything but the Advisor.

She’d thought about that a great deal.

They read Group 3’s lines in their rooms above the archive, the five of them, in the morning.

Hana read them twice. Then she closed her window and sat very still on the end of the bed with her tail wrapped round her ankles and her hands in her lap, and didn’t say anything, and let the others say it.

“Diwa,” said Cursiffe. He’d already got his ledger out. The one with all thirty-five names down the side and the columns across, that he’d kept since the letters started going missing. He found a clean page at the back and wrote it at the top, carefully, in his small square hand. Diwa. A healer. “Somebody should write it down,” he said. “Properly. Not just in the channel. The channel forgets.”

“He took the sword back,” said Milli. He was standing at the window with his arms folded and his tail very still, which meant he was thinking hard. “Out of Salty’s hands. On the anniversary. On the Redmarch.” He turned round. “That’s three things. He’s alive. He’s strong enough to kill three of those things on his own. And he’s close enough to the Redmarch to walk there.” He paused. “We didn’t know any of that yesterday.”

“We knew he was alive,” said Aleaka quietly.

“We believed he was alive.”

“I knew,” said Aleaka. “I knew his voice.”

Milli looked at her for a moment. Then he unfolded his arms. “Yes,” he said. “Sorry, Leaka. You did.”

Jared hadn’t said anything. He was sitting on the floor by the door with his back against the wall and his long legs out in front of him, the way he sat when he was guarding a room without wanting anyone to notice he was guarding it. He had his window open. He was reading the channel, which was still going, line after line, faster than anyone could keep up.

“Tiger wants someone to ask the Advisor,” he said.

Hana looked up.

“SOMEONE ASK THE ADVISOR,” said Jared, reading. “In capitals. Then Rachael. Can someone in the capital ask? Then Kei. Then Newish.” He scrolled. “Then Gabe. hana. you’re there. please.” He closed the window and looked at her across the room. “Gabe wrote please, Alice.”

Nobody else in this world called her that. Only Jared. Only Jared, now.

Hana sat on the end of the bed for a long moment.

“All right,” she said.

“You could just charm him,” said Milli.

He said it that evening, when the others had gone down to supper and it was only the two of them in the room. He didn’t say it the way Lord Vane’s people said it, in corridors, when they thought she couldn’t hear: the cat girl, the one who gets inside your head. He said it the way he said everything. Calmly. Laying it out. Looking at it from every side.

“You could,” he said. “You could sit down across from him and say his name—“

“I don’t know his name.”

“—say Advisor, then, and let it come up, and he’d tell you everything. Where Ice is. Everything. And then Gabe would know where to go, and Deston, and all of us.” He sat down beside her on the bed. “I’m not saying do it, Honey. I’m saying you could.”

“I know I could.” She looked at her hands. “I don’t even know if I could. He’s Comet. It might go off him like rain off a roof.” She was quiet for a moment. “But I know I won’t.”

“Why?”

She thought about it. Honestly. Milli always made her think about things honestly; that was what he was for.

“Because it isn’t mine,” she said at last. “Where he is. It isn’t mine, and it isn’t the Advisor’s either, really. It’s his.” She turned her hands over. “If I charm it out of him, I’ve taken it. From both of them. And then I’ve got it, and I didn’t ask, and nobody let me.” She looked up at Milli. “I said it at Vane’s banquet. I won’t use it on anyone who can’t tell it’s happening. I meant it then. I’m not going to stop meaning it the first time it’s hard.”

Milli looked at her for a long time.

“I married you for a reason,” he said.

“You married me because I’m cute.”

“That was one of the reasons.” He took her hand. “Ask him plainly, then. With your mouth. No charm.”

“With my mouth,” Hana agreed.

She asked Oswin first. That was the proper way. Oswin was their guide; Oswin reported to the Advisor.

Oswin went very red, and then very pale, and then said, “I’ll ask,” in a strangled voice, and went off down the archive stairs at something close to a run. He came back an hour later, even paler.

“Five days,” he said. “At dusk. In his study.” He swallowed. “He said, I wondered which of them it would be. I’m glad it’s her.”

Hana sat with that for a while.

She took Aleaka and Milli. Not all five. She thought about all five, and Jared, of all people, talked her out of it, sitting on the floor by the door with his long legs out.

“Three of us is a question,” he said. “Five of us is a delegation. He’s been ambushed by delegations his whole life.” He looked at her with his cool level eyes. “Take Leaka, so you’ll know if he’s lying. Take Milli, so Milli hears it himself and doesn’t have to take your word for it.” The corner of his mouth went up. “No offence, Milli.”

“None taken,” said Milli. “He’s right.”

“I’m always right,” said Jared. “Trust.”

Cursiffe threw a cushion at him. It was the first time any of them had laughed all day.

The Advisor’s study was at the top of the west tower, up a stair so narrow Hana’s tail brushed both walls.

She’d never been in it. Nobody she knew had, except Oswin, once. It was smaller than she’d expected. A plain square room with a plain desk, and shelves of books on three walls, and a window on the fourth looking west, where the sun was going down red over the roofs of Solmere. A lamp on the desk, already lit. A fire in the grate, low. A chair behind the desk, and three in front of it, waiting.

He was standing at the window when they came in. He turned round.

Hana had seen him perhaps a dozen times in a year. In the summoning hall. At the Council. Crossing the courtyard with Kestra, or the king. She’d never been this close to him, in a small room, in lamplight. He was older than she’d thought. Grey all through his beard, and lines round his eyes that went very deep, and a stoop to his shoulders that he hid, mostly, in public, and wasn’t hiding now. Hyvred had said, the first day, that his light was nearly out. That he’d poured it all into the circle. Hana couldn’t see light the way Hyvred could. But looking at him now, in his own room, at the end of the day, she thought she understood what Hyvred had meant.

“Hanarisu,” he said. “Aleaka. Millianna.” He’d learned their names, too. She noticed. “Please. Sit.”

They sat. He sat behind the desk, and folded his hands on it, and waited.

Hana felt the charm rise.

It always did, when she was frightened. It came up in her warm and sweet and easy, the way it had in the Council chamber, the way it had at Vane’s banquet, the way it had a hundred times in a year. Wanting to go through. Just say it. Just let it out a little. He’s tired. He’s sad. He’d never even notice.

She let it rise. All the way up, until she could feel it at the back of her throat.

Then she let it go back down. Slowly. The way you’d lower something heavy. All the way down, until it was gone.

The Advisor was watching her face. She saw him see it. Something moved in his tired eyes, and was gone.

“Thank you,” he said quietly.

“I’m going to ask you plainly,” said Hana. “With no charm. I want you to know that.”

“I know,” said the Advisor. “I watched you not do it.” He paused. “Kestra wrote down the first time, at Vane’s banquet. She drew her own line tonight. The king read it. So did I.” He looked at her. “Ask.”

Hana breathed out.

“Is the Hero from our world?” she said. “Is he our friend?”

The Advisor didn’t answer for a long time.

He looked down at his hands, folded on the desk. Then at the window, where the red was going out of the sky. Then at the desk again. At a drawer, Hana noticed. The second drawer on the left. He didn’t open it. He just looked at it.

“I’ve known him,” he said at last, “for nearly twenty years. Since he was younger than any of you. A swordsman at a border garrison, with nothing to his name but a borrowed sword and a very bad temper.” Something that wasn’t quite a smile. “He was the first man I ever knew who had a window. He showed me how to look at mine. Every window in this kingdom, every native who can see one, comes back to that afternoon. To him, sitting on a wall at a garrison, saying, here, let me show you how to look.”

He was quiet for a moment.

“In twenty years,” said the Advisor, “I never once asked him where he came from.”

Hana didn’t say anything. Beside her, Aleaka had gone very still.

“I wondered,” he said. “Of course I wondered. A boy who’d had a window since he was born, when nobody else in the world had one. Who called the parts of it by words nobody had ever heard. Status. Inventory. Skills.” He said them in that accented English, the way he had in the summoning hall, the first day. “I wondered where a commoner’s son had learned words like that. And I never asked. Not once.” He looked up at Hana. “I think I was afraid of the answer. I think I knew, somewhere, that if I asked, he’d tell me the truth. And then I’d have to know it.”

“So I can’t tell you where he’s from,” said the Advisor. “I never asked. That’s my failure, not yours.” He unfolded his hands and laid them flat on the desk. “But I can tell you what I know. And I will. All of it that’s mine to give.”

He held up one finger.

“He’s alive. And he’s well. Better than he’s been in eight years, I think.” A pause. “He took his sword back from your friends on the Redmarch, on the anniversary. Aldous’s report came this morning, by the fast courier. He sent word through Aldous: Tell Halvard I’ve taken it.” Something moved in the Advisor’s face, very briefly. “That’s the most he’s said to me since the battle. Four words, through someone else.”

Halvard, thought Hana. She held on to it. She didn’t say it. It wasn’t hers either.

A second finger.

“He knows you’re here. All thirty-five of you. He’s known since the summoning.” The Advisor’s voice was very even. “When I told him, a year ago, that I’d brought thirty-five people through the floor, he didn’t ask who they were.” He looked at her. “Not one question. Not who, not where from, not what are they like. Nothing. A man who didn’t know you would have asked a great many questions.”

Hana felt Aleaka’s hand find hers under the edge of the desk, and hold on.

A third finger.

“And when I asked him, this summer, what he wanted me to do about you,” said the Advisor, “I’d read your sword by then. The carving. Your scholar knelt in front of it and read it, and his face changed. So I wrote to him. I told him you’d read it. I asked him what he wanted done.” He reached into his coat, and took out a scrap of paper, folded small, soft and grey with handling, and laid it on the desk between them, and unfolded it.

It was market paper. Cheap and coarse, the kind you’d wrap fish in. Hana could smell it from her chair: woodsmoke, and something cold and green, like lake water.

There were two words on it, in a square careful hand.

Let them.

Nobody said anything.

Hana looked at the two words on the market paper for a long time. She couldn’t read the script. None of them could; the window translated speech and print, but handwriting slid off her eyes the way English slid off the natives’. But she didn’t need to read it. She’d heard him say it. She knew exactly what it said.

“Let them,” said Milli, very quietly. “Let us what?”

“I’ve asked myself that every day since,” said the Advisor. “I thought, at first, he meant: let them be. Leave them alone. Don’t hold them back.” He looked down at the paper. “I’ve come to think he meant more than that. More than I wanted him to.” He was quiet for a moment. “I think he meant: let them look. Let them find what they find. Let them work it out.” He looked up. “So I’ve stopped standing in your way.”

“Where is he?” said Milli.

He said it plainly. Not rudely. Just plainly, the way Milli said everything that mattered. Hana didn’t stop him. It was the question all thirty-five of them had been asking for a week, in capitals and lowercase and Malcolm’s IFs, and it was Milli’s right to ask it as much as anyone’s.

The Advisor looked at him for a long time.

“That isn’t mine to give,” he said. “I’m sorry. It’s his.” He folded the market paper up again, very carefully, along its old creases. “He gave you let them. He hasn’t given you that. Not yet.” He put the paper back in his coat. “When he does, you won’t need me to tell you.”

Aleaka spoke for the first time.

“You miss him,” she said.

The Advisor looked at her, startled.

Aleaka’s eyes were wet. She was holding Hana’s hand very tightly under the desk. She was looking at him the way she looked at the castle cats, and at Brisk, and at the biting grey horse that let nobody else near it: steadily, without blinking, feeling for what was there.

“You’re carrying something,” said Aleaka slowly. She stumbled over it a little, the way she did when she was saying something she’d only just found. “Something you wrote. A lot of things. And never gave him.” She swallowed. “It’s very heavy. I’m sorry. I can feel it from here.”

The Advisor didn’t say anything for a long time.

His hand, on the desk, had moved toward the second drawer on the left. It stopped before it got there.

“Yes,” he said at last. Very quietly. “I miss him.”

He stood up. They stood up too.

“There’s one more thing,” said the Advisor. “It isn’t for you. But you should see it.” He went to the door of the study and opened it. “Oswin.”

Oswin was on the stair. Hana didn’t know how long he’d been there. Long enough, by his face. He came in looking as if he expected to be sent straight back down again, and stood just inside the door, red to the ears, holding his hat.

“You’ve asked me three times,” said the Advisor. “In a year. Why a summoning would catch thirty-five people who all knew each other, when no summoning in four hundred years had caught more than three, and never people who knew each other. Three times, you’ve asked.” He looked at Oswin steadily. “And three times I told you to leave it.”

“Yes, sir,” said Oswin miserably.

The Advisor reached into his coat again, the other side. He took out a key. It was old, and black, and as long as Hana’s hand, with a bit like a little iron castle on the end of it. He held it out.

“The sealed records,” he said. “The summoning archive. Four hundred years of it. The king’s seal is on the door, and mine. Nobody’s been in since I went in, before the summoning.” He held the key out a little further. “Don’t leave it, Oswin. Not anymore.”

Oswin stared at the key.

Then he took it. His hand was shaking so badly that the key rattled against his ring.

“Sir,” he said. “Sir, I—“

“Go on,” said the Advisor gently. “You’ve been wanting to since the day you met them. Go and read.”

They walked back down through the castle in the dark, the three of them, not saying anything.

Along the long gallery, where Pellam had swept the floor that afternoon and it still smelled of wet stone. Down the archive stairs. Past the guard on the door, whose name was Hew, and who had a sister in Kingsferry, and who nodded to them. Hana nodded back, and said his name, and didn’t feel anything rise.

Halfway down the last stair, Milli stopped.

“He knows us,” he said.

Hana stopped too. So did Aleaka.

“That’s what he was saying,” said Milli. He was standing on the step above them, with the lamp from the archive door lighting half his face, and his tail had gone completely still. “He didn’t ask who they were. A man who didn’t know you would have asked a great many questions.” He breathed out. “He was telling us. As much as he could. Without breaking whatever he promised.”

“Yes,” said Hana.

“I said it couldn’t be him.” Milli’s voice was very calm, the way it went when it was hardest. “For a year. In the channel. I said it didn’t fit. I said the timeline couldn’t work. I said a man who’d been here twenty years couldn’t be someone who died three years ago.” He looked at the wall. “It fits. I said so, the night of the sum. But I don’t think I believed it. Not really. Not in here.” He touched his chest. “I believe it now.”

Hana went up a step and put her arms round him.

He let her. For a long time. On the archive stairs, in the dark, with Aleaka standing below them very quietly crying, for once not trying to hide it.

“He didn’t ask who we were,” said Aleaka, to nobody. “He knew.”

Hana wrote it in the channel that night.

Not all of it. Not Halvard. Not the drawer. Not the key. Those weren’t hers either. She wrote what the Advisor had given them, plainly, the way he’d given it, and nothing else.

[ Hanarisu: We asked the Advisor. Me and Milli and Leaka. No charm.
Hanarisu: He said the Hero is alive and well. Better than he’s been in years.
Hanarisu: He said the Hero knows we’re here. All 35. He’s known since the summoning.
Hanarisu: He said when he told the Hero about us, the Hero didn’t ask who we were.
Hanarisu: Not one question.
Hanarisu: He said when he asked the Hero what to do about us, the Hero wrote back two words. Let them.
Hanarisu: He said where the Hero is isn’t his to give. It’s the Hero’s.
Hanarisu: I’m sorry, Gabe. That’s everything. ]

The channel was quiet for a long time.

Then:

Gabe: ok

And then, after a while, from somewhere in Thornwall:

[ Tiger: let them
Tiger: thats the most ice thing ive ever heard
Tiger: two words. no explanation. let them.
Drew: Trust. ]

Hana laughed. She didn’t mean to. She sat on the end of the bed with her window open and laughed until she cried, and Milli held her, and didn’t ask why.

Kestra came to their rooms three nights later, after supper, which she never did.

She stood in the doorway in her plain dark coat, with her sword on her hip, and looked at the five of them one after another, the way she did before a drill.

“I’m going away,” she said. “Three weeks. Perhaps more.”

“Where?” said Jared.

“Away.”

“Who’s going to say again to me?” said Milli.

Kestra looked at him. Then at Jared.

“Again,” said Jared, to Milli.

“There,” said Kestra. “Done.” She turned to go, and then didn’t. She stood there in the doorway a moment longer, and looked at Hana.

“You didn’t use it,” she said. “With the Advisor.”

“No.”

“I’ll be writing that down.”

“I know,” said Hana.

Kestra nodded once. “The king will read it,” she said. And then, after a pause that was very long for Kestra: “He reads all of yours twice.”

She went.

Hana saw them leave.

She hadn’t meant to. She’d woken before dawn, the way she did sometimes, with her tail twitching and her head full of names, and couldn’t get back to sleep. So she’d got up and gone out along the long gallery to the end, where the big window looked down over the north courtyard and the north gate of the castle, and stood there in the grey half-light, watching the city wake up.

There was somebody else at the window already.

Oswin. Still in yesterday’s clothes, with ink on his fingers and dust in his hair and the black key on a string round his neck, and his eyes red-rimmed and enormous. He looked as if he hadn’t slept at all. He hadn’t.

“Hana,” he said, without surprise.

“Have you been in all night?”

“Every night since the Advisor gave me the key.” He rubbed his face with an inky hand. “There are four hundred years in there. I’ve read nine.” He laughed, a little wildly. “Nine years. In four nights. Every summoning they ever tried. Every medium they ever used.” He stopped. “I’ll tell you. When I know what I’m telling you. I promise.”

“I know you will.”

They stood at the window together, and looked down.

There were four horses in the north courtyard. Two to ride, two to carry. No banners. No guard of honour. No heralds, no trumpets, no Council lords in their furs come out to see them off. Just four horses, and a groom holding them, and two people in plain dark travelling coats.

One of them was Kestra.

The other was the king.

Hana knew him by the way he stood. She’d only seen him three times in a year, and never without the room going underwater around him, the Ascendant’s pressure that made it hard to breathe. She couldn’t feel it from up here. But she knew the shoulders. The plain coat, like the one he’d worn in the summoning hall. The stillness.

He wasn’t wearing a crown. He wasn’t wearing anything a king would wear. He looked like a big grey-haired man in his sixties setting off on a long ride north on a cold morning.

As they watched, he went to the second of the pack horses, and lifted something off the cobbles, and tied it on behind the saddle himself, carefully, the way you tie something you care about. A long roll of dark leather, heavy, bound with straps. As he settled it, the end of the roll fell open a little, and Hana saw the handles. Wooden, dark with age and polished with use. Three of them, four. Sticking out of the end of the roll the way handles stick out of a carpenter’s bag.

Hammers, she thought.

“He’s taking his hammers,” said Oswin softly, beside her.

Hana looked at him.

“He was a smith,” said Oswin. “Before he was king. Before the war. In a river town in the south. Plough blades and horseshoes.” He watched the king tighten the last strap. “Nobody remembers. The songs never mention it. I only know because I’m the archivist, and it’s in a ledger somewhere, in a clerk’s hand, forty years old.” He was quiet for a moment. “He still goes down to the castle forge at night, sometimes. Sends the smith to bed.”

Below them, the king swung up into the saddle. Kestra mounted beside him. The groom stepped back. The north gate opened.

“Where’s he going?” said Hana.

Oswin didn’t answer straight away.

“North,” he said at last.

They watched the four horses go out through the north gate of the castle, and down through the waking city, small and quiet and unremarked, and out through the north gate of Solmere onto the north road, and away.

“The books come from the north,” said Oswin. Very softly. To nobody. “Lake country.”

Hana didn’t say anything.

She stood at the window for a long time after they’d gone, with the city waking up below her and the cold dawn on her face. She could have followed. She could have asked a groom, or a guard on the north gate, or Pellam, who knew everything, and said their names, and let the charm come up just a crack, and found out exactly where a king was riding with a roll of hammers and no crown.

She didn’t.

It isn’t mine, she thought. It’s his. And then, after a while, standing there with her tail wrapped round her ankles in the cold: He’ll give it. When he’s ready. He always did.

She went back down the gallery to wake the others. On the way, she passed Pellam, coming the other way with his broom.

“Morning, Pellam,” said Hana.

The old man’s face opened, just a crack, the way it always did.

She let it.
