The Banner

7,275 words · 32 min read

Who taught you to stand there?

Kestra had said it to Jared in the third week, in the castle courtyard at Solmere, in the rain. He hadn’t known her name yet. He’d known she was the guard, and that she’d been in the king’s own guard before that, and that she looked at people’s feet before she looked at their faces. She’d put a practice sword in his hands and one in Milli’s and stepped back and watched them for a long time without saying anything. Then she’d come over to Jared and looked at his feet, and at where he’d put himself, half a pace in front of Milli and a little to the left, between Milli and the door, without thinking about it.

Who taught you to stand there?

And he hadn’t had an answer. Nobody had taught him. He just always had. In games, on Earth, every night for years: he was the one who went in front. The one his friends stood behind. It wasn’t a decision. It was just where he went. Like water going downhill.

He’d said nobody, in the end.

Kestra had looked at him for a moment longer. Then she’d said good, and gone to drill Milli, and said again to him forty times, and never mentioned it again.

He’d thought about it every day for two years.

Because it was a lie. Somebody had taught him. Not on purpose; nobody taught you that on purpose. But he’d learned it somewhere, the way you learn anything you do every night for years. In voice. In the dark. From a guy in Malaysia who said I would never betray you in this game, trust, and then went in first through every door anyway, every single time, and died loudly, narrating it, so the rest of them could see where the enemy was.

He’d learned to stand in front from a man who’d always stood in front of him. Half a world away. On a screen.

And then one day the man had stopped. And Jared had been half a world away, and there had been absolutely nothing he could do.

“It’s a hill,” said Gigichi.

“It’s a mound,” said Kei.

“It’s a hill with ideas,” said Gigichi.

It was a mound. Lucan’s carpenters had built it in the night, in the middle of the great sand of the Lists, out of earth carted in from the market gardens and packed down hard and turfed over, so that it rose out of the raked sand like a green island: chest-high, maybe twenty paces across, with gentle sides and a flat top. On the top, a pole. On the pole, a banner. White, with a black kettle on it, because Calvin had been up at dawn with Bee and a pot of paint and nobody had been able to stop him.

The sun was past noon and very hot. The whole bowl of the Lists was full again, forty thousand people, more, after the morning’s Fledgling rounds and an hour of eating everything the sellers at the gates could carry. They’d been told the rules. Lucan had shouted them from the master’s platform in his best voice: seven to hold the mound, six to take it, the banner off its pole and the hold is over. Sand-glasses, a quarter-hour each, turned by a marshal in the royal box where everyone could see. Most glasses wins.

And the six.

The guards were standing at the far end of the sand, under the west stand, in a loose row. Not the guides. The guides were in the stands, watching, with notebooks. The guards. The ones who’d stood behind the thirty-five for two years with swords on their hips while the guides taught them to read their windows. Six of them, because Hild was holding a mountain pass five hundred miles away and had sent a letter that said only don’t lose.

Bram, Group 1’s, big and grey and grinning, with a wooden axe. Sabine, Group 4’s, tired and sharp, with two short wooden blades. Petra, Group 6’s, very straight, with a wooden sword, looking at them the way she looked at everything. Rhea, Group 3’s, practically vibrating, with a wooden greatsword nearly as long as she was. Ysolde, Group 7’s, with her curled ram’s horns and a wooden longsword held loosely in one hand like a walking stick.

And Kestra. Group 2’s. With a plain wooden practice sword, the same one she’d used in the courtyard in the rain for two years, standing very still in the middle of the row, looking at Jared’s feet.

“No aura,” Drew had said that morning, at the long table, to the six of them. “That’s the rule. You fight with your hands. Wood, not steel. No aura, not a drop. The marshals will be watching. If any of you so much as draws a breath of it, the hold goes to the squad.” He’d looked round them all. “You’re Reaching and Gleaming and you’ve been fighting for twenty years. They’re two years in and most of them are Initiate. Even without aura, you’re going to take that banner off them in about a minute. I want to know how long it takes.”

“How long do you think?” Ysolde had said.

Drew had smiled the smile that was his whole job.

“Longer than you think,” he’d said.

Squad A had drawn first.

That was the luck of the helmet. Wil had pulled the slip at breakfast, standing on a bench at the Broken Lance with the helmet on his head, and Maren had read it out: the Kettle. And Calvin had stood up with his kettle and cheered, alone, and everybody else in Squad A had looked at each other.

Seven of them. Gigichi, from Wrenhaven, gruff and small and practical, with a gold pocket watch in her fist. Jared, from the capital. Wendy, from Cinderhold, Drawn, with a bow and a quiver of blunted arrows with padded heads. Calvin, from Kingsferry, Spark, with his kettle tied to his belt like a shield. Kei, from Thornwall, with Hisui round his neck. Azac, from Duskhollow, the biggest of the thirty-five after Gabe, in a borrowed leather jerkin that didn’t fit. Hako, from here, with his spear.

Two of them had tiers. Wendy and Calvin. The other five were Initiate. Jared was Initiate. He’d been Initiate for two years. His window had said ninety-something percent for six weeks, and he’d stopped looking at it.

They’d had one night to work out how to hold a hill with seven people who’d never stood next to each other.

“Nobody calls,” Gigichi had said, at the long table, last night, before the Lists. Very firmly. “That’s the first thing. I’ve seen the Committee. That’s what happens when everyone calls.” She’d pointed at Kei. “He calls. Only him.”

“Why me?” Kei had said.

“Because you’re calm and everyone trusts you,” Gigichi had said, “and your snake can tell when they’re going to move.” She’d looked round at the rest of them. “And because I’m not doing it. I’m doing the time.” She’d held up the watch. “Somebody has to know how long we’ve got.”

Nobody had argued with her. Jared had found, in the year and a half he’d watched Gigichi in the channel, that people mostly didn’t.

“In position,” said the marshal.

They went up the mound.

Azac first, because he was biggest, planting himself at the front of the flat top facing west, with his feet wide. He didn’t have barriers yet. His window said he was a barrier mage on the mage path, and his window hadn’t let him cast one in two years. So he was the barrier. He’d been the barrier since Ice died; he’d taken the tank role in every game after, because somebody had to, because Ice had always done it. He stood at the front of the mound in his borrowed jerkin and looked at the six guards a hundred yards away and said, in his big cheerful voice, to nobody:

“And here we are, folks, the Kettle takes the hill, they’re looking nervous, they’re looking sweaty, the big man at the front has never been more scared in his life—“

“Azac,” said Gigichi.

“Sorry. I narrate. It helps.”

Hako on the left with the spear, low, the long reach of it covering the slope. Wendy on the very top, by the pole, with her bow and her silent headphones round her neck. Calvin on the right, with the wind already stirring round his boots. Kei in the middle, slightly back, with his head tipped, listening to the snake. Gigichi at the foot of the pole, with one hand round it and the watch in the other.

And Jared.

He’d thought about where to stand all night. He’d lain awake in the king’s house on the hill, with Cursiffe breathing in the next bed and Hana’s half-heart warm two doors along, and thought about it. Where. In front of Azac? Beside him? At the pole?

In the end he hadn’t decided. He’d just gone up the mound and stopped where his feet stopped him. Half a pace in front of the pole. A little to the left. Between Gigichi and the far end of the sand.

He looked down at his feet. Then up, at the stands.

Fourth tier. East. The end of a bench.

He couldn’t see him from here. He couldn’t see his face from anywhere; nobody could. But he knew where he was, the way you know where a light is in a dark room. A man with a rod across his knees and two bees on his head, three hundred feet away. Close.

He’d never been this close. Not once. Not in all those years of playing every night. Half a world. A screen.

Three hundred feet.

“Turn the glass,” said Lucan.

The first glass went faster than any glass Jared had ever seen in his life.

The six came across the sand at a walk. Then a jog. Then, a long stone’s throw out, all at once, at a run, spreading as they came, the way a net spreads when you throw it, so that they hit the mound from six sides at the same moment.

It should have been over in a minute. Drew had said so. Jared had believed him.

It wasn’t.

“Left,” said Kei. Very calm. “Two. Rhea and Bram. Now.”

Hisui had told him. Jared didn’t know how; he’d never know how; but a green snake round a calm man’s neck had tasted something on the hot air, and Kei had said left a breath before Rhea and Bram came round the left side of the mound together, and Hako was already there. The spear came round in that long, patient, cheerful arc, low, at their shins, and Rhea jumped it, laughing, and Bram didn’t, and went down on the turf like a tree.

“THAT’S IT,” shouted Azac. “BRAM’S DOWN, BRAM IS DOWN, THE OLD MAN GOES OVER—“

“I’m not down,” said Bram, from the turf. “I’m resting.”

“Right,” said Kei. “Sabine. And Petra behind her. Now.”

Calvin wrote Tailwind in the air with his finger, the way he did, and a gust came off him across the right-hand slope like a door slamming in a storm, full of sand. Sabine put her arm over her eyes and swore. Petra didn’t stop at all. She came through the sand with her eyes shut, straight up the slope, the way she’d come through Alea’s guard on a wet verge on the road south, and Wendy put a padded arrow into her chest from the top of the mound, thock, hard enough to knock her back a step.

Petra opened her eyes and looked up at Wendy.

“That’s a dead arrow,” said Wendy. “Back to the line.”

Petra looked at her for a moment longer. Then she grinned, the first time Jared had ever seen her grin, and went back down the slope to the sand, and touched the chalk line at the bottom, and came back up again.

“That’s legal,” shouted Lucan, from the platform, delighted. “Back to the line and she’s live again!”

“How long,” said Kei.

Gigichi looked at her watch. “Not long enough.”

Ysolde didn’t come at all, in the first glass.

She stood at the foot of the mound, on the west side, with her wooden longsword held loosely in one hand like a walking stick, and watched. She watched Hako. She watched Calvin’s wind. She watched Kei, with his head tipped. She watched Jared.

And Kestra stood beside her, and did the same.

Jared knew what they were doing. He’d watched Kestra do it in the courtyard for two years. You didn’t come in until you’d seen where everybody stood. Feet first. Then hands. Then faces. And when you’d seen it all, you went exactly where they weren’t.

“They’re reading us,” said Jared quietly.

“I know,” said Kei. “Hisui says they taste like they’re waiting.”

“What does waiting taste like?”

Kei listened.

“He says like a cat,” said Kei. “Outside a hole.”

The second glass, they lost Calvin.

Rhea got round the back of the mound while Sabine and Petra came up the front, and Calvin turned to put the wind on Rhea and she just went through it, laughing, a wooden greatsword nearly as long as she was coming round in a flat arc that caught Calvin across the kettle tied to his belt with a noise like a gong. Calvin sat down on the turf. The marshal’s flag went up. Out.

“NO,” shouted Azac. “NOT THE KETTLE—“

“The kettle’s fine,” said Calvin, from the turf, examining it. “There’s a dent. It’s fine. It’s got character now.”

“Go,” said Gigichi.

He went, down the slope with his dented kettle, to the out-line, where Lucan’s marshals were sitting on a bench with jugs of water. He waved at the crowd as he went. The crowd roared. They’d decided they loved the man with the kettle.

They lost Hako in the third glass.

Not to Rhea. To Ysolde. She’d stood at the foot of the mound for two whole glasses, watching, with her longsword like a walking stick, and then in the third, without any warning that Jared could see, she went. Straight up the left-hand slope, at Hako, not fast, not slow, with her wooden sword held low. And Hako did what he always did, what Ice had taught him in eight months on Earth and the man with the rod had been teaching him all week on wet sand at dawn: he waited. The spear held. Low. Patient.

And Ysolde stopped.

Just for a breath. Right at the edge of his reach. The way a heron stops.

Hako moved.

He moved early, the way he’d been told. While it’s still deciding to. The spear came round in its long arc, earlier than it ever had, at the exact place Ysolde should have been.

She wasn’t there. She’d been deciding to be there. And then she’d decided not to, a hair before he decided to move, and the spear went through empty air, and her wooden sword came up under it and touched Hako very lightly on the chest.

“Out,” said Ysolde.

Hako looked down at the wooden blade on his chest. Then up at her. His face lit up like a lamp.

“How did you—“

“Twenty years,” said Ysolde. “And he’s teaching you the right thing. You’re just not there yet.” She took the sword away. “Ask him again tomorrow.”

Hako went down the slope beaming as if he’d won.

That left five. Then, in the middle of the third glass, four, when Sabine got Kei from behind while he was listening to the snake.

Kei went down on the turf on one knee and Sabine’s wooden blade touched his shoulder, and he looked up at her and laughed, quietly, the way he did. “Hisui says sorry,” he said. “He was looking at the other snake.”

Suzu was in the stands. On the scythe-shaft. On the fourth tier of the east stand, two benches along from the end. Jared hadn’t realised. Hisui had been looking at her for a glass and a half.

“Tell him it’s fine,” said Sabine.

“He says it’s not fine, he’s very embarrassed, and he’d like everyone to stop looking at him,” said Kei, and went down the slope.

Nobody called after that.

Jared found he didn’t need anyone to. He could see them coming. He’d been watching the guards come up the mound for three glasses, and he’d started to see what Kei’s snake had been tasting. Not in the air. In their feet. Rhea’s feet went quick and light before she came. Bram’s went heavy. Petra’s didn’t change at all, which meant she was always about to come. Sabine’s went still. Feet first, Kestra had said, in the rain, two years ago. Then hands. Then faces.

He’d thought she was teaching him to fight. She’d been teaching him to read.

“Left, Wendy,” he said.

Wendy turned left at the top of the mound and put a padded arrow into Rhea’s shoulder as she came over the lip. Thock. Rhea swore and went back to the line.

“Azac, she’s going right.”

Azac turned right and put his whole body in front of Sabine like a door shutting. Sabine bounced off him. Azac said “OOF,” and then, recovering, “and the big man holds, folks, he holds—“

“How long,” said Jared.

“Three glasses,” said Gigichi. “Three and a bit.” She was still at the foot of the pole, with one hand round it and her watch in the other, and she hadn’t moved once. Her voice was very calm. Too calm. Jared knew that voice. It was the voice of somebody who was absolutely terrified and had decided not to let anybody see. “Fourth glass is turning.”

Three glasses.

Drew had said a minute.

He heard the stands, then. He hadn’t, before. He’d been on the mound too long, and he’d stopped hearing anything but Kei and Gigichi and the guards’ feet on the turf. Now it came up all at once, all round the bowl of the Lists, forty thousand people on their feet. Roaring. For the Kettle. For a squad of strangers holding a hill with a banner on it that had a kettle painted on it in black, for no reason anyone in the stands could understand.

They didn’t care. They didn’t need to understand. Three glasses. They’d watched the guards come up the mound twenty times and get turned back twenty times by a big man narrating and a girl with headphones and a man with crimson hair calling out feet.

Three glasses, said somebody up on the master’s platform. Jared heard it, faintly, through the noise. Lucan. Three glasses, they’ve held three glasses, nobody’s ever—

And Drew’s voice, very calm: “I said longer than you think.”

They lost Azac at the start of the fourth glass. They lost Wendy halfway through.

Azac went to all four of them at once, Rhea and Bram and Sabine and Petra coming up the front of the mound together in a line, the way you’d bring a battering ram up to a door. He held for a breath. Two. He narrated it. And the big man’s under pressure, he’s under pressure, he’s under—and then he was on the turf with four wooden weapons touching him, laughing so hard he couldn’t get up.

“Out,” said four guards at once.

“I KNOW,” said Azac, delighted, and rolled down the slope.

Wendy went to Ysolde. She’d been on the top of the mound by the pole the whole time, with her bow, putting padded arrows into anyone who came over the lip, and she’d put one into Ysolde’s chest at twenty paces and Ysolde had kept coming, because it had glanced off a fold of her tunic. The marshal hadn’t called it. Wendy had lowered her bow and looked at the marshal with the face of a woman who’d been cynical about the world for twenty-eight years and was being proved right again, and Ysolde had touched her very lightly on the arm with the wooden sword.

“Out,” said Ysolde. “Sorry. It was a good arrow.”

“It was a dead arrow.”

“It was,” agreed Ysolde. “Your marshal’s blind.”

Wendy went down the slope. Halfway, she turned round and looked back up at the top of the mound. At the two people left on it.

Gigichi, at the pole, with the watch.

Jared, half a pace in front of her. A little to the left.

There were six of them at the foot of the mound now. All six guards. Bram and Sabine and Petra and Rhea on the slopes, breathing hard, grinning. Ysolde at the bottom on the west side. And Kestra beside her.

Kestra hadn’t come up the mound once. Not in four glasses. She’d stood at the foot of it the whole time with her plain wooden practice sword, watching. Feet, then hands, then faces.

She came up now.

On her own. The other five stopped where they were on the slopes and let her through, without anyone telling them to. Jared saw them do it. He saw Rhea open her mouth and then shut it. He saw Ysolde, at the bottom, lower her sword and lean on it, the way you lean on a fence to watch something.

Kestra came up the west side of the mound at a walk. Not fast. Not slow. With her practice sword held low. And stopped on the flat top, five paces from Jared.

She looked at his feet.

“Gigichi,” said Jared, without turning round. “How long.”

“Four glasses,” said Gigichi behind him. Her voice had gone very small. “Nearly. The marshal’s looking at it. It’s—there’s a bit left. A little bit.”

“How little?”

“I don’t know. It’s sand. It doesn’t tick.” He heard her breathing. “Jared. I can’t—I’m not a fighter. I’m not anything. I can’t cast. I’ve been Initiate for two years. I’m holding a pole.”

“I know,” said Jared.

“She’ll take it off me in a second.”

“I know.”

“Then what do I do?”

Jared looked at Kestra. Kestra looked at his feet.

“Hold the pole,” said Jared. “I’ve got the rest.”

Kestra came in.

He’d seen her do it a thousand times. In the courtyard at Solmere, in the rain, every morning for two years, to Milli and to him. He’d seen her come in at Milli forty times in a row and say again every time. He knew exactly what she was going to do. He’d known it for two years.

He didn’t move.

He stood half a pace in front of the pole, a little to the left, between Gigichi and the sword, with his blunted sword held low in both hands and his feet exactly where they’d stopped him when he came up the mound, and watched her come.

She came at him, not at Gigichi. She didn’t have to. She could have gone round him. Any one of the six could have gone round him in a breath and taken the banner off the pole out of Gigichi’s shaking hands. She came at him. Straight. The way she’d come at him every morning for two years. As if there were nothing on the mound except a man in a place he’d chosen to stand, and the only question was whether he’d stay there.

Her wooden sword came round.

He took it on his blade.

It hit like a door. He’d known it would. He’d been hit by Kestra’s practice sword two hundred times and it always felt like being hit by a door. His arms shook. His feet slid back on the turf, a hand’s width, and stopped. He didn’t step back. He didn’t step aside. He stood where he’d chosen to stand, with the pole and a girl with a gold watch behind him, and held.

She hit him again.

He held.

“Who taught you to stand there?” said Kestra.

Quietly. Not to the crowd. To him. The same five words she’d said in a courtyard in the rain in the third week, with a practice sword, before she knew his name.

Jared looked past her.

Three hundred feet. Fourth tier. The end of a bench. A man with a rod across his knees and two bees on his head, sitting very still, in a crowd that couldn’t see him. Close. The closest Jared had ever been to him in his life, except for one riverbank, and a long table, and a gate square where he’d turned his face to the wall.

“He did,” said Jared.

Kestra’s sword stopped.

“He did,” said Jared again. His voice came out rough. “I told you nobody. In the rain. It wasn’t nobody. It was him. Every night. For years. In front. First through the door. Dying so we could see where they were.” He breathed. “I learned it off him. Half a world away. And then he stopped, and I couldn’t do anything, and I was half a world away, and he was gone, and I never once got to stand in front of him.”

Kestra didn’t move.

“He’s right there,” said Jared. “He’s three hundred feet away. He’s never been that close. Not once. Not ever.” He tightened his hands on the sword. “I’m not moving.”

Something happened in his hands.

He felt it start. A warmth. Down in the bones of his wrists, where the shock of Kestra’s sword had gone in. Not heat. Something under heat. Something that had been sitting there for two years, under the ninety-something percent he’d stopped looking at, waiting. Like a held breath. Like a man standing in a doorway who has finally decided which way to go.

It came up.

It came up out of his hands and into the sword, and out of the sword, and round him, very faint, like the shimmer over a road in summer, and then it came up out of his feet into the turf of the mound, and he felt himself stand. Not the way he’d been standing. The other way. The way a post stands when it’s been driven in, and the ground has closed round it, and the post has stopped being a thing somebody put there and has become part of the ground.

Kestra felt it. He saw her feel it. She’d been Reaching for ten years; she knew what it was. She stepped back. One step. Her wooden sword came down.

His window opened.

He didn’t open it. It opened on its own, the way they all said it did, the first time. Right in front of his face, in the hot sun on top of a mound in the middle of the Lists, in front of forty thousand people who couldn’t see it.

He looked at it.

Behind him, Gigichi made a sound.

“Jared,” she said. “Jared. Your—you’re—”

“I know,” said Jared.

“TIME,” roared the marshal in the royal box.

The sand had run out of the glass. Jared didn’t see it. He heard it: the marshal’s voice, and then Lucan’s, from the platform, cracking straight down the middle, FOUR GLASSES, FOUR GLASSES AND THE KETTLE HOLDS, and then the noise.

The noise of the Lists.

He’d heard it from the inside of the tunnel that morning, and from the mound for four glasses, and he’d thought he knew what it sounded like. He hadn’t. It came down on him all at once from every side, forty thousand people on their feet in a bowl of pale stone in the afternoon sun, screaming, stamping, holding up paper horns and paper kettles and paper fishing rods, for seven strangers who’d held a hill for an hour with a banner on it that had a kettle painted on it. For no reason they could understand. Because the squad of strangers had stood next to each other and not moved.

Kestra was still standing on the mound in front of him, with her wooden sword lowered.

She looked at his feet. Then his hands. Then his face.

“Good,” said Kestra.

That was all. One word. The one she’d said in the rain in the third week.

Then she did something she had never done, not once, in two years of mornings in a courtyard. She put her wooden sword under her arm and held out her hand.

Jared looked at it.

He took it. Her grip was very hard. She held on a moment longer than a handshake, and looked at him, and he saw in her face the thing he’d only ever seen in it once, in the dusk on a riverbank, when Hana’s wrist went warm.

“Perhaps anyway,” said Kestra, very quietly, so that only he heard it. And let go, and turned round, and walked down the mound to the sand, where the other five were waiting for her.

Gigichi hadn’t let go of the pole.

She was standing at the foot of it with both arms round it now, the watch still in one fist, the banner with its painted black kettle hanging limp above her in the still hot air. She was shaking. Her face was doing what Jared had seen it do in the channel, in the winter, the night a lamp got shoved at somebody at a door in the snow: furious, and wet, and refusing to admit to either.

“I held a pole,” said Gigichi.

“You held the pole,” said Jared.

“I didn’t do anything. I stood here. For an hour. With a watch.” She glared at him. “You did everything.”

“I stood in front of you,” said Jared. “That’s all. Somebody had to hold the pole, or there’s nothing to stand in front of.” He looked at her. “That’s how it works. You need both.”

Gigichi looked at him for a long moment. Then she let go of the pole with one arm, and punched him in the shoulder, hard, and then put the arm round his neck and held on, and said into his collar, very gruffly, “You okay?”

It was the thing she said. Jared knew it from the channel. She’d been saying it to everybody for two years and nobody ever answered it properly.

“Yeah,” said Jared. “I’m okay.” He breathed. “I’m actually okay.”

The other four squads went after them, through the rest of the long hot afternoon.

Jared watched them from the out-line bench with the rest of the Kettle, with a jug of water, with his window still open in front of his face because he couldn’t make himself close it.

The Stronghold held three glasses and a half. Salty stood at the pole like a tree with roots, and Gabe stood in front of him like a wall, and Tiger ran round the whole mound in circles the entire time so fast the guards couldn’t decide where he was, and PenguChi stood very calmly beside the banner saying steady in a voice that made the guards hesitate without knowing why. They lost the banner to Rhea, in the end, who came over the top of Salty like a girl climbing a tree, and took it off the pole, and held it up over her head, and screamed.

Wolf held three. Bee drew a chalk dog on the turf at the foot of the mound before the glass turned, and it barked at every guard who came near, and Malcolm had a contingency for everything except the dog, which he hadn’t planned for and which saved them twice. Rachael patched everyone who went down, very fast, telling them off the whole time, and sent them back up the slope before the marshal could decide they were out. That’s not legal, said Bram. Show me where it says that, said Rachael.

Except Newish held two and a half. Drew left the master’s chair to Lucan and went up the mound with his bladed knuckles wrapped in cloth, and Newish stood at the pole and slowed every guard who came up the slope by half a beat, so that they looked, from the stands, like people walking through deep water, and the crowd loved it, and Newish ragebaited Rhea so effectively that Rhea forgot the rules and used a drop of aura and the marshal gave the squad an extra half-glass for it, and Rhea went and sat down on the sand with her face in her hands.

The Committee held one glass and a quarter.

All seven of them called. All at once. From the first breath. Thea called left, and Xav called hold, and Hyvred called they’re going right, they’re going right, I can SEE them going right, and Deston called something gentle and reasonable that nobody heard, and Hana called it smoothly and very persuasively and differently, and Chris logged everybody’s calls out loud as they happened, and Yan stood at the pole with his scythe and Suzu round his neck and said okay about four seconds after everyone else had finished. Bram took the banner off the pole at a walk while they were still arguing about which way he was coming.

The stands laughed so hard that Lucan had to stop the next glass.

The Committee came down the mound together, in a line, the way they’d gone up it, and sat down on the out-line bench beside the Kettle, and didn’t say anything for a while.

“We should have had one caller,” said Thea eventually.

“We should have had me,” said Xav.

“We should have had Kei,” said Hyvred. “Kei was amazing.”

“Kei went out in the third glass,” said Chris, consulting his notebook.

“Kei went out heroically,” said Hyvred.

“Hisui was looking at Suzu,” said Kei, from the far end of the bench.

And Yan, at the end of the Committee, with his scythe across his knees and the white snake round his neck, turned his head and looked down the bench at Kei, and at the green snake round Kei’s neck, and Jared saw the white snake round Yan’s neck lift her head and look at the green one.

“Okay,” said Yan.

The Kettle won the Banner.

Four glasses. Nobody else came close. Lucan read it out from the master’s platform at the end of the afternoon in a voice that had given up entirely on being steady, and Drew wrote it on the forty-foot bracket in black paint with a gold line under it, in the first box of the first trial: THE KETTLE. IV. The crowd roared again. Calvin held up his dented kettle and the crowd roared at that too.

Jared didn’t go up to the platform. He didn’t do anything. He sat on the out-line bench with a jug of water in the long evening light, with Gigichi beside him and the rest of the Kettle round him, and his window still open in front of his face.

Tier 1: Drawn.

The twentieth, Chris said. He’d come down from the Committee’s end of the bench to write it down. Twentieth of thirty-five. Two years, and a mound, and a woman with a wooden sword asking him the same question twice.

He found Jared at dusk.

Jared had known he would. He was sitting on the bottom tier of the east stand by then, on his own, with the Lists emptying round him in the long gold evening and the sand going blue, and the bracket on the wall with its first box filled in. He’d been sitting there for a while. He didn’t want to go back to the Lance yet. He didn’t know why.

The man with the rod came down the stand and sat beside him.

He didn’t say anything. Jared didn’t either. They sat on the bottom tier of the Lists and watched the marshals pull the banner down off the pole on the mound in the middle of the sand. The two bees on the man’s head had gone to sleep.

“Three hundred feet,” said Jared, eventually.

“About that.”

“Closest I’ve ever been. On a mound. In front of forty thousand people.” Jared looked at the sand. “All those years. And the closest I ever got was three hundred feet with a woman hitting me with a stick.”

“You got closer than that,” said the man. “Yesterday. On a riverbank.”

“That’s different.”

“Why?”

Jared thought about it.

“Because on the riverbank you were standing in front of me,” he said. “Like always. There’s always a pike. Trust.” He turned his head. “Today I got to stand in front of you.”

The man didn’t say anything for a long time.

“I saw,” he said. Quietly. “From the fourth tier. Half a pace in front of the pole. A little to the left.” He paused. “That’s where I used to stand. In every game. Half a pace in front. A little to the left.”

Jared stared at him.

“I didn’t know that.”

“Nobody did,” said the man. “I didn’t either, till today. I watched you go up the mound and stop and I thought: that’s my spot.” He turned the rod in his hands. “Azac’s got the tank spot. He’s had it since I died. He’s earned it. But that one. Half a pace in front of somebody, between them and the door. That one was always yours. You just never knew where you’d learned it.”

“I knew,” said Jared. “I told her. On the mound.”

“I heard.”

“You heard? From the fourth tier?”

“I hear a lot of things,” said the man. “It’s a problem.”

Jared laughed. It came out of him cracked down the middle, the way it had on the riverbank. Low and cool and collected and then not collected at all.

The man reached into his coat.

Jared watched him do it. He took something out, a flat square of stiff paper, folded once. He held it out.

“I owed you one,” he said.

Jared took it. He unfolded it.

It was a postcard.

Not a real one. A real one didn’t exist in this world. A square of stiff cream paper, the king’s paper, the kind he’d heard about from Group 1: for when you’ve got something to say. On the front, a drawing. Bee’s, Jared could tell at once; nobody else drew like that, one line, quick and sure. A small figure on a green mound, with a banner on a pole behind it, and a sword held low in both hands, and its feet planted. Half a pace in front of the pole. A little to the left.

And across the top, in big square English capitals that weren’t Bee’s:

GREETINGS FROM VALCREST.

Jared turned it over.

On the back, in a plain narrow hand he’d seen once before, on the first page of Oswin’s book, under a single letter. Small. In English.

Twelve. I missed you.

Trust.

Jared looked at it for a long time.

The Raising Cane’s postcards. Eleven of them, on Earth, over four years. A picture of Jared eating chicken in a car park, turned into a postcard by a man in Malaysia to tease a girl who’d never been to either. GREETINGS FROM RAISING CANE’S. He says he doesn’t miss you. It had been the joke. Eleven times. He’d kept every one. They were on a computer in a different universe.

This was the twelfth.

“That’s a lie,” said Jared. His voice had gone. “You put trust on it. That makes it a lie.”

“I know.”

“You always put trust on the lies.”

“I know,” said the man. “That’s the joke.” He looked out at the sand. “It’s the only true thing I’ve ever written with trust on the end.”

Jared didn’t say anything. He sat on the bottom tier of the Lists with the twelfth postcard in both hands, in the last of the light, and didn’t say anything at all for a long time.

Then he put his arm round the man’s neck from the side, the way you put your arm round a friend at the end of a long night when you’ve won something, and pulled him in, and held on. And the two bees on the man’s head woke up and buzzed in protest, and Jared didn’t let go.

“Trust,” said Jared, into his shoulder.

Rhea came and found them at dark. With her arms.

She stood in front of the bottom tier with her fists on her hips, red-faced, sweaty, grinning, with her wooden greatsword over her shoulder, and said: “After the Banner. If I’ve still got arms.” She held them up. “I’ve still got arms.”

The man looked at her.

They arm-wrestled on the bottom tier of the Lists, on the stone, in the dark, with Jared holding the lamp. It took about as long as it took Jared to blink. Rhea’s arm went over and hit the stone, and she stared at it, and then at him, and then she threw her head back and laughed so loudly that the marshals pulling down the banner in the middle of the sand turned round.

“AGAIN,” said Rhea.

“Tomorrow.”

“AGAIN.”

“After the Gate,” said the man. “If you’ve still got arms.”

And Rhea went off across the sand in the dark toward the Lance, swinging her wooden greatsword, shouting AFTER THE GATE at the empty stands, delighted.

Jared watched her go. Then he looked down at the postcard in his hand.

“You’re going to do that every day,” he said. “Aren’t you. Tomorrow. After the thing.”

“Probably.”

“She’s never going to win.”

“No,” said the man. “But she’ll keep coming back.” He stood up, and put the rod on his shoulder. “That’s how you learn to stand somewhere. You keep coming back to it.”

A figure was coming down the stand toward them in the dark. Thin. Grey. Careful on the steps, the way a man is careful when he’s been sitting at a table all day and his knees have stopped trusting him.

The Advisor stopped on the tier above them. He didn’t come down any further. He looked at the man with the rod for a moment, and at Jared, and at the postcard.

“He’s asking,” said the Advisor quietly. “Tonight. Midnight. Here.” He paused. “He asked me to ask if the girl will come.”

The man with the rod didn’t say anything for a long moment. The bees on his head had gone back to sleep.

“She’ll come,” he said.

The Advisor nodded. Once. Then he turned and went back up the stand, slowly, careful on the steps, and was gone into the dark at the top.

Jared looked at the man beside him.

“Who’s asking?”

“The king,” said the man. “Midsummer. He said he’d ask in person.” He looked at the empty sand, blue in the dark, with the mound in the middle of it and the pole with no banner on it. “He’s waited twelve years. He gets to ask first.”

“Ask what?”

The man was quiet.

“Where I came from,” he said.

And then he went off across the sand toward the Lance, on his own, with the rod on his shoulder, to find his daughter. And Jared sat on the bottom tier of the Lists in the dark with the twelfth postcard in his hands and his window still open in front of his face, and watched him go, three hundred feet, and then more, and then out through the gate.

He didn’t stand up to follow. He didn’t need to.

He knew exactly where he’d be.
