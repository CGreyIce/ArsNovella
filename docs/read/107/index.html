The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 4: Threads

Chapter 107

No Bells

5,065 words · 22 min read

Wrenhaven was the quietest town Dennis had ever heard.

He didn’t mean the noise. There was plenty of noise. A mill wheel turning at the bottom of the square, and a smithy, and hens, and a market twice a week where the fishwives shouted prices at each other across the cobbles like they were settling a feud. He meant the other thing. The thing only he could hear.

Nobody here rang.

In Kingsferry, every street was a bell tower. Every quay, every counting-house, every tavern at closing time, a whole city of small cracked bells going off all day long, clank, clank, every time somebody said I’ll pay you Thursday or it fell off the cart or I’ve never seen that man before in my life. He’d learned to live with it. He’d learned to like it, a bit, the way you get to like the noise of a city you live in. It told him things.

In Wrenhaven, almost nothing.

He’d walked up from the barge yesterday through a whole town that had come out to watch them, and stood at the top of the lake road with Group 1, and listened, out of habit. The baker had said we’ve got beds, and it was true. The mayor had said he’s been waiting for you a long time, and it was true. A small girl with a stone in her hand had said I’m going to put this on the pile for the lady, and it was true, and Dennis didn’t know what the pile was or who the lady was, and the girl had meant every word of it, the way small children do.

And every single person in that square, when they looked down the lake road at the cabin at the end of it, and said anything at all about the man who lived there, said it in a voice without a single crack in it.

That’s Ars. He fishes.

He gave my mam a pike when she was poorly.

He hasn’t got a temper. He just sits.

No bells. Not one.

Dennis had stood in that square and thought: I’ve heard this before. Not here. On Earth. In a kitchen in Malacca, and at a table in a hawker centre, and on a call at three in the morning. A voice that never rang. Not once, in nine years. Not even when it was lying. Not even when it said trust.

He came up the lake road at three in the afternoon, carrying a kettle.

Dennis saw him from Rowan’s terrace. The mayor’s house was at the top of the square, an old stone place with a walled garden at the back and a terrace looking down over the roofs to the lake, and Rowan had put them there for the day, and fed them, and then gone away and left them alone, which Dennis was beginning to understand was something people in this town were very good at.

They’d all been on the terrace since lunch. Pretending not to wait.

Calvin had been pretending hardest. He’d been up and down the steps to the garden about forty times. He’d climbed Rowan’s apple tree and come down again. He’d asked Thea, who’d come up with bread at noon, three separate times whether she was sure. Thea had said she was sure. He’ll come up. He said he would. He’s never once not done a thing he said he’d do. And it had been true, and Dennis had heard it be true, and it hadn’t helped Calvin in the slightest.

“There,” said John.

John saw everything first. He’d been sitting on the terrace wall all afternoon with his back to the town and his face to the lake, the way he’d sat on the barge’s crosstree, and now he stood up.

“There,” he said again. “That’s him. On the road. He’s carrying something.”

Everyone stood up.

Dennis stood up with them and looked down over the roofs, and there he was. A plain man in a brown coat, walking up the lake road from the shingle with the afternoon sun behind him. Not fast. Not slow. Barefoot. With a girl walking beside him, tall, with fox ears and a black sword on her back that was nearly as wide as she was. With Xav on his other side, and Petra, of all people, a pace behind. And Gabe and Deston behind them both, side by side, the way they’d walked down that road yesterday.

He was carrying a kettle.

He was carrying it in front of him, in both hands, held out a little, very carefully. Like a trophy. Like something he’d won.

Calvin made a noise.

It wasn’t a word. It wasn’t anything. It was the noise Calvin had made yesterday on the shingle when the man on the end of the jetty said I’ve got a kettle, and it came out of him again now, louder, and he sat down on the terrace wall very suddenly, as if somebody had cut his strings.

The town watched him come.

Dennis watched the town watch him. He couldn’t help it. It was what he did. And what he saw was a whole square of people who’d lived alongside this man for eight years stop what they were doing, and turn, and watch him walk up his own road with a kettle in his hands and a crowd of strangers waiting for him, and not one of them say anything. The baker. The smith from the forge on the square. The fishwives. They just watched. And then, one by one, without anybody telling them to, they went back to what they’d been doing, and left him alone.

He came across the square and in at Rowan’s gate, and up the garden path, and up the terrace steps.

And stopped at the top.

He stood there. On the top step. In the sun, with the kettle in his hands and his daughter beside him. And looked at them. At each of them. Slowly, one after another, the way Group 1 said he’d looked at them on the market road on the very first day. As if he were reading something written on their faces and wanted to get every word.

Rachael. Dennis. Calvin, on the wall. Malcolm with his book. Azac. John. RX, who was there, and visible, and standing very still, as if he’d forgotten for once how to be anything else. Emrys, in his blanket. Sabine, by the steps, with her arms folded.

Nobody said anything.

He held up the kettle.

“This,” he said, in English, “is the kettle.”

Calvin laughed.

It came out of him like something breaking. He was sitting on Rowan’s terrace wall with his face in his hands and he was laughing and crying at the same time and couldn’t stop either. Dennis had known Calvin for years. He’d never seen him like this. He’d never seen anyone like this.

“It’s the same one,” Calvin said, through his hands. “It’s the same kettle. It’s got wire on the handle. How has it got wire on the handle.”

“I bought it off a tinker,” said the man. “The first autumn. For two coppers and a pike.” He looked down at it. “I didn’t notice it was the same until last night. I swear. Eight years.”

“You didn’t notice.”

“I didn’t let myself notice.” He came across the terrace to the wall and stood in front of Calvin, and held out the kettle. “It makes terrible tea. It tastes like a hedge. Do you want it?”

Calvin took his hands off his face.

He looked at the kettle for a long time. Then he took it. In both hands. Carefully. The way you’d take a baby off somebody.

“Calvin,” said the man. Quietly. “You came all this way.”

“You said this is the kettle,” said Calvin. “In Malacca. An hour after I met you. Like it was the best thing you owned.” He held it against his chest. “I thought there’d be more. More trips. More kettles. I was twenty-five. I thought I’d just met someone I was going to know for the rest of my life.” His face did something. “And then I didn’t.”

“You did,” said the man. “It just took longer than you thought.”

Dennis listened.

No bell.

Calvin had a question.

He’d had it since the start of the winter. Since a night on a roof in Kingsferry, when Deston said it’s him, and Calvin had sat there in the dark and gone very quiet and then said, to nobody in particular, I know what I’m going to ask him. He’d never said what. Not once. Not on the barge, not in the Narrows, not on the long brown river. It’s a question, he’d said, when Dennis pushed. The one nobody else would dare to.

Dennis had wondered about it all winter. He’d tried to guess. Where have you been. Why didn’t you come. What happened to you. Those were the questions everybody had. None of them needed daring.

Calvin looked up at the man standing in front of him, with the kettle against his chest.

“Can I ask you something?” said Calvin.

“Anything.”

Calvin took a breath. Dennis saw him take it. Saw everyone on the terrace see him take it. Rachael’s hand went to Dennis’s arm.

“Are you happy?” said Calvin.

The terrace went completely silent.

Dennis understood, then, why nobody else would have dared. It wasn’t a hard question. It was the easiest question in the world. It was the one you ask a friend at a wedding, on a birthday, over a drink.

It was the one you didn’t ask unless you were ready for the answer.

They’d all heard the stories. Everybody in the kingdom had heard the stories. The Black Blade on the Redmarch. The healer who died. The hero who went mad with it and turned the border red and then walked away from everything, the war and the king and the court, and was never seen again. They’d heard it at a hundred firesides, in a dozen versions, for a year and a half. And then they’d found out it was him.

None of them had asked. None of them had known how. Because if you asked are you happy, and the answer was no, there wasn’t anything in either world you could do about it.

The man didn’t answer straight away.

He looked at Calvin. Then past Calvin, at the lake. At the cabin at the end of the road, very small from up here, with its chimney going. At the jetty. At the girl beside him, who had gone very still.

“Yes,” he said.

Dennis listened with everything he had.

No bell.

“Not every minute,” the man said. “Not every day. I carry some things I’ll always carry. There are mornings when it’s heavy.” He looked at Calvin. “But yes. I’m happy. I’ve got the lake, and the fish, and a daughter who’s cleverer than me and tells me so. I’ve got a king who came all this way and spent half his visit looking at my hens. I’ve got a town that leaves me alone.” He breathed out. “And since yesterday morning, I’ve got all of you. Standing on a mayor’s terrace, in my world, in the sun. With my kettle.” Something moved in his face. “Today, Calvin, I’m the happiest I’ve been in eight years. That’s the true answer. You can ask Dennis.”

Everybody looked at Dennis.

Dennis found that he couldn’t speak. He nodded instead.

No bell. Not one. Not a crack. Not a whisper. It was the clearest sound he’d ever heard come out of a person.

Calvin put the kettle down very carefully on the wall beside him. Then he stood up and put his arms round the man’s neck and held on, and the man held him back, and over Calvin’s shoulder, Dennis saw the girl with the fox ears put both hands over her mouth and turn away.

After that, it was easier.

It was like a dam going. Not all at once. One at a time. Each of them stepping up to him on Rowan’s terrace in the afternoon sun, while the rest stood back, the way you queue at the end of a funeral to say something to the family. Except it wasn’t a funeral. It was the opposite of one. It was whatever the word was for the end of one.

He said each of their names. In English. Every one.

And every time he did it, Dennis saw them flinch, very slightly, the way you flinch when something touches a place you didn’t know was sore. Saw their eyes go, just for a moment, to the empty air in front of their faces, where their windows lived.

He checked his own later. That night, by a candle, on his own. He knew what it would say before he opened it.

The sealed door, finally open. And no bell. Not even from the window.

Rachael went next.

She didn’t hug him. Not at first. She walked up to him, and stopped, and looked at him, very hard, the way she looked at a patient on the infirmary steps in Kingsferry. Up and down. The way Sister Maud had taught her. Look before you touch. They’ll lie to you with their mouths. Their bodies won’t.

“You look tired,” said Rachael.

“I didn’t sleep.”

“You didn’t sleep last night, or you don’t sleep?”

“Last night,” he said. “I sleep now. Mostly. Gabe made me promise.”

“Gabe can’t make anybody do anything.” She held out her hand. “Give me your hand.”

He gave it to her.

Dennis watched her close her fingers round his wrist, the way she did. Watched her go quiet. Watched her hands start, very faintly, to glow, that soft gold that had come into them in the first winter in Kingsferry and got brighter every month since. She shut her eyes. She was listening, Dennis knew. The way she listened to a body, for where it hurt. She’d once found a cracked rib in a bargeman by holding his elbow.

Her face changed.

She opened her eyes. She didn’t let go of his wrist. With her other hand, slowly, as if asking permission, she reached up and put her palm flat against the middle of his chest. Over the bone. Over his heart.

“There’s something here,” she said.

Very quietly. Not to the rest of them. To him.

“I know,” he said.

“It’s old. It’s very old. It’s…” She frowned, the way she did when a body told her something that didn’t make sense. “It isn’t in you. Not in the meat of you. It’s further in than that. I’ve never felt anything like it.” She looked up at him. “It hurts. It’s hurt for a long time.”

“I know.”

“But it’s closing,” said Rachael. Wonderingly. “It’s closing. Slowly. From the edges in. I can feel it. It’s…” She stopped. Her eyes had gone wet. “It’s healing on its own. Nobody’s doing it. It’s just healing.”

“I know,” he said, very gently. “I’ve been told.”

Dennis saw him look down at her hand on his chest. At the soft gold glow coming from her fingers.

“Your hands are warm,” he said.

He said it strangely. As if it surprised him. As if he’d been expecting something else, and wasn’t sure whether he was glad or sorry. Rachael didn’t understand it. Dennis could see her not understand it. But Gabe, behind him, made a small sound, and looked away. And so did Deston. And so did Xav.

Rachael took her hand off his chest.

“Don’t make it weird,” she said. Her voice wobbled. “Next.”

And then she hugged him anyway, hard, and he held on to her for a long moment, longer than he’d held on to Calvin. And Dennis, who was standing nearest, heard him say something into her hair, very low. Something meant only for her.

If they ever come for you again, you don’t write me a letter. You tell Deston. He pulls the thread. I’ll feel it. And I’ll come.

Dennis listened.

No bell. Not a hope of one. That one rang truer than anything.

He looked at Dennis next.

Dennis had thought about what he’d say all winter. He’d planned it, the way he planned a ledger. He’d had a version for if he was angry, and a version for if he was sad, and a version for if the man turned out not to be Declan at all, and a very short one, for emergencies, which was just hello.

He forgot all of them.

“Dennis,” said the man.

“You never rang,” said Dennis.

It just came out. He hadn’t planned it. He’d never told anybody on Earth about the bells. He hadn’t had them on Earth. He’d only had a feeling, a sort of itch, the way you’d know a coin was bad by the weight. It had only turned into bells here.

But the man nodded, as if he knew exactly what Dennis meant.

“Not once,” said Dennis. “In nine years. Everybody else I’ve ever known rings sometimes. Everybody. Rachael, when she says she’s fine. Deston, when he says he’s not worried. Gabe never lies, but he rings when he tells people he’s not hungry.” Behind them, somebody laughed. “You never rang. Not even when you were lying. Not even when you said trust. Especially not then.”

“I know,” said the man.

“How?”

The man thought about it.

“Because I always meant it,” he said. “Even when it wasn’t true. Especially then. I’ll be on early tomorrow. I meant it every time. I just never managed it.” He shrugged. “A joke that big isn’t a lie. It’s a joke in a lie’s coat.”

Dennis stared at him.

That was what he’d thought. In Kingsferry, in the autumn, on a roof. Those words. Almost exactly those words. He’d never said them to anyone. He’d only thought them.

“Trust,” said the man, gently.

Dennis listened.

No bell.

He laughed. He couldn’t help it. It came out of him like the bubble out of a bottle, and he stood on Rowan’s terrace in the sun and laughed until his eyes ran, and the man put a hand on his shoulder and waited for him to finish.

“I heard you bought a boat,” said the man, when he had.

“A barge,” said Dennis, wiping his eyes. “The Patient Widow. Eighteen months’ savings.”

“Why?”

“I had a feeling.”

“He did,” said Sabine, from the steps. “I want a cut.”

The man looked at Sabine for a long moment. At her folded arms. At the new pink scar down her cheek.

“I can pay in fish,” he said.

Sabine considered this.

“Fine,” said Sabine.

Dennis listened. No bell. From either of them. He’d suspected, for a year and a half, that Sabine and Declan would get on.

Malcolm was next.

He didn’t say anything. He just held out his book.

It was the IF/THEN book. The third one. Battered from the river, with the cover warped where it had got wet in the Narrows, and a ribbon in it. The man took it, carefully, and opened it where the ribbon was.

Dennis knew what was on that page. He’d seen Malcolm write it, on the barge, a fortnight ago, by a lantern, in a village with no dogs. Eleven pages of plans for going upriver. Every way it could go wrong. And then, at the bottom of the last page, crossed out, written again:

IF he’s up there

THEN no more next year.

The man read it. Twice. Then he closed the book and held it against his chest for a moment, the way Calvin had held the kettle.

“You kept saying next year,” he said.

“I know,” said Malcolm.

“Every year. Next year, I’ll come up. Next year, when work calms down.”

“I know,” said Malcolm. His voice cracked. “I’m sorry.”

“Don’t be.” The man handed him back the book. “Next year came. You’re here.” He put his hand on the back of Malcolm’s head, the way you’d do with a brother, and pulled him in until their foreheads nearly touched. “That’s the only next year that ever mattered.”

No bell.

John held up his shoe.

He didn’t say anything first. He just bent down, and took off his left boot, and held it up in front of the man’s face. The way he’d held up a banana, and a cat, and his own shoe, a hundred times, to a facecam on a stream, to make Declan laugh.

The man looked at the boot.

His face went completely still for a heartbeat. And then he laughed. Properly. Bent over, with his hands on his knees, on Rowan’s terrace, so hard he couldn’t breathe. Behind Dennis, RX had started laughing too, and then Azac, and then the girl with the fox ears, who clearly didn’t know why it was funny and was laughing anyway because her father was.

“Every time,” the man said, when he could. “Every time. In the middle of a match. In the final, John.”

“You played better laughing,” said John. “I worked it out. In the second year. You always played better when you were laughing.”

“I know I did.”

John put his boot back on. Then he looked at the man, very seriously.

“Your lamp’s the loudest light I’ve ever heard,” he said. “From the mast. Two days out. The one at the end of the road by the water. I heard it from the lake country.” He paused. “I think it’s you. Not the lamp. You.”

The man looked at him for a long moment.

“You’re nearly there, aren’t you,” he said. “Tier one. I can feel it on you. Like a word on the tip of your tongue.”

“Ninety-nine,” said John.

“Ninety-nine.” The man nodded. “Don’t push it. It’ll come when it’s ready. Light’s patient.” He smiled. “It waited a long time for you to notice it. It can wait for you to write the line.”

No bell.

RX vanished.

He did it in the middle of everybody laughing at John’s shoe. He’d been there, by the terrace wall, grinning, and then he wasn’t. Not invisible. Just not there, in the way that RX had got better and better at being not there, ever since he went up a tier in the autumn. Your eyes slid over the place he’d been and decided it was empty. Even Dennis’s did. Even Deston said he had to look for RX’s thread on purpose, these days, and it went thin when he was hushed, like a voice going quiet in a crowd.

The man didn’t turn round.

“Hi, RX,” he said.

There was a pause.

Then RX was there again, standing directly behind him, a pace away, with his hands out as if he’d been about to put them over the man’s eyes. His face was outraged.

“How,” said RX.

“You don’t click the circle,” said the man. “You wait till it isn’t there.”

RX stared at him.

Then he made a sound like a kettle boiling over, and sat down on the terrace with his back against Rowan’s wall, and put both hands in his hair.

“That’s Hidden,” he said. “That’s the Hidden thing. You told me that in a Discord call. About osu!. Years ago.”

“It still works.”

“I chased your scores for six years,” said RX. “I never got one. Not one. I had your birthday in my phone for two years. I was going to fly up. I was going to.” He looked up. “I’m going to beat you at something. Here. I don’t care what. I swear.”

“I know you will,” said the man.

He crouched down in front of RX, on the terrace, so that they were level.

“You nearly did, just then,” he said. “I only heard you because I was listening for you. Nobody else on this terrace knew you’d gone.” He tilted his head. “The king wouldn’t have heard you. Do you know that?”

RX looked at him.

“Seriously?”

“Seriously.”

RX put his hands over his face.

No bell.

Azac didn’t step up.

He’d been standing at the back, by the steps, all this time, with his shield on his back out of habit and his face doing something complicated. Dennis had watched him. He’d been watching Azac on and off since the Narrows; Azac had cracked his forearm on the cliff and hadn’t said a word about it till Rachael found it. Azac was like that. He stood in places. He held them. He didn’t talk about it.

When the man looked at him, Azac shook his head. Once.

“Later,” said Azac. “I’ve got a thing to ask you. Not now.” He shifted his shield. “It’s about a spot.”

The man looked at him for a long time.

“Later,” he agreed.

Dennis listened. No bell. From either of them. Whatever the thing was, they both meant to get to it.

And Emrys.

Emrys was the last. He’d hung back further than anyone. He’d been sitting on the bench at the far end of the terrace in his blanket, very pale, with his hands in his lap, and he hadn’t moved, and he hadn’t said anything. He’d been hoarse for a year and a half, since a night at a midwinter market in Kingsferry when he’d thrown everything he had into one sung flash of light, at a man with no thread. His voice had never come back right. He could talk, in a rasp that hurt to listen to. He could hum, cracked. He couldn’t chant. A chanter who couldn’t sing a note.

The man went to him.

He went down the length of the terrace, past all of them, to the bench at the end, and stopped in front of Emrys. And Emrys stood up. And then, before anyone could stop him, Emrys started to go down on one knee on the flagstones. The way Dennis had seen soldiers go down in the chapels in Kingsferry, in front of the old saints.

The man caught him.

He did it so fast Dennis didn’t see him move. One moment Emrys was going down, and the next the man had him by both elbows, holding him up, gently, the way you’d hold up an old man on an icy step.

“Don’t,” he said. “Please. Not you.”

Emrys looked at him. His lips moved. What came out was a rasp, and it broke, and stopped.

“I know who you are,” said the man. “You’re Emrys. You taught them chants as singing, on the road, the first three days. You made Gabe and Deston look at each other.” He smiled a little. “You sang with them in the Narrows. In the gorge. You hummed it, and you left the gap. Deston told me.”

Emrys’s face crumpled.

“You don’t kneel to me,” said the man. “I’m a fisherman. And you’re the one who taught my brothers to sing again.” He let go of Emrys’s elbows, carefully, making sure he was steady. “Thank you.”

Emrys stood there.

He opened his mouth. He tried. Dennis saw him try, saw the cords stand out in his thin neck. A rasp. A crack. Then just air. He shut his mouth, and shook his head, and pressed his hand flat against his own throat, and looked at the man with an expression of such helpless apology that Dennis had to look away.

The man looked at Emrys’s throat for a long moment.

“It’ll come back,” he said. “Not today. Not for me. I can’t do that. I’m not that kind of strong.” He tilted his head. “But I think it’ll come back. I think you just haven’t heard the right thing yet.”

Dennis listened.

There was no bell. But there was something else. Something he’d never heard before, from anyone. Not a lie. Not quite a truth. Something in between. The sound a man makes when he doesn’t know a thing, but believes it so completely that it doesn’t matter.

Dennis didn’t have a word for that sound.

He thought, later, that it might be what hope sounded like.

They stayed on Rowan’s terrace until the sun went down.

Odette brought food, and went away. Rowan came up at dusk and stood at the garden gate for a minute, looking at the terrace full of people, and his face did something, and he went away again too. The man sat on the wall with his back to the lake and his daughter beside him and the rest of them on the flagstones round him in a half circle, the way you’d sit round a fire. And they talked.

Not about anything that mattered. Not about the Redmarch, or the healer, or the war. Deston and Gabe and Xav had gone very quiet whenever anybody got near any of that, and the man had just looked at the lake, and after a while everybody had understood and stopped getting near it. It was his. If he wanted to tell it, he’d tell it.

So they talked about everything else.

About Kingsferry, and Duskhollow, and the Hollow. About Sister Maud, who’d given Rachael her needle case. About the bricked door that breathed. About osu!, and Inferno, and a choir in Singapore, and whether the dancing bear had been happy, which turned out to be a question everybody had an opinion on. About a kettle. About the worst inn on the Wren. About a hundred things that were nothing, and that were the most important things in the world, because they’d thought they’d never get to talk about them with him again.

Dennis sat on the flagstones with his back against the wall and listened.

The whole evening. Every word.

And in all of it, in hours of it, in jokes and stories and arguments and the long comfortable silences in between, from a stranger’s mouth, in a stranger’s voice, he never heard a single bell.

It was the sound of a kitchen in Malacca. Of a table in a hawker centre at two in the morning. Of a call that ran four hours past when anybody should have gone to bed.

It was the sound of Declan.

He’d have known it anywhere.
