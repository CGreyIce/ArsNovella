The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 11

Chapter 307

Every Word

5,511 words · 24 min read

Maren had been a Lantern for thirteen years, and in all that time she had never once burned anything she did not mean to burn, until last autumn, when she had set fire to the curtain in the militia hall.

She still thought about the curtain. It had been green, a heavy wool that the militia had bought from a Kingsferry draper in her first year and complained about the price of ever since, and she had stood in front of it on an autumn evening with both hands out and Thea’s new Lantern burning in the next room like a second sun, and pushed. Torch was more. That had been her theory. She had been thirty-four years old and a Lantern for twelve years, and her student of three years had just come up level with her in a single night, and she had thought: it is more. Louder, hotter, harder. She had been at Lantern so long because she had never been brave enough to push.

So she had pushed. And the fire had come out of her palms the way it always came, a good clean flame, steady, the flame she had held for thirteen years; and then, because she was pushing, it had gone where her hands were pointing instead of where she wanted it, which was a candle on the table, and found the curtain behind the candle, and gone up it like a squirrel.

She had put it out. She had put it out with her hands, because that was quicker than thinking, and she had stood in the smoke afterwards in front of Thea and Bram and two militiamen with both palms blistered white from the wrist to the fingers, and not said anything, because there was nothing to say that would not have been either a lie or an apology, and she did not care for either.

Thea had dressed her hands. Thea had not said anything either, which Maren had been grateful for then and was still grateful for now.

That was half a year ago.

She had not pushed since. She had gone back to the other thing instead, the slow thing, the thing Thea had shown her two winters ago at this same table: reading a spell before you cast it. Every word. Not the sound of it; anybody could say the words; she had said the words of her flame ten thousand times since she was a girl. The meaning. What each word was for, and what it asked, and of what. Thea read spells the way a clerk reads a contract, looking for the clause that does the work, and she had sat across this table from Maren two winters running and read Maren’s own flame back to her, word by word, until Maren had begun to hear it.

It went slowly. It had always gone slowly. Seventy-one at midwinter. Seventy-four at the thaw. Seventy-nine in the spring, on the morning Thea found the empty middle of the valley circle. Eighty-three. Eighty-six.

It had sat at eighty-eight for three weeks.

She knew why. She had known for three weeks, and not said it to anybody, because it was the kind of thing that was only true once you said it. She could read every word of the flame now. She could hear what each of them was for. She could put a candle out across a room by asking one word to stop, and light it again with another, and not touch anything between.

But a candle was a candle. She had never had to aim at anything that mattered.

The girl had been in Odile’s back room for six days.

Lissa Fowler. Eight. A fisherman’s daughter from the huts at the foot of the lake road, with a hook cut across the heel of her right hand and a grey line round the edges of it, a hair wide, that would not heal and would not fester and would not do anything at all. Except wait. And grow. A hair a day. It had been on the edges of the cut on the first night. It had reached the wrist on the fourth. On the sixth morning, when Maren came down the hill in the rain, Odile met her at the door with her soft old face set like a stone and said, very quietly, “Above the wrist. A finger’s width.”

Maren went in.

The back room was small and hot, with a fire going and the shutters closed and a lamp on the table. The girl was on the cot under the window, asleep, with her right arm laid out on a folded sheet beside her like something that belonged to somebody else. Thea was sitting on a stool beside her. She had been sitting on that stool, Maren thought, for most of six days.

She looked worse than she had at the thaw. Worse than she had the night the valley rang. She was grey with tiredness, and there was dried blood in the corner of one nostril, and her hands were in her lap, palms up, the fingers curled, the way they went when she had been reading something with them for too long and they ached.

She looked up when Maren came in.

“It’s a word,” said Thea.

She had found it on the fourth night. She told Maren in the yard, with the rain coming down on the elder tree and the roots of Gigichi’s seats glistening under it, and the girl’s mother asleep in the one against the door with her hand flat on the wood.

“I couldn’t find the edge of it,” said Thea. “For three nights. I kept looking for where it started. A wound has edges. Something done to a body has a shape, and the shape has edges, and you can read along them. This didn’t. It was just grey, under the skin, in a line, waiting.” She rubbed her eyes with the heels of her hands. “And then on the fourth night I stopped looking for the edge of it and looked at the line itself. Up close. As close as I could get. With Parse, all the way down, until my nose went.”

“And?”

“It’s writing,” said Thea.

Maren said nothing.

“The hall’s language. The same one. The summoning circle, and the valley, and Astra’s folds.” Thea’s voice was very flat; it went flat when she was frightened, the way Maren’s went formal. “One word. Very small. Smaller than anything I’ve ever read. Written over and over, end to end, in a line round the cut, like a seam, like a stitch. Thousands of times. That’s what the grey is. It’s not a colour. It’s the word, so close together you can’t see the gaps.”

“What does it say?”

“I don’t know the sound of it. I never do; I can read the grammar and not the sound, the same as Astra.” Thea looked at the infirmary door. “But I know what kind of word it is. It’s the opposite of what’s on Astra. Astra’s folds hold. Stay. This doesn’t hold. It lets go. It’s an emptying word. It’s the word you’d write on the inside of a cup to make it pour.”

The rain came down on the elder.

“It’s not doing anything yet,” said Thea. “That’s why it’s waiting. It’s written, and it’s waiting for the rest of it. I don’t know what the rest of it is.” She was quiet. “It’s going up her arm, Maren. A hair a day. It’s writing itself up her arm toward her heart, and I can read every stroke of it, and I can’t stop it.”

“Can you touch it? With the other thing. Handshake.”

“I could. I won’t.” Thea’s hands closed in her lap. “If I take hold of a word in the hall’s language with Handshake, it takes hold of me back. That’s what Handshake is. And I don’t know what it would do, holding on to me. I don’t know whose hand’s on the other end of it.” She looked at Maren. “I’m not going to find out on an eight-year-old.”

“No,” said Maren.

“Rachael said don’t close it. Odile hasn’t. It would only close it in.” Thea was looking at the door. “It has to come out. Before it goes any further. And I can’t get at it, and Odile can’t, and Mend doesn’t touch it, and Rachael’s a hundred miles down the river.”

Maren looked at the door too.

She thought about a candle across a room, and a word that asked it to stop. She thought about a green curtain going up like a squirrel.

“Then I will burn it out,” she said.

Thea did not say anything for a long time.

“If you burn it,” she said at last, very carefully, “you burn her.”

“Not if I burn only the word.”

“Maren. It’s a hair deep. It’s in her. It’s written through the skin the way a vein runs through it, in and out, all the way along. There isn’t a gap between the word and the girl. Not one you could put a needle in.”

“I am not going to put a needle in it. I am going to put a flame on it.” Maren heard her own voice: formal, precise, every word in its place. It always went like that when she had decided something and was frightened of it. “A flame goes where you point it. That is what I did to the curtain; I pointed at a candle, and the curtain was behind it. But a flame does not have to go where you point it. Not if you have read every word of it. Not if it understands where you mean.” She looked at Thea. “You taught me that. For two winters. At my own table.”

Thea’s face did something complicated.

“I didn’t teach you that,” she said. “You found it. I just read it back to you.”

“Then read it back to me now.”

“No,” said Thea.

Maren had known she would say it. She had come down the hill knowing. And it still landed, somewhere under her breastbone, like a stone dropped in a well.

“I won’t,” said Thea. Her voice cracked on it. “I want to. You don’t know how much I want to. I want to sit down with your flame and read it back to you word by word the way we did in the winter and tell you there, that one, aim with that one, and I won’t, because it isn’t mine. It’s yours. If I hand it to you it’ll be mine in your hands. And it has to be yours.” She looked down at her own hands in her lap. “He told me that. On the jetty, a long time ago. Point. Don’t hand it over. If you hand it over it’s never theirs.” She looked up. “I’ll sit beside you. All night. I’ll tell you what the word is doing, if it moves. That’s reading her, not you. But your flame’s yours.”

Maren looked at her a long moment.

“Good,” she said. And meant it, which surprised her.

She practised on her own hand.

It was the only thing to practise on. She sat at the table in the militia hall that afternoon with the shutters open and the rain coming down outside, and Thea across from her with her hands in her lap, and she took a pen and wrote a word on the back of her own left hand. Her own name, in the kingdom’s letters. Small. In iron-gall ink, black, that sinks into the skin and will not wash off for a week.

Then she read her flame.

She had read it ten thousand times. She read it now as if she had never seen it, the way Thea read things: every word, slowly, asking what it was for. Seven words. The first, which woke the heat. The second, which gave it a shape. The third, which said here. The fourth and fifth, which held it, the Lantern’s words, the ones she had built her life on. The sixth, which said go. And the seventh, which said to.

To. She had never thought about the seventh. She had always taken it for granted, the way you take your own feet for granted. It was the word that said where. And she had always, for thirteen years, filled it with where her hands were pointing.

She lifted her right hand over the back of her left, a finger’s width above the ink, and said the first five words under her breath, and the heat came, a small clean flame on the pad of her finger like a seed. And she said go, and to, and meant the ink.

The flame went where she pointed.

It went down off her finger onto the back of her hand, the whole of it, ink and skin and all, and she felt the skin go white and tight and heard herself make a sound, and snatched the hand away, and there was a blister the size of a thumbnail on the back of it with her own name in the middle, blurred.

Thea didn’t move. She sat across the table with her hands in her lap and her face white and didn’t move a finger, and Maren could see what it cost her.

“Again,” said Maren, to herself.

She did it eleven times that afternoon. On her left hand, and then, when the left was blistered to the wrist, on her right. Her own name, over and over, in black ink, and her own flame coming down on it.

Every time, it went where she pointed.

Not as badly as the first time. She learned to point smaller. She learned to bring her finger so close that the flame was only on the ink and a hair round it; and it still took the hair round it, and blistered, because she was pointing at a place, and the place had skin in it.

At the ninth she stopped, and sat with both hands in a basin of cold water, and thought.

She had been thinking of the seventh word as where. A place. A spot on the back of a hand. And a place was a place; it had everything in it that was there, ink and skin and all. You couldn’t point at the ink without pointing at the skin under it, because they were in the same place.

But the ink wasn’t a place. The ink was a word. Her name. It had a shape; it had a beginning and an end; it had strokes. It was a thing, not a spot.

To didn’t have to mean there. It could mean that.

She took her hands out of the water. She wrote her name again on the back of her right hand, the only bit left that wasn’t blistered, and looked at it. Not at the place. At the word. At each stroke of it, the long downstroke of the first letter, the loop of the second, the little hook at the end that she had made the same way since she was six years old. She read her own name the way Thea read a spell. Every stroke.

And she read the flame again. And when she came to the seventh word, she did not think there. She thought my name. Every stroke of it. The shape of it, and nothing else.

The flame came down off her finger.

It did not go where she pointed. Her finger was pointing a little to the left of the ink; she had moved it on purpose, to see. The flame went down and found the first stroke of her name and ran along it, a thread of heat no thicker than a hair, like a fuse; and followed the loop of the second letter round; and went down every stroke of the word, one after another, to the little hook at the end; and the ink went up off her skin in a thin black smoke and was gone.

The skin underneath was pink and warm. Not blistered. Clean.

She sat looking at it for a long time.

Across the table, Thea had both hands over her mouth.

They went down at dusk.

Odile let them in, and shut the door behind them, and put the chair against it. The girl was awake. She was lying on the cot with her arm on the folded sheet and her eyes very big in the lamplight, looking at the grey above her wrist, the way you’d watch a spider on the wall.

“Hello, Lissa,” said Maren.

“Hello, Mistress Maren.”

“I’m going to try to get that out,” said Maren. “With fire. A very small fire. It might be warm. If it is too warm, you say so, and I stop.” She knelt down beside the cot, so their faces were level. “Do you understand?”

The girl looked at her. Then at the grey. Then at Maren’s hands, which were blistered red and white from the wrists to the knuckles on both sides, and wrapped in linen to the second joint.

“You burned yourself,” said Lissa.

“Yes. Practising.” Maren held them up. “So that I would not burn you.”

The girl thought about this for a long time, the way children do, with her whole face.

“All right,” she said.

Odile sat on the other side of the cot and took the girl’s left hand in both of hers. Thea pulled her stool up beside Maren, close, a hand’s width off, and sat down on it, and put her own hands in her lap, palms down, and held them there. Maren saw her do it. She saw the knuckles go white.

Outside, through the shutter, she could hear the rain, and somebody in the yard moving, and the creak of a root as somebody shifted in a seat that had grown for them.

She looked at the grey.

It ran round the edge of the cut like a stitch, the way Thea had said. And from the cut, up, across the heel of the hand and over the wrist bones and a finger’s width onto the arm, in a thin grey line a hair wide, very straight, like a seam somebody had drawn in pencil with a rule. It did not look like anything. It looked like a mark you would rub at with your thumb.

She did not look at the place. She made herself look at the word.

She could not read it; she was not Thea. But she could see that it was a thing, now, and not a spot. A line. With a beginning, at the cut, where the hook had gone in. And an end, up on the arm, where it stopped. And between, if she looked very hard, so hard her eyes ached, she could almost see what Thea saw: that the line was not smooth. That it was made of something, over and over, close together, the way a rope is made of strands.

A word, written ten thousand times.

She read her flame. All seven words, every one of them, slowly, asking what it was for. She lifted her right hand over the girl’s arm. Her own hand was shaking; the blisters had broken under the linen and were wet. She let it shake.

The first word. The heat woke, on the pad of her finger, a seed.

The second. It took a shape.

Here.

The fourth and fifth, the Lantern’s words. She held it. She had held a flame for thirteen years. She held this one the way you would hold an egg.

Go.

And to. She came to the seventh word and did not think there. She did not think the arm or the hand or the cut. She thought: that. The grey. The word. Every stroke of it, ten thousand times over, from the beginning to the end, and nothing else in the world. Not the skin it was written in. Not the blood under the skin. Not the girl. The word.

The flame went down off her finger.

It found the beginning.

She felt it find it, the way you feel a key find the wards of a lock: a small sure catch. At the edge of the cut, where the hook had gone in, a thread of fire no thicker than a hair went into the grey and began to run along it.

It did not burn the girl.

Maren watched it. She did not breathe. The thread of fire ran along the grey line round the edge of the cut, the whole way round, following the stitch; and where it had been, the grey was gone. Not burned black. Gone. It went up off the skin in a thin smoke the colour of ash, and the smell of it was not a burning smell at all. It was cold. Like a cellar. Like a well with the lid off.

And under it, where the grey had been, the cut was red.

Red. Raw and open and angry and red, the way a cut should be, bleeding a little, a bright bead at a time. Maren felt Odile make a sound across the cot. A wound. A real wound, trying to be a wound, the way they always tried.

The thread went on. Off the edge of the cut, up the line, across the heel of the hand, over the wrist. Slowly. A hair a breath. Maren did not move her hand. She did not point. She held the flame the way you hold an egg, and kept the seventh word full of nothing but that, and the fire followed the word, every stroke of it, wherever it went, up the girl’s arm, and did not once touch anything else.

“It’s warm,” said Lissa. Very small.

“Too warm?”

“No.” The girl was watching it with her mouth open. “It tickles.”

Halfway up the wrist the word moved.

Maren felt it. A pull, under the flame, the way a fish pulls on a line when it feels the hook: the end of the grey line, up on the arm, a finger’s width above the wrist, going. Up. Faster. A hair, two hairs, a third, writing itself up the girl’s arm away from the fire as fast as it had written itself in six days.

“It’s running,” said Thea. Her voice was perfectly flat. Her hands were still in her lap; Maren could see them out of the side of her eye, clenched white. “It knows. It’s going up. Three hairs. Four.”

Every bit of Maren wanted to push. To make the flame go faster, harder, more; to get ahead of it; to chase. She felt the old thing come up in her, the curtain thing, it is more, the voice of thirteen years of being afraid that she was not enough.

She did not push.

She held the egg. She kept the seventh word full of that. She did not think about how fast the grey was running, or where to, or what would happen if it got there. She thought about the word. Every stroke of it. The whole of it, from the beginning, which the fire had already eaten, to the end, which was running up the arm; and the end was still the word, it was still the same word, and the seventh word did not say where. It said that.

The fire did not chase it.

It found it. It went where she meant. It left the line it had been following and went up the girl’s arm in no time at all, without crossing the skin between, without touching anything, the way the meaning of a word gets to the end of a sentence before your eye does, and it was at the end of the grey before the grey had gone another hair; and it went into it from both ends at once.

The last of the word went up in a curl of cold grey smoke.

And was gone.

Nobody said anything.

The girl lay on the cot with her arm on the folded sheet. There was a red cut across the heel of her hand, bleeding a little, and above it, all the way up over the wrist to a finger’s width onto the arm, a faint pink line, like the mark a sleeve leaves on your skin when you take it off. Warm. Maren could feel the warmth of it from where she knelt.

Odile let go of the girl’s left hand. She put both her own hands round the right one, very gently, the way you’d pick up a bird, and closed her eyes.

Mend. Maren felt it go in. And felt it, a breath later, come back: the body taking hold of it, the cut answering, the way Odile had said for thirty years that a cut answered, like a dog’s tail on a floor. The red edges drew in. The bleeding stopped. And Odile, who cried at everything, sat on the edge of the cot with a child’s warm hand in hers and her eyes shut, and her face did the thing it did, all of it, and she said, in a voice like a cracked bell, “There you are.”

Lissa looked at her hand.

“It’s warm,” she said. “It’s mine again.” And then, after thinking about it: “It’s itchy.”

Thea made a sound beside Maren that was not quite a laugh. Maren looked at her. Her hands had come out of her lap at last. They were over her face, and she was shaking, all of her, very quietly, and she did not take them away.

Maren did not touch her. She did not know how, and it would not have helped. She knelt beside the cot with her own blistered hands in her lap, and felt something go up in her.

It went up the way her flame had gone up the girl’s arm. Not chasing. Not pushed. It was simply there, all at once, at the end of a thing she had been reading for thirteen years, the way the meaning of a sentence is there at the end of it: a step, and a light, and the sense of a room she had been standing in the doorway of all her life, and was in.

She opened her window. She did it the way she had done it every morning since she was nineteen and a court mage from Solmere had stood behind her in a cold room and said here, let me show you how to look. She could not have said why her hands were shaking more now than when the grey was running.

She looked at it.

She had read it in other people’s windows, read aloud to her. Emrys’s. Lucan’s. Oswin’s. She had never once seen it in her own.

Torch. Real combat magic, the old books said, and every court mage in the kingdom said it after them; she had said it herself, to the summoned, at this table, in the first week. Torch is real combat magic. She had always thought that meant more. Bigger. Hotter. A spell to burn a field.

It did not mean more. It meant meant. A spell that went where you meant it to go, so you could put it into a fight, into a crowd, into a press of bodies and steel, and burn the one thing you meant and nothing else. Real combat magic. You could not fight beside anybody with a flame that went where you pointed. You could only fight beside them with one that went where you meant.

She closed the window.

“Thea,” she said.

Thea took her hands off her face.

“Torch,” said Maren. Very quietly. “After thirteen years.”

Thea looked at her. Her eyes were red and her nose was bleeding again a little and she did not seem to know it. She did not say congratulations. She did not say anything at all for a long moment, and then she said, in a voice Maren had never heard from her, small and very young:

“I didn’t help.”

“No,” said Maren. “You did not.” She looked at her own blistered hands, and then at the girl’s warm one in Odile’s. “Thank you.”

They told the yard. The mother first, in her seat against the door; she was up and through it before Odile could move the chair, and nobody stopped her, and the sound she made when she saw her daughter sitting up on the cot scratching her own wrist was a sound Maren thought she would hear for the rest of her life. Then the father, at his rail. Then the old woman with the rosary, and the furious brother on the wall, who climbed down at last and came and stood in the doorway with his arms folded and his face working.

And the man in the seat apart, under the elder, who had sat there every day for six days facing the gate and the door at once, and not spoken to anybody.

Maren went to him herself. She stood in front of him in the wet, with her bandaged hands at her sides, the way she had stood in front of him in the first month, with the Advisor’s order in her head and her heart going, the fisherman by the lake is not to be disturbed.

He looked up at her.

“It’s out,” she said. “All of it. It was a word. I burned the word. She’s warm.”

He looked at her for a long time. Then at her hands, wrapped to the knuckles, wet through.

“You aimed,” he said.

“Yes.”

He nodded. Slowly. He did not say anything else; he never did. But he got up out of the seat Gigichi had grown for him, which he had not done in daylight for six days, and stood in the rain a moment longer, looking at the door; and then he went back up the lake road toward the cabin, at a walk, the way he always walked, as if he were going to the end of a garden.

Maren watched him go.

She had stood in front of that man in the first month with her orders and her whole careful life and been more frightened than she had ever been of anything. She had been a Lantern then. She had been a Lantern for nine years already, and she had thought she would die one.

She went back in to Thea, who was putting it in the channel with her bleeding nose and her red eyes, because that was what you did, so thirty-four other people could know.
