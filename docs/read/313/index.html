The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 11

Chapter 313

The Stands

5,276 words · 23 min read

Lucan’s letter had been on the table since ice-out, under the salt jar, where she could see it every time she reached for the salt.

She’d written to him at the thaw. One sheet, in her best hand, which was her mother’s hand; she’d learned her letters off her mother’s old slate, and they still came out with her mother’s slant, leaning forward a little as if they were in a hurry to get somewhere. She’d told him the truth. That she’d be nineteen at midsummer and twenty at harvest, and it was her last summer under the line, and she wasn’t going to sit on the holder’s bench. That she’d promised a boy on the sand last year that he’d be the holder. That she’d come anyway, and sit in the stands, and she’d rather he didn’t make a fuss.

Lucan had written back by return. Four sheets. Both sides. The first sheet was mostly the word no in different sizes. The second was the rule, copied out from the Lists’ own book in a clerk’s hand with Lucan’s notes in the margin in purple: that the holder of the Fledgling sat the bench, and met the last of the tree on the last afternoon, and it had been so for three hundred years, and the master of the Lists couldn’t change it. And then, underneath, a line Lucan had underlined three times. A holder may give the bench back, by her own hand, before the tree is drawn. Then the tree shall go to the end, and the last of it shall be holder.

It has been done four times, Lucan had written in the margin. Every time by a child who was dying or had joined a temple, which I very much hope you have not. I looked. I sat up all night in the book room looking, in my goose gown, and Ysolde found me asleep on the floor with my face in the reign of the king’s great-grandfather.

The third sheet said that he’d keep her a seat in his own box, with cushions, and a jug of something.

The fourth sheet said: But you won’t sit in it, will you. You’ll sit somewhere stupid. Tell me where, so I can send a boy with the jug.

She’d written back: East stand. Fourth tier. Where my father stood.

He hadn’t answered that one. She thought he’d probably cried on it. Lucan cried on most things.

“Three days,” said Higu.

She was sitting on the end of the cabin table with her notebook on her knee, and her moon chart open beside it, the one she’d kept since the hall: every night of four years in small neat pencil, a circle for each, filled in or not. Alea had never seen her without it. She thought Higu probably slept on it.

“Full moon on the fourth night out,” said Higu. “You’ll be at Caddow, or just past, if the carrier’s on time, which he won’t be. Clear, probably. It’s been clear for a week.” She turned a page. “New moon the night of the final. So if anybody wants to stand in the stands in the dark afterwards and look at something, there’ll be a lot of stars and not much else.”

“Thank you,” said Alea.

“I’ve written you the inns.” Higu tore the page out and folded it, twice, very neatly, and held it out. “Maren checked them. Maren says the one at Fennick waters the beer and the one at Pellow’s Cross has a dog that bites, and you’re to sleep at the one with the blue door, because the woman there was a guide’s widow and won’t ask you anything.”

Alea took it. It had eleven inns on it, and a little drawing of the dog.

“You don’t have to,” she said.

“I know,” said Higu. “I’m the one who writes things down. It’s what I do when I can’t do anything.” She shut the notebook. She looked at the door, at the bright square of the lake through it, and the jetty, and the shape on the last plank. Then back. “He’ll be all right.”

“I know.”

“He’s got Gigi,” said Higu. “And us. And the hens.” She was quiet a moment. “And it’s a hundred miles to Kingsferry. Five days by the post road. Three if you kill a horse.” She said it the way she said the moon, as a fact she’d checked. “Whatever happens down the river, it’ll happen in a night. It’ll have happened before he’d ever have got there. Even if he went.”

Alea looked at her.

“I worked it out,” said Higu. “In the spring. After the letter. I wanted to know if it was a choice he was making or just a sum.” She put the moon chart away in her coat. “It’s a sum. Mostly. He’d never have got there. But he’s making it as if it was a choice, because he’d make it the same either way, and that’s worse, and that’s why he’s on the end of the jetty.” She got down off the table. “I’m telling you so you don’t stay. You were going to think about staying tonight. On the step.”

“How do you know?”

“Because you’re his,” said Higu, “and he always thinks about staying on the step.”

She did think about it. On the step. That night.

The lamp was lit behind her in the window. He’d lit it at dusk, the way he had every night since the spring, since she’d held the matches out; and then he’d stood and looked at it, the way he did now, a moment longer than you’d need to see that a lamp was lit, and gone down the jetty. He was on the last plank. She could see the line of the rod against the water, and the little pale shape of the float, and his back.

She sat with her knees up and Astra across them and thought about the Wren.

It went out of the bottom of the lake past Wrenhaven, and down through the Narrows to Kingsferry, a hundred miles, and the sea after. She’d never been down it. She’d been south twice, to the Lists, and east to the grave. Never down the river. She knew it only from the Widow going round the point, and from what Group 4 said, and from the channel, the bits Thea told her, which was most of it now, because Thea had stopped asking whether she wanted to know.

The grey. The hooks. A boy called Ned with it past his elbow under Rachael’s seam. A girl called Lissa at Wrenhaven, and Davy Marsh at the weir, which was nearer still. A man with no hat and a box on the customs-house steps. A hole in the sound on the same steps at three in the morning, and a pike hook left on the bottom one, new. Gabe standing on those steps every night now, with his back to the infirmary. Rachael, who said she was frightened of what she’d hold.

And a rhyme. Mender, mender, sit you up.

She could go down the river. That was the thing she’d been thinking for a week, under everything else. She wasn’t him. Nobody had lit fires on a hill so she would see. She could take Haskett’s next barge from Wrenhaven, or walk the towpath, and be in Kingsferry in five days, and stand in front of Rachael with a sword that wouldn’t dull, and nobody would be watching to see if she came.

She’d promised a boy she’d be in the stands.

She looked at the jetty for a long time.

Then she got up, and put Astra on her back, and went down.

He didn’t turn round. He knew it was her; he knew the weight of her on the planks the way he knew the weight of a fish on the line. He moved the cup along the last plank to make room.

She sat. She put her feet over the edge. The water was very still, the way it went at the end of a clear day, with the last of the light lying on it like oil. The float sat on it and didn’t move.

“I could go down the river,” she said.

He didn’t say anything for a while.

“I know,” he said.

“I’m not you. Nobody’s watching for me.” She looked at the float. “I’d be there in five days. I could stand in front of her. Astra doesn’t dull. Gabe’s a wall and I’m a—“ She stopped. She didn’t know what she was. “I’m another one. In front.”

“They’ve watched for that sword for ten years,” he said.

She was quiet.

“It’s not me they know,” he said. “Not my face. A face is just a face. It’s the sword. A black sword the size of a door, on a man’s back, coming down a hill.” He didn’t look at her. He looked at the float. “If a black sword comes down the Wren to Kingsferry, it doesn’t matter whose back it’s on. It’s what they lit the fires for.”

“I could leave it here.”

“You could,” he said. “And go down the river with your good knife, and a Honed edge on it, and stand in front of Rae. And you’d be very good. And you’d be one more, in front of her, who isn’t what they’re waiting for.” He was quiet. “She’s got four of those. And Emrys, and Sabine. And a woman called Maud who’s run that ward for forty years and frightens me from a hundred miles off. She’s got more in front of her than I ever had on that hill.” He turned the cup on the plank, a quarter turn. “I read what she wrote. This week. I’ve got a river.”

“I know.”

“She meant it,” he said. “She wasn’t being brave. Rae’s never brave. She’s just exact.” He looked at the water. “She’s got a river. You’ve got a boy on a sand. That’s yours. You said it out loud in front of forty thousand people, and he heard, and he’s been at that guard school all winter looking at the whole thing because you told him to.”

She didn’t say anything.

“Keep it,” he said. Very quietly. “That’s what they’re for. You say a thing on a sand, and then a year goes by, and all sorts of things happen, and the year’s very long and very bad in places, and you go and do the thing you said anyway. Because you said it.” He stopped. “I said things on sands. Some of them I didn’t keep. It’s not a good thing to carry. You don’t want it.”

The float moved. Just once. A ring went out from it on the still water and spread and was gone.

He didn’t strike. He watched the ring go.

“And you,” said Alea.

“Me.”

“Rachael’s got a river,” she said. “I’ve got a boy on a sand. What have you got?”

He was quiet a long time.

“A lake,” he said.

“On your own,” said Alea.

He didn’t answer that. She’d known he wouldn’t. She sat beside him on the last plank with her feet over the water till the light was gone off it, and the stars came out in it one at a time, the way they did, upside down; and the lamp up at the cabin lay on the lake behind them in a long gold line that came all the way down to the end of the jetty and stopped at his back.

She took the list down in the morning, before he was up.

It was on its nail by the hearth, beside the drawing, under the new sword on Garrow’s pegs. Higu’s list, his side to the wall: every word on the front of it crossed through but one, in his pencil, and Fish written again at the bottom. And her side out. Her mother’s slant.

Come home. A line through it.

Tell me where you found it. A line through it.

Write first. And under it, smaller: To Halvard. Not an answer.

She sat on the step in the cold first light with the pencil and looked at the third line for a long time.

He had. In the spring, after the Widow went: four lines, by the post road, by hand, to the west tower. I pulled it out. She hadn’t seen it. She hadn’t asked to. And then the rider had come back with an answer, and a worse fish, and a question; and he’d sat in Gigi’s seat apart at Wrenhaven for six days and then come home and written back on one sheet with no drafts, and she hadn’t seen that either. She’d seen him seal it. She’d seen his face while he did. That was enough.

She drew a line through Write first. Then, because it was true and he’d want it exact, she drew a line through Not an answer too. It had been both. He’d written first, and then he’d answered. That was more than she’d put on the list.

And then there was a lot of room.

She knew what it was. She’d known on the jetty, in the dark, when he said a lake and didn’t say anything else. She’d known before that, really. Since the spring. Since the morning she’d seen him in Gigi’s seat by the infirmary gate, the one apart, facing the gate and the door both, the way he’d sat at every door in his life, on his own, a little way off, where he could see and nobody had to sit with him.

She wrote it in her mother’s hand, under the other three.

Let somebody sit with you.

She looked at it. It wasn’t enough. It was the same as last time; it never was. He’d read somebody and think it meant whoever happened to be passing, and he’d be very polite to them, and he’d still be on his own.

She added, smaller, underneath, so he’d know who she meant:

While I’m gone. Gigi.

She hung it back on its nail, her side out, and went to see Gigi.

Gigi was on her bench.

She was nearly always on it, in the mornings, now; she came down from Wrenhaven on the lake road before it was light, Higu said, every morning, and sat, and went back up at noon to Odile’s to help with the children who’d been hooked. She said she liked the bench in the morning. She said it was warmer than it had any right to be. Alea thought that was because it was alive. It had grown out of the roots by the lake road the autumn her father was away west, out of nothing, out of Gigi sitting on a stone every evening and not knowing that was what she was doing. It had a back now, and arms, and in the spring it had put out leaves. And every week, if you looked, it was a hand nearer the water.

It was nearer now. Alea could see where it had been: a pale patch on the grass by the road, like the mark a pot leaves. The bench was a good two paces down the shingle from it, leaning toward the jetty like somebody who’d shifted along to make room without getting up.

“Oh, shut up,” said Gigi, before Alea had said anything. “I know. It does it on its own. I come down in the morning and it’s moved. I asked Thea to read it and she said it was being considerate.” She looked at the jetty. “He’s not up yet.”

“He’s up,” said Alea. “He’s pretending not to be so I can go to the step on my own.”

“That’s very him.”

“Yes.” Alea sat down on the bench. It was warm. She didn’t think it should be; it had been a cold night. “Can I ask you something?”

Gigi looked at her.

She was small and dark and sharp, with the gold watch with the leaf on it ticking in her pocket, and a scratch across the back of one hand from her own briars that she said was a lesson, and her eyes on Alea’s face the way they’d been on Thea’s wrist the night Thea read the folds. She waited. She’d learned to do that, Higu said. She hadn’t been able to, when she came; she’d filled every quiet with something sharp. Now she just waited, with her hands in her lap, and let you get there.

“I’m going south in the morning,” said Alea. “For a month. To the Lists.”

“I know. Xola told everybody. Xola told Odette’s geese.”

“He’s not coming.” Alea looked at the jetty. “He won’t go south while it’s like this. Down the river. He says he’d never get there, and Higu says it’s a sum, and it is. But he’s going to sit on the end of that jetty every night for a month with the channel going in his head, all of it, every line, and he won’t write back in it. He never has.” She stopped. “And I won’t be on the step.”

Gigi didn’t say anything.

“I wrote it on the list,” said Alea. “This morning. Let somebody sit with you.” She swallowed. “And then I wrote your name. Under it. Small. Because he’d read somebody and think it meant anybody, and be polite to them.”

Gigi was very still.

“I’m not asking you to make him say anything,” said Alea. “He won’t. You know he won’t. You ask him if he’s okay and he says no, now. He didn’t used to. He says no, and that’s all, and that’s—“ She stopped. “That’s more than he’s ever said to anybody, nearly. It’s more than he says to me. To me he says it’s fine and puts his hand on my head.” She looked at her own hands. “I’m asking you to sit. That’s all. Where he can see. While I’m gone.”

The bench was very warm under them.

“I can’t grow a seat on the jetty,” said Gigi at last. “It’s planks. It’s dead wood. Room comes up out of things that are alive; I can’t do it on a plank, I tried in the spring, I just got splinters.” She looked down the shingle. “But the ash is at the landward end. By the first post. Its roots go right under. I could—“ She stopped. “I could ask it. If he wanted somebody at the end. Not the far end. The near end. Where you can see him and he can see you and nobody has to do anything about it.”

“Like Gabe,” said Alea.

“Like Gabe,” said Gigi. “Near enough.” She looked at Alea. “I won’t, though. Not unless he sits there first. You can’t grow somebody a seat they don’t want. I found that out. It comes up thorns.” She shrugged. “So I’ll sit on this. And it’ll keep moving. And if it gets all the way down to the first post on its own by the end of the summer, that’s not me. That’s the bench.”

Alea laughed. It surprised her.

“He’ll know it’s you,” she said.

“He knows everything’s me,” said Gigi. “He knows the bench is me. He sat in my seat at Odile’s for six days. He’s not stupid; he’s just very, very slow about some things.” She was quiet. “Like asking. I’ve asked him you okay most evenings since the ice went. He’s said no seventy-four times.”

“Seventy-four,” said Alea.

“I count,” said Gigi. “Higu’s not the only one with a list.” She looked at the jetty. “Last week he said no and then he said you? After. Just you?” She didn’t look at Alea. “First time he’s ever asked. I nearly fell off the bench.”

Alea didn’t say anything.

“So I’ll sit,” said Gigi. “You go and watch your boy.” And then, because she was Gigi, and couldn’t leave a soft thing lying about without putting a thorn on it: “And if he wins, tell him he’s got the stupidest name I’ve ever heard. Tamlin. It sounds like a pudding.”

“I’ll tell him,” said Alea.

“You won’t,” said Gigi. “You’ll go red and say well done.” She put her hand on the arm of the bench, flat. “Go on. He’s pretending not to be up. He’ll pretend till noon if you let him.”

They came down from Wrenhaven in the afternoon. All of them.

She hadn’t asked. She’d meant to go quietly, the way he went east every year: up the lake road with her pack and nobody on the shingle, and over the hill. But Xola had told Odette’s geese, and the geese had told everybody, and at three o’clock the lake road was full of people.

Group 1 at the front. Thea with her hair up in its high tail and her sleeves pushed back, and her eyes on Astra the way they always went, and then away, because she’d promised never to read it without asking. Higu with a second list, of things Alea had forgotten. Xola with a basket of buns from the bakery and Squiddles on top of the buns, eating one. Chi, last, slow and enormous and calm, with Waddles walking at her heel in a way that Higu said wasn’t natural for a penguin and Waddles didn’t care.

Maren behind them, with Bram, and Bram’s boy on Bram’s shoulders. Maren’s hands were bare. They’d been bare since the morning in the lake, Xola’s morning; she’d stopped wearing the gloves. The palms were new and pink and very smooth, and Alea had seen her look at them, sometimes, when she thought no one was watching, the way you’d look at a letter from somebody you’d thought was dead.

Old Garrow, who said he’d come to check the grip. Odette, who said she’d come to make sure Alea had a coat, and had brought one. Rowan, who didn’t say why he’d come and stood at the back with his hands in his pockets looking at the lake.

And Gigi, on her bench, who didn’t get up.

It took a long time. It always did, at the lake. Nobody said anything that mattered and it took an hour. Higu went through the second list. Odette put the coat on her and buttoned it and then unbuttoned it again because it was too hot and folded it into her pack herself. Xola cried and hugged her, and then cried again and hugged her again, and Squiddles got squashed, and complained. Garrow looked at the grip, and at her hand on it, and grunted, and said, “It’s taken,” which from Garrow was a speech.

Chi came last. She bent down, a long way, because she was very tall and Alea wasn’t, and said, very quietly, in the voice that made the hens sit down in snow:

“You can go. It’s all right. Everything here will still be here.”

Alea hadn’t known she was frightened of that until Chi said it. She felt it go. Not all of it. Enough.

“Thank you,” she said.

“I didn’t do anything,” said Chi. “I just said it out loud. Things are easier out loud.” She straightened up. “Waddles says good luck.”

Waddles looked at Alea with one eye and said nothing whatsoever.

Maren was the last to speak. She stood in front of Alea with her new hands at her sides and her face doing its formal thing, the face she’d had on the lake road nearly four years ago when she’d first seen a fisherman on a jetty and gone white.

“I’m told you’re not fighting,” she said.

“No.”

“Then watch properly,” said Maren. “All of it. Not only him. The ones who lose. Watch what they do with their feet after.” She was quiet. “I’ve watched a great many bouts in my life and never once looked at anybody but the winner. I’d like to have.” She looked at her palms. “I’ve been finding out this summer how much I didn’t look at.”

“I will,” said Alea.

Maren nodded, once, the way she did when a thing had been done correctly. And then, very fast, as if she’d decided it before she could stop herself, she put one of her new hands on Alea’s shoulder. Just for a moment. And took it away.

He was at the door when she went up for her pack.

Not on the jetty. At the door of the cabin, in the shade, with his shoulder on the frame, the way he’d stood in it two summers ago, the morning the wagons went south for the Lists, when he hadn’t been south of Wrenhaven in eight years and hadn’t decided yet whether he was coming. He’d come. Both summers. She’d known where he was on the fourth tier without looking, both times.

Not this time.

“You’ve got everything,” he said.

“Higu’s got everything. I’ve got what Higu let me take.”

He nearly smiled.

He had something in his hand. She saw it when he moved: long, and thin, and pale, and very slightly crumpled, held the way you’d hold a thing that might break. He held it out.

It was a fishing rod. A paper one. A thin stick of rolled paper, gone soft and yellow at the ends, with a thread tied to the tip and a little paper fish on the end of the thread, its painted eye nearly rubbed off.

She knew it. Of course she knew it. The first summer, the final, the last afternoon. Forty thousand of them going up all round the bowl, and she’d looked up at the fourth tier of the east stand after the touch, and he’d been on the fourth tier with one in his lap. Holding it in both hands. She’d never asked where it went.

“You kept it,” she said.

“It was in the box,” he said. “With your mother’s things.” He didn’t look at it. He looked at her. “I thought you’d want one. To hold up. For your boy. Lucan gives them out at the gate, but they’re new.” He was quiet. “This one’s been to a final.”

She took it. It weighed nothing at all.

“I’ll hold it up,” she said.

“I know.”

She put it in the top of her pack, very carefully, between Higu’s inns and Odette’s coat, where it wouldn’t get bent. Then she stood up with the pack on and Astra over it and looked at him in the door.

“The lamp,” she said.

“I’ll light it at dusk.”

“Who for?”

He looked at her a long time.

“You,” he said. “You’re the one who’s not home. For a month.” He stopped. And then, carefully, like a man putting his foot on ice to see if it would take him: “You’re allowed to be the one it’s for.”

She laughed. It came out of her wet and broken, the way Gabe’s did, the way it had in the spring when he’d said that’s a door with somebody’s hand on it; and she put her hand over her mouth, and it didn’t help.

“That’s mine,” she said, through it. “I said that. To you.”

“I know,” he said. “I’m giving it back. You can have it back in the autumn.”

And then he put his hand on her head. Between her ears. The way he always had. And she let him, and stood there with her eyes shut and her ears flat under his palm and the pack on her back and the lake loud behind her, and didn’t move, and didn’t move, till he took it away.

“Stop at the top,” he said. “Of the last hill. At dusk. Look back.”

“I will.”

“I’ll be on the jetty,” he said.

She went up the lake road.

She didn’t look back till the top.

Past the shingle, and the people on it, who’d all decided at once to have something to do with their hands. Past Gigi’s bench, two paces nearer the water than it had been in the spring, with Gigi on it, who didn’t get up and didn’t wave, but lifted one hand off the arm of the bench a little way and put it down again. Past the last of the alders, and the turn where you lost the lake, and up.

It was a long hill. It always had been. She’d come down it a hundred times and gone up it a hundred, and it was the only road out of the lake that wasn’t water, and from the top of it you could see the whole of the end of the lake laid out like a dish: the shingle and the alders and the cabin and the jetty going out into the water, and the far shore, and the hills behind. The sun was low by the time she got there. She took the pack off and sat on it, at the side of the road, on the grass, with Astra across her knees.

She waited.

The light went off the water first. Then off the far hills, slowly, from the bottom up, like a tide going out the wrong way. The lake went grey, and then silver, and then a sort of dark that wasn’t black yet. The first star came out over the far shore.

The lamp came on.

She saw it catch. A small yellow point in the window of the cabin, very small from up here, smaller than a star; and it wavered, the way it did when the chimney first went down over it, and then it steadied, and stood.

She looked for the jetty.

He was on it. On the last plank, a dark shape against the silver, where he’d said he’d be. She couldn’t see his face. She didn’t need to. She could see the line of his shoulders, and that he wasn’t casting, and that he’d turned round, which he never did. He was sitting on the end of his jetty with his back to the lake, looking up the hill.

She lifted her hand.

She didn’t know if he could see her. It was a long way, and it was nearly dark, and she was a fox girl sitting on a pack at the side of a road. But he lifted his. She saw it go up, against the water.

And then she saw the other one.

At the near end of the jetty. The landward end, where the planks met the shingle, by the first post and the ash with its leaves. A second shape. Small, and dark, and sitting on the shingle with its knees up, not on the jetty, not at the end, nowhere anybody would have to do anything about. Just there. At the near end, where you could see him and he could see you.

Gigi had come down off the bench.

Alea watched them for a long time. The two of them. The one on the last plank and the one at the first post, the whole length of the jetty between them, and the lamp up in the window behind, and neither of them doing anything at all.

Then she put her pack on, and Astra over it, and the paper rod safe in the top where it wouldn’t get bent; and she turned round, and went over the hill, south, toward a sand a long way off where a boy she’d told to look at the whole thing would be looking for her in the stands.
