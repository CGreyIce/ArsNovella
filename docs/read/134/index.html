The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 5: The Lists

Chapter 134

Hooks

5,647 words · 25 min read

Alea woke up on the sand of the Lists with her head on her father’s shoulder and the sun in her eyes and a king asleep on the bottom tier behind her.

She lay there for a while without moving. She’d done this before. Not on the sand of the Lists, with a king, but on a jetty, when she was small: woken up in the cold just before dawn with her head on his shoulder and his coat over her and the lake going grey and then pale, and lain there very still so he wouldn’t know she was awake, so it could last a bit longer.

He knew. He always knew. He didn’t say anything. That was the deal.

Behind her, on the bottom tier, the king of Aurelan was snoring. Not loudly. A deep slow sound, like bellows. The Advisor was sitting beside him, awake, very upright, with his hands open on his knees and his face turned toward the east, where the sky over the top of the stands was going the colour of a fish’s belly. He looked, Alea thought, like a man who’d been told something in the night and was still turning it over to see if it was really there.

It was. She’d watched her father give it to him.

She’d never heard him tell it to anyone else. All her life. At the top of a stair, at bedtime, in the dark, from when she was small enough that the stories were just stories, about a world with no magic and a box of light and thirty-five friends who laughed at his jokes. A girl called Leaka who teased him. Two boys from a choir who were his brothers. A fisherman’s daughter on a lake hears a lot of stories. She’d thought they were stories, for years. Then she’d been old enough, and one night he’d sat on the end of the bed and told her the true one, and she’d understood that they’d all been true.

Last night he’d told it to a king. On the sand. Plainly, the way he told her how to tie a knot.

She’d held onto his knee the whole time.

“You’re awake,” said her father, without moving.

“No.”

“You’re awake. Your ears moved.”

“My ears always move.”

“They moved awake,” said her father. “Round two’s at the second hour. Petra’s going to be at the practice ground in about the time it takes to walk there.” He paused. “Bring the sword.”

“I always bring the sword.”

“I know,” said her father. “I’m reminding you so you’ll feel told what to do.”

Alea sat up, and pushed her hair out of her face, and looked at him. She couldn’t see his face. Nobody could. She never minded. She’d been looking at it all her life; she knew it the way you know the shape of the dark in your own room.

“You told them,” she said.

“I told them.”

“How do you feel?”

He thought about it. Properly, the way he always did, when she asked him things.

“Lighter,” he said. “Like the morning after a long carry. When you take the pack off and stand up and you keep thinking you’re going to fall forward.”

Alea looked at him a moment longer. Then she leaned over and put her forehead against his shoulder, once, hard, the way she’d done when she was small and didn’t have the words.

“Good,” she said.

Round two was a cadet.

His name was Garth, he was nineteen, he was from the military school at Ashby, and he was very good. He came out onto the near ring in the grey coat of the school with his hair cropped short and a plain soldier’s sword and stood exactly the way soldiers are taught to stand, weight balanced, feet shoulder-width apart, sword at a perfect angle. Alea had watched him in round one. He’d beaten a guild boy with a borrowed axe in about four moves, all of them textbook, all of them clean.

“He’ll come in on the third count,” said Petra, at the edge of the ring. Petra was in her corner, with Xav beside her. Petra had been a recruit once, a long time ago, before a ditch on the Redmarch, and she’d drilled with Ashby cadets. “They teach the third count at Ashby. Three steps, then the cut. Every time.”

“Every time?” said Alea.

“Every time,” said Petra. “That’s what a school’s for.”

The marshal said begin.

Garth stepped in. One. Two.

Alea moved on two.

Not fast. Just early. While it’s still deciding to. The black sword came up off the sand in its long easy line, like a rod lifting, and met his blade halfway through the second step, before he’d ever got to three, at the place where a soldier’s sword is weakest because he hasn’t finished putting his weight behind it yet. And she let it fall.

The cadet’s sword went down into the sand at his feet and stuck there.

He stood looking at it. Then he looked up at her. He had a sensible soldier’s face and it was doing something very unsoldierly.

“I didn’t get to three,” he said.

“No,” said Alea. “Sorry.”

“Nobody’s ever not let me get to three.”

“I know,” said Alea. “That’s why I didn’t.”

The marshal’s flag went up. The near stand cheered. Garth of Ashby pulled his sword out of the sand, and looked at it, and then he did a thing that surprised her: he saluted her, properly, a cadet’s salute, fist to the chest. And walked off the ring with his back very straight.

Xav, at the edge, said nothing. He never said anything. But when she came off the ring he held out his fist, low, and she bumped it with hers, and he nodded once, and that was worth more than the stand.

The Lists were different on the second day.

Alea noticed it walking back from the near ring to the bench under the east stand where the fledglings waited. Yesterday the crowd had come for the Thirty-Five. You could feel it; the whole bowl leaning toward the summoned, toward the paper horns and the squids and the kettle and the oni, the way a field of barley leans toward wind. The fledglings had been something to watch between the real things.

Today, a little, they’d come for the fledglings too.

People looked at her. That was the strange part. People in the stands, as she walked past the rail. Pointing. Saying things to each other. That’s her. The fox girl with the door. Did you see yesterday? One stroke. A man leaned over the rail and held out a paper fishing rod to her, for no reason she could see, and she took it, and then didn’t know what to do with it, and stood there holding a paper fishing rod while the man beamed at her.

She’d never been looked at. Not like that. Not by strangers. She’d been looked at by Wrenhaven all her life, but Wrenhaven knew who she was. Wrenhaven had brought her pies when she was small and taught her her letters and watched her train on the ice. Wrenhaven looked at her the way you look at a girl you’ve known since she was four.

These people didn’t know anything. They looked at her and saw a fox girl from nowhere with a sword too big for her, who’d let it fall on a lord’s son.

She found she liked it. A bit. She didn’t tell her father that.

She sat down on the fledglings’ bench with the paper rod across her knees, next to her sword, and looked up at the fourth tier of the east stand.

He was there. The end of a bench. A man nobody looked at, with two bees on his head. He didn’t wave. He never waved. He just sat there, and she looked at him, and he looked back, and she put the paper fishing rod up on her shoulder like a real one, so he’d see.

On the fourth tier, two bees fell off a man’s head, because his shoulders had started going.

She saw the coast girl in the afternoon.

She’d heard about her already. Everyone had. Round one, the coast girl had beaten a guild boy so fast that Lucan, who’d been reading out the names himself, hadn’t finished reading hers before the marshal’s flag went up. Round two, a noble’s daughter with a rapier and a private master, which had taken a little longer. People on the fledglings’ bench talked about her. Gullhaven. The hooks. Did you see what she did to the Pemberton girl?

Alea hadn’t seen. She’d been on the near ring. She watched now.

Round three. The far ring, at the west end. The coast girl came out onto the sand in a fisherman’s smock with the sleeves cut off and her brown arms bare and salt-burned, and a strip of sailcloth tied round her hair, and in her two hands two boat-hooks.

Real ones. Alea knew what a boat-hook was; there were boat-hooks on every jetty on Lake Ilse. A pole with an iron head on the end, a spike and a curved hook, for pulling a boat in to the dock or pushing it off, or for catching a line that had gone in the water. These had been cut down. The poles were short, a little longer than the coast girl’s arms, and the iron heads were blackened and old and sharp, and she held them loosely, the way you’d hold a pair of oars.

She was sixteen, at most. Shorter than Alea. Square and brown and cheerful, with a grin like a gap in a fence.

Her opponent was a boy with a longsword. A good one. He came in hard.

The coast girl caught it.

That was the only word Alea had for it, afterwards. The boy’s longsword came down in a good cut and the coast girl put one hook up, not to block it but to catch it, the curve of the iron going round the boy’s blade just above the guard the way a hook goes round a mooring line. And then she pulled.

Not hard. The way you pull a boat in. Steady, with her whole weight going back, her bare feet dug into the sand. And the boy, who’d been leaning into his cut, came with it. Forward. Off his feet. His own weight carrying him in, the way a boat comes in when you hook it, and as he came the coast girl’s other hook came round, low, and caught him behind the knee.

He went down on his face in the sand.

The coast girl stood over him with one hook on his blade and the other behind his knee, grinning.

“Gotcha,” she said.

The marshal’s flag went up. The far stand roared.

“She hooks the blade,” said Petra, very quietly, beside her. “And then she doesn’t fight it. She just pulls. She lets your own weight do it.”

“I saw.”

“She’s like you,” said Petra. “Upside down.”

Alea looked at her.

“You let it fall,” said Petra. “You take your sword up and let the weight bring it down. She takes your sword and lets your weight bring you in.” Petra’s face did something that wasn’t quite a smile. “You both let something else do the work. You just do it in opposite directions.”

Alea looked back at the far ring. The coast girl was helping the longsword boy up out of the sand, still grinning, slapping the sand off his back with one of the boat-hooks. He was laughing, a bit dazed. She said something to him and he laughed harder.

“What’s her name?” said Alea.

“Sorrel,” said Xav. He’d been reading the roll over the marshal’s shoulder. “Of Gullhaven. Copper. Aura. Tempered.” He paused. “Sponsor: the Gullhaven fishers’ guild.”

Tempered, thought Alea. Like me.

She’d never fought anyone her own tier who wasn’t Petra. Petra didn’t count. Petra was a soldier, past thirty, Reaching, and had been fighting since before Alea could walk. Petra beat her moving first on wet verges. Petra was meant to beat her.

Sorrel of Gullhaven was sixteen and a fisherman’s girl from the coast with two cut-down boat-hooks, and she’d just pulled a boy off his feet with his own sword.

“She’s on the other half,” said Xav. “Of the bracket. You won’t see her till the end.”

“If we both get to the end.”

“Yes,” said Xav. He looked at the far ring, where Sorrel was walking off with her hooks over her shoulders like oars. “She’ll get to the end.”

Round three was a guild girl with a spear.

She reminded Alea of Hako so much that for a moment, stepping onto the near ring, she nearly laughed. The same long reach. The same patience. The same habit of standing with the spear low, waiting, letting you come to it. She was eighteen and from a guild hall in Fennick and her name was Ivy, and she’d beaten two boys with swords in rounds one and two by simply never letting them get close enough to use them.

Alea had trained at dawn on wet sand with Hako for five days now. She knew how a spear waited.

She waited longer.

They stood in the near ring for what felt like an hour and was probably three hundred heartbeats. The guild girl with her spear low. Alea with the black sword’s point on the sand. Neither of them moving. The near stand got restless, then quiet, then very quiet, the way a crowd goes when it realises it’s watching something that isn’t going to happen until it does. Somebody high up started to slow-clap and somebody else told him to shut up.

Alea didn’t look at the spear. Stop looking at me, her father said, every morning. Look at the whole thing.

She looked at the whole thing. The girl’s feet. Her hands. Her breath. The way the tip of the spear moved, very slightly, with each breath, in and out, like a fishing float on a still lake. In and out. In. Out. In.

The guild girl breathed in.

Alea moved on the breath.

The spear came round, a fraction late, because the girl had been breathing in and you can’t strike and breathe in at the same time. The black sword met it on the long easy line and let it fall, and the spear went down into the sand, and Alea was inside its reach before the guild girl had finished breathing out.

She stopped Astra’s edge a finger’s width from the girl’s ribs.

The guild girl looked down at the black iron.

“Oh,” she said. “You waited.”

“You breathe,” said Alea. “When you’re about to go. You take a breath first.”

The guild girl stared at her.

“Nobody’s ever noticed that.”

“My dad notices things,” said Alea. “It’s very annoying.”

She went back up to the fourth tier at the end of the day, when the rings had been chalked out and the crowd had gone into the city and the long hot afternoon was going gold. She climbed the stand with Astra on her back and sat down on the end of the bench beside him, where nobody looked, and put her feet up on the bench in front.

“Three,” she said.

“Three.”

“Ashby was easy. Fennick wasn’t.” She leaned her head back against the stone. “She breathes before she goes. I saw it.”

“I saw you see it.”

“You were watching the whole thing?”

“I’m always watching the whole thing,” said her father. “That’s what the fourth tier’s for.”

Alea looked out at the empty sand. At the near ring and the far ring. At the far ring, especially, at the west end, where in the afternoon a coast girl had pulled a boy off his feet with his own sword and helped him up and made him laugh.

“Did you see the coast girl?” she said.

“I saw her.”

“She’s Tempered.”

“She is.”

“She pulls,” said Alea. “She hooks your blade and then she just pulls. She lets your own weight bring you in.” She was quiet. “Petra says she’s like me. Upside down.”

“Petra’s right.”

Alea didn’t say anything for a while. She looked at the far ring. At the sand there, where somebody had raked it smooth again and there was no sign anyone had ever stood on it.

“I’m frightened of her,” she said.

Her father didn’t say good. He’d said good on the Last Look. He didn’t need to say it twice.

“You’ve got two more rounds before you’d see her,” he said instead. “And she’s got two more before she’d see you. And then there’s a semifinal for each of you. You might never see her at all.”

“I’ll see her.”

“Probably.”

“Xav says she’ll get to the end.”

“Xav’s usually right,” said her father. “It’s nearly as annoying as Drew.”

Alea laughed. She didn’t mean to. It came out of her on the fourth tier, short and surprised, and some of the stillness in her tail went out.

She met Sorrel that evening, by accident, at the well.

There was a well behind the Lists, in the yard by the practice ground, where the fledglings went to wash after their bouts, because the bath-houses in the city charged a copper and half the fledglings didn’t have one. Alea went down at dusk with a cloth and a bucket because Petra had said you stink of sand and glory, go and wash, and found the coast girl already there, sitting on the edge of the well with her feet in the bucket and her boat-hooks leaning against the stone beside her and a heel of bread in her hand.

They looked at each other.

“You’re the lake girl,” said Sorrel. “With the door.”

“You’re the coast girl. With the hooks.”

“Everyone keeps saying that,” said Sorrel, cheerfully, through the bread. “The hooks. Like it’s a name. They’re my dad’s. Were.” She pulled one foot out of the bucket and looked at it critically and put it back. “He cut them down for me when I was ten. Said if I was going to keep falling off the boat I might as well have something to pull myself back in with.”

Alea sat down on the other side of the well. She put her bucket down. She didn’t put her feet in it.

“Mine’s a fisherman,” she said.

Sorrel looked at her with real interest.

“Yeah? Lake or river?”

“Lake. In the north. Pike, mostly. Perch.”

“Pike,” said Sorrel, with the deep respect of someone who has only ever caught things in the sea and has heard stories. “We don’t get pike. We get cod and mackerel and things with too many teeth.” She considered Alea. “Is he good?”

Alea thought about it.

“Yes,” she said. “He’s very good.”

“Mine was,” said Sorrel. “Best on the Gullhaven quay. Everyone said.” She didn’t say anything else about it, and Alea didn’t ask, because she’d heard the was both times and knew exactly what it sounded like. Sorrel pulled her other foot out of the bucket. “He’d have hated all this,” she said. “The crowd. The shouting. He hated crowds.” She grinned the grin like a gap in a fence. “He’d have loved that I pulled the Pemberton girl off her feet, though. He’d have told the whole quay.”

“I didn’t see that one.”

“It was very good,” said Sorrel, with satisfaction. “She had a rapier. You can’t pull a rapier much, it’s too thin, it just bends. So I pulled her.” She mimed it with one hand, a hook going round an ankle. “Like a crab off a rock.”

Alea laughed.

They sat on the well for a while in the dusk. Sorrel ate her bread. Alea washed her face and her arms in the bucket and didn’t say very much. It was strange, she thought. She’d never had anyone her own age to sit with who wasn’t one of the five girls at the militia hall, or Petra. Wrenhaven didn’t have many girls her age who carried swords. Sorrel was sixteen and loud and salt-burned and had a dead father and a pair of his boat-hooks, and she talked like Maigo, all the time, about everything, and Alea liked her immediately and was terrified of her at the same time.

“What’s his name?” said Sorrel. “Your dad.”

Alea went still.

“Your roll,” said Sorrel. “Lucan read it out. Alea, of Wrenhaven.” She was looking at her with open curiosity, the way she’d looked at the pike. “He didn’t read the rest. He never reads the rest of mine either. It just says Gullhaven fishers’ guild. Because there isn’t anybody.” She shrugged. “Is there anybody? On yours?”

“There’s a box,” said Alea.

“Blank?”

“Blank.”

Sorrel thought about that.

“Not nobody,” she said slowly. “Blank.”

“Not nobody.”

“Huh,” said Sorrel. She looked at Alea for a long moment. Then she grinned. “That’s a story,” she said. “That’s a story somebody’s saving.”

“Yes,” said Alea.

“Are you going to tell it?”

“At the end,” said Alea. “If I win.”

Sorrel threw her head back and laughed, loud, at the darkening sky over the Lists.

“Then I’ll have to make sure you don’t,” she said, “or I’ll never find out.” She picked up her boat-hooks and put them over her shoulders like oars and stood up with her bare feet on the cold stone. “See you at the end, lake girl.”

“See you at the end.”

Sorrel went off across the yard in the dusk, whistling, with her hooks on her shoulders. Alea sat on the well with her bucket and watched her go.

She was frightened of her. She liked her. She found the two things fit together much better than she’d expected.

Round four was a mage.

The Fledgling Lists were any path. Most of the fledglings were swordsmen, because the Lists had always been for swords and most mage children went to the colleges instead. But a few came anyway. Round four was a boy of seventeen from a mage college in Solmere, in a grey scholar’s coat, with a staff and a Spark window and a very nervous face, and he could throw fire.

Not much fire. Spark. A small bright spurt of it, from the end of the staff, the size of a fist. But fast. And he could do it again and again, and his round-three opponent had spent the whole bout ducking.

Alea had never fought a mage.

She’d seen them. She’d seen Thea take spells apart like a clock. She’d seen Ina write thanks in the air in violet fire two days ago. She’d watched Xola heal a cut hand on the hill above Wrenhaven, the way her mother used to heal people, and had to go outside for a while afterwards. She’d never once had anything thrown at her that wasn’t steel.

“He chants,” said Petra, at the edge of the ring. “Under his breath. Spark mages have to. They can’t do it silent yet.”

“How long?”

“Long enough,” said Petra.

Alea went into the near ring and stood very still.

The boy pointed his staff at her and started to chant, very fast, under his breath. She heard it. Not the words; they were in this world’s speech and she couldn’t follow them that fast. The rhythm. A quick little run of syllables, the same every time, and then a breath, and then fire.

She’d learned rhythm from Newish. Not on purpose. At the long table at the Broken Lance, the first night, Newish had been explaining to somebody how she heard everybody’s beat, and Alea had listened, and thought about it in bed.

Everybody has a beat. You just have to listen for where it’s going.

The boy chanted. Run. Breath.

Fire.

Alea was already moving. On the breath. The fist of fire came out of the end of the staff and went through the place where she’d been and hit the sand behind her with a hiss, and she was three steps in before he’d started the next run, and the black sword came round in its long line and touched the end of his staff, very gently, and let it fall.

The staff went out of his hands and onto the sand.

The boy looked at it.

Then he looked at her. His nervous face had gone completely astonished.

“You moved before it,” he said.

“You breathe,” said Alea. “Before. Everyone does.”

“Everyone?”

“Everyone I’ve met,” said Alea, and was surprised, saying it, to realise that it was true.

Round five was hard.

It was on the morning of the third day, the sixty-third, early, before the sun was high. The big crowd hadn’t come in yet. They were coming for the afternoon: the Gate, the second trial, Tiger’s gate that he’d built with the carpenters, the Stronghold. You could feel it in the city all morning, a kind of hum, like a hive before swarming. The stands were half full. The Singles were starting on the side sand behind the Lists. The fledglings were on the four rings, and there were only eight of them left.

Round five was a boy called Corran Tolley, and he was Tempered too.

He was nineteen and from Solmere, a guild boy, not a noble; a big quiet boy with a heavy sword and a heavy face who’d come up through four rounds without anyone noticing him much. Alea hadn’t noticed him. She noticed him now. He came into the near ring and stood in it like a rock, with his sword low, and looked at her, and didn’t do anything at all.

He waited.

He waited like her father. Not like Hako. Not like the guild girl with the spear, who breathed. Like her father, on the end of a jetty, with the line in the water. As if he had all day.

Alea’s stomach went cold.

She stood in the near ring with the black sword’s point on the sand and looked at a boy who was waiting for her to move, and for the first time since round one, she didn’t know what to do. Every bout so far, she’d waited longer. She’d waited until they went, and gone first, while they were still deciding to. That was the whole thing. That was what she’d learned on wet sand at dawn for a year.

You couldn’t go first against somebody who wasn’t going to go.

The near stand went quiet. Then very quiet.

Alea stood there.

She was frightened. Properly. Not the way she’d been frightened of the coast girl at the well, which had been the good kind, the kind you could sit next to. This was the other kind. Cold, in her stomach, coming up into her chest. A hundred and forty fledglings and she’d beaten four of them and here, in round five, was a boy who did exactly what she did, and did it better, because he was bigger and older and had nothing to prove.

She looked up.

She didn’t decide to. She just did. Past the master’s platform, past the royal box, to the fourth tier of the east stand. To the end of a bench.

He was there. He was always there. He was sitting very still, with the rod across his knees. He didn’t wave. He didn’t do anything.

But she remembered a hill. The Last Look. Midnight. A city glowing on a plain. I’m frightened, she’d said. And he’d said good. And then he’d told her about her mother.

Your mother was frightened every time she went into an infirmary tent. Every single time. She’d stand at the flap with her hands together and her ears flat, and count to three in her head, and then she’d go in and be the best healer on the border for twelve hours.

Be frightened. Then count to three.

Alea looked back at the boy in the ring.

She let herself be frightened. All of it. The cold in her stomach and her chest. She didn’t push it down. She stood in the near ring of the Lists with a black sword in her hands and let her ears go flat, the way her mother’s had at the flap of a tent, and counted.

One.

The boy didn’t move.

Two.

The boy didn’t move.

Three.

And on three, she understood.

She didn’t go first. She didn’t wait longer. She did the thing her mother had done at the flap of the tent. She stopped standing at the edge of the thing she was frightened of, and went in.

Not fast. Not early. Just in. She stepped into the ring toward him, one step, two, with the black sword coming up off the sand in its long line, and she didn’t let it fall. She didn’t let anything fall. She brought it round on purpose, all the way, with her own two arms and her whole frightened weight behind it, the way you’d swing an axe at a tree that had to come down.

The boy had been waiting for her to move first, so he could move after. That was his whole plan. He’d been waiting for her to wait.

She hadn’t waited.

He got his heavy sword up. Just. It met Astra halfway, and Astra was half a slab of black iron that had taken six soldiers and a winch to move, swung by a Tempered girl who’d stopped being careful. Alea felt it go through her arms like a bell. The boy’s sword stayed in his hands. But his feet didn’t stay where they were. He went back. One step. Two. Three. Over the chalk line.

The marshal’s flag went up.

Alea stood in the middle of the near ring, breathing hard, with Astra’s point back on the sand, and her ears still flat, and her hands shaking on the grip Gabe had cut to the shape of how she held a cup.

“Out,” said the marshal. “Over the line. The bout to Alea, of Wrenhaven.”

The boy looked down at his feet, outside the chalk. Then up at her. His heavy face did something slow.

“You didn’t wait,” he said.

“No,” said Alea. Her voice came out wobbly. “I counted to three.”

The boy thought about that for a long time, the way big quiet boys do.

“Huh,” he said. And nodded. And walked off the ring.

Petra didn’t say anything when she came off. Petra put her hand flat on the top of Alea’s head, briefly, the way her father did, and took it away again.

Xav said one word.

“Good.”

Alea sat down on the fledglings’ bench, very suddenly, because her legs had decided to. She put Astra across her knees. Her hands were still shaking. She looked at them.

She’d stand at the flap with her hands together and her ears flat.

She’d never once seen her mother go into a tent. She’d been at the cabin, with Rowan and Odette, all through the war. She’d been eight. She’d only ever known her mother in the cabin and on the jetty and in the kitchen with her cold hands, laughing at her father. She’d never seen her be frightened of anything.

And she’d just done it. In the near ring of the Lists. The exact thing. Ears flat, three, in.

She looked up at the fourth tier.

He’d stood up. He was the only one in that section of the stand on his feet, again, the way he’d stood up after round one. Nobody near him noticed. He was standing very still with his hand over his face.

Alea looked at him for a long time.

Then she looked down at her own hands, still shaking on the grip, and found that she was smiling.

Sorrel won her round five on the far ring at about the same time.

Alea didn’t see it. She heard it: a great roar from the west end of the Lists, and then laughter, and then more roaring. When she looked, a big cadet was sitting in the sand outside the far ring with an expression of total bewilderment, and the coast girl was standing in the middle of the ring with her two hooks over her shoulders, grinning at the sky like a gap in a fence.

“She pulled him over the line,” said Xav, who’d gone over to look. “By his belt. With both hooks. Like a sack of fish off a boat.”

“Of course she did,” said Alea.

Four left. Lucan read them out from the master’s platform at noon, with the big crowd coming in for the Gate and the stands filling up round him, in a voice that had given up on being steady three days ago. Four fledglings. Two semifinals, on the morning of the sixty-fifth day. Then a final.

Alea, of Wrenhaven. Copper. Aura. Tempered.

Sorrel, of Gullhaven. Copper. Aura. Tempered.

And two others, a cadet and a noble boy, whose names Alea heard and immediately forgot.

They weren’t drawn against each other. Opposite halves. If they both won their semifinals, they’d meet in the final.

Alea looked across the sand, toward the west end, where the far ring was. Sorrel was looking back at her. Across the whole width of the Lists. With her hooks on her shoulders.

Sorrel lifted one hook. Just a little. Like somebody on a quay lifting a hand to a boat going out.

Alea lifted her paper fishing rod back.

She was frightened. She was smiling. She counted to three anyway, because it was a good habit, and now it was hers.
