The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 4: Threads

Chapter 117

Nobody Follows

6,212 words · 27 min read

Garrow came home on the thirty-seventh day, from the east, behind his oxen.

Rowan saw him from the town hall steps. He’d come out to sign for a cartload of lime and found the square doing the thing it did, these days, a little too often: going quiet in a ripple, outward from one end. Not the lake road, this time. The east road. The old drover’s track that came in past the tannery and the pound, which nobody used much now except for sheep.

And down it, at an ox’s pace, came the brewer’s dray.

Rowan knew it by the wheel. The near rear wheel had wobbled since the autumn, since eight men and the dray had carried an anvil down the lake road and back for a king. He’d told the brewer to get it seen to. The brewer had said he would, in the spring. And then the dray had gone east in the first frost with a black iron stone on it, and a sack of children’s pebbles, and Garrow on the box, and not come back.

It came back now. Slow and dusty, with the two big dun oxen walking as if they’d been walking for a year, and four of the town militia trudging alongside in the coats they’d left in, which were not the coats they’d need for the end of spring. Old Wat, at the front, with his bad leg and his pike over his shoulder. And on the box, with the reins in his big burned hands and a winter’s beard on him that made him look like his own father, Garrow.

The smith saw Rowan on the steps and pulled up the oxen in the middle of the square, in front of half the town.

He didn’t get down. He sat there on the box for a moment, very red, the way he went red at everything, and looked at Rowan.

“Is he still here?” said Garrow.

Not how’s my forge. Not how’s my mother. Rowan felt something in his chest go tight and warm.

“Down at the lake,” said Rowan. “Where else.”

“I’ve got something for him.” Garrow put a hand flat on his coat, over the left side, the way a man touches a purse in a crowd. “I promised I’d put it in his hand. Nobody else’s.”

“Then you’d better come down off that box,” said Rowan, “and walk.”

He walked down with him.

He told himself it was the rule. It wasn’t, quite. The rule had changed, over the winter, the way rivers change: nobody had decided it, and nobody had said so, but it had changed all the same. Five girls had walked down the lake road in the snow with lanterns, and come back up it at midnight, and the town hadn’t followed. Thirteen people off a salt barge had come down it, two of them humming, and the town hadn’t followed then either. These days half the summoned in the kingdom seemed to go up and down that road twice a day, and the hens complained about it, and the town stood at the top and watched and didn’t follow, because that was still the rule. But it was a different rule now. It used to mean leave him alone. Now it meant something closer to let him have them.

So Rowan didn’t need to walk Garrow down. Garrow had worked the bellows at the lake forge for nine days in the autumn. Garrow knew the way.

He walked him down anyway. He’d been walking people down that road, or not walking them down it, for eight years and the better part of a ninth, and he found he wanted to see this.

The lake was very blue. The hawthorn was going over along the wall, dropping its white on the road like late snow. The shingle below the cabin was busy, the way it had been every morning since the barge: a knot of the summoned at the water’s edge with practice swords, and the fox girl among them with her black slab across her back, and the tall swordswoman from Duskhollow, and Xavier, standing very still, the way he did. And on the end of the jetty, with his back to all of it and a line in the water, the fisherman.

He turned round before they reached the shingle. He always did. He’d have felt the oxen come in, Rowan thought, from a mile off. He’d have felt Garrow.

He came down the jetty and across the stones, and stopped in front of the smith, and looked at him. At the beard. At the coat. At the hand on the coat.

“You stayed the winter,” said Ars.

“Snowed in.” Garrow was purple now. “The passes. I didn’t— I should have sent word.”

“You did. Group 3 told me.” Ars was smiling. Rowan didn’t see him smile often, even now, even with the lake full of them; he’d learned, over eight years, to watch for it the way you watch for a kingfisher. “In the channel. Garrow is snowed in and teaching Salty to smith and nobody can stop him.”

Garrow didn’t know what that meant. Rowan didn’t either. Garrow looked at the ground, and then he put his hand inside his coat and took out a folded paper, sealed with candle wax and a little strip of blue across the fold, much handled, soft at the corners, the way paper goes when it’s been carried against somebody’s chest for a month.

He held it out.

“Into one pair of hands,” said Garrow.

Ars took it.

He read it on the end of the jetty.

He didn’t ask them to go. He didn’t ask them to stay, either. He just walked out to the end, with the letter, and sat down with his feet over the water the way he’d sat every night for eight years, and broke the wax with his thumb. Rowan and Garrow stood at the land end of the jetty and didn’t follow him out. Behind them, on the shingle, the sparring had stopped. Nobody had said anything. It had just stopped.

Rowan couldn’t read it from there. He couldn’t have read it from anywhere. He’d seen the outside of it as it changed hands, and it was in the other writing, the one the summoned used, the one with the round letters that slid out of your eyes when you tried to hold them. The writing on the black stone at the grave, that Garrow had carried east.

So he watched the man read it instead.

He’d been watching Ars read letters for twelve years. Letters from Halvard, mostly, the Advisor, in that small dry hand that never said anything it didn’t have to. Ars read those the way you’d read a list of what the frost had killed. Once, a letter from the king; he’d read that one twice, with his jaw tight, and put it in the stove. And once, in the winter, the morning after the five girls came down the road with their lanterns, he’d read something in the air in front of him that Rowan couldn’t see, standing in the egg-woman’s doorway with his eggs in a cloth, and stood there so long the egg-woman had asked if he was well.

He read this one slowly. Line by line. Rowan could see him stop at the end of each one, the way you stop at the top of a stair.

At the first line he laughed. Out loud, short, surprised out of him.

At the second he put his hand over his mouth.

At the third he didn’t move at all for a long time.

And at the bottom, at the very end, after a long stillness, Rowan saw his shoulders go. Not much. Just once, the way a man’s shoulders go when he’s been holding a weight for a long time and somebody comes and takes the other end of it without asking.

Then he laughed again. Quietly. And said something to the lake, in his own tongue, that Rowan didn’t catch.

“What did they say?” said Rowan, later.

It was dusk. Garrow had gone up to his forge and his mother and found both exactly where he’d left them, and his mother had hit him with a ladle for not writing. The oxen were in the brewer’s yard. The summoned were at their fire on the shingle, where they were every night now, loud and slow, with the red pot. Ars had come up the lake road at dusk to buy his eggs, the way he always did, and had stopped at the top by the wall, where Rowan was leaning, and stood beside him without being asked.

“Group 3,” said Ars. “From the fort.” He looked at the lake. “One of them says I owe him eleven dinners. One says the tree’s asleep and it’s fine. One says thank you for the stone.” He paused. “One says something’s holding the cold under her grave, and he doesn’t know what, and he thinks I do.”

Rowan waited.

“And one of them counted,” said Ars. “From a wall, in the snow, a month east. He counted every one of us. And he got the right number.”

Rowan didn’t know what that meant. He didn’t ask.

“And at the bottom,” said Ars, “the loudest one says they’ll be in Valcrest at midsummer, if I’m anywhere near.” He was quiet for a moment. “No pressure, he says.”

“Will you be anywhere near?”

Ars looked at him. In the dusk Rowan couldn’t make out his face, but then he never could; nobody could. He’d stopped trying years ago. He went by the voice, and the shoulders.

“Yes,” said Ars.

He put his hand in his coat, over the left side, where Garrow had kept it. As if he were checking it was still there.

“Garrow says to tell you,” said Rowan, “that the short strong one is making something. At the fort’s forge. Since the winter. He wouldn’t tell me what.” He considered. “He went very red. Redder than usual. He said he was sworn not to say, and that it isn’t for you, and that you’re to act surprised anyway.”

“I’m always surprised,” said Ars. “Ask anyone.”

The town knew by the next morning that he was going.

Rowan didn’t tell it. Nobody told it. It had known since Kestra came down the lake road with a letter from the king, really, the way it knew when the lake was going to freeze: a feeling in the air, a change in how people stood. But it knew the next morning, because at the third hour Ars came up into the square with Alea and went into Odette’s office at the town hall and shut the door, and when they came out Odette had a list.

Odette always had a list. But this one she held in both hands, the way you’d hold a letter from a dead relative, and she stood on the town hall steps and read it out to the whole square in her clear hard secretary’s voice without once looking up, and Rowan, standing at the back by the well, saw that her hands were shaking.

Two wagons. The brewer’s dray, with the wheel seen to, today, and the miller’s long cart. Four horses from the militia stable and the posting house. Barrels. Flour. Oats. Canvas. Rope. Every pair of boots in Wrenhaven that would fit somebody who’d walked up a river in the ones they had on. Waterskins. Pots. And at the bottom, after a pause so long that half the square leaned forward, in a voice that didn’t shake at all:

“Somebody to feed the hens.”

There was a silence.

Then the whole square turned and looked at Rowan.

“Oh, no,” said Rowan.

He went down that afternoon to be shown.

It was absurd. He knew it was absurd. He’d lived in Wrenhaven all his life apart from the war. He’d kept hens. Everybody had kept hens. He did not need to be shown how to throw a handful of grain at three birds and lift a lid and take out what they’d left him.

He went anyway.

“The big red one goes first,” said Ars, in the run behind the cabin, with the grain bowl in one hand. “Or the others won’t eat. She sits on the lid. You have to lift her off, and she won’t like it.”

“She doesn’t like you.”

“She doesn’t like anyone.” Ars considered the big red hen, who was looking at him with the flat yellow loathing of a creature who’d been looking at him like that every morning for years. “She liked Severin.”

“Everyone likes Severin.”

“That’s true.” Ars threw a handful of grain. The red hen ignored it, pointedly, and walked over to Rowan, and stood on his boot. “Oh,” said Ars.

Rowan looked down at the hen on his boot.

“Don’t,” he said.

“I’m not saying anything.”

“You’re thinking it.”

“I’m thinking you’ll be fine,” said Ars. And then, more quietly, looking at the hen and not at him: “You always are.”

Rowan didn’t answer that.

They did the rest of it standing at the cabin door. The rain barrel. The shutters on the lake side, which stuck. The stove, which had a temper. The pumpkins, which would want watering if it didn’t rain, and it would rain. The lines on the jetty, which Rowan was not to touch, and the nets in the shed, which Rowan was also not to touch, and the books on the high shelf, which Rowan had never once been allowed to touch in twelve years and didn’t intend to start.

“And the pike,” said Ars.

“What about the pike?”

“If anybody catches it while I’m gone,” said Ars, very seriously, “I’ll know.”

Rowan looked at him.

“Eight years you’ve been trying to catch that fish,” he said.

“Seven. Alea says seven. It’s had six of my hooks. And Xav’s, last week.” He looked out at the lake. Somewhere far out, as if it had heard, something turned over in the blue, and the rings went out slow and wide. “It’s mine,” said Ars. “Somebody else catching it would be like somebody else finishing your book.”

“I’ll put up a notice,” said Rowan.

“Thank you.”

He came up to the house that night. Late. After the fire on the shingle had gone down to embers and the summoned had gone up the road in their loud slow knot and Rowan had gone to bed and not slept.

He knocked. He’d never knocked, in twelve years. He just came, if he came at all, and stood at the gate until Rowan noticed him, which was usually at once.

Rowan opened the door in his shirtsleeves with a candle.

“You’ll want a cup,” he said.

“I’ll want a cup,” said Ars.

They sat in the kitchen. Odette’s lace on the dresser, that she’d made for the girl’s room and the girl had outgrown. The candle. Two cups of the brewer’s second-best, which was better than his best, though nobody had ever told him. The window open on the warm dark, and the town asleep, and somewhere down the hill the lake.

Ars turned his cup round on the table for a long time.

“Come with us,” he said.

Rowan had known he’d say it. He’d known for two days. He’d lain awake last night working out what he’d answer, and he’d had it by dawn, word for word, and now that it came he found he didn’t need any of it.

“No,” he said.

Ars looked at him.

“Somebody’s got to keep it,” said Rowan. “The road. The lake.” He shrugged, one big slow shoulder. “The hens.”

“Odette could keep the hens.”

“Odette would have the hens keeping ledgers.” He drank. “I’m sixty-one. I haven’t been further south than the posting house at Caddow since the war, and I don’t want to. I’ve seen the south. It’s flat and it’s hot and everyone in it wants to sell you a horse.” He set the cup down. “And somebody’s got to be standing here. At the top of that road. When you come back up it.”

Ars didn’t say anything for a while.

“Twelve years,” he said at last. “You’ve never once let me pay it back.”

“Pay what back?”

“You know what.” Ars’s voice was very low. “You stood in that door for a season. With a sword. While I was two hundred miles away. And she was nine, and she cried every night, and you sat on the stairs with her. Odette told me. Years after. You never did.” He breathed. “And when I came home with nothing, you moved out of the doorway, and you’ve stood in front of every person who ever came down that road since. Every bard. Every trader. Every soldier who’d had a drink and wanted to see the Black Blade. The whole town. For eight years. And you never once let me thank you for it.”

“You thanked me,” said Rowan. “In the autumn. At the bellows. You laughed.”

“That wasn’t—“

“It was,” said Rowan. “That was the whole of it. That was all I wanted. I’d have stood there another eight years for that.”

The candle guttered and steadied.

Ars looked at his cup. Then he looked up, at Rowan, across the table, with that face nobody could hold on to, and Rowan felt, the way he sometimes did, the room go very slightly quiet around them. As if the house were listening.

“They call me something,” said Ars. “The ones from the barge. You’ve heard them.”

“I’ve heard them.”

“And the girls. Something else.”

“I’ve heard that too.” Rowan turned his cup. “I’m not deaf, and the whole town’s got ears. They call you Declan, the ten off the barge. The five girls call you Ice.” He paused. “The girl calls you Dad, and so far as I’m concerned that’s the only one I need.”

“It was my name,” said Ars. “Declan. Before. A long way from here.” He stopped. “I could tell you. All of it. Tonight. I’d like to. You’ve earned it more than anyone in this—“

“Don’t,” said Rowan.

Ars stopped.

“Not tonight,” said Rowan. He said it gently. “Not the night before you go. You’ll tell it badly, and you’ll be thinking about the road the whole time, and I’ll be thinking about the hens.” He reached across and put his big hand flat on the table, between them, near Ars’s, not touching it. “Go south. Go and stand in front of whoever you’ve got to stand in front of. Tell the king. Tell them all.” He breathed. “And then come home, and come up this road, and knock on that door like a stranger, and I’ll get the cups out. And you can tell me then. All of it. From the beginning. And I’ll listen to every word.”

Ars looked at the hand on the table for a long time.

“Which one do you want me to call you?” said Rowan. “When you come back. Which name.”

“The one you’ve always used,” said Ars.

“Then that’s settled,” said Rowan, and picked up his cup again, and they sat there in Odette’s kitchen with the lace and the candle and the open window and finished the brewer’s second-best, and didn’t talk about anything at all, and it was one of the best hours Rowan Hale had spent in his life.

They left on the fortieth day, at dawn.

Rowan had thought, for two days, about how the town would take it. He’d lain awake planning for it the way he’d once planned for a winter siege. He’d thought there’d be crying, and there was. He’d thought there’d be children running alongside, and dogs, and the egg-woman’s youngest under the wheels, and he’d posted Wat and two of the militia along the square to stop exactly that. He’d thought there’d be a speech wanted, and he’d written one, and torn it up, and written it again, and put it in his coat.

He hadn’t thought the whole town would be up before the sun.

But it was. He came out of his door in the grey light with the hens’ grain in a sack over his shoulder, because he’d decided to go straight down after, and the square was full. Silent and full. Every house. Every trade. Edric with flour to his elbows. The miller’s wife. Old Mother Breck, ninety, on a stool somebody had carried out for her, with a blanket over her knees. Garrow, bearded, with his ladle-bruised mother. The brewer, beside his dray with its new wheel, looking at the wheel. The children in a long row along the wall of the lake road, the way they’d stood in the autumn with their pebbles, very straight, not one of them making a sound.

Nobody was at the bottom of the lake road. Nobody had gone down. They were all standing at the top, the way they always had, the way Rowan had taught them to without ever once saying it, and every face in Wrenhaven was turned toward the lake.

The wagons were drawn up in the square already, loaded. Bram, the big gruff guard from the militia hall, on the box of the miller’s cart with the reins in his fists, red-eyed. Sabine, the Kingsferry swordswoman, on the dray, looking at the crowd as if it owed her money. Maren beside them with a list. Of course Maren had a list. Emrys the chanter at her elbow, humming something under his breath that made the nearest of the crowd go quiet in a different way. And around them the summoned, the fifteen, with packs on their backs and new boots on their feet, standing in a loose knot in the middle of the square looking at the town looking at them, and not one of them, Rowan saw, quite able to look back.

Then they came up the lake road.

Not the fifteen. The fifteen were already in the square. It was the others: the three who’d slept on the fisherman’s floor for six weeks, and the tall Duskhollow swordswoman who’d gone down to him every dawn to learn, coming up from the cabin together, slow, the way you walk the last time down a familiar road. Petra, with her pack. The big man, Gabe, with his strange square blade on his back. The quiet one with the threads, Deston, looking up at the town as if he could see something written over it. Xavier.

And in the middle of them, with a pack on his back and a rod over his shoulder and nothing in his hands, the fisherman.

And beside him, with a black sword nearly as tall as she was across her shoulders and her ears straight up and her face perfectly, perfectly still, the girl.

The square didn’t make a sound.

Ars came up the last of the road, past the wall and the row of children, and stopped at the top, where the lake road met the square. Where Rowan had been standing for eight years.

He stood there. And he looked at them. At the whole of Wrenhaven, standing in its square in the dawn with its face washed and its good coat on. At Mother Breck on her stool. At the children along the wall. Slowly. One at a time, it felt like, though there were five hundred of them. The way he’d looked at the five girls on the market road a year and a half ago. The way he’d looked at the square the day he’d asked it for stones.

Rowan put his hand in his coat and touched the speech.

He didn’t take it out.

It was the egg-woman who moved first.

She came out of the crowd at the edge of the market with a basket on her arm. A small brown wiry woman, sixty if she was a day, who’d sold Ars his eggs at dusk every day for twelve years and taken his copper and said evening and never once said anything else. She came across the square in the silence, with five hundred people watching her, and stopped in front of him, and held out the basket.

Eggs. Two dozen, in straw, the brown ones from the speckled hens he liked.

“For the road,” said the egg-woman.

Ars looked at the basket. Then he put his hand in his coat, the way he’d done every evening for twelve years, for the copper.

“No,” said the egg-woman. “You’ve paid.”

She put the basket in his hands, and turned round, and walked back into the crowd, and didn’t look back, and Rowan saw her put her apron over her face as soon as she’d reached it.

After that it was easier.

Not a lot. Nobody cheered. Nobody ran. But people came out of the crowd one at a time, the way they’d come down the lake road with stones in the autumn, and put things into the summoned’s hands and the girl’s hands and his. A loaf, from Edric, still warm. A twist of salt. A pair of the miller’s wife’s socks, for whichever of them had the biggest feet, and Gabe put his hand up, and the whole square very nearly laughed. A clay whistle, from one of the children, for the girl, who took it, and turned it over, and blew it once, very softly, so that the child went pink to the ears. Old Mother Breck didn’t get up off her stool; she held out a stone instead, a small flat white one from the lake shore, and Ars went down on one knee to take it from her the way he’d gone down on one knee in the autumn for every child with a pebble, and she held on to his hand for a moment longer than she needed to, and said something to him that nobody else heard.

He said thank you to every one of them. Every single one. No names. He never used names. He said it the way he’d said it in the autumn, five times to five people, as if each one were the first.

And then it was done, and the square went quiet again, and Maren looked at her list and then at the sun coming up over the hills, and then, for some reason, at Rowan.

So Rowan walked out into the middle of the square.

He hadn’t been going to. He’d decided, somewhere around the egg-woman, that he wouldn’t. The speech was in his coat, and it could stay there. There wasn’t a speech in the world that would do for this, and he’d been mayor long enough to know when to keep his mouth shut.

But they were all looking at him. The whole town. The way they’d looked at him every night for eight years, without knowing they were doing it, when some trader or bard or drunk soldier came into the square asking for the lake road: Rowan will deal with it. Waiting for him to stand in front of the thing.

So he stood in front of it.

He stood in the middle of the square, between the town and the south gate, big and slow and grey, with a sack of hens’ grain over his shoulder, and turned round to face Wrenhaven, and said it out loud. For the first time. After eight years of never once needing to.

“Nobody follows.”

His voice carried. It always had. He’d learned that on a parade ground, a long time ago.

“He’s going south,” said Rowan. “With the girl, and with his friends, to see the king. And we’re not going with him. Not to the gate. Not past it. Not one of you, and not one of your dogs.” He looked along the row of children at the wall. “Nobody follows.”

Nobody moved.

He turned round.

Ars was looking at him. Across the square, with the basket of eggs in one hand and the old woman’s stone in the other and the rod over his shoulder. Rowan couldn’t see his face. He never could.

He could see his shoulders.

Then the fisherman nodded, once, the way he’d nodded at the gate on the morning of the stones. And turned, and walked across the square toward the south gate, with his daughter beside him and his three brothers round him and the tall swordswoman behind, and the fifteen falling in round them, and Bram shaking the reins, and the brewer’s dray with its new wheel creaking into motion behind. And the whole of Wrenhaven stood in its square in the dawn and watched him go, and didn’t follow.

The egg-woman’s youngest broke at the gate.

Rowan had known somebody would. He’d have bet on the egg-woman’s youngest, if he’d been a betting man: a scrap of a boy, six, all knees, who’d run at the summoned every morning since the barge to see the man with the snake-flat sword on his back and been hauled off by his collar every morning since. He went under Wat’s arm like a ferret and through the legs of the militia and out across the square after the wagons, flat out, barefoot, toward the gate.

Rowan caught him at the arch. Not hard. One big hand on the back of his shirt, and the boy’s feet still going.

“Why?” The boy was nearly crying. He twisted round. “Why can’t we go? Everyone’s going. Why—“

Rowan crouched down. It hurt his knees. He did it anyway, there in the gate arch with the dust of the wagons still hanging in the air and the boy’s mother pushing through the crowd behind.

“Because he’s coming back,” said Rowan.

The boy stared at him.

“You don’t follow people who are coming back,” said Rowan. “That’s not what you do. You stand at the top of the road, and you wait for them. That’s the job. That’s the whole job.” He let go of the shirt. “It’s a good job. I’ve done it eight years. You can help me, if you like.”

The boy looked past him. Through the arch. At the south road going away pale between the hedges, and the two wagons, and the knot of people walking with them, getting smaller. And at the front, a little ahead of the rest, the way he’d always walked, one plain man with a rod over his shoulder.

At the first bend in the road, where the hedges turned and the hill would take them, the man stopped.

He turned round.

He stood there in the road for a long moment, small at that distance, a quarter of a mile off, looking back at Wrenhaven. At the gate. At the big grey man crouched in the arch with a small boy beside him. At the town behind.

Then he lifted the rod. Once. High, over his head, the way you’d lift a hand.

Rowan lifted his.

And then the hill took them, and the road was empty, and there was nothing between the gate and the bend but the dust settling and a lark going up somewhere over the barley, and the boy beside him, very quietly, starting to cry.

He went down the lake road alone.

It was the first time he’d walked it on his own with nobody at the bottom. He noticed that halfway down, between the wall and the reeds, and stopped, and stood there, with the sack on his shoulder and the hawthorn dropping on him, and found that he didn’t like it at all.

The cabin was shut. Not locked. It had never been locked. The shutters were closed on the lake side, the stuck one too, and the step was swept, and on the step, where somebody had left it for him, there was a cup. An ugly dented two-note thing that would have been a kettle if a kettle could be ashamed of itself.

No. Not the kettle. The kettle was in Calvin’s pack; the whole town knew that. Rowan picked it up and turned it over. A cup. One of the cabin’s two cups, the chipped one. And inside it, folded small, a scrap of paper in the plain clear script of Aurelan, the kind anybody could read.

Keep the cup. I’ll want it back.

A.

Rowan sat down on the step.

He didn’t go out on the jetty. That was the man’s. He’d never once set foot on the end of it in twelve years, and he wasn’t going to start now, on the first morning, like a thief. He sat on the step instead, with the cup in his hands and the hens complaining behind the cabin about their breakfast, and looked at the lake.

It was very blue. It was very still. And something was missing from it.

He couldn’t have said what. He’d never noticed it was there. But he’d lived by this lake his whole life, and for twelve years now there’d been something about it on still mornings, a kind of weight, a kind of held feeling, the way a room feels when someone’s sitting very quietly in the corner of it. He’d thought it was just the lake. Everybody had. The fishermen said the water by the north shore was the calmest in the lake country, and had been for years, and nobody knew why. The children said the fish came to the jetty because they liked the fisherman. The town said a lot of things.

It was gone. Just like that. The lake was a lake.

Rowan sat with that for a while.

Behind the cabin, the big red hen stopped complaining. He heard her come round the corner of the run, the way she did, as if she owned the hill, and walk across the yard, and stop. And then, after a moment, climb up onto the step beside him, and settle there, with her feathers puffed out, looking at the lake too.

Rowan looked at her.

“Don’t,” he said.

The hen didn’t.

Odette came down at noon with two more cups and a jug, and sat on the step on the other side of the hen, and didn’t say anything for a long time either.

“I wrote to the Advisor,” said Rowan eventually. “This morning. Before they went. My own rider.”

“What did you say?”

“That he’s left the lake.” Rowan turned the chipped cup in his hands. “That he’s on the south road, on foot, with the girl and fifteen of them, and he’ll be at Valcrest for midsummer. That the king should know.” He paused. “And that he was smiling. I put that at the end. I don’t know why.”

“Because the Advisor will want to know that more than the rest,” said Odette.

“Yes,” said Rowan. “That’s why.”

The lake lapped at the posts. Out in the middle, very far off, something turned over in the blue, and the rings went out, slow and wide, all the way to the jetty, and broke softly against the end where nobody was sitting.

“There’s your pike,” said Odette.

“It’s not my pike.” Rowan watched the rings. “If anybody catches it while he’s gone, he’ll know.”

Odette looked at him sideways.

“He said that?”

“He said that.”

She was quiet for a moment. Then she put her hand over her mouth, and her shoulders went, and Rowan realised after a moment that the city secretary of Wrenhaven, who had run the stones like a general and the packing like a siege and never once in twelve years been seen to lose her composure in public, was laughing so hard she couldn’t breathe.

He started laughing too. He couldn’t help it. On the step of an empty cabin, with a hen between them and the chipped cup in his hands, the two of them who’d stood in that door eight years ago with a nine-year-old girl asleep upstairs, laughing like fools at the end of a jetty with nobody on it.

When they’d stopped, Odette wiped her eyes and poured from the jug.

“How long?” she said.

“A week at Valcrest, the king says. The road there and back.” He counted. “Six weeks. Seven. He’ll be back for the late pumpkins.”

“And then?”

“And then he’ll knock on my door,” said Rowan, “like a stranger. And I’ll get the cups out.” He drank. “And he’s going to tell me everything.”

Odette didn’t ask what everything was. She’d known him thirty years. She just nodded, and drank, and the two of them sat on the step with the hen in the warm noon light and looked at the empty end of the jetty, and the lake, and the road going up the hill behind them to the town where nobody had followed.

He’d stood in front of things all his life.

It turned out, Rowan thought, that the hardest thing he’d ever stood in front of was a road with nobody on it, and the easiest thing he’d ever done was not follow it.

He’d do it every day until the man came home.
