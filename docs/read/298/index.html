The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 10: The Sword's Country

Chapter 298

The Lamp

4,331 words · 19 min read

She wrapped the grip the week before the Widow went.

She hadn’t told anybody she was going to. She hadn’t told herself, really; she’d just gone up to Garrow’s on the Tuesday with Astra on her back and asked him for leather. Good leather, she’d said. The best he had. Enough for a grip. And old Garrow had looked at her over his spectacles, and at the black hilt over her shoulder, and not asked why, and gone into the back and come out with a hide she knew he’d been keeping for something.

“Calf,” he said. “Soaked in oak bark a year. It’ll take a shape.” He held it out. “Your father’s never let anybody touch that grip.”

“I know.”

“Does he know you’re doing it?”

“No,” said Alea. She took the hide. “He will.”

She did it in the evenings, on the step, where he could see.

That was the rule. It had always been the rule, since Thea read the folds; it was the rule she’d made for Deston on the jetty. Where he can see. Not because she thought anything would happen. Because it was his sword before it was hers, and you didn’t do things to somebody’s sword behind their back.

She unwound the old leather first. It took two evenings. It came off in a long dark strip, stiff and shiny with use, worn to the shape of a hand that wasn’t hers: bigger, wider across the palm, with a hollow in it near the guard where his thumb had sat for eight years. She’d gripped over that hollow for three. Her hand had never filled it. She’d stopped noticing, the way you stop noticing a step that’s a little higher than the others.

She didn’t throw it away. She rolled it up very carefully and tied it with a bit of Xola’s ribbon and put it in the box on the shelf over the bed, with her mother’s comb, and her mother’s scissors, and the medicine bag.

He watched her do that from the jetty. She saw him see it. He didn’t come up.

Then the new leather. She cut it in long strips with her good knife, the one she’d packed in the autumn to go west with him and unpacked again. She soaked it in the lake. She wound it on wet, the way Garrow had shown her, tight, a little at a time, each turn lying over the last by half its width, so that when it dried it would shrink and hold. It took four evenings. Her fingers went raw. She didn’t hurry.

She wound it to her hand. That was the whole point. Not his. Hers. Where her thumb sat, near the guard, she made it a hair thicker, so there’d be no hollow. Where her fingers went, she made the turns closer. She wrapped it the way you’d make a bed for somebody you loved, with every corner turned, slowly, thinking about them the whole time.

And the whole time, the blade lay across her knees, black, not shining, saying what it said.

She could hear it, now. Since Deston. She didn’t know if she’d always been able to or if she’d just never listened. A note, very low, lower than anything, coming up out of the iron through her knees like the hum off a bell that’s been ringing so long you’ve stopped hearing it. Stay. Thirty-two thousand times. On its own. For three hundred years. With nobody at the other end.

She wound the leather and listened to it.

She asked Deston on the last evening, before they went.

The Widow was loaded. Old Haskett had his rudder back on and his boys had painted something on the stern that Dennis said was a fish and Calvin said was a pike and Haskett said was none of their business. Dennis had his cheese: six wheels of it, from the dairy at the top of the lake road, roped down under a tarpaulin, which he said he’d sell in Kingsferry for three times what he’d paid and which Sabine said she’d want a cut of. Rachael had been to the infirmary in Wrenhaven every afternoon for a month and come back every evening cold and quiet and very pleased with herself, and Sister Odile had cried when she said goodbye, and Rachael had let her hold her hand, which Gabe said was the biggest thing he’d ever seen her do.

And Gabe had sat on the shingle by the ash every night. Counting the leaves. There were nine.

Alea brought Astra down to the jetty at dusk with the new grip on it, dry now, dark and close and tight, and put it down on the planks in front of Deston, and didn’t unwrap it from anything this time, because there was nothing to unwrap.

“Will you look,” she said. “Please.”

Deston looked at the grip. At her hands, raw at the fingertips. At her face.

“You did it yourself,” he said.

“Yes.”

“For you.”

“For me,” said Alea. “Fitted to me. My thumb. My fingers.” She looked at the black blade. “You said made things have a thread. If somebody made them carefully. For somebody. And it hasn’t got one. Not to anybody. Not to them, not to Dad, not to me.” She swallowed. “I’ve carried it three years and it’s never been mine. Not really. It’s been his, that I’m holding. And theirs, that nobody’s holding.” She put her hand flat on the new leather. “So I made a bit of it mine. On purpose. And I want to know if it worked.”

Deston didn’t say anything for a while.

Then he let his eyes go loose, the way she’d seen him do it on the jetty with Astra on the blanket, the way he did it with everything now: looking past things. At what went between them.

She watched his face. She’d watched it the first time, when there hadn’t been anything, and seen it go strange and far off, like a man hearing a door close in a house he thought was empty.

It didn’t go like that.

It went soft. At the corners.

“There,” said Deston.

Alea didn’t move.

“It’s a hair,” he said. “It’s the faintest thing I’ve ever seen. It’s not on the blade. The blade’s still nothing. Still just the word, on its own.” He leaned closer. “It’s on the leather. From the grip. Going up—“ He turned his head. Followed it with his eyes, through the air, very slowly, the way you’d follow a hair of spider silk in the evening light. Up from the hilt. Across the space between them. To her. “To you.”

“To me,” said Alea.

“To you,” said Deston. “It’s new. It’s very thin. It’s the newest thread I’ve ever seen; it’s thinner than your cups.” He sat back. “But it’s there. Somebody’s at the other end of it now. A bit.”

She didn’t cry. She thought she might. She didn’t.

She put her hand on the grip, the new leather, fitted to her thumb, with no hollow in it. And under her hand, down in the iron, the low note went on saying what it said. Stay. The same. Thirty-two thousand times. She didn’t think it had changed. She didn’t think she’d changed the word at all.

But it wasn’t on its own.

On the end of the jetty, her father was sitting on the last plank with his back to them, the way he’d sat every evening for a month. He didn’t turn round. But she saw his shoulders go, a little. The way they went when he let a breath out that he’d been holding a long time.

The Widow went at first light.

They all came down to see it. Group 1 from Wrenhaven, on the lake road in the grey, with Higu at the front and Xola holding Chi’s hand. Maren, and Bram with his boy on his shoulders. Gigi on her bench. Old Garrow, who said he’d come to see his leather off, which was a lie, because the leather wasn’t going anywhere.

They said goodbye on the shingle. It took a long time. It always did. Calvin hugged everybody except Rachael and then stood in front of Rachael with his arms out and his face asking, and Rachael looked at him for a long moment and said, “No,” and he said, “Worth a try,” and she nearly smiled. Dennis shook hands with everybody as if he were buying something. Sabine looked at the lake, and at the cabin, and at Alea’s father on the jetty, and said, “So that’s the lake,” to nobody, and got on the barge.

Gabe went down the jetty last.

Her father was on the last plank. He’d been there since before dawn with the old rod, the one with the hook on it, RESURGAM, casting. He turned round when Gabe got to the end. He stood up.

Alea didn’t hear what they said. She wasn’t meant to. She stood at the top of the shingle by Gigi’s bench and watched them: the big shape and the other one, standing very close on the end of a jetty in the grey, not touching, for a long time. And then her father put his hand on Gabe’s shoulder, once, the way he put his hand on her head between her ears. And Gabe put his own hand up and held it there a moment. And then let go, and came back up the jetty with his face doing the thing it did, wet and loud and too much, and got on the Widow, and stood in the bow with his hands in his pockets like somebody trying to look smaller than he was and failing.

Deston was the last on. He stopped at the top of the shingle by Alea.

“Names,” he said. “That’s what he asked. Their names. Where they came from. What they were holding. The girl at the well.” He looked at her. “We’ll find out. Rule four. It’ll take a while.”

“I know.”

“We’ll write,” said Deston. “By the post road. Not the channel. Not till he says.” He was quiet. “And Alea. The thread. On the grip.” He looked at the black hilt over her shoulder. “Keep holding it. That’s all a thread is. Somebody keeps holding the other end.”

He went down and got on the barge.

Calvin’s wind came up out of nowhere, soft, from the north, the wrong way for anybody but him, and the sail filled, and the Patient Widow went down the lake toward the mouth of the Wren in the first sun with Gabe in the bow not waving and everybody on the shingle waving at him anyway.

Her father stood on the end of the jetty and watched it go till it was round the point.

Then he sat down, and cast.

She hadn’t given him the lamp back.

She knew it. Everybody knew it. Nobody had said anything; that was the thing about the people at the lake, they didn’t say things, they sat on benches and waited for you. But she knew.

She’d said the thaw. On the night he came home, at midwinter, at the bonfire, with sparks going up: I’ll keep it till the thaw. You can have it back in the spring. And the thaw had come at ice-out, a Thursday, and the ice had gone off the lake, and the first boats had come up the Wren, and the Widow, and Group 4 with the hook and the song and the whole of the story, and the ash had nine leaves. It was spring. It had been spring for weeks.

And every night at dusk she’d lit it.

She did it the way he’d always done it. She’d watched him do it every evening of her life: trim the wick, a hair too high, because he never trimmed it quite enough; strike the match; the small blue flame catching and going yellow; the chimney down over it; and then the lamp on the table in the window, where you could see it from the end of the jetty, and from the lake road, and from the top of the last hill on the way home. Every night. Nineteen years. She’d never once asked him who it was for.

She’d found out in the autumn. When he went west, and she’d stood on the step with her pack on and her good knife and said I’m coming, and he hadn’t said no, and she’d looked at the lamp. Who lights it. If we both go.

It was for whoever wasn’t home yet.

He’d lit it for her mother, for eight years, every night she was in the infirmary late. He’d lit it for himself, maybe, the years after, when there was nobody to come home but him and he’d be out on the lake till dark. He’d lit it for Alea, every night she was in Wrenhaven, every night she was at the Lists, every night she’d ever been anywhere that wasn’t the cabin. So whoever it was would see it, coming over the last hill, and know.

She’d lit it for him. All autumn. Forty-one nights. Till he came over the hill at midwinter and the hen saw him first.

And then she hadn’t stopped.

She sat on the step that evening, with the Widow gone and the lake quiet and him on the jetty, and the lamp on the table behind her, not lit yet, because it wasn’t dusk; and she made herself think about why.

It wasn’t that she didn’t trust him to light it. He’d light it. He’d lit it every night of her life.

It was that she didn’t trust him to light it for himself.

That was it. That was the thing. She’d sat on the step for a month watching him on the jetty, every evening, with his back to them, since the night Group 4 put the whole story on the table and he’d said I pulled it out. He’d sat on the last plank every dusk with his cup going cold. He’d stood up on the night Thea read the valley and said like a nail hole. He’d said no to Gigi. He’d said not yet to Deston. He’d sat on the end of a jetty in the dark with all of it, the hill and the girl at the well and a mouth with nothing in it, and he hadn’t once come up to the cabin before she’d lit the lamp.

He came up because it was lit. Because somebody had lit it for him.

And she thought: if I give it back, he’ll light it for me. Every night. The way he always had. For whoever wasn’t home yet. For her in Wrenhaven, for her at the infirmary, for her anywhere. He’d light it every single night for somebody else, the way he’d answered every letter and never written one, the way he’d carried a sword for eight years and never asked whose. And he’d go on sitting on the jetty in the dark till it was lit, and never once light it for himself, because it would never once occur to him that he might be the one who wasn’t home yet.

That was why she hadn’t given it back.

She’d been lighting it for him. So there’d be somebody lighting it for him.

She sat on the step and looked at the jetty.

Nobody should have to say a good word on their own for three hundred years. She’d said that. To Deston, on the jetty, with her hand on the grip. She’d meant the two of them, Aldemar’s two, in the hill with nobody at the other end.

She looked at her father on the last plank, with his back to her, casting.

He’d said stay for nineteen years. Not out loud. Never out loud. But every night, to a lamp. Stay. Come home. I’m here. To her mother, to her, to everybody. And nobody had ever said it back to him, because he’d never once let anybody be the one waiting on the step.

Except her, in the autumn. For forty-one nights.

And she’d liked it. That was the other thing, the thing she didn’t want to look at. She’d liked being the one who lit it. She’d liked being the one who kept the lake. She’d said to him in the autumn, on the step, somebody has to keep the lake, it’s me, and she’d meant it, and she’d been proud of it, and she’d done it, every night, and he’d come home.

You couldn’t keep a person by keeping their lamp.

She’d read that somewhere. No. She hadn’t read it. She’d just thought it, sitting there. It sounded like something her mother would have said.

She took the list down off its nail first.

It was on the wall by the hearth, beside the drawing, under the sword. The new sword, on Garrow’s pegs; not Astra. Higu’s list, every word on the front of it crossed through but one, in his pencil, over last summer. And the back of it, which had been blank at midwinter, with her two lines on it in her mother’s slate hand.

Come home. A line through it.

Tell me where you found it. A line through it.

And a lot of room.

She’d known what the third one was for a week. Since the night of the valley. Since he’d said I didn’t ask Halvard why the gate took so long on the jetty to Deston, and she’d been sitting on Gigi’s bench up the shingle in the dark and heard it, because you could hear everything from that bench if the wind was right. She’d thought about it every night since, lighting the lamp.

She had Higu’s pencil. She’d kept it. She sat on the step with the list on her knee and wrote it, in her mother’s hand, under the other two.

Write first.

She looked at it. It wasn’t enough. She thought about it a long time, and added, smaller, underneath, so he’d know who she meant:

To Halvard. Not an answer.

Then she hung the list back on its nail, her side out, and went and stood at the table by the window, beside the lamp, and waited for dusk.

He came up at the edge of the dark.

Before it was lit. That was the first time in a month. She was standing at the table with the matches in her hand and the wick trimmed, and she heard his feet on the shingle, and then on the step, and then he was in the door, with the rod over his shoulder and his cup in his other hand, cold, full. He hadn’t drunk it.

He stopped in the door and looked at her. At the lamp. At the matches in her hand.

He didn’t say anything. He wouldn’t. He’d never ask.

She held the matches out to him.

He looked at them for a long time.

“It’s the spring,” said Alea. “I said the thaw. I said you could have it back in the spring.” She swallowed. “It’s been the spring for weeks. I’m sorry. I should have given it back at ice-out.”

“You don’t have to be sorry.”

“I know,” said Alea. “I’m being sorry anyway. Rule five. Gabe’s.” She held the matches out further. “I kept it because I liked lighting it. And because I thought if I gave it back, you’d light it for me. Every night. For whoever’s not home.” She looked at him. “And I don’t want you to light it for me.”

He didn’t move.

“I want you to light it for you,” said Alea. “Because you’re the one who’s not home yet. You’ve been on the end of the jetty for a month with all of it. The hill and the girl and the nail and the two of them with nobody at the other end.” Her voice went, a little. She let it. “You’re the one who’s out on the lake in the dark. Not me. I’m right here. I’ve been right here the whole time.” She took a breath. “So you light it. And you look at it, from the end of the jetty, and you know somebody’s home. And it’s you. You’re the somebody. You’re allowed to be the one it’s for.”

The cabin was very quiet. The kettle on the side of the stove ticked. Outside, on the lake road, very faintly, she could hear Gigi on her bench humming something tuneless to the dark.

Her father put the rod down against the wall. He put the cup down on the table, beside the lamp. He held his hand out.

She put the matches in it.

He struck one. The small blue flame. He held it to the wick, which she’d trimmed a hair too high, the way he always did; and it caught, and went yellow. He put the chimney down over it. And he picked the lamp up and put it on the table in the window, where you could see it from the end of the jetty, and from the lake road, and from the top of the last hill on the way home.

And he stood there and looked at it.

Not out of the window. At the lamp. A long time.

“Whose is it,” he said at last. Very quietly.

Alea thought about it.

“Yours,” she said. “It was always yours.” And then, because it was true, and because Deston had said it on the shingle: “Somebody kept holding the other end.”

She showed him the list after.

He read it standing by the hearth, with the lamp lit behind him in the window. The two lines crossed through. And the third.

Write first.

To Halvard. Not an answer.

He read it a long time. She watched his face do nothing, the way it did when there was a great deal going on underneath.

“I’ve never written to him first,” he said.

“I know.”

“Not once. Eighteen years I’ve known him. He writes, and I answer. How are the hens.” He looked at the list. “I don’t know how to start a letter that isn’t an answer.”

“I know,” said Alea. “That’s why it’s on the list.”

He looked at her.

“You heard,” he said. “On the jetty. With Deston. The gate.”

“From the bench,” said Alea. “The wind was right.” She didn’t look away. “You said you never asked him why it took so long. And you never will, if you wait for him to write and ask you something you can answer. He’s not going to. He’s been waiting eighteen years for you to.” She stopped. “Leaka said, in the channel, when her window went. Thea told me. She said she’d felt what somebody had carried for a very long time, and she wouldn’t say whose.” She looked at him. “It’s not hard to guess, Dad. There’s only one person it could be.”

He didn’t say anything.

“And the king has to know,” said Alea. “About the hill. About the nail. Everybody’s going to have to know, one day; Deston said, they’re keeping it off the channel till you say. But the king’s back in Solmere, and the Council wants an army, and there’s a valley with a hole in the middle shaped like the thing you pulled out.” She swallowed. “Somebody has to tell him. And it should be you. Not Group 4. Not Cursiffe. Not a cord. You.”

Her father looked at the list for a long time.

Then he took it off the nail, and turned it over, and looked at the front. Higu’s list. Every word crossed through but one, in his own hand, over last summer. Fish, at the bottom, written again.

He turned it back. Her side out. And hung it on its nail.

“Not tonight,” he said.

“I know.”

“Not yet,” he said. And then, after a moment, the way Deston said Gigi had taught him: “That’s a door with somebody’s hand on it.”

Alea laughed. It came out of her before she could stop it, wet, the way Gabe’s did. She put her hand over her mouth.

He put his hand on her head. Between her ears. The way he always had.

She opened her window that night on her pallet in the loft, on the fourth rung of the ladder that he’d heard every night of her life, with the lamp lit below her in the window and him on the step under it with a cup of tea that was, for once, hot.

Thirty-eight. It had been thirty-four at midwinter.

She didn’t know what had moved it. She thought it might have been the grip. Four evenings of winding wet leather on a blade, slowly, to her own hand, every turn lying over the last by half its width. Honed: the edge that won’t dull. She thought it might be about knowing what was yours to hold.

Or it might have been the matches. Holding them out. Letting go.

She shut the window.

Below her, on the step, she could hear him. Not doing anything. Sitting, with his tea, with the lamp lit in the window behind him and the lake going dark in front. And after a while, very quietly, so quietly she wasn’t sure afterwards that she’d heard it at all, he said something to nobody.

She thought it was thank you.

She thought he might have been saying it to the lamp.

Out on the lake road, on her bench, Gigi stopped humming. And then, after a long time, started again. Something with a tune, this time.

Alea lay in the loft and listened to it till she fell asleep.
