The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 11

Chapter 315

The Edge

5,916 words · 26 min read

The band was deeper.

Salty could see it from the west gate, which he shouldn’t have been able to. In the spring it had been frost: a long curved stripe of white across the red where the ground had been stood on all night by a hundred people and then left, crusted up in ridges, the way frost goes over footprints. It had melted by noon the first day. Wendy had pegged it anyway, in the cold, for two hours, with her knotted rope, and come back with her lips blue and said forty miles.

That was the spring.

It was nearly midsummer now. The red was dry and cracked and hot enough at noon to cook an egg on, Tobin said, and Salty had tried it, and it couldn’t. The frost had been gone for two months. There was nothing on the red at all, from the west gate to the old west fort, a mile off, nothing but the cracks and the dust and the heat coming up off it in shakes.

And the band.

You could see it now. Not white. Dark. A line in the red, a mile out, curving away north and south so slowly you’d only know it curved if somebody had told you. Like a cart track. Like the path cows make going the same way to the same gate every evening for a hundred years. Trodden down into the dirt a hand’s breadth below the red on either side, and packed hard, and smooth.

Nobody had trodden it.

The west tower had a watch on it every night, two men and a lamp, since the spring. The commander had put them there himself. Nobody had seen anything. Not a hunter, not a scout, not one of the threadless, not a fox. Nothing had walked on that line since the morning a hundred people turned round and went east with nine hunters at their feet.

And every morning it was deeper.

“A finger,” said Wendy. “A finger a week. Since the thaw.”

She was sitting on the floor of their room in the long barracks with her back to the bed and the rope in her lap, knotting a new length into it, the way she did every evening now, whether it needed it or not. Salty was on the bed with his boots off. He’d been on the wall all afternoon with the draft, doing shield drills in the heat, and he smelled of it, and she’d told him so, and he’d said he knew, and neither of them had moved.

“Every three days I go out,” said Wendy. “Same twelve pegs. I measure the depth with the knife.” She held up her thumb and forefinger. “It’s this much in the middle now. It was nothing in the spring. It was just frost.”

“It’s dirt,” said Salty. “Dirt settles.”

“It doesn’t settle in a line.” She didn’t look up from the knot. “And it doesn’t settle evenly. I’ve measured it at twelve places a mile apart. It’s the same at all of them. Exactly. To the width of a knife.” She pulled the knot tight. “It’s not settling. Something’s going over it.”

He didn’t say anything.

He didn’t like it when she was right about things like this. Not because she was right. She was usually right; she was the one who saw things, who’d seen the threadless on the red before anybody, who trusted nothing she couldn’t see and could see more than anybody. He didn’t like it because when Wendy was right about a thing like this, she went quiet afterwards. Not frightened. Wendy didn’t do frightened; she did cynical, which was frightened in a coat. She’d go quiet and knot rope and not look at him, and he’d know that she’d seen something she hadn’t put into words yet, because if she put it into words it would be true.

“Say it,” he said.

Wendy knotted another knot.

“It’s being written,” she said.

Salty looked at the ceiling.

“Thea said,” said Wendy. “About the grey. On Rachael’s boy. One tiny word, written ten thousand times in a line round the cut.” She held up the rope. “And the valley. Thirteen rings, all the same word written round. Come. Over and over.” She pulled the knot. “You know when you go over a letter with a pencil. Again and again. Because it’s not dark enough. Till the pencil goes through the paper.” She looked at him at last. “That’s what it looks like. From the gate. Somebody going over it.”

“With what?”

“I don’t know,” said Wendy. “That’s the bit I haven’t said.”

Yan said the same thing on the knoll, in different words, the next evening.

Salty walked him out. He always did. Every dusk since the autumn: out of the north postern, past the burned stump of the old signal post, along the track to the knoll a mile off with the ash on it and the cairn and the headstone and the spike. Yan in front with Suzu round his neck, white against his collar. Salty behind. Not close.

He stopped at the bottom of the slope. He always did that too.

He’d never said why. Nobody had asked. Yan had looked at him the first time, in the spring, when he stopped, and then gone on up on his own, and that was how it was. Salty knew why. It was Ice’s. That hill, that tree, the woman under it. Ice had carried her off the rise and up here and dug the hole himself, and stood a sword in it, and walked away, and come back once a year on his own for nine years and never let anybody come with him. Salty had known Ice since he was twenty. He’d talked to him most nights for two years, on the other side of a screen, and never once been in a room with him; he’d been going to, with Wendy, and then there wasn’t any going to. And this hill was the most Ice place in the world. More than the lake. It wasn’t Salty’s to walk up. Not without being asked. And nobody had asked.

He sat on a stone at the bottom and watched Yan go up.

Yan knelt by the headstone. He put his hand on the ground, the way he did, flat, by the place where the spike went in. Suzu came off his neck and coiled on the grass by his knee, and put her head down. And then nothing happened for a long time, the way nothing ever happened on that hill, except the sun going down behind them over the west and the ash’s leaves moving with no wind, which they did, and which Salty had stopped noticing.

When Yan came down it was nearly dark. He sat on the stone beside Salty. He didn’t say anything for a while.

“It leans,” he said.

“What does?”

“The cold. Under her.” Yan looked back up the hill. “In the spring it lifted its head and listened east. Past the black. Further than Suzu can taste. Now it doesn’t listen. It leans. West. Toward the band.” He was quiet. “Every night, a little more. Like a dog at a door when it hears somebody on the step.”

“West,” said Salty. “The band’s west of here.”

“The band’s the west edge of a ring forty miles across,” said Yan. “And the knoll’s inside it. Wendy worked it out. We’re inside it. The fort, the knoll, the rise, all of it. The middle’s twenty miles east in the black.” He looked at his hands. “The cold’s leaning toward the edge because the edge is calling it. Something’s being written on that line every night, and every night it’s a bit more written, and every night the cold leans a bit further toward it. Out from under the spike.”

Salty sat with that.

“It’s people,” he said. “You said. The cold. Hundreds of them. Put out and kept.”

“Yes.”

“And somebody wants them.”

“Somebody wants them poured,” said Yan. “That’s what they’re for. That’s what they were for, on the rise. You pour them into a cup and the cup’s someone wearing someone.” He touched Suzu’s head. “A pour needs a cup. And a cup goes in the middle. The rhyme says. Who’s the cup. The girls skip round in a ring and whoever’s in the middle when it stops sits down.” He was very quiet. “They’re writing the ring. To call the pour out from under the spike. And then they’ll want something in the middle to pour it into.”

“Rachael,” said Salty.

Yan didn’t answer that.

Up on the hill the ash’s leaves moved, with no wind, all of them, toward the west.

“Then we watch it,” said Salty. “At night. On it. Not from the tower.”

He said it at supper, in the long barracks, over the bowls. He’d made the stew. He made the stew most nights now; it was his thing. Malcolm had the bowls at Thornwall and Salty had them here, and he made the stew with whatever the draft brought in, and everybody ate it, and nobody complained, because the last man who’d complained had been Tobin, in the winter, and Salty had looked at him.

“From the tower we can’t see the ground,” he said. “Two men and a lamp at a mile. They wouldn’t see a cow. We go out. We sit on it. We see what’s going over it.”

Rhea put her spoon down.

She was Reaching now. Since the winter, on the wall, in the fog, when she’d let her light go down into the stone because she couldn’t see. She was still loud and still competitive and still wanted to arm-wrestle him every night, which she’d lost every time for three years; but she’d gone a little quieter since the fog. She listened more. She listened now.

“In the dark,” she said. “A mile out on the red. On a line that something’s walking.”

“Something’s writing,” said Wendy.

“Worse,” said Rhea.

“The old west fort’s on it,” said Salty. “Right on it. Wendy’s rope goes through the gate. Walls, a well, a roof on half the barracks. Nobody’s been in it in twenty years.” He pushed his bowl away. “We sit in the gate. On the line. Four of us. You. Niko for the light. Wendy for the seeing.” He looked at Yan. “Not you. You’re on the knoll. You tell us what the cold does.”

“And you,” said Niko.

“I’m the front,” said Salty.

Niko looked at him across the table. He’d been very quiet since the spring, since Flash; not unhappy, the opposite. He’d stopped chasing. He ate his stew. He didn’t burn his hands on the grip any more. He sat on the east wall at night with the sword across his knees, and every so often, when nobody was looking, a white point of light would come and go on the edge of it like a star coming out, and he’d smile.

“There’s no front,” said Niko. “It’s a line on the ground. Nothing’s on it.”

“Then I’ll be the front of nothing,” said Salty. “It’s still a front.”

They went out on the third night before midsummer. Aldous came to the west gate to see them off, in his chaplain’s black, with his hands folded, and didn’t try to stop them, and said a thing over them in the old church words that Salty didn’t understand and didn’t ask about. Tobin wanted to come. Rhea told him no in a voice that made him sit down on the step.

The red at night was a different thing.

He’d been out on it in the day a hundred times. In the day it was just ground: cracked, hot, red, flat, with the black at the far edge of the east sky like weather that never came. At night the heat came up out of it into the dark and you walked through it like walking through bathwater, and there were no sounds. None. No crickets. No birds. No wind. Aldous had said it, about the red, the first week: nothing grows and nothing sings. Salty had thought that was a chaplain making it sound better. It wasn’t. It was worse. You could hear your own boots so loudly you wanted to stop walking.

The old west fort came up out of the dark. A square of broken wall, chest high in places and nothing in others, round a yard with a well in it and a long barracks with half a roof. The gate was two stumps of wall with nothing between them. And straight through it, from the dark on one side to the dark on the other, the band. A hand’s breadth deep. Smooth as a floor.

Wendy knelt and put her hand on it.

“It’s cold,” she said.

It was. Salty put his hand down beside hers. The red was warm still, warm as a loaf, everywhere; and the band was cold. Not cool. Cold, like a cellar step, like the inside of a well. Like a thing that had been in the shade a long time.

“Midnight,” said Wendy. “If it goes over every night, it goes over at a time. Everything does.” She sat down against the stump of the gate with her bow across her knees and her rope beside her, and got out the knife, and stuck it in the dirt at the edge of the band, upright, with the blade’s edge at the lip of the groove. “I’ll know if it moves.”

They sat.

Rhea at the other stump of the gate, with her sword across her knees, facing north along the band. Niko on the wall above, facing south. Wendy on the ground with her knife. And Salty in the middle of the gate, on the band, sitting on it, with the greatsword across his knees and his back to the fort and his face to the dark outside.

He’d thought about that. Where to sit. On it or beside it. He’d decided on it, because if something came along it, it would have to come through him, and he’d rather know.

Nothing came.

The stars came out. There were a lot of them, on the red, because there was nothing else; and they went round, slowly, the way stars go. The black at the far edge of the east sky was a different black from the sky, and you could see the line where one stopped and the other started, and Salty didn’t look at it. Down on the knoll, a mile and a half behind him, there was a candle, very faint: Yan, sitting up with Suzu, the way he’d said he would.

He felt it before Wendy said anything.

It started in his seat. Not a sound. Not a cold. A weight. As if somebody very big had sat down on the band a long way off, north, and then stood up, and sat down again a little nearer. And again. Nearer. And it came down the band toward him in the dark the way a wave comes down a beach, and he couldn’t see anything, and nothing moved, and the weight came.

“Wendy.”

“It’s going,” said Wendy. Her voice was very flat. “The knife. The lip’s going down. Look.”

He didn’t look. He couldn’t. The weight was here.

It came into him through the band, up through the seat of his trousers, into his spine, the way the stone had come into him the first winter: the headstone with Ice’s English on it, TO DIWA, MY LOVE, that he’d carried out of the fort to this knoll with his ribs knitted crooked and wouldn’t put down. Bear. The first thing he’d ever had. He’d always been able to carry things. And this was like that, except there was nothing in his arms; it was all coming down on him from above and up through him from below at once, as if the band were a page and he were a crumb on it and somebody was pressing a pen into the paper right through him.

He didn’t move.

“Niko,” said Rhea.

Niko lit.

He did it from the wall. Salty didn’t see the sword move; nobody ever saw it move, now. He saw the light. A white point at the edge of the blade, and then everything was white, the whole yard, the walls, the red outside, the band, for one heartbeat, the way the world goes white when lightning’s close. Flat and white and no shadows.

And in it, Wendy saw.

She told him afterwards. He didn’t see it; his eyes were shut by then. She saw the band, going away north and south in the white from the gate, as far as the light went, a quarter of a mile each way. And the ground in it going down. Not in one place. Everywhere. All along it, the whole length she could see, the dirt in the groove sinking a hair, all at once, at the same moment, like a word being gone over all along its length by a pen that was everywhere. No feet. No prints. No one. Just the line going deeper, smooth, the way a line goes deeper on a page when the pencil goes over it.

Except in the gate.

In the gate, where Salty was sitting on it, there was a gap. A man’s width. The dirt under him and either side of him hadn’t moved at all. The groove went down on both sides of him, north and south, and came up against him, and stopped, and went on past.

He was a blot in it.

The white went out.

He was on his back. He didn’t remember going on to his back.

The weight was gone. It had gone over and on, south, down the band, into the dark, the way it had come, and he could feel it going, a long way off now, sitting down and standing up and sitting down. His spine hurt. His arms hurt. His mouth tasted of iron. Wendy was over him with her hands on his face, and Rhea was standing in the gate with her sword out, facing out, and Niko was coming down off the wall.

“I’m fine,” said Salty.

“You’re bleeding out of your ears,” said Wendy.

“I’m fine.”

“You’re bleeding out of your—“

“I held it,” said Salty. He sat up. He had to do it in two goes. “Didn’t I. It didn’t go through. Where I was.”

Wendy looked at him. Her face was doing the thing it did when she wanted to be angry and wasn’t, quite, and was angry about that.

“No,” she said. “It didn’t go through where you were. There’s a gap.” She sat back on her heels. She held up the knife. “It went down a hair everywhere else. Everywhere. I saw it. All along. And where you were it didn’t go down at all.”

Niko crouched by the band. He put his hand on it, north of the gate, in the groove. Then on it in the gate, where Salty had been sitting.

“It’s warm,” he said. “Here. Where you were.” He moved his hand. “And cold there.”

They all looked at the gap.

“So it can be stopped,” said Rhea.

“A man’s width of it,” said Wendy. “For one night. By a man bleeding out of his ears.” She looked north, along the band, into the dark. “It’s a hundred and twenty miles round. Something over that. I worked it out in the spring.” She didn’t look at Salty. “We’re four people.”

“Then we stand on a bit of it,” said Salty.

That was when the hunters came.

Two of them.

Out of the band. Not along it; out of it. North of the gate, thirty paces off, where it went away into the dark: the ground in the groove heaved, the way a blanket heaves when a dog under it gets up, and two long shapes came up out of the dirt of the band and stood up on it.

He’d seen hunters before. In the probe in the spring, from the gate tower, he’d had one on Oakes’s big shield and been put back a pace by it, the first time anything had ever put him back a pace. They were tall, and wrong at the joints, and their arms were too long, and they didn’t make any sound at all. These had been lying in the band, under it, packed into the trodden dirt the way hares lie in a form. Waiting. For somebody to sit on the line.

They came for him.

Not for Rhea. Not for Wendy. For the gap. For the man’s width of warm dirt where the line hadn’t gone down.

Salty got up.

He did it in one go this time. He didn’t know where it came from. He came up on to his feet on the band with the greatsword in both hands and his spine screaming, and put his feet where he’d been sitting, in the gap, one each side of the place where the warm was, and didn’t move them.

“Off the line,” said Rhea.

“No.”

“Salty—“

“If I get off it, it closes,” said Salty. “That’s the whole point.” He set his feet. “I’m the front.”

The first one came at him down the band, fast, low, its long arms going out wide on either side like somebody opening a door. He’d seen that in the spring. The arms came in together and you were between them, and that was how it had broken Niko’s ribs.

He didn’t step back. He couldn’t. He brought the greatsword round, flat, two-handed, the whole weight of it, into the left arm as it came in.

Forge.

He felt it come up in him. The thing that had come up in him last summer, at the Lists, the first time: the heat in his chest going down into his arms and into the steel, so that the blade landed heavier than a blade should. It came up in him now off the bottom of his spine where the weight had been, as if the weight had left something behind. The flat of the greatsword hit the hunter’s arm at the elbow and the arm went the wrong way. All the way the wrong way. With a sound like a green branch going under a cartwheel.

The hunter didn’t make a sound. It never did. It came on anyway, with one arm, and the other arm swinging loose, and its face, which wasn’t a face, a foot from his.

Rhea took its head.

She’d come down the band from the north in three strides and her sword was already going: low, flat, from five paces, the way she cut now, with her light gone down into the ground first. It didn’t touch the hunter. It didn’t need to. The air in front of the edge did it. The hunter’s head came off its shoulders and went over the wall of the old fort and the body stood on the band for a heartbeat and then lay down.

The second one wasn’t coming for him.

It had gone round. Wide, out on the red, off the band, the way the first one hadn’t; and it came in from the side, from the dark, at Wendy.

Wendy was on one knee by the gate stump with her bow up.

He saw her draw. He’d seen it a thousand times and never got used to it: three arrows on the string at once, and then not on the string; in the air in front of her, held there, hanging, the way she’d done it for the first time at the Lists last summer. Count. One, two, three. She let them go one after the other into the hunter’s chest as it came, and they went in, and it didn’t stop.

Salty didn’t move his feet.

He wanted to. It was the hardest thing he’d ever done, harder than the stone, harder than waiting at the Lists for Xav to be bad at it. Wendy was ten paces off and a thing was coming at her out of the dark with three arrows in its chest and its arms opening, and he was standing on a line in the dirt, and if he stepped off it the line closed, and it was only a man’s width, and it was only one night, and it didn’t matter, and he didn’t move.

Because she’d said. We stand on a bit of it. No. He’d said that. She’d said we’re four people. And if they were four people, then everybody had to be where they were.

“Niko,” he said.

Niko was already there.

He came off the wall in the dark and Salty saw him only by the light: the white point at the edge of the blade, coming out like a star, and then the hunter’s arms were open round Wendy and Niko was inside them. He didn’t swing. He’d stopped swinging in the spring. He turned the blade. And the white point went through the hunter from one side to the other like the sun coming through a gap in a shutter, and the hunter came apart in two halves, at the waist, the cut and the light the same instant, and the halves fell either side of Wendy on the red.

Wendy sat down.

Not hurt. Just sat, on the red, with her bow in her lap and the two halves of a hunter on either side of her, and looked at Niko, and then at Salty, standing on the band with his feet planted in a gap of warm dirt and blood coming out of his ears.

“You didn’t come,” she said.

“No.”

“I was ten paces off.”

“Yeah,” said Salty. His voice didn’t do what he wanted. “Niko was nearer.”

Wendy looked at him for a long time across the dark.

Then she got up, and came over, and stood in front of him on the band. Not on the gap. Beside it. Her boots in the cold groove, his in the warm. And she put her forehead against his chest and stood there, and didn’t say anything, and he didn’t put his arms round her because he still had the greatsword in both hands and couldn’t make his fingers let go of it.

“Idiot,” she said, into his shirt.

“I held the front.”

“You held a line on the ground.”

“It’s a front,” said Salty, “if somebody’s coming at it.”

He stood on it till dawn.

Nobody told him to. Rhea said, around two, that the weight had gone a long way south by now and wouldn’t be back till tomorrow and he could sit down. He said he’d rather not. Rhea looked at him, and then at Wendy, and then sat down herself on the stump of the gate with her sword across her knees and said, “Then I’m not either,” which wasn’t true, because she was sitting down; but he knew what she meant.

At dawn the red went grey and then pink and then red. And the band came out of the dark on either side of the gate, north and south, going away so slowly round its curve you’d only know if somebody had told you. A hand’s breadth deep and a hair deeper than last night. Smooth. Cold.

And in the gate, a gap.

A man’s width. Warm dirt, red as the rest of the red, a hand’s breadth higher than the groove on either side of it, like a stone in a stream. Like a smudge in a line of writing where somebody had put their thumb on the ink before it dried.

Wendy measured it with the knife. She wrote it down.

Then they walked back to the fort. He couldn’t walk properly. Niko took one side of him and Rhea took the other, and Wendy walked in front with her rope over her shoulder, and nobody said anything, and the black at the far edge of the east sky went pale in the sun and didn’t move.

Yan was at the west gate.

He’d come straight from the knoll. He’d been up all night, Salty could see it; there was frost on the left side of his collar where Suzu had been, the way there was when he’d been looking at the cold for a long time. He looked at Salty’s ears, and at the blood dried down his neck, and at his face.

“It leaned less,” said Yan.

Salty stopped.

“At midnight. It leaned, the way it does. Every night it leans further.” Yan’s voice was very quiet. “Last night it leaned, and then it stopped. Partway. And went back a hair. Like a dog at a door when the step goes quiet.” He looked west, at the band, a mile off, dark in the red in the morning light. “There was a gap in it. Wasn’t there. In the writing.”

“A man’s width,” said Wendy.

“It didn’t finish the word,” said Yan. “Last night. It went round and it didn’t finish. Because there was a bit it couldn’t go over.” He looked at Salty. “So it couldn’t call as hard.”

Nobody said anything.

“It’ll go over again tonight,” said Wendy. “And the gap’ll still be there, if we stand on it. And it’ll go over everywhere else. Deeper.” She looked at the rope on her shoulder. “We can’t stand on a hundred and twenty miles.”

“No,” said Yan.

“So it just takes longer.”

“Yes,” said Yan. “Every night somebody stands in that gate, it takes longer.” He touched Suzu’s head on his collar. “That’s not nothing. That’s—“ He stopped. He looked up the track toward the knoll, where the ash was, with its leaves turned west. “Rachael wrote it. On the rise. It took him all afternoon to get through to her, an hour’s walk any other day. And it took them all afternoon to turn her, and when he got there she was still half her.” He was quiet a long time. “Because it took longer than they wanted. Longer’s not nothing. Longer’s how anybody ever gets there.”

Salty stood in the west gate of Cinderhold with his ears ringing and his spine on fire and his hands still not quite able to open, and looked at the band.

“Tonight,” he said. “Me again.”

“You’re bleeding out of your ears,” said Wendy.

“Tomorrow, then,” said Salty. “Rhea tonight.”

Rhea looked at him. She’d been looking at him all night, on the stump of the gate.

“I’ll stand on it,” she said. “I’m Reaching. It’ll be a wider gap than yours.”

“It won’t,” said Salty.

“Arm-wrestle you for it,” said Rhea.

He looked at her. Then he laughed, which hurt everything, and sat down on the step of the west gate, all at once, because his legs had decided for him.

He lay on the bed in the long barracks all day with the shutters closed and Wendy on the floor with her back to the bed, knotting rope.

About noon she said, without looking up: “I measured it again. At eleven. I went out.”

“On your own?”

“With Tobin. He made me take Tobin.” She pulled a knot tight. “The gap’s still there. In the sun. It hasn’t filled in. It’s warm and the rest’s cold.” She was quiet. “I put a peg in it. Each side. So we’ll know where.”

He didn’t say anything.

“I’ve measured that line twelve places a mile apart every three days since the thaw,” said Wendy. “And every time it’s been the same everywhere. Exactly. To the width of a knife.” She put the rope down. “It’s not now.”

“No.”

“It’s not exactly any more.” She turned round, on the floor, and put her chin on the edge of the bed by his arm, and looked at him. Her two eyes, the brown one and the violet one, very close. “You put a thumb on it.”

“Yeah.”

“It was very stupid.”

“Yeah.”

“I’m going to stand on it on Thursday,” said Wendy. “After Rhea. Before Niko. I’ve worked out the order.” She looked at him a long time. “I’m not going to stand on it as well as you. I don’t carry things. I count them.” She put her hand on his arm. “But I’ll be standing on it. And it’ll be a gap. And it’ll be mine.”

He looked at her for a long time.

“Okay,” he said.

Outside, through the shutter, very faint, he could hear the draft on the wall doing shield drills in the heat, and Tobin shouting the count, and somebody laughing. And far off, west, a mile out on the red, there was a line in the dirt a hand’s breadth deep that went round a forty-mile ring in the dark every midnight and came up against a peg either side of a man’s width of warm red ground, and had to go round.
