The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 4: Threads

Chapter 102

The Lake Country

3,066 words · 13 min read

John used to hold things up to the camera.

That was what he’d been, on Earth, for a while. When Declan streamed with a facecam, in the first year of poly, John had been in the background of almost every stream. Sitting on the bed behind him. Leaning in at the edge of the frame. And every so often Declan would turn round in his chair, in the middle of a game, in the middle of a sentence, and say John, hold that up, and point at something, and John would hold it up to the light so the camera could see.

A banana, once. A cat, once, which hadn’t been John’s cat and hadn’t wanted to be held up. Once, and Declan had laughed so hard at this that he’d lost the round, John’s own shoe.

He’d written about the shoe in Hako’s book, in the summer, in the channel. Everybody had laughed. He’d sat in the common room at Mother Annick’s and read them all laughing at a shoe, three years later, in another world, and had to go and stand in the yard.

He’d drifted, after Declan moved to Malacca. They all had, the poly lot. Not on purpose. The way you do. Fewer calls, fewer games, then we should catch up, then nothing for months. If they hadn’t drifted, John thought, he’d have been one of the closest. He’d been close, once. At orientation camp, at seventeen, sitting on a dormitory floor at two in the morning with a boy he’d met that afternoon who wouldn’t stop talking.

He’d meant to fix it. He’d meant to fly up.

Here, he saw light.

Not like Hyvred, who saw everything. Light was loud to him, was the only way he’d ever been able to put it. Every lamp, every candle, every patch of sun on a wall had a kind of volume. He could hear a lantern being lit three rooms away. He could see light change before something came round a corner, because the light got there first. He’d made a mote, three times, in Duskhollow, in the dark: a tiny star in his palm, for a heartbeat, when he wrote Here. Look. in the window’s language.

He’d never got further than that. Ninety-seven percent. For a month.

He’d held things up to the light for Declan. He thought, sometimes, that the world had looked at him and decided that was what he was for.

The country changed above Mallow’s Ford.

They’d come out of the hills on the second day after the gorge, and John had felt it go before he saw it: the light changing. The hard grey light of the gorge and the forest, bouncing off rock, loud and flat. And then, as the river came out between the last of the hills, something softer. Wider. Light that came from everywhere at once and didn’t bounce off anything, because there was nothing to bounce off. Light on water. Lots of water.

The lake country.

He’d heard the name for a year and a half. The books come from the north. Lake country. Oswin had said it, in the first month, and it had gone round the channel a dozen times. Deston had said it, pointing north from a roof.

It was nothing like he’d imagined. He’d imagined something grand. Mountains, maybe. Something you’d put on a postcard.

It was quiet. That was the first thing. The river came out of the hills and slowed down and spread out, and the land went soft and low on either side, long green valleys going away into a pale haze, and in every valley there was water. Small lakes, first, like puddles left behind by a giant. Then larger ones. Strung along the river like beads on a cord, one after another, flat and grey and silver under a wide white sky, with reeds at the edges, pale green with the spring, and birds he didn’t know the names of standing in the shallows on one leg.

The air smelled of water, and cut grass, and woodsmoke from somewhere a long way off.

Everybody on the barge went quiet.

The villages came back.

Real ones. With dogs. John heard the first dog before he saw the village, a furious yapping from a jetty at a bend, at a salt barge coming up the river under a sail that should have been empty, and the whole barge went still. Then somebody laughed. Calvin, on the crosstree. And then everybody, at once, laughing at a dog barking at them, the way you laugh when something you didn’t know you were holding finally lets go.

There were children on the jetty. Waving. A woman hanging washing. A man in a boat with a net who stared at them going past and didn’t wave back, but didn’t look away either.

Their light was right.

John checked. He couldn’t help it. He’d been checking every face on every bank for four days, since the first village with no dogs. Ordinary light. Warm. Messy. The kind of light people have when they’re alive and thinking about their washing.

Deston, at the bow, said quietly: “Threads. All of them. Everyone’s got threads.”

Rachael sat down very suddenly on a coil of rope and put her face in her hands.

Deston didn’t leave the bow after that.

He’d been at the bow the whole way. Since Kingsferry. With his hand out over the water, flat, pointing north, the way he’d pointed from the roof above the chandler’s for four months. But since the gorge it was different. John could see it. He wasn’t pointing anymore. His hand had come down onto the rail and stayed there, holding on, the way you’d hold the rail of a boat in a swell. And he leaned forward over it. All day. Like a dog straining at a lead.

John went and stood beside him on the third afternoon.

“Is it far?” he said.

Deston didn’t answer for a long time.

“I can’t tell distance,” he said. “I told you. Threads don’t thin.” He was quiet. “But I’ve never felt it like this. Not even in the gorge. In the chorus.” He swallowed. “It’s like standing next to a fire with your eyes shut. You know exactly where it is. You could walk straight to it.”

“How close?”

Deston looked at the river ahead. At the next lake, opening out beyond the reeds, grey and silver, and the next valley beyond that.

“Close,” he said.

Xav worked it out that night.

They’d tied up at a village on a causeway between two lakes, with dogs and smoke and a tavern with a lamp in the window, and Haskett had gone to the tavern and come back with bread and a jug and the news that the big lake was a day north. Lake Ilse, the tavern-keeper had said. The big one. The one the river comes out of. And there was a town at the foot of it, where the river left the lake. The last town before the lake country went on into the hills and the forest and nothing.

Haskett said its name.

The whole deck went quiet.

John saw it go round them. Face to face. The way a cloud’s shadow goes round a field. Malcolm first, who’d stopped writing. Then Dennis, with his ledger shut. Then Rachael, and Calvin, and Azac, and RX, and Gabe. And Xav, sitting on the deck with his back against the mast and his eyes on the dark north, not moving.

Every one of the thirty-five knew where every group was posted. They’d been writing to each other for a year and a half. Thornwall west, Valcrest south, Cinderhold east, the capital in the middle. Kingsferry and Duskhollow on the river.

And north. At the edge of the lake country. Group 1.

“They’ve known,” said Xav.

Nobody said anything.

“Group 1,” said Xav. Very quietly. “Thea. Higu. All five of them. They’re there. They’ve been there the whole time. A year and a half.” He was looking at the dark. “That’s why they went quiet. The night of the sum. Thea and Higu didn’t write anything, all night. Chris noticed. Winter noticed. They know something.” He let his breath out. “They didn’t know something. They knew everything. They’ve been living down the road from him.”

“And they never said,” said Malcolm.

“And they never said,” said Xav.

John waited for somebody to be angry.

He thought somebody would be. It was the kind of thing you’d be angry about, wasn’t it. Five people who’d known, for months, maybe longer, where he was. Who’d sat in the channel every night while thirty other people wondered, and Gabe wrote declan, if you can somehow read this, I’m coming to find you, and said nothing. Not a hint. Not a word. Not north, not lake, not we know.

Nobody was angry.

Gabe, in the stern, put his face in his hands. But not the way you do when you’re angry. He was laughing. Very quietly. Into his hands.

“Of course they did,” said Gabe. “Of course they did.” He took his hands away. His eyes were wet. “He’d have told them not to. Or he wouldn’t have needed to.” He shook his head. “Thea built the channel. Thea built it in his window. They’re right there.” He started laughing again. “They’ve been keeping him. The whole time. For us.”

“For him,” said Deston, from the bow, without turning round.

Gabe stopped laughing.

“They didn’t keep it from us,” said Deston. “They kept it for him. Because he hadn’t said they could tell.” He was holding the rail. “We did exactly the same thing. All winter. Not the channel. We kept Groups 4 and 6 out of the channel so that nobody would know we were coming. So that he’d find out with his own eyes.” He was quiet a moment. “They’ve been doing that for a year. For him. On their own. Five of them.”

Nobody said anything for a long time.

“Then they’ll be waiting,” said Xav.

John couldn’t sleep that night.

He lay on the deck in the middle of everyone, the way they all lay now, with Petra awake at the bow and Sabine at the stern, and looked up at the stars. They were very bright, out here. No town light. No lamps. Just the lakes, all round them, black and still, with the stars in them, so that the barge seemed to be floating in the middle of a sky with stars above and below.

He got up, in the end. Quietly. He went to the foot of the mast, where Calvin was asleep with his arms round it, the way he’d slept every night since Ferrow’s Bend.

“Cal,” he said.

Calvin opened one eye.

“Can you show me how to get up?” said John. “To the top.”

Calvin looked at him for a long moment. Then at the mast. Then he grinned, and unwrapped his arms from it, and stood up.

“Finally,” said Calvin. “Somebody wants to come up.”

It was harder than Calvin made it look. Much harder. The ratlines swung, and the mast creaked, and John was an athletic man who’d climbed walls in the Hollow and down a stair into the dark, and he still had to stop halfway and hold on and breathe with his eyes shut. Calvin waited for him at the crosstree, sitting with his legs round the mast, not saying anything. Just waiting. The way Xav waited.

John got there.

He sat on the crosstree beside Calvin, with the barge tied up below them at its causeway, and the lakes all round, black and silver under the stars.

And he looked north.

He saw it at once.

Not with his eyes. With the other thing. The thing that made light loud.

North. A long way. Past the causeway, past the next lake, past the low dark line of the next valley. Where the land opened out into something bigger than any of the lakes they’d passed. Much bigger. A great flat darkness, with the stars in it, going away north to a black line of hills.

And at the near end of it, where the river came out, light.

A town. He could hear it. All the small lights of a town at night, lamps in windows, a lantern at a gate, a fire somewhere banked down to red. The ordinary murmur of a town’s light, the way you’d hear the murmur of a crowd through a wall.

And one more.

Not in the town. At the edge of it. Down at the water, at the end of what must have been a road, along the shore. One lamp. In one window. On its own.

It wasn’t bright. That was the strange thing. It was a lamp, in a window, the same as every other lamp in the town. Not brighter. Not bigger.

It was loud.

John sat on the crosstree and listened to it.

He’d heard light all his life here, the way other people heard music. He’d heard the pale breathing light come up under the bricked door in the Hollow every morning, like something enormous breathing in its sleep. He’d heard the wrong light in the turned on the banks, flat and strange, like a note played on a broken instrument. He’d heard Emrys’s sung flash at the night market, Hyvred had told him about it, and he’d thought it must have been like a trumpet.

He’d never heard anything like this.

One lamp, in one window, a long way north. And it was the loudest light he’d ever heard in his life. Not loud like a shout. Loud like a bell. Like something very big and very still, that had been ringing for a very long time, so steadily that you stopped noticing it, and then one day you noticed.

Like the light round somebody, he thought. Not the lamp. Somebody near the lamp.

Somebody sitting beside it.

“John?” said Calvin.

John didn’t answer.

He’d held things up to the light for Declan. A banana. A cat. His own shoe. John, hold that up. And Declan had laughed so hard he’d lost the round.

He held his hand up now. Flat, palm out, toward the north. Toward the loud lamp at the end of the road by the water. The way you’d hold your hand up to the light of a fire to feel how warm it was.

He didn’t decide to. It was just there, the edge, at the end of the light. Where Thea had said it would be.

He wrote the thing he’d written three times before, in Duskhollow, in the dark. The thing that had made a star sit in his palm for a heartbeat.

Here. Look.

The mote came.

A small light. In his palm. White, like a star. The size of a fingertip. It sat there, on his open hand, on the crosstree of a salt barge in the middle of the lake country, in the dark.

It didn’t go out.

Not straight away. That was the thing. Every other time, it had gone out in a heartbeat. This one sat in his palm for two. Three. And in the third, very slowly, the way a compass needle turns, it turned, until it was pointing north.

At the lamp.

Then it went out.

John sat on the crosstree with his hand still held up and his heart going much too fast. Three heartbeats. The longest it had ever lasted. And for one of them, it had pointed.

He opened his window. Progress to Spark: 99%. It had been ninety-seven at sunset.

He laughed. He couldn’t help it. It came out of him so loud on the crosstree that somebody on the deck below woke up and said what, and Calvin grabbed his arm so he wouldn’t fall.

“Cal,” said John, when he could talk.

“Mm?”

“The town at the end of the lake. Where the river comes out.”

Calvin looked north. He couldn’t see it. Nobody could, from here, except John. But he looked anyway, the way Deston had looked north for four months.

“There’s a lamp,” said John. “At the end of a road. By the water. On its own.” He looked at his empty palm, where the star had pointed. “It’s the loudest thing I’ve ever heard.”

Calvin didn’t say anything.

“I think he’s sitting next to it,” said John. “Right now. In the dark. I think that’s him.”

They didn’t tell the others until morning. They sat on the crosstree until the sky started going grey in the east, and John listened to the lamp at the end of the lake the whole time, until the dawn came up and drowned it out. Then they climbed down.

Deston was at the bow. He’d been there all night. He turned round when John came down the ratlines, and looked at his face, and didn’t need to ask.

“You saw it,” said Deston.

“I heard it,” said John.

Deston looked at him for a long time. Then he turned back to the rail, and put both hands on it, and leaned forward over the water, north, the way he’d been leaning for four days.

“Tomorrow,” he said.

“Tomorrow,” said John.

He didn’t post anything in the channel. None of them did. Not the lake, not a lamp, not tomorrow. He stood at the rail of the Patient Widow in the grey dawn, with the lake country going away all round him, flat and silver and quiet, and scrolled back through the night’s two hundred lines out of habit.

Mashi’s heartbeats. Maigo in capitals. Chris’s evening numbers, all thirty-five, and the blank one at the end.

And from Wrenhaven, from Group 1, nothing. Not a word since supper the night before. Five people who used to write every evening, and lately wrote only in the mornings, as if they’d been somewhere else at night.

John thought he knew where.

He held his hand up, flat, palm out, toward the north. Toward a town at the end of a lake, and a road along the water, and a lamp at the end of it that he couldn’t see from here and could still, very faintly, hear.

He didn’t make a light this time.

He didn’t need to.
