The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 6: Nine

Chapter 169

The Tree Was Told

5,300 words · 23 min read

Maigo had been not-talking for a whole day and it was the hardest thing he’d ever done.

Harder than the first winter. Harder than the red, which hurt, all the time, through his bare foot, like standing on a bruise that went all the way down. Harder than the night among the markers when the ground stopped flinching and he wrote stay and a tree came up out of the dirt in front of him. He’d been talking since he was two. His mum said so. First word, and then no last word, ever. He’d talked through school, and work, and every game he’d ever played, and every call, and two years of being a tree in a world where nobody had ever seen one. He’d talked to the horse for two years. He’d talked to the tree every month since he planted it.

And yesterday, from the gate to the moon, he hadn’t said anything at all.

He’d counted the things he wanted to say. He did that, when he had to be quiet. On the red a year ago, following a man with a sword on his shoulder to a grave, he’d counted a hundred and three questions and asked none. Yesterday he’d counted things he wanted to tell the tree. He’d got to two hundred and eleven by the time the moon came up, and then he’d stopped counting, because Yan went green.

So when he sat up on the fallen tree in the morning, in the gold light, and looked up the slope at the ash with its leaves going gold at the edges, he had two hundred and eleven things and a lot more, and they were all piled up behind his teeth like water behind a dam.

“Okay,” said Maigo, to the tree, very quietly. “It’s morning.” He took a breath. “I’ve got so much to tell you.”

He didn’t go up straight away.

He wanted to. Every leaf on his antlers wanted to; he could feel them, turning, all of them, up the slope, the way they turned toward the sun. But it wasn’t his morning. It was hers. He’d told the tree that, last night, under his breath, when Alea gave it a stick in a pot. It’s hers. You’re hers. I just planted you.

So he sat on the fallen tree and waited.

And at the top of the knoll, at the edge of the green, Alea sat up on the blanket.

He watched her. He couldn’t help it. She sat up slowly, the way you sit up when you’ve slept somewhere you’ve never slept before, and looked round. At the black stone. At the cairn. At the two halves of the sword standing side by side in the green, a hand’s width apart, shining in the low sun. At her father, beside her, already awake, sitting with his back against nothing and his knees up, looking at the stone.

Then down the slope. At the fallen tree. At Maigo, sitting on it with his sunglasses pushed up and his leaves all turned up toward her.

She looked at him for a long moment.

Then she lifted her hand, and beckoned. Once.

Maigo was off the trunk and halfway up the slope before he’d decided anything. He made himself stop at the edge of the green. Where the red ended. Where you stopped. He stood there, with his bare foot on the red and his shoe on the edge of the grass, and the red hurting the way it always hurt through his foot, and the grass not hurting at all, the way it never did.

“Can I?” he said.

“You planted it,” said Alea. “You don’t have to ask.”

“I do,” said Maigo. “It’s yours.”

Alea looked at him with her mother’s eyes, which he’d never seen until a road outside Valcrest and had been told about by a soldier on the red and by a man by a fire and had known at once, the first time, because the tree had leaned toward her.

“It’s both of ours,” said Alea. “Come on.”

He went to the tree.

Across the green, between the flowers, white and blue and silver with dew. Past the cairn, with its ring of children’s stones round the foot and the white one at the head and Mother Breck’s on the top catching the light. Past the black stone, with the four words on it, and Astra beside it. To the ash.

It was taller than him. It had been taller than him since the first summer, and taller than Salty since the second, and taller than Rhea since the winter, and he’d told it so every time, very proudly, the way you tell a kid how tall they’ve got. He stood under it now and looked up. At the smooth grey trunk. At the branches going up. At the leaves, thousands of them, green, going gold at the edges with the end of summer, all moving very slightly in a wind that wasn’t there.

Toward him. The way they always did.

He sat down at the foot of it. Cross-legged, on the green, with his back against the trunk. He took his shoe off, the one shoe he wore, and put his bare foot, the root foot, flat on the ground beside the other one. The wooden toes spread out on the grass. And he put both his hands flat on the earth either side of him, palms down.

And felt it.

He’d always been able to feel it. Since the spring he planted it. A tree is a tree; it’s in the ground; and he was a tree, more or less, from the inside. When he put his foot on the earth near it, he could feel it the way you’d feel somebody’s hand in a dark room: there. Alive. Drinking. Growing. Its roots going out under the knoll in every direction, fine as hair at the ends, holding the red together the way fingers hold a handful of sand.

He felt it now. Stronger than ever. It had grown so much this summer.

And under it, the other things.

He’d felt them before, too. The first night among the markers, with the rain stopped and the stars out: a root running under the red. Warm. Stubborn. From somewhere, toward the grave. Like a hand that somebody had put down on the ground a long time ago and never quite lifted. He’d followed it to the grave in the spring, and planted the tree beside it, and the tree’s roots had found it and wrapped round it, and held on.

He’d thought it was the healer’s. Hers. Something she’d left.

It wasn’t. He’d found out a year ago, on this green, when the man knelt by the cairn and put his palm flat on the earth and the warm root under Maigo’s foot lit, like a lamp, all the way along, and Maigo had felt, with every leaf on his head, whose hand it was.

His. The man’s. His aura, going down into the red from the place where he’d planted a black sword over her nine years ago, and staying. Feeding the flowers. Every spring. For eight years. Without knowing he was doing it. The only green on the whole of the red.

Maigo had never told him. He’d told the tree. Trees liked being told things.

He felt it now. The warm root. Running under the green, round the cairn, through the tree’s roots, like a hand holding a hand.

And beside it, new, something else.

He sat very still.

It was faint. So faint he nearly missed it. Another warmth, in the ground, near the first. Smaller. Newer. Not a root yet. More like the place where a root was going to be, the way you can feel where a seed’s going to come up in the spring before it does. Lying alongside the old warm root, very close, in the earth under the edge of the green.

Where a blanket was. Where a girl had slept all night, on her father’s arm, with her hand on the grass.

Maigo looked up.

Alea was sitting on the green a few paces off, with her knees up, watching him. With her hand flat on the grass beside her. She hadn’t moved it all night, he thought. She’d slept with it there.

“What?” said Alea.

Maigo opened his mouth. And all two hundred and eleven things came up behind his teeth at once, and he found he couldn’t say any of them.

“There’s two now,” he said instead. Very small. “In the ground. Two hands.”

Alea didn’t understand. He could see her not understand. He didn’t explain. It wasn’t his to explain yet. He’d tell the tree first.

“Can I talk to it?” he said. “Out loud? I always do. Every month. I tell it everything.” He looked at her. “I don’t have to. If you want it quiet.”

Alea looked at the tree for a long time. At the leaves, moving toward Maigo in no wind. At the black stone a pace away, that the tree was leaning toward.

“Tell it everything,” she said. “I want to hear.”

So he told it.

Out loud. For an hour. Sitting at the foot of the ash with his back against the trunk and his bare root-foot flat on the green and his hands on the earth, in the gold morning, with Alea sitting a few paces off with her knees up, listening, and the man sitting at the edge of the green where he’d slept, listening too, not saying anything.

He told it everything.

“Okay. So. Last time I was here it was the spring, before we went south, and I said I’d tell you about Valcrest when we got back. So. Valcrest.” He took a breath. “It’s huge. It’s the biggest place I’ve ever seen in this world. It’s got a thing called the Lists in the middle that’s like a stadium, like a proper one, like the ones at home, and forty thousand people fit in it. Forty thousand. And they all had paper on. Paper horns, for Ina. Paper squids. Paper kettles, there was a guy called Lucan who made paper kettles, I don’t know why, it’s a long story. And antlers. Paper antlers, with paper leaves on, for me. There were kids in the street with my antlers on.” He swallowed. “I cried. I’m telling you because you’re a tree and you won’t tell anyone. I cried at the paper antlers.”

The leaves moved.

“And all thirty-five of us were there. All of us. At once. For the first time since the hall. And there was a roll call, and everybody said here.” He was quiet, for Maigo. “And he was there.”

He didn’t look at the man at the edge of the green. He looked at the tree.

“He was standing on the road outside the gate when we got there,” said Maigo. “On his own. Waiting. And he said my name.” His voice went, a little. “Not my name. The name. The one he used to call me when I got four kills with a pistol on a round where I had no business getting any. Maigotron.” He laughed, wet. “THE MACHINE. And every leaf on my head came out at once. In Valcrest, in the road, in front of everybody. Full leaf. Like spring.” He looked up at the branches above him. “You’d have liked that. You’d have done it too.”

The leaves moved. All of them. A long slow ripple through the whole tree, from the bottom branches to the top, the way a field of barley moves when wind goes over it. In no wind.

Behind him, at the edge of the green, he heard the man make a very small sound.

He told it about the king.

“There’s a king. A real one. Big, with a beard, and hands like a smith, because he was a smith, before. He made the stone.” He nodded at the black stone, a pace from the tree. “That one. Her stone. He made it on a beach by a lake with a hammer his dad had. And he had a dinner, in Valcrest, for all of us, at a long table, with geese.” He was quiet. “And he came down the table with the first bit of goose, the very first slice, before anyone else, and he put it on my plate. And he said, you planted the tree.”

He looked at the ash.

“He meant you,” said Maigo. “A king. Gave me the first bit of goose. Because of you.” He wiped his face with the back of his wooden hand. “I didn’t know what to do. I ate it. It was really good goose.”

Alea had put her hand over her mouth.

He told it about the road back. About the fires, and Rhea pushing the cart, and the horse, who still hadn’t told him his name. About Niko on the tailboard going eleven, eleven, eleven, and then nineteen. About eleven dinners in a forge and coconuts and Salty crying at the fish. About a girl on the wall every morning for five weeks, looking out here, at the tree, and not coming.

“She didn’t come,” he told it. “For five weeks. She could see you from the wall. Every day. And she said she wouldn’t come till the night. With him. And she didn’t.” He looked at Alea. “That’s the hardest thing I’ve ever seen anyone do. I’d have run out on the first morning. I’d have run out in my socks.”

“You don’t wear socks,” said Alea, behind her hand.

“I’d have found some,” said Maigo.

He told it about last night.

Not the words. He hadn’t heard the words; none of them had. They’d sat on the fallen tree at the foot of the knoll with their backs nearly to the grave and listened to a girl’s voice and not heard what it said, on purpose, all of them, for hours. He told the tree that. That they’d kept their backs to it. That Rhea had cried the whole time and not wiped it. That Salty had held Wendy’s hand. That Aldous had come down from saying the old words with the white cloth folded on his knees and his eyes shut and his face like a man who’d finished something he’d been carrying for nine years.

“And then Yan went green,” said Maigo.

The tree went still.

He felt it. Through his foot, through his hands. The whole of it, every leaf, every root, going still at once, the way he’d gone still yesterday from the gate to the moon. Listening.

“He reached Candle,” said Maigo. “On the tree. Our tree. The fallen one down there.” He nodded down the slope. “He said he wouldn’t, last night. He said it was her night and he’d keep his back to it and he wouldn’t do anything. And he kept his back to it. All night. And then she said something up here, at the end, and he went green. All of him. His hands. His hair. Suzu. Like a lamp in a wood.” He swallowed. “And Aldous said a word. An old one. From the rites. Soul Eater.”

The tree didn’t move.

“It’s okay,” said Maigo, quickly, to the tree. “It’s okay. Aldous said it’s not a bad one. He said it’s who carries. Not who eats. Who carries.” He put his hand flat on the trunk behind him. “Yan sees colours now. He told us at dawn. Everybody’s got a colour. He says most of us are green. He says green’s loving.” He was quiet. “He says Alea was the greenest thing he’s ever seen. Up here. Last night. He says it lit the whole red.”

He turned his head and looked at Alea.

She’d taken her hand away from her mouth. She was looking at him with her ears flat and her eyes wet and her face doing something he’d never seen a face do.

“He said that?” said Alea.

“He said that,” said Maigo.

He told it about the cutting last of all.

“I gave her one of yours,” he said. “A kid. From the spring. I took a bit off your bottom branch before we went south, and put it in a pot in the barracks window, and it grew all summer. It’s got three leaves.” He looked at Alea. “It’s going home with her. To the lake. Where she lives. Where her mum stood on the end of a jetty and said here.” He put both hands flat on the earth again. “So you’ve got a kid at the lake now. So there’s one of you at both ends.”

The leaves moved. Slowly. All of them. Not toward Maigo this time.

Toward Alea.

She saw it. He watched her see it. A whole tree, every leaf on it, turning, very slowly, in no wind, toward a fox girl of seventeen sitting on the grass at the foot of her mother’s grave. The way a person turns toward somebody they’ve just been introduced to, and decided they like.

Alea didn’t say anything. She got up. She came across the green, between the flowers, and sat down at the foot of the tree beside Maigo, with her back against the trunk next to his. And put her hand on the bark. Flat. The way he did.

“Hello,” said Alea. To the tree. Very quietly. “Thank you for looking after her.”

That was when he told it the other thing.

He hadn’t meant to. He’d meant to tell it later, on his own, the way he told it the things that were too big to say in front of anyone. But Alea’s hand was on the bark beside his, and he could feel it, through the tree, the way he could feel everything through the tree: warm. And new. The same as the faint new warmth in the ground under the edge of the green, where she’d slept with her hand on the grass.

“There’s two now,” said Maigo. To the tree. Not to her. “In the ground. Under you. You know there are. You’ve had your roots round the old one for a year and a half.” He swallowed. “The warm one. That goes under the red from the fort to here. That I followed in the spring. That I thought was hers.” He was quiet. “It’s his. You know it’s his. He put it there nine years ago when he put the sword in, and it’s been feeding the flowers ever since, and he doesn’t know.”

Behind them, at the edge of the green, the man had gone very still. Maigo felt it through the ground. The old warm root, going still, the way the red went still round his boots.

“And now there’s another one,” said Maigo. “Next to it. New. Little. Since last night.” He looked at Alea’s hand on the bark. “Hers. She slept with her hand on the grass all night, and it went down. Into the ground. Next to his.” He swallowed. “Two hands now. Holding.”

Nobody said anything.

“Holding what?” said Alea. Very quietly.

Maigo didn’t answer at once.

He felt for it. Down. Through the tree’s roots, through the two warm hands in the earth, through the red. Down to where Yan said it was. Where Yan had shown them, at dawn, with his hands, when he told them about the colours: the cold’s gone down, a long way, further than it’s ever been, when the swords rang.

He couldn’t feel the cold. He’d never been able to. It wasn’t a living thing; it wasn’t a root or a seed or a heart; it wasn’t anything he knew how to feel. He was a tree. Trees didn’t feel cold things. They just grew round them.

But he could feel his tree’s roots. And they’d gone down.

He’d never felt them go so deep. Last night, when the swords rang, while he sat on the fallen tree not looking, the roots had gone down after something. Following it. The way roots follow water. Further than an ash’s roots had any business going, down past the red into the dark, and wrapped round something there that he couldn’t feel, and held on. The way you’d hold on to somebody’s wrist in a river.

“I don’t know,” said Maigo. “Something. Yan knows. Something cold. Down there. It went down when the swords rang, and the tree went after it.” He put both his hands flat on the earth. “It’s holding on. With your dad’s hand, and yours, and the spike. And it’s not letting go.”

He looked up at the branches. At the leaves, green and gold, moving very slightly in no wind.

“Thank you,” Maigo said to the tree. “For holding. I didn’t know you were. I’d have said thank you a year ago if I’d known.” He wiped his face. “You’re a really good tree.”

The leaves moved. All of them. The long slow ripple from the bottom branches to the top, like barley in wind.

Trees liked being told things.

The others came up at midday.

Alea asked them. She went to the edge of the green and stood there and called down the slope, and they came up, one at a time, slowly, the way you go up into a church. Aldous first, with his book and his folded cloth. Then Rhea, red-eyed, quiet. Then Salty and Wendy. Then Niko with his hands in his pockets. Then Yan, last, with the scythe on his shoulder and Suzu round its shaft, and his eyes doing something different now, Maigo thought, looking at people. Looking at the colours.

They came onto the green. And stood round the cairn, in a loose ring, the way they’d stood a year ago when the man sat by it with the sword across his knees.

Salty went to the black stone.

He stood in front of it. Short and square and very broad, with his crooked ribs that ached when it rained and when he laughed and when he lifted things. He looked at the stone for a long time. At the four words. At the long black spike under it, that went down into the red where you couldn’t see it any more.

Then he put his big square hand flat on the face of it. Very gently. The way you’d put your hand on a horse.

“It was heavy,” said Salty.

Nobody said anything.

“Carrying it,” said Salty. “In the snow. The last stretch.” He kept his hand on the stone. “I said it was fine. It wasn’t fine. It was the heaviest thing I’ve ever carried. Heavier than the sword.” He was quiet. “I didn’t know why. It’s smaller than the sword. It’s half.”

“Because you knew where it was going,” said Alea. Quietly. From the edge of the cairn.

Salty took his hand off the stone and looked at her. His blunt square face did the thing it did, these days, more often than it used to.

“Yeah,” said Salty. “Probably.”

Wendy found the white stone.

At the foot of the cairn, in the middle of the children’s ring, where she’d put it herself, kneeling in the frost, in the white winter. She knelt now, in the green, and looked at it. Small and flat and smooth as an egg.

“I put that there,” said Wendy. “At the head. Where she’d see it first.” She didn’t look up. “Somebody put it on the top of the pile, at the forge. I never found out who.”

Alea looked at her father.

Her father, at the edge of the green, didn’t say anything. But Maigo felt it through the ground: the old warm root going still. And Wendy, who saw things first, looked up from the white stone, at him, and saw him not say anything, and Maigo watched her understand.

“Oh,” said Wendy. Very quietly.

“From under the end of the jetty,” said Alea. “Where Mum stood. When she said here.” She looked at Wendy. “He went in up to his chest in the dark to get it. The night before the dray.”

Wendy looked at the white stone she’d put at the head of a grave in the snow, without knowing what it was or who’d put it on the pile. Then at the man at the edge of the green.

“You could’ve said,” said Wendy. A bit thickly. “On the first night out of Valcrest. When she asked. You went still. I saw you go still.”

“I know,” said the man.

“You could’ve said.”

“It was hers,” said the man. “Not mine. To say.”

Wendy looked at him for a long time. Then she looked down at the stone again, and put one finger on it, very lightly, the way you’d touch something to see if it was warm.

“It was the right place,” she said. “Wasn’t it. Where I put it.”

“It was exactly the right place,” said the man.

They ate there.

Nobody planned it. The man went down the slope to the fire Salty had built at the foot of the knoll and came back with a sack Alea had carried out from the fort, and in it there was bread, and cheese, and the last of the dried fish from the stream that he’d been drying on the wall all week, and apples from the fort’s orchard, the small hard green kind, not show apples, nothing like show apples, and a flask of something from Oakes that Oakes had handed him at the gate without a word.

And they sat on the green round the cairn and ate it. All of them. Ten. Aldous with his book on his knee. Rhea, who ate four apples. Niko, very neat, breaking his bread into pieces before he ate any of it. Yan with Suzu round his neck, and the colours in his eyes, looking at each of them in turn, quietly, as if he were learning them. Salty and Wendy. Alea with her back against the tree. The man at the edge, with his rod beside him, eating fish.

And Maigo, at the foot of the ash, talking. Of course. He’d stopped not-talking at dawn and he wasn’t going to start again. He told them about the paper antlers, again, because Rhea hadn’t heard, and about the king’s goose, again, because Niko asked, and about the horse, who still hadn’t told him his name.

“She’d have fed everyone,” said the man. At some point. Quietly. To nobody, or to the stone. “She always fed everyone. You’d come into the infirmary with a cut and leave with a bowl of soup you hadn’t asked for.” He looked round the green at the ten of them sitting there eating bread and cheese and fish round a cairn in the sun. “She’d have liked this. People eating. At her place.”

Alea leaned her head back against the trunk of the tree, and closed her eyes, and smiled.

They went back at midday. Group 3 and Aldous and Rhea, down the slope and out onto the red toward the fort, an hour off, in the sun.

The man and the girl stayed.

A few days, he’d said. The way I always do. Three days, at the grave, on his own, every year for eight years, sitting by the cairn, saying whatever he said to her, a month’s walk from anyone who knew him. This year it was three days with two of them, and a blanket, and a fire at the foot of the knoll, and Astra standing beside the black stone where Alea had said it would stay as long as they did.

Maigo was the last to go.

He stood at the edge of the green, with his shoe back on and his bare root-foot on the red, hurting, the way it always hurt. And looked at the tree.

“I’ll be back next month,” he told it. “Like always. I’ll tell you how they got home. And about the snow. It’s going to snow again this year, I think. Calvin says it’s going to be a hard winter.” He was quiet. “And next year, he’s coming back. For the tenth. With his brothers. Gabe and Deston and Xav. You’ll like them. Gabe’s really big.” He swallowed. “Malcolm wrote in his book that the rest of Group 6 are going to ask if they can come too. And if he says no, they’ll stand at the top of the road.” He grinned. “He won’t say no. He never says no to anybody standing at the top of a road.”

The leaves moved.

“Look after them,” said Maigo. Very quietly. “For three days. And then look after her. Like you have been.” He put his hand up, flat, toward the tree, the way you’d put your hand up to a window. “And hold on to the cold thing. Don’t let go. You’ve got two hands now. Three, with the spike.”

He turned to go.

“Maigo,” said the man.

He stopped.

The man was sitting at the edge of the green, where he’d slept, with his rod across his knees, looking at him. Maigo couldn’t see his face. Nobody could. But he didn’t need to; he’d never needed to. He’d known the man by his voice for two years on Earth and by the way the red went quiet round his feet for one year here.

“The root,” said the man. “Under the red. From the fort to here.” He was quiet. “You said it was mine.”

“It’s yours,” said Maigo.

“I didn’t know.”

“I know you didn’t.” Maigo looked at the ring of green, the only green on the whole of the red. “That’s why the flowers grow. Every spring. Nothing else out here does. It’s you. You put your hand on the ground nine years ago when you put the sword in, and you never lifted it.” He swallowed. “I told the tree a year ago. I didn’t tell you. I didn’t know if you’d want to know.”

The man was quiet for a long time.

“Thank you, Maigotron,” he said.

Every leaf on Maigo’s antlers came out at once.

Not budded. Not turning. Came out. Full green leaf, the whole crown of them, all at once, in the middle of the end of summer, on the edge of the red, the way they’d come out in a road outside Valcrest. So suddenly that his hood fell back. So green that Yan, halfway down the slope, turned round and stared.

Maigo stood at the edge of the green with his antlers in full spring leaf and his face doing something he couldn’t stop, and couldn’t say anything. For the second time in two days. And then, because he was Maigo, and this was the most he’d ever not been able to say anything in his life, he said the only thing that would come.

“THE MACHINE,” said Maigo, very loudly, to the whole of the Redmarch.

The man laughed. On the green. With his rod across his knees. The helpless kind. And behind him, at the foot of the tree, the girl put her face in her hands and laughed too. And the tree moved, all of it, every leaf, the long slow ripple from the bottom to the top.

And the red, below the knoll, all the way to the mountains, didn’t hurt.

Not for a while. Not under Maigo’s bare foot, all the way back to the fort. For the first time in two years.

He told the tree about that next month. It was the first thing he told it.
