The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 9: The Other Front

Chapter 263

There and Back

4,697 words · 20 min read

From the well to the north gate. Touch it; flat hand on the wood, or it doesn’t count. And back.

He still ran it.

Every morning, in the grey, at the end of the night, when the call went from a sea to a tide and the long room sat down one at a time and the king on the gatehouse went from holding to standing. Tiger came out of the long room past Azac’s shoulder, with Drew’s hand only just off his back and the call only just off him, and went to the well in the guardhouse yard, and put his hand on the rim. And ran.

Up the lane. Round the corner by the Bell. Up the long street with the lamps still lit in every window, the way the order said, burning low and yellow in the grey. To the north gate.

The doors were in. They’d been in since the first night: pushed back on their hinges, the bar cracked through, the great iron-bound leaves standing open against the inside of the arch like a pair of hands spread. And across the arch, at the outer end, where the doors should have been, a wall. Pale, curved, shimmering, taller than the woman holding it, with the cobbles of the road showing through it rippled like the bottom of a stream. Hild, in front of it, with her sword up in both hands and her head on one side.

And on the other side of it, pressed up against it, the neighbours.

The post was inside the arch. The old one, the oak one, the one he’d touched every morning for three years, black with hands. He ran into the arch, past the soldiers with their grey shields, past Hild’s back, and slapped it. Flat hand on the wood.

And looked up.

Through Hild’s wall, through the ripple, past the faces of the first ring pressed up against the light with their hands flat on it and their eyes on the tower, past the second; ten paces out, in the third ring, in the grey, a freckled face. Turned toward the arch a hair further than anybody else’s. Looking at the post.

“Tomorrow,” said Tiger.

And the face turned. A hair.

He’d been doing it for eleven days.

Eleven mornings. The first morning after the gate came in, he hadn’t meant to. He’d been carrying all night, round and round the town in the dark, from the gate to the square to the tower to the Bell, with the call on him every step, Tiger, there you are, oh, there you are, pulling at him toward the north wall the way a current pulls at your legs; and every hour he’d gone into the long room and put his hand flat on Drew’s back and it had come off him like getting out of a river. And at the end of the night, when the call let go a little, he’d gone to the well. Because he always went to the well at that time. Because his legs did.

He’d run it. He’d got to the arch and seen the wall across it and the neighbours against the wall and stopped, and nearly not touched the post, because it was ten feet from Hild and a crowd of people who’d once been the town.

Hild had said, without turning round: “Touch it. You always touch it.”

So he’d touched it. And looked up. And there was Ossie, in the third ring, ten paces off.

Not eleven miles. Ten paces.

“He’s at the gate,” said Tiger.

He said it to Drew, on the fourth morning after Kei’s dose, in the long room, in the grey, sitting on the floor against the bench with his shoulder against Drew’s bad side. Drew was in a bad way. Everybody was. The long room had slept, the night of the dose, all forty, for the first time; but the next night the call had come back and found another edge, the way Hild had said it would, somewhere further in, and the forty had stood up again; and Kei had sat on the well for two days making his root smaller and hadn’t got it small enough yet. So Drew had held them again. And the night after. And the night after that.

Three ribs, now. Kei said the third was only cracked.

“I know,” said Drew.

“You said.” Tiger’s tail was round both of them. “On the bridge. Not here. It’s coming down. He’ll come to the gate. It’s his race.” He swallowed. “He’s at the gate.”

Drew didn’t say anything for a while. He was looking at the ceiling with the red eye and the clear one.

“I said he’d come to the gate,” he said.

“Yes.”

“I didn’t say what you’d do when he did.”

“No,” said Tiger. “You said not yet.”

Drew turned his head, slowly, and looked at him. He looked for a long time. He looked at Tiger the way he’d looked at him for twelve years, since long before either of them had a tail or a pair of bladed knuckles or a name a valley could say; the way you look at somebody whose face you know better than your own, to see if it’s changed.

“What do you want to do?”

“Touch him,” said Tiger. “Flat hand. Like the post. He’s got his hand up. Rui Xiang said. Open, flat, like it’s on the gate.” He held his own up, palm out. “He’s been holding it like that for two years waiting for somebody to put their hand on it. I want to put my hand on it.” His voice went. “And say I won. And come back.”

“There and back,” said Drew.

“There and back.”

“It knows your name,” said Drew. “It says it every step you take out there. You told me. If you go out there, through Hild’s wall, into the rings, with your hand on his—“

“I know.”

“It’ll have you by the hand.”

“I know,” said Tiger.

Drew shut his eyes.

He was quiet for so long that Tiger thought he’d gone to sleep. The long room was quiet round them. Annet on her bench with her head on Ivo’s good shoulder. The Dacey girls back to back. Joss asleep on the floor on Tiger’s other side, against his arm, with his red scarf over his face, the way he slept every morning now; Kei had told him, the day after the dose, sitting on the floor of the long room with Hisui round his neck, very carefully, the way Kei told anybody anything: her hands came off the wall a hair after the others. She’s in there. Not far down. And Joss had cried, and then slept for eleven hours, and woken up and asked Kei to say it again.

“At the end of the night,” said Drew, with his eyes shut. “When it goes from the sea to the tide. When they take their hands off and turn round to go back.” He breathed, carefully, round the ribs. “That’s when it’s weakest. That’s when I’ve got my hands back.” He opened his eyes. “I’ll be in the arch. Behind Hild. You go through, and you touch him, and you come back to me. Not to the post. To me. I’ll be standing there.”

“You can’t even stand.”

“I’ll stand,” said Drew. “I’m the one who stays.”

He asked the king.

He didn’t have to. He wanted to. Rui Xiang had asked; Rui Xiang had stood in the forge door for a quarter of an hour, Agnes said, waiting for the hammer to stop, and been told not yet, and gone away and come back with the answer. You didn’t do a thing like this without asking the man who’d written don’t kill them if you can help it and stood on a gatehouse roof for eleven nights holding the dark off a girl on a tower.

So he went up the gatehouse stair at the end of the night, the next night, the fifth after the dose. In the grey. With the call going from the sea to the tide, and the king going from holding to standing.

The king was at the parapet. He looked old. Tiger had never thought of him as old; he was the king, he was enormous, he made shields at an anvil all day with his sleeves rolled and stood on a roof all night. He looked old now. His beard was dark down the front where his nose had been bleeding every night for a week and Kestra had wiped it with her sleeve. His big scarred hands were flat on the parapet and shaking, very slightly, the way a smith’s hands shake at the end of a long day. Kestra stood a length behind him, soaked to the knee from the grating, the way she was every morning now.

The king didn’t turn round.

“Tiger,” he said.

“Majesty.”

“You’ve come to ask if you can go out.”

Tiger stopped on the top step.

“I can feel you,” said the king. “All night. Going round and round the town like a dog round a sheepfold. With it on you. Pulling.” He was looking north. At the fields, and the crowd at the foot of the wall below him, beginning to take its hands off the stone. “And every morning you go to the post and touch it, and I feel him turn.”

“Yes, Majesty.”

“Drew says yes.”

“Yes, Majesty.”

The king was quiet. Below them, all along the north wall, the first ring was taking its hands off the stone. Slowly. One at a time, then more, the way the long room sat down.

“In the spring,” said the king, “your friend came to the gate and asked you to let him in. Bee’s dog barked at him. And you didn’t let him in.” He looked at the hands coming off the wall. “That was right. It wasn’t him.”

“No.”

“Hyvred says it’s him now.” The king turned round, finally, and looked at Tiger. His eyes were red at the rims. “In there. Under it. When you touch the post. She says he turns because he wants to see who won.”

“Yes.”

“Then go and tell him,” said the king. “There and back, son. And if it takes your hand, you let go. You hear me? You let go. I’ll not lose a tiger to it for a race.” He turned back to the parapet. “Go on.”

He went down the stair into the arch.

Drew was there. Standing. Behind Hild, against the inside of the gatepost, the oak one, the black one. With his chest bound and his face grey and his hands up in front of him, open, with the blades on. Standing, the way he’d said.

Rui Xiang was beside him. Tiger hadn’t known he’d be there. He was just there, the way he was everywhere now, a slight young man in a coat with his hands in his pockets, plain as a post; and nobody had noticed him until Tiger looked straight at him.

“Please come back,” said Rui Xiang.

“I am.”

“Please.”

“I am,” said Tiger.

Hild didn’t turn round. She had the wall up across the outer end of the arch, pale and shimmering, with the first ring on the other side of it beginning to step back from it now, one at a time, taking their hands off the light.

“When I drop it,” said Hild, “you go through. I’ll put it back behind you. I’ll drop it again when you come.” She was listening. “I’ll hear you coming.”

“Okay.”

“Three,” said Hild. “Two.”

He felt his legs go.

Bolt. The thing he’d had since the first summer, when Hild had stood him on the sand and said once a day, until your legs learn to pay for it. The aura coming up into his legs from wherever it lived, all at once, hot, the way it came up every morning at the well. He’d run on it every day for three years. He’d never run on it toward anything.

“One,” said Hild.

The wall fell off her blade like rain.

He went through.

Out of the arch, into the grey, into the crowd. The first ring was right there; a pace off, turning away from the wall, with their hands coming down to their sides. A woman in an apron. A man in a carter’s smock. He went between them. They didn’t see him. They weren’t looking at him; they were turning round to go back, all of them, at once, slowly, like a congregation turning to leave a church, and their faces were turning with them, away from the town, toward the valley.

Not all of them.

He’d known he’d have a moment. Rui Xiang had said: they don’t see you, they’re not looking, you’re just in the way. He hadn’t known what it would feel like to be in the way of three hundred people turning round. They didn’t push him. They didn’t need to. They just turned, and walked, and he was in the middle of them like a stone in a river, with the call coming up under him like a current, Tiger, Tiger, there you are, oh, there you are, pulling at his legs.

He ran through it. Sideways, between shoulders, through gaps, the way he’d gone through the crowd at the Lists with Drew shouting LEFT from somewhere behind. Into the second ring. Through it.

Into the third.

There.

Ossie was the only one who hadn’t turned round.

Every face in the third ring had come round to the valley, the way the first ring had, and the second; the whole crowd turning to go back the way it had come, faces and all. And in the middle of the third ring, one face hadn’t. One freckled face, turned back over its own shoulder toward the arch, toward the post, the way it had turned every morning for two years; a hair further than anybody’s. While the body under it turned to go.

He was taller than Tiger remembered. Or Tiger was smaller. Big feet. Freckles. A Western Watch coat gone pale at the shoulders. Frost on his eyelashes. Breathing, the breath going up out of him into the grey.

And his right hand, down at his side. Open. Flat. Palm out. At the height of the top bar of a gate.

Tiger put his hand on it.

Flat. Palm to palm. The way you’d slap a post, except he didn’t slap it, because it was a hand; he put his whole hand flat on Ossie’s open one, cold, frost-cold, as cold as the oak in the arch on a winter morning; and held it there.

“I won,” said Tiger.

Ossie turned round.

Not his face. His face was already turned. The rest of him. The way you turn round when somebody you know says something behind you in a crowd. And his face changed.

Tiger had seen it once. On the north gate in the spring of the first year, in the dark, with Bee’s chalk dog barking: Ossie’s face with something else wearing it, smiling a smile that wasn’t his. Patient. Interested. He’d seen that face in his sleep for two years.

This wasn’t that.

The smile went. All at once. Like a cloth pulled off a table. And under it there was a freckled boy in a Western Watch coat with frost in his eyelashes, looking at a tiger demihuman with his hand flat on his, in the middle of a field, in the grey, with his mouth a little open. Bewildered. The way you’d look if you’d woken up standing in a field with no idea how you’d got there, and the first thing you saw was your mate.

His own face. Big and loose and a little bit stupid.

He looked at Tiger’s hand on his.

He looked at Tiger.

“Tiger?” said Ossie.

His voice. Not the one from the gate in the spring, the warm one, the one that said let me in, I’ll do the roof. His own. Hoarse, from two years of not using it. A boy’s voice.

Tiger couldn’t say anything. He’d had a whole speech. He’d had it for two years. He’d said it to the post every morning. He couldn’t find any of it.

“I won,” he said again. It came out wrong.

Ossie looked at him. And something happened to his face that Tiger had seen a hundred times in the first spring in the mud of the north road, every time they’d raced and Ossie had lost and come up out of the puddle laughing, with mud to the eyebrows: the grin. Big and loose and stupid. The grin of a boy who’d lost a race and didn’t care, because he’d had fun.

“Tomorrow,” said Ossie.

That was the heartbeat.

Tiger felt it end. He felt it the way you feel a rope go tight in your hands when the boat it’s tied to catches the current. The grin was still there; it was still Ossie’s face, a breath, half a breath, and then something came up under it from a long way down and took hold.

The hand under his closed.

Not Ossie’s grip. He knew Ossie’s grip; they’d shaken on every race. This was something else wearing Ossie’s hand. It closed on Tiger’s, fingers between fingers, and held. And it was cold. Not the cold of frost. The cold of the inside of the long room at the balance, when forty people stood up at once.

And it pulled.

The whole crowd was walking. Back across the fields, toward the valley, at a market-day pace, in their rings. And Ossie was walking with them. His body was. His face was going; Tiger watched it go, the grin sliding off it like water off a window, the smooth patient smile coming up under it. And his hand held Tiger’s and walked, and Tiger went with it.

A step. Two. He dug his heels in on the frosted stubble and they slid.

Tiger, said the call.

Not in his ears. In his chest, in his legs, everywhere. Louder than it had been in eleven nights, because he was in it now, in the middle of the rings, with his hand in the hand of the third ring; Tiger. There you are. Oh, there you are. You came. You came all the way. Come on, then. Come with him. It’s warm. You’ll like it. You were always going to come.

He was walking.

He didn’t decide to. His legs did it. The way they’d done the well and the gate every morning for three years without being asked. He was walking with Ossie, hand in hand, across the frosted fields, away from the town, with three hundred people walking round him in rings; and the call was in his legs, come, and it was so easy. It was the easiest thing he’d ever done. Like running downhill.

There and back, he thought. From somewhere a long way off. There and back. You’ve done there.

He couldn’t remember what the other half was for.

Somebody hit him in the chest.

Not hard. Two hands. Open. Flat. Palms against his chest, through his coat, either side of his breastbone, the way you’d put your hands on a door to stop it swinging. And a pair of steel blades along the knuckles, cold, against his jacket.

And the call, coming in through his hand from Ossie’s, through his arm, into his chest, went into the two hands—

And turned.

He felt it go. He’d felt it every hour for eleven nights, going off him through his own hand flat on Drew’s back. He’d never felt it from the front. It went into Drew’s hands and Counter took it and turned it and sent it out again, along exactly the line it had come in on: back down Tiger’s arm, through his hand, into Ossie’s hand, up Ossie’s arm, into the third ring, into the rings, into the thing walking in front of them all across the frosted fields. Exactly as hard.

The pull stopped. Not all of it. Enough. His heels caught.

Drew was standing in front of him.

On the stubble, in the grey, in the middle of the crowd, with three hundred people walking past him on both sides at a market-day pace and not seeing him. Grey-faced. Blood down his front. Standing. With his hands flat on Tiger’s chest and his feet planted, the way he’d planted them on the millstream bridge in the autumn with Tiger coming at him at full speed; and the call going into his hands and back, and into his hands and back.

“Let go,” said Drew.

Tiger looked at his hand. In Ossie’s. Fingers between fingers.

“I can’t.”

“You can.”

“He said it.” His voice cracked all the way. “Drew. He said it. He said tomorrow. He knew me. It was him. It was him for a—“

“I know,” said Drew. “I heard. I was right behind you.” His hands didn’t move. “There and back. You’ve done there. You did it. You touched him. You told him.” He breathed, short, round the ribs. “Now you have to do back. That’s the half that counts. That’s the half nobody ever does. Let go.”

Ossie was still walking.

He’d never stopped. His body walked on, slowly, with the third ring round him, toward the valley; and his hand held Tiger’s and pulled, and Drew’s hands held Tiger and sent it back, and between them Tiger’s arm was stretched out straight in the grey between two people who were going two different ways.

And Ossie’s face had turned round again.

Over his shoulder. Back toward Tiger. Toward the town. The smooth patient smile was on it; but under the smile, a hair under, the way you can see a face under water, Tiger could see the other one. Looking at him. The way it had looked at the post every morning for two years. To see who’d won.

Tomorrow.

Tiger opened his hand.

It was the hardest thing he’d ever done. He’d known it would be. He hadn’t known it would feel like nothing at all, like letting go of a cup; one finger, then the next, and the cold hand sliding out of his, fingers from between fingers, and gone. And his own hand left in the air in front of him, open, flat, palm out, at the height of the top bar of a gate.

Ossie’s hand went back down to his side. Open. Flat. Palm out. Waiting.

And he walked on, with the third ring, across the fields, toward the valley, with his face turned back over his shoulder toward the town; the only face in three hundred that was. Getting smaller. A freckled face in a pale grey coat, walking backwards into the valley without walking backwards, looking at the arch.

Tiger stood on the stubble with Drew’s hands flat on his chest and watched him go.

He didn’t fall down. Drew did.

Not far. To his knees. On the frosted stubble, in the grey, with his hands still up, open, where Tiger’s chest had been, and his face gone the colour of the frost. The ribs. All three. Tiger heard the sound he made.

“Drew—“

“I’m fine.”

“You’re not fine—“

“No,” said Drew. “But I’m here.” He was looking past Tiger. At the crowd going away, at the third ring, at a face turned back over a shoulder. “He said tomorrow.”

“Yes.”

“Then that’s tomorrow,” said Drew. “He’ll be at the gate tonight. And you’ll touch the post in the morning. And he’ll turn round.” He shut his eyes. “That’s what tomorrow is. It’s the one after this one. You don’t have to win it today.”

Tiger got down on the stubble beside him.

He didn’t know how long they were there. The crowd went away across the fields, slowly, at a walk, in its rings; the thing in front of it, the thing he couldn’t see; the curve on the left that didn’t breathe. Back to the edge of the valley, where it would stand all day in the cold facing the town. The fields got lighter. The frost went pink on the stubble round them. And somewhere behind them, the wall came down off Hild’s blade like rain, for good, for the day; and people came out of the arch across the fields toward them at a run. Rui Xiang first, who wasn’t plain as a post any more; and Xav; and Kei with his bag; and Bee, with her wings going so hard she was nearly off the ground, which she’d never been, not once, in three years.

Tiger put his arms round Drew on the frosted stubble and put his face in Drew’s shoulder, the bad one, and Drew said ow, very quietly, and didn’t move.

He opened his window in the Bell, at noon, sitting on the end of Drew’s bed while Sister Agathe bound Drew’s ribs again and Drew told her a story about a goose to stop himself making noises.

He read it on the end of Drew’s bed. Drew had stopped telling the goose story halfway through and gone to sleep with his mouth open and Sister Agathe’s knot only half tied.

In the morning he ran it.

The grey. The end of the night. The long room sitting down; the king on the gatehouse going from holding to standing. The well. The lane. The long street with the lamps burning low.

The arch. Hild’s wall across it, pale, curved. The neighbours on the other side, taking their hands off the light, turning to go.

He slapped the post. Flat hand on the wood.

And looked up. Through the ripple. Past the first ring and the second. Ten paces out, in the third, a freckled face, already turned toward the arch; the only one; with the smooth patient smile on it, and under the smile, a hair under, like a face under water, something else. Looking at the post. To see who’d won.

“Tomorrow,” said Tiger.

He meant it, this time. He hadn’t meant it for two years. He’d said it the way you touch a doorframe in a house you grew up in. He said it now the way you say it to somebody at the end of a race, standing in the mud, with your hands on your knees, laughing: again. Tomorrow. Same time. I’ll beat you.

The face turned. A hair.

Behind him, against the inside of the gatepost, bound to the armpits, grey, a man who couldn’t stand up said good very quietly; and stood there anyway.
