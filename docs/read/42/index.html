The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 2: Letters

Chapter 42

The Faint Thread

3,761 words · 16 min read

When the letters stopped, Deston still had the thread.

That was what got him through it. Five weeks with nothing from Duskhollow. Five weeks of writing to Xav every few days and getting silence back, while the rest of Group 4 started to look at each other over supper and not say what they were thinking. Five weeks, and every evening, Deston went up onto the roof of the chandler’s on Rope Street, and sat down among the chimney pots, and closed his eyes, and found it.

Xav’s thread. Gold, and taut, and running away from his chest across the city and out over the river and upriver, north, into the dusk. He could follow it now. That was new, since the winter. He couldn’t see where it went, not really, but he could feel along it, the way you feel along a rope in the dark, hand over hand, until you reach the other end and something tugs back.

Something always tugged back. Every evening. Tight and worried and stubborn, the way Xav had always felt, the way he’d felt when they were thirteen and Xav was losing a game he was determined not to lose.

So Deston knew Xav was alive. He knew Xav was frightened, and busy, and too stubborn to stop. He knew whatever was happening in Duskhollow, it hadn’t happened to Xav.

It wasn’t a letter. But it was the next best thing.

Kingsferry in spring was a loom.

That was how Deston saw it now, whether he wanted to or not. He’d walk down Rope Street in the morning to the wharves, and the whole city would be threads. Grey ones, mostly, between strangers: thin and slack, the shopkeeper and the man buying bread, the bargemen who’d nodded to each other on the river for twenty years and never learned each other’s names. Gold ones, here and there, between people who loved each other: a mother and a child; two old men on a bench; a girl leaning out of a window toward a boy on the street below, and a thread between them so new and bright it practically hummed.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Running everywhere, crossing and recrossing, over the river and along the quays and in and out of every door in the city. The whole of Kingsferry, woven together.

He’d stopped being overwhelmed by it somewhere in the winter. Now he mostly found it beautiful.

Except for the gaps.

He looked for those too, now, every time he went out. People with no threads at all. He’d seen four in his life: the polite stranger outside the infirmary in the autumn, turning his hat by the brim, and then the three at the midwinter market, standing in a triangle round Rachael. Four people walking through the loom of the city without touching a single strand of it, like holes in a cloth.

He hadn’t seen one since the night market. He looked every day. Every face on Rope Street. Every barge that tied up at the quay. Every man in a good coat.

None. Not since midwinter.

He didn’t know if that was good or not.

Rachael’s healing had grown with the spring.

Deston went down to the infirmary at the river gate to walk her home, the way one of them always did now, both ways, every day, without discussion. It was late afternoon. The infirmary was a long low whitewashed room that smelled of vinegar and lavender and boiled linen, and Sister Maud was at the far end of it shouting at a dock foreman about a crane, and Brother Tamsin was rolling bandages by the window, and Rachael was kneeling beside a cot near the door with her hands on a man’s arm.

He was a bargeman, a big one, grey-faced and gritting his teeth. Somebody had wrapped his forearm in a rag and the rag was red right through. A rope burn, Tamsin said quietly, coming over to Deston at the door. A mooring line had run through his hands and taken the skin off his arm from wrist to elbow, all the way down to the meat.

“Watch,” said Tamsin. He was smiling, faintly. “She’s been doing this all week.”

Rachael peeled the rag back. Deston made himself look. It was bad: raw and red and weeping, a long ragged stripe of torn flesh. The bargeman hissed through his teeth and looked at the ceiling.

Rachael didn’t flinch. She’d spent twenty-six years being the patient, she’d told them once. Hospitals, needles, other people’s hands on her. She knew exactly what it felt like to be on the cot, and she’d decided, the first week here, that she was never going to make anyone feel the way the bad nurses had made her feel. So she didn’t flinch, and she didn’t hurry, and she didn’t say this won’t hurt.

She said, “This is going to sting, and then it’s going to stop. I promise.”

And she laid her hands on either side of the wound, not touching it, and closed her eyes.

Deston saw her threads first. He couldn’t help it. She had more of them than anyone he knew now, apart from Gabe: gold ones to each of Group 4, and to Sister Maud, and to Tamsin, and then dozens of thin new ones, running out of the door and away across the city to every dockworker and washerwoman and child she’d ever sat beside in this room. All of them faintly warm. All of them people she’d held.

And then the light came.

Gold. Soft. Running from her palms into the bargeman’s arm like water into dry ground. Deston watched the raw red edge of the wound, at the wrist end, go pink, and then pale, and then close: new skin, thin and shiny, creeping across the burn like frost across a window. A finger’s width. Two. Three. All the way up the man’s forearm, slowly, steadily, over the course of a long minute, while the bargeman stared at his own arm with his mouth open and Rachael knelt beside him with her eyes shut and sweat running down her face.

It stopped about two fingers below his elbow. Rachael opened her eyes and sat back, breathing hard.

“That’s all I’ve got,” she said. “Sorry. The rest you’ll have to grow yourself. Keep it clean. Come back tomorrow, and I’ll do another bit.”

The bargeman looked at her. Then at his arm. Then at her again.

“Lady,” he said hoarsely, “you can do whatever you like to me, any day of the week.”

“Don’t make it weird, Hob,” said Sister Maud, from the far end of the room, without turning round.

“Thirty-eight,” Rachael said, on the walk home, looking at her window. She was still a bit pale. “Seven this week. Maud says I’m going to be insufferable.”

“You’re already insufferable,” said Deston.

“I know. It’s great.” She closed her window. Then she was quiet for a few steps, along the quay in the evening light, with the barges knocking against the wharves and the gulls crying.

“It’s because of the line,” she said. “Thea’s line. Close. I wrote it into my hand in the winter, when I burned it on Gabe’s forge, and it worked for one second.” She flexed her fingers. “And now every time I put my hands on someone, I can feel where it is. The gap. Like a word on the tip of my tongue. And I just say it again.” She looked at him. “Every day, a little longer. A finger’s width more.”

“Like a song,” said Deston. “You learn it a line at a time.”

“Like a song,” Rachael agreed.

He walked her up Rope Street with her threads running away behind her across the city, gold and warm, dozens and dozens of them. She was the brightest thing on the street. Brighter every week.

And every week he looked a little harder at every face that passed her.

The letters came the next morning.

All of them, all at once, in a satchel carried by a rider in the king’s colours who’d come downriver by fast barge, and who wouldn’t let go of the bag until Emrys had signed for it in a ledger. Inside there were twenty-two letters, five weeks of them, the seals broken and pressed back badly, along with a packet of narrow strips of paper covered in tiny coiled blue writing, and a letter from Cursiffe marked No. 1.

They read them at the table in the room above the chandler’s, all five of them and Emrys and Sabine. Emrys read aloud, the ones in English he could hear but not read, while Deston translated what he couldn’t follow, and Emrys’s voice was still hoarse from the night market, nearly four months on. He’d thrown everything he had into one sung flash of light that night, and it had cost him. It was coming back, slowly. He could hold a note again, if it wasn’t a high one.

So it was Deston, in the end, who read Tiger’s.

It had been copied out by somebody in Group 2. Jared, Deston thought; the hand was quick and slanting and very clear. Copy, for Group 4. The original went to Xav. It was numbered and sealed with one of the blue strips. Deston slit it open along the other edge, the way Cursiffe’s letter said, and read it aloud in the quiet room.

The gate. The dogs. Ossie’s face. It knew about the roof. They’re kind. They asked about everyone. They’d like to meet the one who looks.

When he finished, nobody said anything.

Then Rachael said, very quietly, “My master’s household. In the hills. West.”

Everyone looked at her.

“The man outside the infirmary,” said Rachael. “In the autumn. With the hat.” Her voice was steady. Her hands, on the table, weren’t. “He said he wanted me to come and work in his master’s household. In the hills. In the west.” She swallowed. “He was so polite. He said they’d look after me. He said I’d be safe there.”

“Kind,” said Dennis flatly. He’d gone very still in his chair. “They’d be kind. They asked about everyone.” He shook his head. “That’s the same lie. Different mouth, same lie. I could hear it on the stranger in the autumn like a cracked bell. I’d hear it on this one too.”

Gabe hadn’t said anything. He was sitting at the end of the table, a big quiet shape with forge-black on his hands, and he was looking at Rachael. Deston saw the thread between them. Always gold; tonight it was tight as a bowstring.

“Nobody’s going west,” said Gabe. “Nobody’s going anywhere. Not on their own.”

“I know,” said Rachael.

“I mean it.”

“I know, Gabe.” She reached across the table and put her hand over his. “I know. I’m not stupid. I’m not going anywhere.”

Sabine, by the window, had her arms folded. She’d been listening to all of it without moving.

“Thornwall’s three weeks from here by road,” she said. “The passes are a week past that. Whatever’s over those mountains has had people walking in and out of Kingsferry since the autumn.” She looked at Rachael. “It’s not finished with you.”

“Thanks, Sabine,” said Rachael. “Very comforting.”

“I’m not paid to be comforting,” said Sabine. “I’m paid to be right.”

There were letters from Xav, too.

Three of them. Five weeks old, four weeks, three. Deston read those last, on his own, on the roof, in the evening. They were short and tense and very Xav, all plans and contingencies, with Malcolm’s IF/THEN lists folded into them. The light under the guild seal was getting brighter every week. The guild was talking about sending an expedition down. Group 6 had asked to go and been told no. Garrick says it’s adventurers’ work. Tier 3 and up. We’re not ready. Underneath, in smaller writing: I know we’re not ready. I hate that we’re not ready.

And at the very end of the last one, three weeks old: Haven’t heard from you in a month. Tell me you’re alive, Deston. Tell me something.

Deston sat on the roof with the letter in his lap and felt sick.

Three weeks Xav had been waiting. Thinking the same thing Deston had thought. Something’s happened. Nobody’s told me. And Deston at least had had the thread, every evening, tugging back. Xav didn’t have threads. Xav had had nothing.

He closed his eyes and found it.

Gold, taut, running north. He followed it, hand over hand, out across the city and over the river and up, up the Wren, into the dusk, the way he did every evening. Further than usual tonight. He wanted to feel the other end properly. He wanted to make sure.

There. Xav. Tight and worried and stubborn and alive. And underneath that, tonight, something new: a held breath. Like someone standing very still at the edge of something, waiting to see what came up.

Something’s happening, Deston thought. Something’s happening in Duskhollow right now.

He held on to the thread, and sent the only thing he could along it. Not words. Threads didn’t carry words. Just a sort of pressure. A squeeze. I’m here. I’m alive. I’ve got you.

He didn’t know if Xav felt it. He’d never known.

And it was then, holding on to Xav’s thread at the very far end of how far he could reach, that he noticed the other one.

He almost missed it.

It was running right alongside Xav’s. Close, the way two ropes run close when they’re tied to the same post. That was why he’d never seen it before: every time he’d followed Xav’s thread upriver, the gold had been so bright that anything beside it disappeared. Tonight, holding on at the very end of his reach, he’d gone a little further than ever, and a little past, and for a moment Xav’s thread had been behind him instead of in front.

And there it was. Going on.

Past Xav. Past Duskhollow. North. Further than Deston had ever reached. Up the Wren, toward wherever the Wren came from, into dark he couldn’t feel the bottom of.

It was very strange.

It wasn’t thin. That was the first thing. If he’d been asked to guess, without looking, he’d have said it was one of the thickest threads he had: woven of many strands, close and tight, the way Gabe’s was. The way threads got after years and years. A rope, not a string.

But it had no colour.

None. Not gold. Not grey. It was pale, almost clear, like glass, like something that had been dyed once and then left out in the sun so long that every bit of colour had bleached out of it. He could barely see it at all. If he hadn’t been holding on so hard, right at the end of his reach, it would have gone straight past him.

Distance didn’t do that. He knew that much. He’d known it since the first month. Distance didn’t thin threads, and it didn’t fade them; Xav’s was as gold in Duskhollow as it had been beside him on the river road. Whatever had happened to this one, it wasn’t miles.

It looked old. That was the only word for it. Like a rope that had hung in a window for thirty summers. Like something that had been there a very long time.

And it ran from his own chest.

“What?” said Gabe.

Deston opened his eyes. Gabe had come up onto the roof at some point without him hearing, the way he did most evenings, and was sitting against the chimney with his knees up, eating an apple.

“You’ve got the face,” said Gabe. “The thread face. What?”

Deston didn’t answer straight away. He was still holding on to it, with the part of him that held threads. Pale and strong and faded and going north, into the dark.

“There’s another one,” he said slowly. “Running next to Xav’s. I’ve never noticed it before. It goes past him. Further north. Upriver.”

“Another what? Thread?”

“Yeah.”

“Whose?”

“Mine.” Deston touched his chest, without thinking, where it started. “It’s mine. It’s really thick. Like yours. Like it’s been there for years.” He hesitated. “But it’s got no colour. It’s like… faded. Bleached. I can hardly see it.”

Gabe took another bite of his apple and chewed it, thinking. He’d always been like that. He let things land before he said anything about them.

“Upriver,” he said. “North.”

“Yeah.”

“What’s north?”

“I don’t know. Duskhollow. Then…” Deston tried to remember Emrys’s maps. “Wrenhaven, eventually. The lake country. Where the Wren comes from.”

Gabe looked at him.

“Who do you know up north?” he said.

Deston opened his mouth.

“Nobody,” he said.

They hummed, later, the way they did most nights.

They didn’t plan it. They never had, not since the first week. One of them would just start, on the roof, in the dark, with the river going by below and the lamps coming on along the quays. Low, without the words. The song they’d learned at thirteen, in a choir room in Singapore, in the back row, trying not to laugh in the quiet part. A song about how you measure a year of someone’s life.

Three parts. Gabe took the low line. Deston took the middle. And in the gap where the third line should have been, the one that went over the top of the other two and held it all together, they left a space, and didn’t fill it.

They always left the space. Neither of them had ever said why.

Tonight, in the middle of the second verse, with the space open between them in the dark, Deston felt the faded thread move.

Not much. Hardly at all. Just a tremor, very far away, at the other end. Like a rope going taut for a heartbeat when someone on the far side shifts their weight. Or turns their head.

He stopped humming.

Gabe stopped too, a beat later, and looked at him.

“What?”

“Nothing.” Deston’s heart was going very fast. “Nothing. I thought… nothing.”

He held on to the thread with everything he had, and waited. Nothing came. It was just a thread again: pale and old and strong and silent, running north into the dark, past Xav, past everything, to wherever it went.

He’d imagined it. He must have. He was tired, and frightened, and he’d been reading Tiger’s letter about a dead boy walking up to a gate, and Xav’s about a light coming up out of the ground, and he’d just felt Xav standing at the edge of something, holding his breath. Of course he was imagining things.

“Keep going,” he said. “Sorry. From the bridge.”

Gabe looked at him for a moment longer. Then he started again, low, from the bridge. Deston came in on the middle line.

And they left the space, the way they always did.

He couldn’t sleep.

He lay in the dark in the room he shared with Gabe and Calvin, listening to Calvin talking in his sleep about the wind and Gabe breathing slow and heavy on the other side of the room. He held on to the faded thread, and thought about it, carefully, the way Emrys had taught him to think about a chant he didn’t understand yet. One bar at a time.

Every thread he had, he knew. He’d counted them, in the winter, lying awake like this. Gabe, thickest, oldest, nearly rope. Rachael. Dennis. Calvin. Xav. RX, and John, and Malcolm, and Azac; Gabe’s friends and his, from before, from home. Then the others, the thirty-five, fainter, newer, bright. Emrys. Sabine. Sister Maud. The baker on the corner. All of them made here, or brought here, and all of them with colour in them. All of them gold or grey or somewhere in between.

All of them accounted for.

So whose was this?

Thick. Woven over years. Older than anything else he had. Older than he’d been in this world. Much older, if he was reading it right. As if it had been tied at the far end a very long time ago, and had hung there in the dark, faded and patient, waiting for him to look.

And it went north.

There was a thought. He could feel it, just past where he was letting himself think. Like the third part of the song. A shape in the dark, in the gap, where something should be.

He didn’t let himself finish it.

He turned over in the dark and pulled the blanket up and lay with his back to the room. And he held on to the faded thread, very gently, the way you’d hold someone’s hand in a hospital, until at last, toward dawn, he slept.

In the morning he wrote to Xav.

No. 1, at the top, and one of Cursiffe’s strips across the fold. He told Xav he was alive. He told him about the letters, and Rachael’s arm, thirty-eight percent and insufferable, and Tiger’s copy, and Rachael’s stranger in the hills, and everything else. Thirty-three, he wrote, at the end, under his name, in the column. Gabe 27. Rachael 38. Calvin 31. Dennis 29.

Then he sat for a long time with the pen over the page.

I felt you last night, he wrote at last. On the thread. You were standing very still, like you were waiting for something. I don’t know if you felt me. I squeezed.

He thought about writing the rest. The other thread. The faded one, running north past Xav, toward the lake country.

He didn’t. He didn’t know what he’d say. There’s a thread I can’t explain and it goes somewhere I’ve never been and when Gabe and I sang last night I thought something moved at the other end. Xav would read that and think he’d lost his mind. Or worse, Xav wouldn’t.

He wrote: Be careful. Whatever you’re waiting for, be careful. Gabe says the same, but louder.

And then he signed it, and sealed it, and gave it to the king’s rider, who took it upriver on the fast barge that afternoon.

Deston stood on the quay and watched the barge go, north, up the Wren, until it was out of sight round the bend. Running alongside it, very faint, where only he could see, the old pale thread went on ahead of it into the distance, as if it knew the way.
