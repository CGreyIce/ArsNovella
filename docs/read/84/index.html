The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 3: The Anniversary

Chapter 84

First Ice

3,221 words · 14 min read

The snow came to Lake Ilse in the night, and Alea woke to the silence it made.

She always did. Every year. It was the first thing she remembered about winters, from when she was very small: waking in the dark in the room under the eaves and not knowing why she was awake, and lying there, and slowly understanding that it was because the world had stopped making noise. No wind in the reeds. No water on the shingle. No hens. Just a thick soft quiet, pressing on the shutters, the way a hand presses on your ears.

She lay there in the dark and listened to it for a while.

Then she got up, and went to the window, and pushed the shutter open a crack, and looked.

White. The hen-yard, the woodpile, the long beds where the pumpkins had been, the roof of the shed and the long sailcloth roof over the cold forge on the shingle, the jetty. All of it white, and soft, and still. And beyond the jetty, the lake, black, enormous, smoking very faintly in the cold, the way it did the first night of the winter, as if it were breathing out.

And on the end of the jetty, under the snow, a shape. Sitting. With a rod.

She’d known he’d be there. He always was, the first night.

She watched him for a long time. He wasn’t fishing. His window was open on his knees; she could see the faint shine of it from here, the way you’d see a candle through a curtain. Reading. And then he closed it, and sat there, not moving, with his face turned east.

She didn’t know what he’d read. She found out in the morning. Group 3 had carried her mother’s stone across the red, and set it in the hole where the sword had stood, and the girl with the bow had shot an arrow at a star.

She’d thought, watching him at the window in the dark, that he was smiling.

He was.

The lake froze from the north shore. It always did.

Alea had watched it every winter of her life. First a thin white line along the far side, under the pines, the morning after the first hard frost. Then the line creeping out, a little further every night, grey and then white, pale and cracked at the edges, the colour of an old plate. It took a week, usually, for the ice to reach across. Another week before it was thick enough to walk on. Her father always went out first, alone, with a pole, testing, and came back and said not yet, and went out again the next day.

This year it took eight days. On the ninth, he went out with the pole at dawn, and walked out a hundred paces, and stood there for a long time, listening to it. Then he came back, and stood on the end of the jetty, and looked at her.

“Bring it,” he said.

She’d trained on this ice since she was thirteen.

Three years, every morning, every winter, in the dark before the sun came up over the hills, with a plain wooden practice sword and her father standing ten paces away with his arms folded, saying the same things over and over. Your weight’s on your heels. Let it rise. Don’t push. Down through your back foot. Three years of it, until the first tier came up in her one morning in the spring, on the last of the ice, so quietly that she didn’t notice for a moment, and then noticed, and sat down on the ice and cried.

The ice was different with a real sword.

She found that out on the first morning. She carried Astra down off the jetty in Garrow’s harness and unbuckled it and lifted it down and stood there on the grey ice, in the dark, with the long black blade in both hands, and her father ten paces away, and the whole of the lake round her, white and still, going away to the pines on the far shore.

She let the aura come up. Into her bones, the way the second tier was meant for. She felt herself go hard all over.

She lifted Astra over her shoulder, and let it fall.

And her feet went out from under her.

She lay on her back on the ice with Astra across her chest and the wind knocked out of her and the stars going round, and heard her father not laugh.

She heard him very carefully not laugh. It was worse than laughing.

“Footing,” he said. From ten paces away. Not moving.

“I know,” said Alea, to the stars.

“On the shingle, the ground takes it. The sword falls, your feet push, the ground pushes back. On the ice, nothing pushes back.” He was quiet a moment. “You have to take it yourself. All of it. Through your back foot, into the ice, and the ice doesn’t help you, and doesn’t hinder you. It just lets you.”

Alea lay there and thought about that.

“That’s why you made me do it on the ice,” she said. “For three years. With a stick.”

“Yes.”

“So that when I had a real one…”

“So that when you had a real one,” said her father, “you’d already know how to stand on something that didn’t help.”

She sat up. She got Astra off her chest, which took two tries. She stood up, carefully, with her boots slipping, and got her feet under her the way she’d got them under her a thousand times with a stick: weight on the back foot, not the heels, knees soft, letting the ice hold her up without asking it for anything.

She lifted Astra over her shoulder.

She let it fall.

It came round in a long flat arc over the ice and roared, the way it had on the shingle the first time, and dragged her half a turn, and her boots slid, and she didn’t fall. Not quite.

“Again,” said her father.

By the end of the first week, the town was coming down to watch.

Not down the lake road. Rowan was at the top of it every morning, the way he always had been, big and grey and immovable with his chain on, and nobody came down past him. But the lake had a shore, and the shore didn’t belong to anybody. They came along it from the town, from the boat landing below the mill, in the dark before dawn, in twos and threes, wrapped up against the cold, with lanterns. And they stood on the snow on the shore, a long way off, a hundred paces and more, and watched a fox girl with a black sword on the ice.

Alea didn’t mind. She’d stopped minding, at the forge.

By the second week there were fifty of them, every morning. Children, mostly, with their parents. The egg-woman’s boy, who brought his little sister. Edric, with a basket of rolls, who sold them to the crowd and pretended that was why he was there. Garrow’s apprentice, a gangly boy called Pip, who stood at the very front and watched her feet and her shoulders and her hands the way Garrow had watched the king, with his mouth open.

The children copied her.

She saw it on the fourth morning, between strokes, when she stopped to breathe and looked toward the shore. A row of them at the edge of the ice, where the reeds stuck up through the snow, six or seven of them, with sticks. Lifting the sticks over their shoulders, solemnly, all together. Letting them fall. Sliding, and falling over, and getting up again, and doing it again, while their parents laughed.

Pip was at the end of the row, with a length of fence rail nearly as tall as he was.

Alea watched them for a moment with Astra’s point resting on the ice. Then she looked at her father.

He was watching them too. Ten paces away, with his arms folded, his face doing nothing at all. But she saw his eyes go along the row of children with their sticks, one after another, the way they went along a line of fish in the basket in the morning, choosing the best.

“Your weight’s on your heels,” he said. Not to her. Quite loudly. Across a hundred paces of ice, to a row of children on the shore who’d never been spoken to by him in their lives.

The row of children froze.

Then, all together, very carefully, seven small people on the edge of a frozen lake shifted their weight off their heels.

Alea had to turn away so they wouldn’t see her laugh.

They cheered when Astra sang. Every time. It sang more often now. Not always. One stroke in five, maybe, when everything was right, her back foot and her bones and the breath and the fall: the long black blade coming round over the ice and the air not tearing for it but opening, and closing behind it, and that low clean note going out across the lake in the dark.

One in five. By the end of the second week, one in four.

And Group 1 watched from the hill.

She could see them. Every morning. The long timber hall up above the town, with its yard, and the upper window that looked down over the roofs to the lake. Five shapes in it, in the dark, crammed together, the way they’d been crammed in it every day of the forging. Too far to see faces. She didn’t need faces.

They didn’t come down. Not to the shore, not with the town. She’d wondered if they would. They didn’t. They watched from the window, every morning, and then she’d see them come out into the yard for Maren’s drills, small dark shapes on the snow, and she’d go up the hill herself an hour later with the basket, and they’d look at her, and look away, and not say anything about it.

Except PenguChi.

PenguChi was in the yard before anyone, every morning, the way she’d been since Kestra left.

Alea found her there on the first morning she went up after the ice, in the half-dark, alone, with Waddles sitting on the edge of the frozen water trough watching her with one eye shut. Standing in front of the split practice post in the middle of the yard with a practice sword. Striking it. Over and over. Saying something under her breath before every stroke.

“Again,” PenguChi was saying. “Again. Again.”

She stopped when Alea came in at the gate. She turned round. Her golden eyes went to the black grip standing up over Alea’s shoulder, and then to Alea’s face, and something in her own face lit up, slowly, like a lamp being turned up.

“You did it on the ice,” said PenguChi.

“I fell over.”

“Of course you fell over.” PenguChi smiled. “Everybody falls over on the ice.”

“Who told you that?”

PenguChi thought about it.

“Nobody,” she said. “I just thought it would be true.”

They compared numbers.

They did it every morning after that, the two of them, by the trough, before the others came out. It had started as a joke. It wasn’t a joke anymore. PenguChi would open her window, and Alea would open hers, and they’d stand there side by side in the cold, showing each other, the way the five of them had compared windows every night in the barracks since the first week. The way the two of them, the only two in Wrenhaven on the hard path, had never had anyone else to compare with.

“Fourteen,” said Alea. “That’s three since Kestra.”

“Seven,” said PenguChi. “That’s two since the ice.”

“The ice is hard.”

“The ice is hard,” PenguChi agreed, very gravely, and they both laughed, and Waddles flapped his stubby wings on the edge of the trough and nearly fell in.

“How did you get three?” said Alea.

PenguChi told her. Again, every morning. Twenty times, thirty. And somewhere around the twentieth, every time, the thing Kestra had shown her: waiting. Not for the aura to rise. For it to arrive. “Rising isn’t arriving,” said PenguChi. “That’s what she said. You let it come up, and then you wait, and then you wait a little longer, and then.”

Alea stood very still by the trough.

“That’s what he says,” she said. “About fishing.”

PenguChi looked at her.

“Not the words,” said Alea. “The same thing. He says you don’t pull when you feel the tug. You wait for the second one. Most people miss the fish because they pull on the first.” She stopped. “He wrote it in his book. On Waiting. Cursiffe sent you the—“ She stopped again.

She’d never said that before. His book. Not out loud. Not to any of them.

PenguChi didn’t look away. She didn’t say anything about it. She just looked at Alea with her calm golden eyes for a long moment, in the cold, by the trough. And then she nodded.

“Everybody who’s good,” she said, “learned it from somebody.”

It went on like that for three weeks.

The ice in the dark, and the town on the shore, and Astra singing one stroke in four, and then one in three. The hill, and the basket, and five people in the yard not saying anything. PenguChi by the trough, and their windows side by side. Fifteen. Eight. Seventeen. Nine. The nights getting longer, and colder, and the snow coming again, and again, until the town was buried to the windowsills and the lake was white from shore to shore and the paths between were dug out of the snow like trenches.

Her father watched her every morning from ten paces away on the ice with his arms folded. He said again and footing and, once, at the end of the third week, after a stroke where Astra sang so long and so clear that the whole crowd on the shore went silent instead of cheering, better.

He said it the way Petra said it, the girl in Duskhollow, in Xav’s letters. The way Kestra said it. Alea didn’t know where he’d learned it. She thought maybe they’d all learned it from the same place.

And every night, he sat on the end of the jetty with his window open on his knees and read.

She asked him at the end of the third week.

She hadn’t meant to. She’d been meaning not to, for a month. Since the king rode away. Since he’d sat on the end of this jetty with Severin in the dark and showed him his window and said halfway, and then come in and showed her too, and she’d held on to his neck from behind and said I’m so glad.

She’d thought, then, that it would be soon. She’d thought the king’s visit had changed something. The forge, the town coming down the lake road, the stones, him laughing at the bellows, him saying thank you to every child, five times to five people with no names. She’d thought: he’s ready. Any day now.

Two months. And every morning she took the basket up the hill, five fish, and five people looked at her and looked away. And every night he sat on the jetty and read their lines in the channel and didn’t write any.

They were on the jetty. Dusk. Snow coming down again, slowly, in big soft flakes that didn’t seem to be in any hurry to land. Astra leaning on a post beside her. His rod across his knees. Neither of them fishing.

“When?” said Alea.

He didn’t ask what she meant. He never did.

“Soon,” he said.

Alea looked at him.

“You’ve said soon since the king,” she said.

He didn’t answer.

“You said not yet to Rowan in the summer,” said Alea. “And not yet to the king in the autumn. And soon to me, every week since. And they’re up there. Every night. All five.” She pointed with her chin, up the hill, at the hall, at the single lamp in the upper window. “They’ve been up there every night for a year and a half. Watching the lake. Waiting.” She swallowed. “They’ve never once asked me. Not once. Not is he, not when, not does he know. They just look at me every morning and look away.”

He was looking at the lamp too.

“I know,” he said.

“Then why?”

He didn’t answer for a long time.

The snow came down between them and the lamp on the hill. It settled on his shoulders, and the rod, and Astra’s black grip, and didn’t melt.

“Because I’m frightened,” he said.

Alea stared at him.

He’d never said that. Not in her whole life. Not about the war, not about the hunters, not about the Redmarch or the king or the thing in the orchard or anything. She’d asked him once, when she was ten, the first winter after, if he’d been frightened on the Redmarch, and he’d thought about it, honestly, the way he always did, and said no. And she’d believed him.

“Of what?” she said. Very quietly.

He looked at the lamp on the hill.

“Of their faces,” he said.

He didn’t explain. She didn’t ask him to. She sat there on the end of the jetty in the snow beside him and thought about it, and found, after a while, that she understood. Not all of it. Enough.

Five people who’d known a funny boy on a stream, half the world away, who made them laugh and gifted them subs and modded their chat and beat them at word games. Who’d died in his sleep, three years ago, or thirty-five, depending how you counted. And who’d see, when he opened the door, a man of sixty in a body of thirty-five, who barely smiled, who’d killed more things than they could count, who’d buried her mother on the Redmarch with his own hands.

Who wasn’t the funny boy anymore. Not really. Not all the way.

Of their faces. Of what their faces would do, when they saw what he’d become.

Alea put her hand on his arm.

“Dad,” she said. “They’ve already seen you.”

He looked at her.

“At the market. On the road. At the gate when you went to the Redmarch. At the forge. Every day for a year.” She held on. “They’ve seen you. They know exactly what you are now. They worked it out, Dad. All of it. On their own. And they’re still up there. Every night. Waiting.” She swallowed. “Their faces aren’t going to do anything you haven’t already seen them do.”

He didn’t say anything for a long time.

Then he put his hand over hers, on his arm, and held it there.

“Soon,” he said again.

But it sounded different, this time. Alea couldn’t have said how. It sounded like the second tug on a line, after the first one, when you finally know it’s a fish.
