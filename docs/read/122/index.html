The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 5: The Lists

Chapter 122

Twelve

6,978 words · 30 min read

Deston had been listening to them since the last milestone.

That was the only word he had for it. Listening. He’d tried others, on the road, for Thea, who wanted the right word for everything: seeing, feeling, reading. None of them were right. A thread wasn’t a thing you looked at. It was a thing that sounded. Every one of them had its own note, the way every voice in a choir does, and if you stood in the middle of a room full of them with your eyes shut you could hear the whole chord: who was near who, and who was leaning which way, and who was holding something under their breath and not singing it.

He’d stood in the middle of the fifteen for sixteen days on the road south and listened to them sing. Gold, all of them, warm, close. And the man at the back with the water, gold all the way through, the loudest note in the whole thing and the one that never once raised its voice.

And for the last two miles, coming down between the market gardens toward a city full of paper fishing rods, he’d been listening to the others.

Fifteen threads, in a square, a mile off and then half a mile and then right there. The far threads, he’d called them on the downs. The ones that ran out of the fifteen and the man with the water and away across the kingdom to the other groups. He’d seen them curve in on four roads to Valcrest from an old stone on a hill, and he’d seen their colours, and he’d said a number to Xav in the dark and Xav had said okay, sit down, then, again.

Three of them were warm. Chris and Ina and Winter. They’d been warm since the winter; something in Valcrest had told them, or nearly told them, and they’d held it and said it’s not ours to give you and gone on holding it, and the holding had turned their ends of the thread a colour like late sun on a wall.

Twelve of them were faded.

Not grey. Grey was the other kind: the thirty-six threads that went out of the world toward people who’d stood at a funeral. These were just pale. Worn. The colour a thread goes when the person at the other end of it has been carrying it for three years in the belief that the man at this end is dead. He knew that colour better than anyone alive. His own end of his own thread had been that colour for three years and more, in Kingsferry, until a night on a roof when it had suddenly pulled upriver like a fish on a line.

Twelve. Group 2, all five. Group 5, all five. And Newish, and Hako.

He’d felt them all morning, getting closer. Thin and pale and pulling, all twelve of them, the way a faded thread still pulls, the way you still reach for somebody’s hand in a dark room years after they’ve stopped being there to take it. And he’d walked down the road between Gabe and Xav with his hands in his pockets and said nothing, and listened, and waited.

They’ll all go at once, he’d said, on the downs.

He’d been guessing.

He wasn’t watching the gate when it happened. He was watching Declan.

He’d watched him all the way in. The water-carrier at the back, behind Alea, with the yoke a bit crooked where Tiger had knocked it, and his front soaked, and the buckets half empty. He’d watched him stop where Hyvred sat down in the road. He’d watched him set the buckets down, carefully, so they didn’t spill, the way he did everything with his hands. He’d watched him lean the yoke on the wall and straighten up and turn round.

And because Deston was watching Declan, and because he’d been listening for sixteen days, he heard the very first note.

Hi, Leaka.

One thread went.

It went gold the way a struck string goes: all at once, from end to end, ringing. Aleaka’s. He heard it more than saw it. A note that had been held thin and pale for three years under somebody’s breath suddenly let out full, the whole of it, loud. He felt Gabe beside him go still. Gabe couldn’t hear threads. Gabe could hear Deston, though, after fourteen years, and whatever Deston’s face had just done, Gabe had heard it.

Then the girl said you’re late, in a voice like a headmistress, and Declan said yes, ma’am, meekly, in English, the way he must have said it a hundred times at two in the morning to somebody half a world away. And Aleaka put her hand over her mouth.

And then she laughed.

It came out of her round her hand, cracked down the middle, half a sob. Not loud. Just a girl in a gate square laughing at a water-carrier with her hand over her mouth and her eyes running. But every one of them was looking at her. That was the thing. Deston understood it later, much later, lying awake. They’d all been looking at her. The fifteen in the square had all been watching her since dawn, because she’d been first, because she’d come before the light and sat on a mounting block by herself with a hawk, and they all knew what that meant. And when Hyvred sat down in the road, every one of them had looked to see what Aleaka would do.

And they saw her face.

And the other eleven went at once.

It was like a choir finding the chord.

He’d stood in a choir for four years. He knew the moment. The conductor’s hands come up, and twenty voices that have been breathing out of time all take the same breath, and the hands come down, and on the downbeat every one of them comes in together, and the sound in the room turns from twenty separate things into one thing, and the hair goes up on your arms.

Eleven threads went gold on the downbeat.

Hana’s first, by a hair: from the edge of the square where she’d been standing with Milli, a thin pale line that suddenly sang, warm and soft and so full it hurt. Then Milli’s, a heartbeat after hers, because Milli always went a heartbeat after her, because Milli wanted proof and had got it off his wife’s face. Cursiffe’s, slow, the way he did everything, but all the way. Jared’s, by the gate, from a man who’d been standing too far away from something for three years, all at once and nowhere to put it.

Kei’s, from the mounting block, with a little bright cold coil wound round it that was Hisui, talking very fast. Drew’s, steady, as if he’d known all along and had only been waiting for the bracket to be drawn. Bee’s, sharp and astonished and cross about it. Tiger’s, from somewhere up the road behind them, where he’d stopped shouting halfway through a word. Hyvred’s, from the cobbles at Declan’s feet, where she sat looking up at him: and hers didn’t so much go gold as open, like a shutter going back, as if it had been gold underneath the whole time and she’d only just let herself see it.

Hako’s, bright and easy and not even surprised, the way an optimist isn’t surprised when the thing he said would be fine turns out to be fine.

Newish’s.

Newish’s went gold and then went very still, like a held note. Gold, all the way. But held. As if she’d opened her mouth to sing and then decided to wait for her bar.

And the three warm ones went with them. He’d never thought about what would happen to those. Chris’s and Ina’s and Winter’s, warm as late sun since the winter, and on the downbeat they caught too, the way a warm ember catches when you blow on it, and went up gold with the rest.

Fifteen threads, in a square, ringing.

He’d been wrong on the downs. Not about at once. About what it would feel like. He’d thought it would be like a door opening. It wasn’t. It was like standing in the middle of a room when every voice in it comes in on the same beat, and the sound goes through the floor and into your feet and up through your chest and out of the top of your head, and you’re not standing in the room any more. You’re in the chord.

Deston’s knees went.

Gabe caught him under the arm without looking, the way he’d caught him on the jetty in the spring when the song stopped. Xav, on his other side, put a hand flat between his shoulders and left it there. Neither of them asked. They didn’t need to. They’d stood next to him for too long.

“Okay,” said Xav, very quietly. “Okay.”

“Twelve,” said Deston. He couldn’t get anything else out. “Twelve. Gabe. All of them. At once.”

“I know,” said Gabe. He didn’t. He couldn’t hear it. But he was looking at the square, at fifteen people who’d all just put their hands over their mouths, and his voice had gone thick. “I know, Dest. I can see.”

Then Declan went round them.

He did it the way he’d done everything in the six weeks Deston had been watching him: slowly, without fuss, one thing at a time, and the right thing. He didn’t make a speech. He didn’t stand in the middle of the square. He went to each of them where they were, the way you’d go round a room at a party making sure everybody had a drink, and to each of them, in English, very quietly, he said one thing.

One thing only Ice could have said.

He went to Aleaka first, because she was nearest, and because she’d been first. She’d stopped laughing. She was just standing there with her hand over her mouth and her tail wrapped so tight round her own legs she was going to fall over. He didn’t say anything to her at all. He’d already said it. He put his hand on top of her head, between her ears, the way he put his hand on Alea’s head on the jetty at night. And Aleaka took her hand off her mouth and put both arms round him and her face into his wet coat and made a noise Deston would hear for a long time.

The hawk on her shoulder, pressed flat against her neck, didn’t bite him. Deston would think about that later, too.

He crouched down by Hyvred, after. She was still sitting in the road against the wall, under the rods, with her glasses up in her hair and her gold eye still locked on him, the little red mark in it like a sight that had found what it was for.

“You looked,” said Declan.

“I said I would,” said Hyvred. Her voice was wet and enormous and hyper, the way it always was in the channel, and it cracked straight down the middle. “I said. I said properly.”

“You did.”

“You’re—“ Hyvred stopped. Whatever she could see, she didn’t say. She never did, afterwards, to anyone; Deston asked her, much later, on a different road, and she just looked at him with the scope eye and shook her head and said it’s his. Now she put her hand out and took hold of his sleeve, the wet sleeve, in her fist, the way a child holds onto a coat in a crowd. “You’re here,” she said instead.

“I’m here.”

She didn’t let go of the sleeve. He didn’t make her. He stayed crouched in the road beside her, with his sleeve in her fist, and looked up at Hana.

Hana had come across the square. Deston hadn’t seen her do it. She was just there, in front of him, with Milli a pace behind her, and her one visible violet eye very wide, and her tail very still.

“Alice,” said Declan.

That was all. One word.

Deston knew the rule. Everybody in the fifteen knew it; it had been in the channel in the first month, in Jared’s capitals, when somebody new had called her Alice by mistake and Jared had written NO. two people. me and him. Two people in the world had ever called her Alice. One of them was standing behind her. The other one had died in his sleep in Malacca three years ago.

Hana made no sound at all.

Deston could feel her charm. Everybody could, a little, in a crowd; it was what made her dangerous. It came up off her in a slow warm wave the way it did when she was frightened or overwhelmed, the people-charm, the dangerous half, the half she kept pushed down in the capital where people would burn her for it. It came up off her across the gate square like a tide. The carters nearest stopped talking. A woman with a basket of eggs sighed and smiled at nothing.

And Hana felt it go, and for once in her life didn’t push it down. She let it rise. Let it go out across the square, warm and soft and enormous, a hundred people suddenly and inexplicably happy at seven in the morning without knowing why. Because it wasn’t hers, that morning. Deston understood that much. She’d said it once, in the channel, about a secret: it isn’t mine, it’s his. This was the other way round. For once, what she was feeling was so big it was everybody’s.

Milli put his arms round her from behind and his chin on her shoulder.

He was looking at Declan over her head. Rational. Realistic. The one who’d wanted proof of everything since the first day in the hall, proof of the way home, proof of the Advisor, proof of every word. Deston could see the thread from him, gold now, and under the gold, something stubborn and analytical that hadn’t caught up yet and was trying to.

“Prove it,” said Milli. His voice wasn’t steady. He tried to make it steady. “Sorry. I have to. I’m sorry. Prove it.”

“Bingo,” said Declan.

Milli stared at him.

“Minecraft bingo,” said Declan. “You needed one square. You were looking for it for forty minutes. Alice had to mute because she was laughing so hard you could hear it through the mute.”

Milli’s face did something complicated.

“That’s not proof,” he said. “Anybody could—“ He stopped. “Nobody could know that. That was one stream. Years ago. On another planet.”

“I was in chat,” said Declan.

Milli shut his eyes.

“Okay,” he said. “Okay. That’s proof.” And then, very quietly, into his wife’s hair, in a voice Deston had never heard him use: “Okay, Honey.” And Hana laughed, wet, and put her hand up behind her and held his face.

Jared didn’t say anything.

Declan went to him at the gate. Jared hadn’t moved from where he’d been standing all morning, on the left of the gateway, a little in front, with his arms folded and his weight forward. He didn’t move now. He stood there and looked at the man in front of him. Taller than the man he’d gamed with every night for years, probably. Different hands. Different face, if you could look at it, which you couldn’t. Not a single thing about him that Jared had ever seen.

Because Jared had never seen him. Not once. Not in a room. Not anywhere. Half a world. A phone, at the wrong time of day.

“Jared,” said Declan.

Jared’s jaw went tight.

“I owe you a postcard,” said Declan.

The Raising Cane’s postcard. Deston didn’t know the story. He found out later, from Hana, who told it on the second night with Jared sitting beside her pretending not to listen: a photograph of Jared eating fried chicken in a car park in America, turned into a postcard by a man in Malaysia, to annoy a girl who’d never been to either. It had gone round for years, that postcard. It had been the joke between the three of them. Deston didn’t know any of that, standing in the gate square. He just saw Jared’s face when Declan said it.

Jared turned round.

He turned his back on all of them, on the square and the crowd and the man in front of him, and put his forehead against the stone of the gatepost, and stood like that with his arms still folded. His shoulders went. That was all. He didn’t make a sound. He didn’t say a word. He stood with his face to the wall the way a man stands when there’s nothing in the world he can do about what’s happening to him and he’s decided to just let it.

Declan stood behind him. He didn’t touch him. He just stood there, close, a pace behind, the way Jared had stood behind the others all morning, the way Jared stood behind everybody. As if, for once, it was somebody else’s turn to stand in front.

After a while Jared reached back without turning round, and found Declan’s wet sleeve, and held onto it.

Cursiffe wrote in the air.

He did it slowly, the way he did everything. He unstoppered the little vial at his throat and wet the tip of one finger with the dark blue ink, and lifted his hand, and wrote, in the morning air of the gate square, in a line of blue that hung there glowing faintly where his finger had gone. Deston could see it from across the square. He couldn’t read it from there. Nobody in this world could read it from anywhere, except thirty-five people from somewhere else, and one more.

Cursiffe’s ink worked in English. No native in this world could read it, or counter it. Their eyes slid off it like water off a duck. He’d written seals in it all winter that the castle scribes couldn’t even copy.

The only person born in this world who could read it, he’d said in the channel once, without knowing who he meant, would be the Hero.

Declan looked at the line of blue in the air. Then he read it out loud, in English, in an even voice, the way you’d read a sign:

“If you can read this, say something stupid.”

Cursiffe’s sly half-smile had gone. His head, which he always held tipped back a little as if he knew something you didn’t, was very straight.

“Your handwriting,” said Declan, “is terrible.”

There was a pause.

Then Cursiffe laughed. Slow and relaxed and then not relaxed at all. He put the stopper back in the vial with fingers that didn’t quite work, and the line of blue in the air came apart into flakes like ash and drifted down onto the cobbles, and he stood there laughing with his hand over his eyes, and said, “That’s it. That’s him. That’s the stupidest thing anybody’s ever said to me,” and kept laughing until it turned into something else.

Tiger came back down the road at a run, from his second go at Maren, and stopped dead in the middle of the square with his ears all the way back.

He looked at the water-carrier. He looked at Hyvred on the cobbles with her fist in the man’s sleeve, and Jared at the wall, and Cursiffe laughing into his hand. He looked at the water-carrier again.

“I hugged you,” said Tiger, in a small voice. “I said whoever you are.”

“You did.”

“I spilled your water.”

“You did.”

Tiger’s mouth opened and shut. Then Declan said, quite seriously, as if he were asking after a relative:

“Did it stand?”

For a second Tiger didn’t understand. Deston saw him not understand. Then he did.

A house. A house built for weeks in a game, on a server, three years ago. A home. And a super-modded boss that had come up out of nowhere one evening and blown up the entire spawn, and the house with it, while Tiger watched, and screamed, and the whole call had screamed with him. Tiger had told that story in the channel in the first month. He told it to everybody. He told it to soldiers at Thornwall and to the cook and to a goat. My house didn’t survive. Here, nothing he built survived either. Huts. Sheds. A wall. A watchtower that fell down in a high wind before anybody had climbed it. It had become the joke of the whole kingdom: Tiger’s houses don’t stand.

But nobody had asked him, here. Not like that. Not as if it mattered whether it had.

“No,” said Tiger, with tremendous feeling. “NO. It did NOT stand. It blew up. The whole spawn.” His voice cracked into pieces. “You were there. You were on the call. You laughed.”

“I’m sorry I laughed.”

“You should be,” said Tiger, and burst into tears, and hugged him again, harder than the first time, with nothing in the way now, no buckets, no yoke, and lifted him clean off the ground.

He went to Drew next, after Tiger put him down.

Drew hadn’t moved off the mounting block. He sat there with his hot cup going cold in his hands and both Pollen Puffs asleep on his head, very calm, watching it all the way he watched a bracket. Patient. A counter-fighter. Somebody who read people and waited for them to show him what they were going to do. Deston could hear his thread, gold now, steady as a held bass note, as if it had always been going to end up there and Drew had simply been waiting for the rest of the room to catch up.

“Drew,” said Declan.

“Ice,” said Drew. Quite calmly.

“You ran the one I won.”

“I did.” Drew turned the cup in his hands. “You and Yan. You were terrible in the semis. You got carried.”

“I got carried,” agreed Declan.

“I’ve got a bracket up,” said Drew. “In the Lists. It’s forty feet long. There’s a spider on it.” He looked up, then. “You’re not in it.”

“Good.”

“I knew you’d say that.” Drew looked at him for a long moment, and then he smiled, the slow, easy smile of a man who’s just watched the last piece of something fall into place, and put out his hand. Declan took it. They shook hands, once, like two men at the end of a tournament. Then Drew didn’t let go, and pulled, and Declan sat down on the mounting block next to him, and Drew put his arm round his shoulders without saying anything else at all, and the Puffs on his head woke up and buzzed indignantly and went back to sleep.

Kei was beside them on the cobbles, with his head tipped. Hisui was looped round his neck, and Deston could hear the little bright cold coil of him in Kei’s thread, talking so fast it was almost a hum.

“He says,” said Kei, and stopped, and listened. “He says you smell like a lake. He says you smell like a lake and a fish and something very old and very big, and he doesn’t like it, and he likes it.” He listened again. “He wants to know if you’ve met the other snake.”

“Not yet,” said Declan. “Three days.”

“That’s what I told him.” Kei’s calm, trustworthy face did something unsteady. “He says three days is too long.”

“It is.”

Kei looked at him. Declan looked back. And then Declan said, very quietly, so that Deston, three paces off, only just heard it:

“You said you were fine. That night. You weren’t.” A pause. “Are you now?”

Deston didn’t know what night. He didn’t ask, then or afterwards. It wasn’t his. It was a call, once, three years ago, about something Kei hadn’t wanted to talk about with anybody, and a man in Malacca who’d stayed on the call until four in the morning so Kei wouldn’t have to put the phone down. That was all Deston ever knew, and all he needed to.

Kei’s thread didn’t change colour. It was already gold. It just went warmer, the way a fire does when somebody puts another log on.

“I am now,” said Kei.

Hisui said something. Kei laughed, unsteadily. “He says don’t tell anyone he was worried.”

Bee was standing with her arms folded on the edge of the bedsheet, under the wolf, with her antennae flat back and her wings very still.

“You hardly knew me,” she said.

She said it first. Before he could say anything. Blunt, the way she said everything, out of spite and confidence and refusal to be anything but exact. You hardly knew me. It was true. Four months. They’d never really talked. She’d been Hyvred’s friend on the edge of a call, a name in a list. When he’d died, she’d been sad for Hyvred, and that was all. She’d said so, once, in the channel, without apologising for it.

“I know,” said Declan.

“I came anyway,” said Bee.

She said it with her chin up, furious, as if somebody had told her she wasn’t allowed to. As if she’d been told off for turning up to a funeral she hadn’t been invited to. She’d been at the gate since before sunrise. She’d painted a wolf on a bedsheet.

“I know,” said Declan again. “I saw the wolf.”

“It’s a dog.”

“I know.”

Bee looked at him for a long, hard moment. Then her wings buzzed once, a short sharp sound, the sound they made when she was about to fly and couldn’t, and she said “fine,” and put her hand out, and he shook it, and she held onto it much longer than a handshake.

Hako grinned at him.

He’d been grinning the whole time. Sitting on the bedsheet with his wrapped spear across his knees and his elbows on it, watching it all happen with his whole face open, like somebody watching a match where the team he’d backed from the start was winning. Deston could hear his thread: bright and easy, gold, singing along with the rest of it as if it had always known the tune.

“Head height,” said Declan.

Hako’s grin got bigger.

“Always,” he said. “You told me. First day. Keep your crosshair at head height. Stop looking at the floor.” He laughed. “I still do it. Here. With a spear. Ysolde thinks I’m weird.”

“You are weird.”

“You taught me,” said Hako, with tremendous satisfaction, and then, because he couldn’t help it, because he’d said it every day for a year and a half and was going to say it again: “I told everyone it’d be fine. Every day. In the channel.”

“I read it.”

“Every day.”

“I know,” said Declan. “I read every one.”

Hako’s grin didn’t go anywhere. But his eyes went very bright, suddenly, and he looked down at the spear across his knees, and said, a lot more quietly, “I wanted to know you better. That’s all. I was only getting started. I wanted to know you better.”

Declan sat down on the bedsheet beside him.

“You can,” he said.

Chris had his notebook out.

He’d had it out since the gate opened. Deston had seen him standing at the edge of the square through all of it, with the pen in his hand and the book open against his forearm, logging, the way he logged everything: the hour, and the light, and how many people were in the square, and who’d arrived when. Logging, he’d said to everyone he passed. He’d written the whole thing down, Deston thought, as it happened. Every name.

Declan stood up from the bedsheet and went to him.

“Logging?” he said.

“Logging,” said Chris. His voice was perfectly steady. His pen hand was not. He looked at the notebook. He turned it round, so Declan could see. A page Deston had heard about in the channel for months, without ever seeing it: thirty-five lines, in Chris’s small square hand, every one with a name and, beside it, in a different hand, in this world’s script, what Lucan’s paper-makers had made for it. Horns and masks and antlers and squids and bees.

And at the bottom, a thirty-sixth line. With paper fishing rods written very small at the end of it, months ago, in the space where the thing a crowd would want to wear went. And no name.

“I didn’t write it,” said Chris. “I nearly did. Lots of times. I didn’t want to, until.” He stopped. He looked up. Chris, who was the oldest of the thirty-five, thirty-three, nearly exactly the age of the body in front of him. Who’d wanted, once, to meet a man in person one day. “Until,” he said again.

“Write it,” said Declan.

Chris wrote it.

Deston saw him do it. Small and square and careful, at the start of the thirty-sixth line, in English:

Ice.

And then Chris closed the notebook, and put it under his arm, and put his pen behind his ear, and stood there with his face doing absolutely nothing, the way a man’s face does when he’s got a lot of practice at being the steady one and has decided he’s going to go on being it for exactly as long as it takes everybody else to stop looking at him.

Declan put a hand on his shoulder.

Chris’s face stopped doing nothing.

Winter was beside Ina, a little apart, the way she always stood: very straight, very still, her long white hair down her back and her pale-and-pink eyes half-lidded and her face doing nothing at all. Ina stood next to her in the mask, with his hands behind his back.

Declan didn’t go close. Deston saw him not go close; saw him stop a good two paces off, the way you stop short of a cat you don’t know, and stay there. He knew. Deston didn’t know how he knew. He’d read every word in the channel for a year; perhaps that was how. He knew Winter didn’t like men standing close, and he stopped where she’d want him to, and didn’t come any nearer.

“Winter,” he said.

Winter looked at him.

“You’re not the last,” said Declan. “This time.”

Winter’s face did nothing for a long moment.

She’d been in Vietnam, when he died. Offline, mostly, for two months. And she’d come home and opened her messages and found out, two months late, that he’d been gone the whole time she was away. She’d been the last of all of them to know. She’d said so once, on a roof, in the summer. She’d said she didn’t intend to be the last to know anything ever again.

Round her feet, on the cobbles, very slowly, the frost came.

It came out from her boots in a ring, white and fine and crackling faintly in the morning sun: little feathery flowers of ice, opening on the stones one after another, the way they’d opened in a water trough at the east gate in the autumn when she wrote Still. A ring of frost flowers on the cobbles of the north gate of Valcrest, on a midsummer morning, with the sun on them.

“No,” said Winter. Very quietly. “I’m not.”

And her face, which had been doing nothing very hard all morning, at every stranger in the square, did something very small and very real. And then she turned, quickly, and put her face into Ina’s shoulder, against the indigo coat, and Ina put one gloved hand on the back of her head and left it there, the way he’d stand between her and a crowd.

Declan looked at Ina. At the grey fanged mask, with its red-rimmed eyes and its red cords and its grin. The demon’s face. The one everybody in this city looked away from.

“Ina,” he said, in a tone of enormous relief, the tone of a man who’s been waiting in a lobby for twenty minutes for the last player to load in. “Finally. Okay. You’re on support.”

There was a silence behind the mask.

Then Ina laughed. It came out of the oni’s grin, warm and helpless and very British, the laugh of a man at a garden party who’s just been told the funniest thing he’s ever heard. It went on for a long time. He didn’t take the mask off. He kept his hand on the back of Winter’s head and laughed behind the demon’s face until he had to put his other hand on the wall.

“I’m always on support,” said Ina, when he could. “Always. Three years. You’ve no idea.”

“I’ve some idea.”

“You’ve no idea,” said Ina, and laughed again.

And then Declan looked at Newish.

She was on the edge of the bedsheet still, where she’d been all morning, with her knees up and her hands in her pockets. She hadn’t moved. Her face had gone white when Hyvred sat down and it had stayed white the whole time, all the way round, every name. Deston could hear her thread. Gold, now. All the way. But held. A held note, like a singer who’s opened her mouth and is waiting, very carefully, for her bar.

Declan looked at her for a long moment across the square.

And didn’t say anything.

He didn’t go to her. He didn’t say her name. He looked at her, the way he’d looked at each of them, and Deston saw Newish see him look. And then he looked away, and turned round, and went back across the square to where his buckets were, as if that were the end of it.

Newish didn’t move. Her hands stayed in her pockets.

But her thread, the held one, didn’t go thin. Deston was listening for it, and it didn’t. It stayed gold, and it stayed held, and under the held note, very faintly, so faintly he’d never have heard it if he hadn’t been listening to people’s threads for two years, he heard something that sounded almost like a laugh.

He’s saving it, Deston thought. He’s skipped her on purpose. He’s saving it for later.

And she knew. She’d heard it too.

The gate sergeant had come out of his hut some time ago, and was standing in the middle of his square with his list in his hand, looking at it all with the face of a man who’d been a gate sergeant for twenty years and had never once seen anything like this and didn’t intend to see it again.

Thirty of the summoned were in his gate square. Every one of them crying. The squid lady was crying, and her squid was waving four arms at nobody. The penguin girl was crying, very calmly, with her penguin leaning on her ankle. The tall one in the middle was crying, and being held up by the big one and the quiet one. The horned girl had made ice come out of the ground. The oni was laughing behind a demon mask with his hand on the wall. The tiger boy had cried on everybody twice. And all of them, every single one, the famous ones, the ones the ballads were being written about in the market and the paper-makers had sold ten thousand horns for, were crying round a water-carrier.

The sergeant went to Maren. Maren was standing by the cart with her list against her chest, looking at nothing, with her mouth very firmly shut.

“Mistress,” said the sergeant. “Begging your pardon. What’s going on?”

Maren looked at him.

“I have no idea,” she said.

“Who’s he?”

Maren looked down at her list, which she had kept every day for sixteen days on the road south with one line on it that wasn’t a name, and read it out loud, in a perfectly level voice:

“Water.”

The sergeant looked at her. He looked at the list. He looked at the man with the water, who was picking up his yoke again in the middle of the square with thirty people crying round him, as if this were a thing that happened to water-carriers every day.

“Right,” said the sergeant at last, faintly, and went back into his hut and shut the door.

Declan didn’t get to carry the water in.

He’d slid the yoke back onto his shoulders, and bent for the buckets, and Jared took it off him. Just like that. Jared, who hadn’t said a word yet, who still hadn’t, with his eyes red and his jaw tight, came up behind him and lifted the yoke off his shoulders, buckets and all, and put it on his own. Then he walked off through the gate into the city with it, without looking back, very straight, carrying the water. As if he’d been waiting three years to carry something of Ice’s and this was the first thing that had come to hand.

Declan stood in the gate with his empty hands, and watched him go. And didn’t argue.

They went in like that. All of them. Thirty of them and a water-carrier with nothing to carry, and the guides and the guards, and two wagons and four horses and a squid. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Hung in the windows over the bakers’ and the saddlers’ and the inns, swaying a little in the wind off the plain, all the way up the street toward the long pale bowl of the Lists, the way reeds sway along a lakeshore.

The crowd came with them. It didn’t know why. It followed thirty famous people into the city because they were famous, and because they were crying, and because the squid was waving at everyone. Children ran ahead. Somebody started singing; Emrys, probably, who was still crying too. Somebody else threw flowers. Nobody threw anything at the water-carrier in the middle of it all, because nobody looked at him. He walked up the high street of Valcrest under ten thousand paper fishing rods hung up for the Hero, just in case, and not one person in the whole crowd looked at him.

Except thirty.

And except one girl with a big sword, walking beside him, who’d come round the corner of the gatehouse a few minutes earlier with both hands over her face and her shoulders shaking, and had been unable to look at Aleaka since without her ears going straight up.

Deston walked behind him with Gabe and Xav. He could hear all of it. Every thread. Thirty gold threads in a street, close, close enough to touch, going up the hill together, ringing. The loudest chord he’d ever stood inside. He’d hum it later, he thought. He’d find the middle part of it. Not now.

Gabe was still holding his arm. He didn’t need it any more. He didn’t say so.

It was at the top of the high street, where it opened into the market square below the Lists and the hill rose up behind with the big houses on it, that Deston heard the other one.

He’d almost forgotten it was there. In the middle of the chord, in the middle of thirty gold threads ringing in one street, he’d stopped listening for anything else. And then the street opened, and the hill was in front of them, and there it was. Pulling.

Not a gold one. A silver one. A window-thread, the first of all of them: the one the others were copied from, the root of every native window in the kingdom, running down out of a big house on the hill. Thin, and very fine, and very old. Deston had found it on the downs from a stone and followed it here with his eyes. It ran straight down the hill, through the market and the crowd, into the man beside him.

And round it, wound tight, a frayed grey strand.

Frayed at both ends. That was what he’d seen on the downs. He looked at it now and saw that one end had changed. Not the far end. The far end, up the hill, in the big house, was frayed as it had always been: grey and worn and fraying more as he watched, pulling, pulling, the way a rope frays when someone at the other end has been holding on to it far too tight for far too long.

But the near end. Declan’s end.

Declan’s end was quiet. Not mended. Not yet. But waiting. As if it had been frayed for eight years by somebody who couldn’t put it down, and had only this morning, in a gate square, among thirty people crying, decided that it was time to.

Beside it, so close the two threads almost touched, ran another one. A gold one. Beaten and hammered, like a smith’s work, square and plain and enormously strong. The king’s.

Declan had stopped walking.

He stood at the top of the high street in the noise and the flowers and looked up the hill at the big house, with the sun on its windows. Deston didn’t know if he could hear the threads. He’d never asked. He’d never needed to; Declan had always seemed to know where everybody was without being told.

“Tonight,” said Declan, very quietly, to nobody.

Then he turned round and went on into the square, after Jared, who’d stopped by the fountain with the water and was waiting for him, and hadn’t spilled a drop.
