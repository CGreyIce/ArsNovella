The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 5: The Lists

Chapter 123

You Can Stop Wondering

5,361 words · 23 min read

Nobody called him Halvard.

The king did, when they were alone, and when he was angry, which in forty years had come to much the same thing. Kestra had heard it once and never used it. The seven guides called him my lord in person and the Advisor in their reports, and the council called him the Advisor to his face and several other things behind his back, and the kingdom at large, if it thought of him at all, thought of a thin grey man in a tower in Solmere who had pulled thirty-five people out of the sky without asking anyone, and did not think of him as having a name.

There had been one other person who used it.

Halvard sat at a table in a room on the first floor of the king’s house in Valcrest, with the window open on the city and his hands flat on the wood in front of him, and had been sitting there since the seventh hour of the morning.

It was a good table. Old oak, a little warped, scarred by somebody’s knife at one corner a long time ago. The king’s house on the hill had belonged to the crown for two hundred years and had been lived in for perhaps four weeks of every one of them, at midsummer, for the Lists, and everything in it had the faintly bewildered air of furniture that is mostly kept under sheets. The sheets were off now. Four hundred people and two hundred horses had come down from Solmere and filled every room and stable and attic, and the house rang from cellar to roof with servants and soldiers and somebody’s children and, somewhere below, a cook shouting about geese.

Halvard had chosen this room because it was small, and because it faced north.

He had not chosen to put his hands flat on the table. They had gone there on their own, at the seventh hour, when a page boy came running up the stairs with his cap in his hand and said, breathless, my lord, the fifteen are in, the north gate, they say the whole square was crying. And Halvard had said thank you, and the boy had gone, and Halvard had found that his hands were flat on the table, pressing, the way you press your hands on something to stop them shaking, and that he could not quite make himself lift them.

He had been a mage for forty-five years. He had been Comet-tier for twelve. There were perhaps four people alive who could do what he could do with his hands. He sat at a warped oak table in the king’s house in Valcrest with the sun going round the sky outside, and could not lift them.

He’d known it would be today since the reports from the road.

Maren’s had come every second day, by rider, folded small. They had been Maren’s usual reports: exact, dry, lists. Day nine. Caddow. The miller’s cart has a wheel that talks. The summoned are known in every village now; they are asked for blessings; I have instructed them not to give any. Then, at the bottom, in the same small hand, the thing she always put at the bottom, because he had asked her to put it there two years ago and she had never once forgotten:

He carries the water.

And under that, in the report from the night after the Sallow, a line he had read so many times since that he could have drawn the shape of every letter with his eyes shut:

I think he is happy.

Halvard had sat with that report on his knee in the king’s tent on the road down from Solmere for a whole evening, while the camp went to sleep around him, and had not been able to decide what he felt. He decided, eventually, that he felt it was none of his business. That Maren had not written it for him. That the line had been written by a woman who did not write anything she did not mean, about a man she had watched for two years, and that it was true, and that it was the best news he had had in eight years, and that he had no right to it at all.

He had folded it up and put it in his coat. It was in his coat now.

He had not brought the others.

He had thought about it for a week before they left Solmere. Whether to bring them. The drawer in the study at the top of the west tower, the bottom one, on the left, that stuck in wet weather; and what was in it; and whether it was a thing you brought three hundred miles in a writing case to a city full of people, or a thing you left in a tower where it had been safe for eight years from everyone, including the man it was written to. In the end he had left it. He had locked the drawer and put the key in his pocket and ridden south with the king, and the key was in his coat too, beside Maren’s report, small and cold, and every time he moved he felt it there.

You miss him, the fox girl had said to him once, in his study, with her slow careful voice. Something you wrote. And never gave him.

He had said yes. He had not been able to think of anything else to say to her.

The king came in at noon.

He didn’t knock. He never had, in forty years, at any door Halvard was behind. He came in with his sleeves rolled to the elbow and soot on his forearms, because he had spent the morning down in the house’s forge with the farrier, re-shoeing a horse that did not need it, so that he would have something to do with his hands. He looked at Halvard at the table. He looked at Halvard’s hands on it.

“You’ve been there since the seventh hour,” said Severin.

“Yes.”

“Eaten?”

“No.”

The king went out again. He came back a while later with a bowl of something and a cup of something and put them on the table beside Halvard’s hands, and sat down on the edge of the windowsill with his own bowl, and ate, looking out at the city.

“They’re at the Broken Lance,” he said, with his mouth full. “All thirty of them. Lucan’s opened the whole inn and paid the landlord for the week and is weeping in the common room. The tiger boy has hugged half the street. Kestra’s on the roof.” He chewed. “The girl’s with them. The water-carrier’s with them.”

“I know.”

“Of course you know.” Severin looked at him over the bowl. “Eat, Halvard.”

Halvard ate. It was bread and something with onions in it. He couldn’t have said what.

The king finished his own and put the bowl down on the sill and sat there, with his big scarred smith’s hands loose between his knees, looking at Halvard in the way that he had, the way that made men feel the weight of the ninth tier without his lifting a finger. Halvard had known him forty years. He had stood beside him in council and on walls and in tents. He still felt it.

“He’ll come,” said the king.

“You don’t know that.”

“He’ll come when he’s done.” Severin shrugged. “He’s got thirty of them to get through. He’ll go round every one, the way he does. He’ll sit with them. He’ll let the tiger boy hug him. He’ll eat whatever Lucan puts in front of him and say it’s good.” He looked out at the city. “And then he’ll come up the hill. On his own. At dusk. Because that’s when you’ll have stopped expecting him, and he’ll know it.”

Halvard didn’t say anything.

“I’ll be in the forge,” said the king, getting up. He paused at the door. “Halvard.”

“Majesty.”

“Whatever’s in that drawer,” said Severin, quite gently, “it’ll keep.”

He went out. Halvard heard his boots on the stair, and then nothing, and then, a while later, from far below, the slow steady ring of a hammer on an anvil. Not shaping anything. Just ringing. The sound a smith makes when he is waiting for something and has decided to wait at an anvil, because that is where he waits best.

He thought about the wall.

He hadn’t meant to. He had sat at the table all morning not thinking about it, the way he had not thought about it in the tower in Solmere every night for eight years: carefully, the way you walk round a hole in a floor. But the afternoon came on, and the sun moved off the table and onto the floor and then onto the wall, and the city outside went loud and then quiet and then loud again, and he found that he had stopped walking round it.

Not that wall. The other one. The first one.

Cinderhold, twenty years ago. Autumn. He had been thirty-eight, and the youngest court mage the crown had ever sent to the border, and so sure of himself that he could feel it in his teeth. He had been sent to the eastern garrison as its strategist, to count things: men, arrows, days, demons. He had been very good at counting things. And on his fourth night there he had gone up on the wall to look at the Redmarch in the dark, which was not yet red, and found a boy up there already.

Fifteen. A garrison recruit, a commoner’s son from a village nobody had heard of, with a borrowed sword that was too long for him and a temper that was too short. Sitting on the parapet, alone, at midnight, with his legs over the drop. Looking at nothing. Looking, Halvard had thought at first, at the dark over Kharvos.

He hadn’t been looking at the dark. He’d been looking at something in front of his face that wasn’t there.

Halvard had stood behind him for a long time before the boy noticed. And when he did notice, he didn’t jump, the way a boy should have when a court mage came up behind him in the dark. He turned his head, and looked at Halvard, and Halvard had the strangest feeling, for a moment, that the boy was reading him. Not his face. Something next to his face.

You’ve got one too, the boy had said. You just can’t see it.

And then he’d shown him.

Halvard could still remember the words. Not the boy’s words. The other ones. The ones the boy had told him to think, in a language Halvard didn’t know and couldn’t pronounce and had never heard before or since except from the boy’s mouth. A short, strange, flat word, like a stone dropped on a step. The boy had said it slowly for him, twice, three times, patient, the way you’d teach a child to whistle. Say it in your head. Not out loud. Like you’re opening a door.

He had said it in his head. And a door had opened, in front of his face, in the dark on the wall at Cinderhold, and there had been words on it. His own name. His own tier. A line of light that showed him, for the first time in his life, exactly how far he had come and how far he had to go.

He had sat down on the wall walk. Like a fool. He had sat down on the stones with his back against the parapet and his hands over his mouth, a court mage of the crown, and stared at it until dawn. And the boy had sat beside him, with his borrowed sword across his knees, and not said anything at all, the way he would go on not saying anything for twenty years.

He had never asked where the boy learned the word.

He had thought about it, once or twice, in the first year. He was a strategist; it was his nature to ask. A commoner’s son from a village nobody had heard of, fifteen years old, who had been born with a door in front of his face that no one else in the history of the kingdom had ever had, and knew a word in a language that no one spoke. Every instinct he had told him to ask. And he hadn’t. He had told himself it was courtesy. He had told himself that the boy was prickly, and proud, and would close up like a fist if he were pushed. He had told himself all sorts of things.

I think I was afraid of the answer, he’d said to the cat girl, two years later, in his study.

He had been. He still was.

He thought about the other wall, after that. He couldn’t help it, once the first was open.

He didn’t let himself think about it long. He had had eight years of practice at not thinking about it long. A night at Cinderhold, twelve years after the first one, when the boy was twenty-seven and not a boy and the most dangerous swordsman in the east, and the Redmarch was about to be red. Fires on a rise an hour’s walk from the wall, lit in a ring so that anyone on the wall could see what was inside them. A healer with cold hands.

He had been at Cinderhold that night. He had been on the wall.

He stopped there. He always stopped there. He took his hands off the table for the first time since the seventh hour of the morning, and put them over his face, and sat like that for a while, in a small room facing north, with the hammer ringing very faintly far below.

The sun went down.

He watched it go, through the open window. It went down red over the plain, the way it had gone down every night of the road south, and the city lit its lamps one at a time and then a street at a time, and in every window on the high street below the hill, he knew without looking, there was a paper fishing rod. Lucan had told him about them, in the winter, in a letter. For the Hero. Just in case. The girl with the tempo asked for them. Halvard had read the letter in his tower and put it in the drawer. Not the bottom one. The one above.

It got dark in the room. He didn’t light the lamp.

He heard the king’s hammer stop, far below, at last. He heard the house go quieter, the way houses do at the end of a day when everyone has eaten. Somewhere a child was crying and being sung to. A dog barked twice and stopped.

He had stopped expecting it some time ago. He was aware of that. The king had been right; the king was always right about people, which was why he was a better king than Halvard would ever have been a strategist. Halvard had sat in the room all day expecting it, and at some point, about when the sun touched the plain, he had stopped. Not because he thought it wouldn’t happen. Because he had been expecting it for eight years and had worn the expecting thin, and there was only so much of it in a man.

He was sitting in the dark with his hands back on the table, not expecting anything, when there was a knock at the door.

It was a perfectly ordinary knock. Two knuckles, twice. The knock of a man who has been raised to knock on doors.

Halvard didn’t answer it. He found he couldn’t.

After a moment the door opened anyway, and somebody came in, and stood in the doorway with the lamplight from the passage behind him, and Halvard couldn’t see his face. Not because of the dark. He’d never been able to see it, not properly, not in twelve years; his eyes went to it and slid off, the way everyone’s did, and went somewhere else. To his shoulders. To his hands. To the long thin shape over his shoulder, which was a fishing rod.

To the way he stood in a doorway. Easy. Weight on the back foot. Ready to go in either direction and in no hurry to choose. He’d stood like that at fifteen on a wall, and at twenty-two on a parade ground with a black sword, and at twenty-seven in a tent arguing with a king. He was standing like that now.

“Halvard,” said Ars.

Halvard stood up.

He did it too fast, the way a young man does. The chair went over behind him and he didn’t pick it up. He stood at the table in the dark with his hands at his sides and looked at the man in the doorway and his mouth opened and nothing came out of it, nothing at all, not one of the ten thousand things he had written at the bottom of a drawer.

“Ars,” he said.

It came out quite steady. He was distantly proud of that.

Ars came in and shut the door behind him, and set the rod against the wall, and righted the chair that Halvard had knocked over, and sat down on the other side of the table.

He didn’t light the lamp either. The window was open behind Halvard and there was a little light from the city, and that was enough. Halvard found he was grateful. He sat down again, slowly, in the chair Ars had picked up for him, and put his hands back on the table, and Ars looked at them.

“You’ve been sitting there all day,” said Ars.

“Since the seventh hour.”

“Severin said.”

“Severin,” said Halvard, “talks too much.”

“He was always going to.” A pause. “He’s in the forge. He wanted to come up. I asked him not to, yet.”

“Thank you.”

They sat there.

Halvard had imagined this. Of course he had. He had imagined it so many times over eight years that it had worn a groove in him, the way water wears a groove in a step. He had imagined it in the study in Solmere, in the dark, with the drawer open. He had imagined anger. He had imagined coldness, which would have been worse. He had imagined Ars at the end of a jetty with his back to the door and not turning round. He had imagined the four words that came through Aldous, tell Halvard I’ve taken it, said to his face, in the voice of a man who would never say anything else to him again.

He had not imagined Ars sitting across a warped oak table from him in the dark, with his hands loose on the wood, looking at him the way he looked at a lake.

“Maren says you ask how I am,” said Ars. “At the bottom of every report.”

“Yes.”

“Every one.”

“Every one,” said Halvard. “For two years. And before that the trader. The one who takes your pages south. I paid him to tell me. Whether you looked well. Whether the girl was growing. Whether you were still fishing.” He looked at his own hands. “He was very bad at it. He said he looks the same every year for six years. I don’t think he ever once looked at you.”

“Nobody does.”

“No.” Halvard breathed. “No. Nobody does.”

“I’m well,” said Ars.

Halvard looked up.

“That’s the answer,” said Ars. “To the question. You’ve asked it for eight years. Somebody ought to answer it in person.” He was quiet a moment. “I’m well, Halvard. I fish. I grow pumpkins. The hens hate me. Alea’s seventeen and Tempered and she nearly beat Petra last week on a wet verge, moving first. I’ve got thirty friends in a city tonight, and five more on the road. I’ve got a channel I read every night that I don’t write in.” He paused. “Maren said I was happy. She was right. You can stop wondering.”

Halvard didn’t say anything.

He couldn’t. He sat at the table with his hands flat on it and his throat closed and looked at the man across from him, whose face he couldn’t see, and found that he was weeping. Not much. Not the way Lucan wept, at everything, loudly. Just enough that the little light from the window went soft and broke up into pieces.

“Maren said,” Ars went on, gently, as if Halvard weren’t weeping, “that she thinks you’ve sat in that study every night for eight years wondering whether you broke me.”

“Yes,” said Halvard.

It came out of him like something torn. He hadn’t meant it to. He’d meant to say something measured, something a strategist would say. I have had concerns. I have reflected on my choices. Instead it came out of him like a thread pulled through cloth, the one true thing, ragged.

“Yes,” he said again, quieter. “Every night.”

Ars looked at him for a long time.

“You didn’t break me,” he said.

Halvard had heard men say things to him in forty-five years that were meant to be kind, and been able to tell, every time, from the inside of the words, how much they cost the man saying them. It was a strategist’s trick. You learned to hear the weight under a sentence; you learned to hear what it was carrying.

He listened to you didn’t break me with everything he had.

And there was nothing under it. No weight. No cost. It wasn’t being given to him. It wasn’t kindness, or forgiveness, or a thing a man says because the other man is weeping and somebody ought to stop it. It was just true. Ars had said it the way he’d said I fish. As a thing that was so, and had been so for a long time, and that he was a little surprised anybody needed telling.

“You took the sword,” said Ars. “Off her grave. You used it to pull thirty-five people out of the sky. You didn’t ask me.” He said it quite calmly, like a man reading items off a list. “You sent it back with five of them on the anniversary. I don’t know if you meant that.”

“I meant it.”

“I thought you might have.” Ars nodded, slowly. “I took it back. Severin and I cut it in two. Half of it’s Alea’s now. Half of it’s standing on the Redmarch at the head of the grave with her name on it, in a script nobody can read, and there’s a tree beside it that a boy with antlers planted so she wouldn’t be alone.” He was quiet. “None of that’s broken. All of that’s mended. You don’t have to carry the sword any more, Halvard. I’ve got it.”

Halvard put his hand over his eyes.

“There’s a thing,” he said, behind it. His voice wasn’t working properly. “There’s a thing I have to tell you. There’s—it isn’t the sword. The sword is the least of it. There’s a thing from that night, from the wall. I’ve written it down. I’ve written it down more times than I can—“ He stopped. He couldn’t go on. He could feel the key in his coat, small and cold, three hundred miles from the lock. “I can’t,” he said. “Not tonight. I’m sorry. I can’t. I thought I could, and I can’t.”

“Then don’t.”

Halvard took his hand away from his eyes.

“Don’t,” said Ars again. “Not tonight.” He leaned back in the chair. “A man in Wrenhaven said that to me, a few weeks ago. At his kitchen table. I was going to tell him something I’d carried for thirty-four years, and he stopped me. He said not tonight. He said come home, and knock on that door, and I’ll get the cups out, and you can tell me then.” A pause. “He was right. I didn’t know he was right until about a day later, on a road. Some things you have to carry a bit longer before you can put them down properly. Otherwise you just drop them.”

“Eight years,” said Halvard.

“I’ve had thirty-four. You can have a while.”

They sat in the dark for a long time after that.

It wasn’t the silence Halvard had imagined, either. It wasn’t the silence of two men who had run out of things to say. It was the other kind, the kind he had sat in once before, on a wall walk at Cinderhold at dawn, with his back against a parapet and a door open in front of his face, and a boy beside him with a borrowed sword, not saying anything at all.

“Do you still look at it?” said Ars, eventually. “Every morning?”

Halvard knew what he meant.

“Every morning,” he said. “For twenty years. Before I get up.” He hesitated. “I’ve taught it to seven guides. They’ve taught it to their girls and boys. Maren has taught it to half of Wrenhaven, I gather. There are thousands of them now, opening their windows in the morning. And every one of them learned it from someone who learned it from someone who learned it from me.” He looked at the dark shape across the table. “And I learned it from you. On a wall. I never once asked you where you learned it.”

“No,” said Ars. “You didn’t.”

“I was afraid of the answer.”

“I know.” Ars was quiet. “I’d have told you. Probably. If you’d asked. I nearly told you a dozen times.” Something in his voice moved, very slightly, the way water moves when a fish turns under it. “Severin’s going to ask. In four days. At midsummer. He said he’d ask in person, and he’s earned it. He’s going to ask where I came from, and I’m going to tell him. All of it.” He paused. “You should be there.”

Halvard went very still.

“I’d like you to be there,” said Ars.

There was a boy in Wrenhaven, Halvard knew, from Maren’s reports, who could see threads.

Not a boy. A man, nearly thirty, the tallest of the thirty-five, with a voice like a held note. Deston. The one who had followed a thread upriver from Kingsferry and found a lake at the end of it. Maren had written that he saw lines running between people, coloured by what the people at either end of them felt; and that he had told her, once, on the road, that there was a thread from the man who carried the water to a house on a hill in the king’s city, and that it was frayed.

Frayed at both ends, Maren had written. He says it is the oldest of all the silver threads. He would not say more. I think he thought it was not his to say.

Halvard couldn’t see threads. He never would. He was a mage of the old kind, a counter and a strategist; his magic had always been the kind that moved things, not the kind that saw them.

But he felt something, then, sitting at the table in the dark. Somewhere about the middle of his chest. He couldn’t have said what. The way you feel it when somebody at the other end of a rope you’ve been holding for years, far too tightly, suddenly stops pulling against you. Not lets go. Stops pulling. Takes up the slack on their side, quietly, so that the whole weight of the thing isn’t on you any more. Only some of it.

Only yours.

“The tall one,” said Halvard, slowly. “Deston. He says there’s a thread.”

“There is.”

“Frayed.”

“It was,” said Ars. “He told me this morning. Up on the high street. He said my end went quiet when I looked up at this house.” He was quiet. “I’d guess it’s mended now. At my end. He’ll tell me tonight. He’ll have been listening from the inn; he can’t help it. He’s very bad at pretending he isn’t listening to things.”

Halvard looked at the window, and the city, and the lamps.

“And mine?” he said.

Ars didn’t answer for a moment.

“Yours is yours,” he said. “That’s the thing about the ends. Nobody can do your end for you.” He stood up, and went and got the rod from where it leaned against the wall. “You’ll do it when you open the drawer. You’ll know when.”

The door opened.

It didn’t knock. It never had.

Severin stood in the doorway with the lamplight behind him and soot still on his forearms and a candle in his hand, which he had evidently brought up from the forge and forgotten to light. He looked at the two of them. At Halvard at the table with his face wet, and his hands, for once, not on it. At Ars by the wall with his rod.

He didn’t come in. He leaned on the doorframe, a big plain man with a smith’s shoulders, the only Ascendant alive, who had once sat beside a grave on the Redmarch for an hour with the man by the wall and not said a word.

“Midsummer,” said the king. “I said I’d ask in person.”

“You did,” said Ars.

“Four days.”

“I know.”

“Good.” Severin looked at Halvard. “Did he tell you?”

“Not yet,” said Halvard. His voice still wasn’t working properly. He didn’t care. “He’s waiting for you.”

“Good,” said the king again, with deep satisfaction, as if something long overdue had at last been put on a shelf in the right place. He looked at Ars. “You’ll eat with us tomorrow.”

“I’m eating with thirty people at the Broken Lance tomorrow. And the day after. And every day until a girl wins or loses a thing on the sand.” Ars put the rod over his shoulder. “You can come to the Lance. Lucan would weep for a week.”

“Lucan weeps anyway,” said the king. “Bring the girl, though. To the yard. I want to see her move with it again.” He stood back from the doorway to let Ars through. And then, as Ars went past him, without turning his head, the way he’d once said a name to Halvard in a cold yard at dawn: “Ars.”

Ars stopped.

“Thank you,” said Severin, “for knocking on that one.”

Ars didn’t say anything. He put his hand on the king’s shoulder, briefly, in passing, the way a man steadies himself on a post. Then he went out down the stair, and Halvard heard his steps go all the way down, unhurried, and across the hall, and out of the front door of the king’s house into the city, toward an inn full of thirty people who would be waiting up for him.

Severin stood in the doorway a moment longer with the unlit candle.

“Light the lamp, Halvard,” he said. “You’ve been sitting in the dark all day.”

Then he went too.

Halvard didn’t light the lamp. Not for a while.

He sat at the warped oak table in the small room facing north, with the window open on the city and the lamps of Valcrest burning below him, and the paper rods in every window, and somewhere down there, in an inn by the Lists, thirty people and a man who carried water, and a girl with a sword, and a song he’d never heard.

He put his hand in his coat and found the key. Small and cold. He held it in his fist for a long time.

He didn’t feel the thread. He couldn’t. But he sat there with the key in his fist and the slack taken up, very quietly, at the far end of something, and thought, for the first time in eight years, not about what was in the drawer, but about what it would be like to open it.

Then he got up and lit the lamp.
