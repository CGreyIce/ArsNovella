The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 6: Nine

Chapter 157

The Third Seal

5,947 words · 26 min read

Oswin had stood in front of the door a hundred and sixty-one times.

He counted. He was an archivist. Every night since the winter, after supper, with the black key on its string round his neck and a lantern in his hand, he’d gone down the ninety-one steps, and unlocked the vault, and walked down the long low room between the shelves to the back wall, where the last shelf had been until he moved it. And stood there. In front of a small iron door in the stone, about the size of a cupboard, with a seal on it that nobody alive could use.

He never touched it. He’d been told not to, once, and that was enough; he’d learned his lesson about being told things. He just stood there with the lantern, for a quarter of an hour or so, the way you’d stand at the bedside of somebody asleep. And then he went back to his reading, and read until the candle went, and went up.

A hundred and sixty-one times. Through the winter and the spring, while the summoned wrote to each other and the king planned his tournament and the Lists filled up with paper horns. While he carried the circle he’d spent all autumn copying on his knees in the summoning hall, ten feet of it rolled in a tube, south to Valcrest with the king’s household, and watched a girl with pink and violet hair Parse it like a machine and write TODO in the blank half.

He hadn’t stood in front of it while he was in Valcrest, of course. He’d thought about it, though. Every night. A hundred and sixty-one, and then fifty-two nights of thinking about it from three hundred miles away, which he’d counted separately.

And then, on the road home, on the second day out of Valcrest, the king had ridden up beside him in the dust, on the big grey horse that was the only horse in the kingdom that could carry him all day, and said, without any preamble at all:

“The night we get back.”

Oswin had nearly fallen off his mule.

They got back on the eighty-ninth day, at dusk.

Four hundred people and two hundred horses, coming up the long hill to Solmere in the red evening, with the castle on its rock above the city and the bells going in every tower because the king was home. The streets full. Children on walls. Somebody’s paper horns, left over from the summer, on a little girl at the gate. The summoned riding near the front, the five of Group 2, and people in the crowd calling their names, Hana, Milli, Lady Aleaka, and Aleaka going red under her hawk and Cursiffe reading on horseback, which he’d done the whole way from Valcrest.

Oswin had expected the king to wash. To eat. To sit, at least, for an hour, after twenty days in the saddle; he was sixty-five, whatever else he was.

The king got off his horse in the castle yard, and gave the reins to a groom, and turned round, and found Oswin in the crowd of the household with his eyes, and nodded once. Toward the archive.

He was still in his riding coat. There was road dust on him to the knees.

“Now?” said Oswin. His voice came out very high.

“Now,” said the king. He looked round the yard. “Halvard.”

Halvard was already there. Thin and grey at the king’s elbow, the way he’d been at the king’s elbow for forty years, with his riding gloves still on. He didn’t say anything. He looked at Oswin, and Oswin saw that his face, which never did anything, was doing something very small round the eyes.

“And them,” said the king. He looked past Oswin. At the five summoned getting down off their horses at the back of the yard. “If they’ll come.”

“Sir,” said Oswin. “Sire. It’s the vault. It’s the summoning archive. Nobody’s allowed—“

“It’s their summoning,” said the king. “Isn’t it.”

Oswin opened his mouth, and shut it again.

“Ask them,” said the king.

They came, of course.

Hana first, with her purple cat’s tail very straight behind her and the cord on her wrist with seven halves and a half-heart on it, and Milli beside her. Milli didn’t say anything when Oswin asked. He just looked at Oswin for a moment, very calmly, with his tail gone completely still, the way it had gone still in the winter at the dinner table when Oswin had said there’s a door. And nodded.

Aleaka gave her hawk to a page at the archive door, and the page took it as if he’d been handed a live coal, and Brisk glared at him all the way up the stairs. Jared came half a pace in front of Aleaka and a little to the left, the way he went everywhere, saying nothing. Cursiffe came with a lantern and a bottle of the blue ink and a stack of paper, because Cursiffe would have brought a lantern and a bottle of the blue ink and a stack of paper to his own wedding.

And Kestra came, because Kestra went where the king went. She stopped at the top of the vault stair, at the bottom of the old cellars, and put her back against the wall there with her book under her arm, and didn’t come down.

“Somebody should be at the top,” she said.

Nobody argued with Kestra.

The stair was the trouble.

Oswin had never thought about it before. He’d gone down it a hundred and sixty-one times, and a hundred more before that, in the autumn, and he was a narrow man with narrow shoulders who’d spent his life in narrow places between shelves. You went down it sideways, with your lantern held in front of you and your other hand on the wall, counting. It was so narrow and so steep that your shoulders touched the stone on both sides if you went down it facing forward.

The king was not a narrow man.

He was the biggest man Oswin had ever seen. Broad as a door, with a smith’s shoulders and a smith’s arms and a chest like a barrel, and he’d been the biggest man in every room he’d walked into for fifty years. He stood at the top of the vault stair with a lantern in his hand and looked down it, at the steep narrow dark going down into the rock, and said, quite mildly:

“Ah.”

“Sideways, sire,” said Oswin, faintly. “You go down it sideways.”

“I’ll go down it sideways,” the king agreed.

He did. Oswin went first, with the lantern, counting out loud, because he always counted. And behind him the king came down, sideways, one step at a time, with his back scraping one wall and his chest scraping the other and the dust coming off the stone onto his riding coat, breathing hard through his nose and not saying anything. Halvard behind the king. Then the summoned. Oswin could hear them behind, the sound of six people going down a stair built for one at a time: the scuff of boots, a lantern knocking the wall, Hana saying sorry to somebody and Cursiffe saying it’s fine.

“Forty,” said Oswin. “Forty-one.”

“How many?” said the king, behind him. A little strained.

“Ninety-one, sire.”

“Who built this?”

“Nobody knows, sire. It’s older than the archive. Older than the castle, maybe.”

“Of course it is,” said the king.

At the seventieth step he stopped. Oswin heard him stop. He turned round with the lantern, frightened that the king was stuck, which would have been a thing nobody in four hundred years of the kingdom’s history had ever had to write down. The king wasn’t stuck. He’d stopped, sideways on the stair, with his great shoulders against the stone, and put his hand flat on the wall beside his face.

“Somebody cut this,” he said. “By hand. With a chisel. Look.” He ran his thumb along the stone. “Every step. You can see the strokes.”

Oswin looked. He’d never looked. You could see the strokes.

“Somebody very patient,” said the king, “who didn’t want anybody coming down here who wasn’t very patient too.” He took his hand off the wall. “Go on.”

Ninety-one.

The vault was the vault.

The long low room. The shelves to the ceiling on every wall. Four hundred years of paper and wax and old tape and boxes Oswin still hadn’t dared open, and the smell of stone and old candle smoke that he’d breathed every night for two years and would know, he thought, on his deathbed. He lit the lamps along the near wall with a taper. The summoned stood just inside the door in a row, the way people stand in a church they’ve never been in, looking at the shelves.

“All of it?” said Milli. Very quietly. “This is all of it?”

“Thirty-one attempts,” said Oswin. “Four hundred years. Eleven worked. Thirteen people.” He’d said it so many times. “It’s all here. Everything they wrote down. Every summoner, every circle, every medium.” He looked at the shelves. “And then it stops.”

“What stops?”

“The records.” He’d told them this. In the winter, at the long table; and the king had told them all again at his own table, in Valcrest. He said it anyway. “Every one. Thirteen people came. They’re all in here, coming. Some of them for years after. And then the records just stop. Not went home. Not died. Nothing. The page ends.” He swallowed. “Every one of them.”

Milli didn’t say anything. His tail didn’t move.

“At the back,” said Oswin.

The door was where it had always been.

The back wall of the vault, behind where the last shelf had stood until the winter, when Oswin had moved it to get at a box that had fallen behind. Bare stone. And set into it at about the height of a man’s chest, a small iron door. Square. About so big: Oswin held up his hands, the way he’d held them up at the dinner table, a little way apart. Black with age. No handle. No lock. No hinges you could see.

Just the seal.

It sat in the middle of the door, a disc of dark metal the size of a man’s palm, set into the iron so that you couldn’t see where one stopped and the other started. And on it, raised, worn soft by three hundred years of nobody touching it, a hand.

An open hand. Palm out. Fingers together. The way you’d hold your hand up to show somebody it was empty.

“Aldemar,” said Oswin. “That’s his device. The open hand. It’s on his coins, the few there are left. On his tomb, in the old crypt under the chapel.” He was whispering. He didn’t know why. “The chronicles call him the Open-Handed. They don’t say why.”

Aleaka had come up beside him. She was looking at the door. Not the seal. The door, all of it, with her slow careful eyes.

“It’s carrying something,” said Aleaka.

They all looked at her.

“Heavy.” She didn’t look away from the door. That was what Aleaka did; she felt what people carried. Oswin had seen her do it to the Advisor, in his study, in the first winter, and watched the Advisor’s face change. “Like a person. Like somebody holding their breath.” She was quiet. “It’s been holding it a very long time.”

The king came down the room between the shelves.

He had to stoop under the low ceiling, all the way, with the lantern held out in front of him. He came and stood in front of the small iron door, with the summoned behind him and Halvard at his elbow and Oswin pressed back against the end of the last shelf with his heart going like a bird’s, and looked at it.

He looked at it for a long time. The way he looked at iron in a fire, Oswin had seen him, in the forge at the king’s house in Valcrest. Not touching. Seeing what colour it was.

Then he put his hand on the seal.

Nothing happened.

Oswin hadn’t known what he’d expected. Light, maybe. A sound. The door swinging open on its hidden hinges, the way doors did in the old stories. He’d lain awake in the winter a hundred and sixty-one times and imagined it a hundred and sixty-one ways.

The king stood with his big scarred smith’s hand flat over the open hand on the seal, palm to palm, and nothing happened at all.

“Open,” said the king.

Nothing.

“I am Severin,” said the king, “King of Aurelan.” He said it the way he said it in council, which was flatly, as if it were the name of a road. “Open.”

Nothing.

The king took his hand off the seal and looked at it.

“No,” he said. “No, of course not.” He looked at his own palm. “It’s not for the crown. A crown’s a hat.” He looked at the seal again. “Halvard. What did you say? In the winter. When the boy asked you.”

“That one needs the king,” said Halvard, quietly, behind him. “And the king needs a reason.”

“A reason,” said the king.

He didn’t put his hand back on it at once. He stood there in front of the door, stooping under the low stone ceiling, with the lantern hanging from his other hand, and Oswin watched him think. He’d never seen the king think before. Not like this. The king decided things. He looked at something, and it was the colour it was, and he decided, and that was the end of it. Oswin had watched him decide the tournament on one page. He’d watched him decide to ride north to a fisherman’s cabin with a roll of hammers.

He wasn’t deciding now. He was looking for something.

“I could tell you I want to know,” said the king. To the door. Quite conversationally. “That’s true. I’m a smith. I want to know what’s inside everything. I’ve wanted to know what’s inside you since the boy came and told me there was a door.” He put the lantern down on the floor, carefully, beside his boot. “But that’s mine. That’s no reason. I don’t think you’d care about that.”

He put his hand on the seal again.

“Two years ago,” said the king, “thirty-five people came up out of the floor of my hall.”

The vault was very quiet. Oswin could hear the lamps.

“Nobody asked them. My Advisor didn’t ask them. He didn’t ask me, either, to be fair to him, and I’d have said no, and he knew it, and he did it anyway, because he thought the kingdom would die if he didn’t. And he might have been right.” The king didn’t look round at Halvard. “He thought he was pulling strangers out of the sky. They weren’t strangers. They’d come because a man we both know loved them, and they loved him, so much that the circle couldn’t tell where one of them stopped and the next one began, and it caught all of them at once in one net.” He was quiet. “The girl with the code said that. The anchor was tangled in someone who was tangled in everyone. I didn’t understand it. I understand it now.”

His hand was flat on the seal.

“They’ve got mothers,” said the king. “At home. Fathers. Brothers. Somebody setting a place at a table. Two months, it’s been, there. Two months of somebody looking at a door.” He breathed. “One of them’s a boy with a white snake who keeps everyone else going and never says what for. I’ve watched him at my table. I know that face. I had it at twenty, writing home from a wall.” He was quiet. “One of them’s in this room. He’s been saying it’s a plan since the day he came up out of my floor, because he needs it to be a plan, because the alternative is that nobody’s coming.”

Behind Oswin, very quietly, Hana made a sound. Milli didn’t.

“I’m sixty-five,” said the king. “I’m one tier off the top of my path, and I’ll never be what it’d take to send them back; nobody’s been that in four hundred years. Halvard’s three off his. The man they came for isn’t going anywhere, whatever happens. He’s got a lake. He’s got a girl.” He looked at the open hand under his own. “I’m not asking for me. I don’t want anything that’s in there. I’d be glad never to see it.”

He pressed his palm flat.

“I’m asking for them,” said the king. “Hanarisu. Milli. Aleaka. Jared. Cursiffe. And thirty more, all over my kingdom, tonight, writing to each other in a language nobody else can read. So they can choose. So it’s theirs to choose. That’s all.” His voice didn’t change. It was the flat council voice, the name-of-a-road voice. “They didn’t get to choose coming. They should get to choose going. And I can’t give them that, and I don’t know who can, and if there’s anything behind you that’d help, anything at all, I’d like it. Please.” He paused. “For them.”

The seal rang.

Once. Very quietly. Like a bell struck by somebody a long way off, in a tower Oswin couldn’t see. The sound went through the stone and through the floor and through Oswin’s feet, and the shelves all down the long room hummed with it, and the lamps flickered, every one at once.

And the open hand on the seal cracked.

Down the middle. A single fine line, from the tip of the middle finger to the heel of the palm, the way iron cracks when it’s quenched wrong: very straight, very clean, all at once. Oswin saw it happen. He’d remember it all his life. The hand on the seal, worn soft by three hundred and twelve years, splitting in two under the king’s broad scarred palm as if it had been waiting all that time to be asked properly.

The two halves of the disc fell out of the iron. Onto the stone floor. With two small sounds. Tink. Tink.

And the door was open.

It didn’t swing. It didn’t creak. It had simply stopped being shut. There was a dark square in the stone where there had been black iron, about so big, and cold air coming out of it, and a smell Oswin had never smelled in his life: old, and dry, and clean, like stone that had never once been wet.

Nobody moved.

“Oh,” said Aleaka. Very quietly.

Oswin looked at her. She had both hands pressed over her mouth, and her eyes were wet.

“It put it down,” said Aleaka, through her hands. “Whatever it was carrying. It put it down.”

The king stepped back.

He did it slowly, with his hand still held up a little in front of him, palm out, as if he’d forgotten it was there. He looked at it. Then at the dark square in the wall. Then round, for the first time, at Oswin, pressed against the end of the shelf.

“It’s yours,” said the king.

“Sire?”

“You found it. You moved the shelf. You’ve stood in front of it every night since the winter.” The king’s mouth did something in his beard. “Halvard told me. The guards on the archive door tell Halvard everything.” He stepped aside. “Go on, then. Archivist.”

Oswin went.

He didn’t remember crossing the floor. He was just there, in front of the dark square, with the lantern held up. And he looked in.

It was a cupboard. That was all. A space cut into the rock behind the iron, about an arm’s length deep, with a flat stone floor. And on the floor, in the middle, a box.

Grey. Not wood, not stone, not metal; something Oswin had no word for. Smooth and dull and faintly cool, like a river pebble, about the length of his forearm and the depth of his hand. No lid that he could see. No lock. No mark on it anywhere.

And on top of the box, folded once, a sheet of paper.

Old paper. Brown, gone soft at the edges. Folded once, and sealed once, with a blob of dark wax pressed with the same open hand.

Oswin looked at it for a long time with the lantern shaking in his hand.

“Take it out,” said the king, behind him. Quite gently. “Before you fall in.”

He took out the paper first.

He broke the wax with his thumbnail, because his hands were shaking too much for a knife, and unfolded it, and held it to the lantern, and looked. It was a king’s hand. Three dynasties old. Upright and plain and a little cramped, in ink gone brown, the letters older than any that were used now and still letters he knew, the way you know your great-grandmother’s handwriting on the back of a portrait. He could read it. He could read every hand in the kingdom. It was the only thing he’d ever been sure he was good at.

Behind him, Cursiffe had uncorked the blue ink.

“Read it out,” said Cursiffe. Quietly. “I’ll write.”

Oswin read it out.

To the king who opens this.

If the door has opened for you, you have asked for someone who is not yourself. I made the lock so. I could think of no other that a bad king would never find the key to, nor a frightened one.

These leaves lay under the first stone of the summoning hall, in this box, when my masons lifted the stone in the ninth year of my reign to mend the floor. They are in the hand that drew the circle. That hand is older than this kingdom and older than the one before it. No one alive can read it. I gave twenty years to the trying, and the best scholars in Aurelan, and they gave me their best guesses, which were bad.

My summoners wished them burned. They said that a thing which cannot be read is a thing which was not meant to be. I have never yet met a thing which could not be read that did not, in the end, meet someone who could. So I have kept them, and shut them where they will not be burned by a better summoner than mine.

In the eleventh year of my reign, the circle brought two. A man and a woman. They did not know each other, and they did not know us. They asked me every day for nine years to send them home. I asked these leaves every night for nine years how it might be done. Neither of us was answered.

If you can read them, do what I could not.

If you cannot, find the one who can, and let no one burn them while you look.

Aldemar. King, for what that was worth.

Nobody said anything.

Cursiffe’s pen stopped. Oswin heard it stop. He lowered the paper and stood there in front of the dark cupboard with the lantern and the letter, and the vault was so quiet he could hear the lamps again.

“Nine years,” said Milli.

It was the first thing he’d said since the door. Oswin turned round.

Milli was standing where he’d been standing, by the end of the shelves, very still, with Hana’s hand in his. His face was perfectly calm. It was always perfectly calm; it was the thing about Milli. His tail had gone so still it might have been carved.

“They asked him every day for nine years,” said Milli. “And he asked the leaves every night.” He was quiet. “And then the record stops.”

“Yes,” said Oswin.

“That’s what happened to them. To all of them. The thirteen.” Milli wasn’t asking. “Somebody asked. And nobody could answer. And then they—“ He stopped. “And then the page ends.”

Nobody said anything.

“That’s not going to happen to us,” said Milli. His voice didn’t change. “Is it.”

He wasn’t asking Oswin. He wasn’t asking anybody. He was looking at the king.

The king looked back at him.

“No,” said the king.

That was all. One word. The way the man with the rod said things. Not a promise, Oswin thought; the king didn’t make promises, he’d said so at his own table. Proof would’ve been a lie. Something else. Something heavier and plainer than a promise, like iron set down on an anvil.

Milli looked at him for a long moment.

Then he nodded. Once.

They opened the box on the vault floor.

It didn’t have a lid. Oswin had been right about that. But when the king lifted it out of the cupboard, very carefully, in both his big hands, and set it down on the stone in the lamplight, and they all knelt round it, Cursiffe put his fingertips on the top of it, curious, the way he touched everything, and the top of it simply wasn’t there any more. It had been. And then it was open, the way the door had been open: not moving, just not shut. And inside, the leaves.

Forty of them, perhaps. Oswin didn’t count. It was the only time in his life he didn’t count. Thin, and grey, and cool, like slate split so fine it bent. When he lifted the first one with the tips of his fingers, holding his breath, it moved in his hand like a page of good paper, and was as heavy as stone.

And on it, the hand.

He’d seen it before. He’d spent all last autumn on his knees in the summoning hall copying it, with his nose an inch from the floor. The hand that drew the oldest rings of the circle, the middle ones, the ones Thea had called legacy code and Cursiffe had written DO NOT TOUCH across in English. Not letters. Not pictures. Lines that went round. Rings, with lines in them, and lines that went out from the rings and came back, and small careful marks along the lines like the marks on a ruler. The same hand. He’d have known it anywhere. He’d have known it in the dark.

It filled the leaf from edge to edge.

He turned it over. The other side the same. He lifted the next one. The same. And the next.

“Can you read it?” said Hana.

“No,” said Oswin.

He’d known he wouldn’t be able to. Aldemar had said so. But he’d hoped, a little, for a hundred and sixty-one nights, that a king three hundred years dead had been wrong. He hadn’t been. Oswin looked at the leaf in the lamplight, at the rings and the lines and the little careful marks, and there was nothing in it for him at all. It wasn’t a hand he couldn’t read. It was a hand that wasn’t writing, the way music isn’t writing, the way a map isn’t.

He passed it to Cursiffe.

Cursiffe took it. He looked at it. And Oswin watched his eyes go to the lines and slide off them, the way the summoned’s eyes slid off any hand at all, the way Oswin’s own slid off their English. Cursiffe frowned. He tried again. They slid.

“No,” said Cursiffe. He sounded almost relieved. “It’s handwriting. I can’t hold it. None of us can.” He turned it a little in the light. “But it’s the circle, isn’t it. It’s the same as the middle of the circle. The bit Thea Parsed.”

“Yes.”

“Thea Parsed your copy,” said Cursiffe. Slowly. “Not the circle. She couldn’t read the floor any more than I can. She read your drawing of it. You drew it, on oiled paper, and she looked at your lines, and her window read them.” He looked up at Oswin. “Your hand. Not the circle’s.”

Oswin stared at him.

“If you copied these,” said Cursiffe. “The way you copied the circle. Every line. Exactly. Onto paper.” He looked down at the box. At forty grey leaves, edge to edge, rings and lines and marks. “She might be able to Parse those too.”

“It’ll take a year,” said Oswin.

He’d done the sum before Cursiffe finished speaking. He couldn’t help it. The circle had taken him an autumn, and it was one drawing, ten feet across, and he’d made a mistake in the fourteenth ring and had to do it again. These were forty leaves, both sides, edge to edge, in a hand finer than the circle’s, and every line would have to be exact or the girl with the code would be reading his mistakes instead of the leaves.

“More,” he said. “A year. More than a year.” He looked at the box. “Every line.”

“Then take a year,” said the king.

Oswin looked up at him.

The king was sitting on the floor of the vault. Oswin hadn’t seen him sit. He was sitting on the stone with his back against the end of the last shelf and his knees up and his dusty riding boots on the floor, the way he must have sat on forge floors when he was young, between heats. He looked tired. He looked sixty-five. He was looking at the box.

“Take a year,” said the king. “Take two. Take whatever it takes to get every line right. You’ll have whatever you need: lamps, paper, ink, quiet. Nobody comes down those stairs but you and the people you ask.” He looked at Oswin. “I said I’d spend the kingdom on it. I meant it. This is the first coin.”

Oswin didn’t trust himself to say anything.

“And then,” said the king, “send it to the girl with the code. Wherever she is. And we’ll see what it says.”

Jared had been very quiet.

He was always quiet, with anyone outside the five. He talked to his own, low and cool and quick, and laughed with them; with everyone else he mostly stood half a pace in front of Aleaka and a little to the left and let the others talk. He’d come down the stair like that, and stood like that the whole time, between her and the door, and hadn’t said a word. One of the few words Oswin had ever heard of him saying to anyone outside the five was Trust, to the man with the rod, at a fence by the Lists in the mist, and Oswin had been told about it and never stopped wishing he’d been there.

He stepped forward now.

He knelt down by the box, beside Milli, and looked at the leaves. Then at Milli, beside him, very calm, with his tail like carved wood.

He put his hand on Milli’s shoulder. Just for a moment.

“Trust,” said Jared.

Milli’s tail moved.

Just once. Just the tip. The first time since the door. A small, slow, curling movement at the very end of it, the way a cat’s tail moves when it’s decided something is going to be all right and isn’t going to discuss it.

Hana, beside him, put her face in his shoulder and laughed, a little wetly, and Milli put his hand up behind him without looking and held the back of her head.

They went up at midnight.

Oswin carried the box. The king offered, and Oswin said no, sire, before he could stop himself, and the king looked at him and laughed, the short deep sound like a hammer landing once, and let him. He carried it up the ninety-one steps sideways in both arms, with Cursiffe ahead of him with the lantern, counting for him out loud because he couldn’t count and carry at once. Forty grey leaves in a box that wasn’t there until you touched it, and a letter from a king three hundred years dead, in Oswin’s coat, against his chest, beside the black key.

The king came up behind him, sideways, scraping, breathing through his nose, and said nothing all the way.

Halvard came last.

Oswin noticed that at the top. He’d got to the bottom of the old cellars, where Kestra was standing against the wall with her book under her arm exactly where they’d left her, and turned round to make sure everyone was up, and counted. The king. Hana and Milli. Aleaka and Jared. Cursiffe. And a long way below, very slowly, one step at a time, the Advisor.

He came up into the light of Kestra’s lamp, thin and grey, with his riding gloves still on. He’d been at the back of the vault the whole time. He hadn’t said a word since that one needs the king. He stood at the top of the stair and looked at the box in Oswin’s arms. And then at the king.

“Severin,” he said.

Oswin had never heard him use the king’s name. Not once. In two years.

The king turned round.

“I have a drawer,” said Halvard.

The king looked at him for a long time.

Oswin didn’t know what it meant. He knew there was a drawer; everybody who’d ever been in the Advisor’s study knew there was a drawer, the bottom one on the left, that stuck in wet weather, and that the Advisor looked at it sometimes when he thought nobody was watching. He didn’t know what was in it. He’d never asked. He’d learned his lesson about asking.

“In Valcrest,” said the king, slowly, “I told you it’d keep.”

“Yes.”

“It won’t, will it,” said the king. “Not any more.”

“No,” said Halvard.

The king didn’t say anything else. He put his hand on Halvard’s shoulder, the big scarred smith’s hand that had just been flat on a seal three hundred years old, and left it there a moment, heavily, the way you’d put your hand on a piece of iron to feel if it had cooled.

Then he took it away. And Halvard went up the cellar stairs ahead of all of them, quite fast for a man of his age, and was gone.

Oswin didn’t go to bed.

He took the box to the archive, to the long table by the window where he’d done all his reading for two years, and set it down very carefully in the middle, and sat in front of it. He didn’t open it. He just sat there, with Aldemar’s letter in his coat and the key on its string, the way he’d stood in front of the door a hundred and sixty-one times. Cursiffe sat with him for an hour, and then went to bed, and touched him on the shoulder on the way out and said well done, Oswin, which nobody had ever said to him before in his life.

He sat there until the sky went grey.

And at some point in the night, looking up from the box, through the archive window, across the dark courtyard, he saw a light come on at the top of the west tower. In the Advisor’s study. A single lamp, very small and steady, in the high narrow window.

It stayed on all night.

Oswin didn’t know what it meant. He thought, sitting there, that he might be the only person in the castle who saw it, apart from the sentries, who wouldn’t think anything of it, because the Advisor’s lamp was often on late.

But it was still on at dawn, when he heard the gate open below, and a horse go out of it at a gallop, one rider, alone, east.
