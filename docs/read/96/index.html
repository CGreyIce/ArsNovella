The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 4: Threads

Chapter 96

Rehearsal

3,753 words · 16 min read

The last thing he ever said to Newish was love you guys, except Newish.

She’d said rude. That was the whole conversation. It was nearly two in the morning in Malacca and she’d been playing Counter-Strike for about three days in her entire life, and they’d lost thirteen to nine, partly because of her, and Ice had blamed the server for a shot he’d missed and promised to be on early tomorrow, trust, and Yan had said he’d never been early in his life. And then he’d said goodnight to everyone, one at a time, the way he did. Love you guys. Except Newish.

Rude, she’d said.

And he’d laughed, and logged off, and not woken up.

She’d told the table at the Broken Lance about it in the summer, the night after she shouted Ina, down! across the Lists and a blade rang off Hako’s spear instead of going into Ina’s back. She’d said: the last thing I said to him was rude. And then: today I heard someone about to die, and I said down, and they didn’t. And everybody had gone quiet, and Chris had put his hand on her shoulder.

She’d decided, that night, what she was going to say when she saw him.

She’d been rehearsing it ever since.

“Rude,” said Newish, to the mirror.

It was a bad mirror. A square of polished tin on the wall of the room she shared with Winter above the Broken Lance, warped in one corner so that the left side of her face looked like it was melting. She looked at herself in it, in the grey rain-light, with her hair up and her arms folded, and said it again.

“Rude.”

No. Too angry. It wasn’t supposed to be angry. It was supposed to be funny. That was the whole point. He’d said except Newish to be funny and she’d said rude to be funny and then he’d died and it had stopped being funny for three years, and she wanted to make it funny again. That was all. She wanted to look at him, whatever he looked like now, wherever he was, and say rude, and have it be the joke it was supposed to be.

And then hit him. On the arm. Not hard.

She’d said that too, in the channel, the night of the sum: I’m going to say rude, and then I’m going to hit him.

“Rude,” she said to the mirror. Lighter. With her eyebrows up.

Better.

Behind her, from the bed, without opening her eyes, Winter said: “You’ve been doing that for three months.”

“I’m rehearsing.”

“For what?”

“For when I see him,” said Newish. “I need to get it right. I’ve only got one go.” She looked at the melting side of her face in the tin. “You can’t say rude twice. The second time it’s just rude.”

Winter opened one eye.

“You’re insane,” said Winter.

“I’m prepared,” said Newish.

Valcrest didn’t get snow. It got rain.

All winter. Grey and warm and endless, coming in off the southern sea in long soft curtains, so that the streets ran with water and the Lists, the great empty oval of the arena, filled up at the bottom like a bowl and had to be drained every morning before Ysolde could drill them on the sand. The flower of ice was still in the trough by the east gate. It hadn’t melted. Winter said it wouldn’t until she let it. It stood there in the rain, perfect, every petal edged with rime, with the water running off it and not touching it, and people came from all over the city to look at it.

Lucan had started rehearsing.

Not them. Himself. He’d go down to the Lists in the afternoons, when the rain eased, with his red cloak and his lantern and a little folding stool, and set the stool in the very middle of the sand, and sit on it, and look up at the empty tiers going up and up on every side. And then he’d stand up and spread his arms and say things. Out loud. To nobody. To twenty thousand people who weren’t there.

Newish had gone down to watch, the first time, because she’d heard his beat change from across the city. A dance tune all year, a fiddle at a wedding. A march, since the king’s letter. She’d followed the march down to the Lists in the rain and found him in the middle of the empty sand with his arms out, addressing the north stands.

“People of Valcrest,” Lucan had been saying, to the empty stone. “Two summers ago, thirty-five strangers fell out of the sky.” He’d stopped. “No. Fell out of the sky is wrong. They didn’t fall. Were brought.” He’d tried it again. “Two summers ago, thirty-five strangers were brought—”

He’d seen her, then, on the steps of the east gate, and gone red to his beard.

“I’m rehearsing,” he’d said.

“Me too,” said Newish.

Lucan’s plans had got bigger.

That was the thing about Lucan. Every time he had a plan, it got bigger. It had started, in the autumn, the night the king’s letter came, as twenty thousand paper horns. By the end of the first month of winter it was every paper-maker in Valcrest and half of the ones in the next three towns, working from dawn to dark in a long rented warehouse by the river, cutting and folding and painting. Horns, still, ten thousand of them, for Ina and Winter. Little white paper masks for Ina. But more. So many more.

Chris’s list had done that.

He’d written it at the long table at the Broken Lance the night the king’s letter came, while Lucan watched. Thirty-five names, in his small square hand. And beside each one, the thing a crowd would want to wear. Lucan couldn’t read it, because it was in English. So Chris had read it out to him, one line at a time, and Lucan had written down the native version in his own big looping hand, and then sat back and looked at it and said oh, very quietly, and gone away and cried in the yard.

Paper antlers with leaves on, for Maigo. Small white paper snakes, for Yan and Kei. Cat ears and little crowns for the capital, for Hana and Milli and Aleaka. Tiny yellow penguins, for PenguChi. Purple paper squids, for Xola, which had taken the paper-makers a week to work out how to fold. Paper bows. Paper moths. Paper chalk dogs, for Bee, after the channel told them about Bee’s dog. Paper bees. Paper hammers. Paper kettles, for Group 4, because Calvin had written in the Ice book about a kettle and Lucan had decided, without asking anyone, that the kettle was important.

It went on. Thirty-five lines. Every one of them something.

And at the end of Chris’s page, in Chris’s notebook, there was a thirty-sixth line.

Empty.

Newish had noticed it in the autumn.

She noticed most things, if they had a beat. And Chris’s beat changed when he looked at that line. It went from his normal one, a steady, careful, slightly-too-fast click, like somebody typing, to something else. Something that held. A held note. The way a beat holds at the end of a song, before the last chord, when everybody knows it’s coming and nobody’s played it yet.

It wasn’t just Chris.

It was Ina, too. And Winter. All three of them. Whenever anyone said north, or lake, or a name that started with W. She’d hear it from across the room. Three beats, three different people, a cello and a click and water dripping in a cave, all at once, holding. Like three people holding their breath at exactly the same time.

She’d asked Winter, once, in the dark, from her bed.

“What is it?”

Winter had been quiet for a long time.

“What’s what?”

“When somebody says north. You and Chris and Ina. Your beats stop. All three. At once.” Newish had turned over to look at the dark shape of Winter’s bed across the room. “You know something.”

Winter didn’t say anything for a very long time.

“We think something,” she said at last. “We don’t know it.”

“Can I think it too?”

“No,” said Winter. Not unkindly. “It’s not ours to give you. It’s not even ours.”

And Newish, who’d played Apex with all of them until four in the morning, and trusted them with her life on the sand, had lain in the dark and thought about it, and decided that was fine.

She’d hear it, when it was time. She always heard things.

The cup fell on a Thursday.

It was the long table at the Broken Lance, at supper, in the rain. All five of them, and Ysolde at the end, and Lucan at the other end with a pile of paper samples in front of him, holding up a purple paper squid to the lamp. Hako was reaching across the table for the bread. He was always reaching across the table for the bread. He was tall, and had a spear arm, and thought nothing of reaching over three people to get to the loaf.

His elbow caught the cup.

Ina’s cup. A tall clay one, full of hot spiced wine, the kind the Broken Lance made in the winter and served too hot. Newish saw it go. It tipped, and teetered, and went over the edge of the table, falling toward Ina’s lap and Ina’s bare hands, full of wine hot enough to scald.

And she heard it.

She’d heard beats all her life. Here, she heard everything’s. People’s, mostly. Lucan’s march. Ysolde’s slow sure drum. The thing at the Lists at midsummer with the slow bell-like beat in its chest, which she’d heard before anyone saw it move, and shouted INA, DOWN. But other things too, sometimes, if she listened. Rain had a beat. The river did. A horse, a cart, a crowd.

The cup had a beat.

Falling things did. She’d never noticed before. A fast one, too fast, like a song played at double speed by somebody who didn’t know it. The cup going over the edge of the table, and the wine going over the edge of the cup, and everything going down toward Ina’s hands much too fast, tak-tak-tak-tak, like a drum roll.

It was wrong. It was the wrong tempo. She heard it the way you hear a bandmate rushing, and every part of her that had ever played a game with someone who was panicking wanted to say the same thing.

She found the edge. She didn’t look for it. It was just there, the way it was always there when you didn’t look for it. At the bottom of the beat.

She didn’t write stop. You couldn’t stop a song. That was what she’d learned, a year and a half of hearing beats; you couldn’t make anything stop. You could only change how fast it went.

She wrote the thing she’d have said to a panicking friend on a call, at three in the morning, in a game they were losing.

Easy.

The cup slowed down.

Everybody saw it. That was what made it real, afterwards, in the way things hadn’t been real for her before: everybody at the table saw it at once. A tall clay cup, falling off the edge of the table, full of hot wine, and halfway down it simply slowed. As if the air had gone thick. As if somebody had turned the tempo of the whole world down, just round that one cup, from a drum roll to a slow walk. It drifted down through the air like a leaf. The wine that had spilled out over the lip of it hung there, a long red ribbon, curling, slowly, catching the lamplight.

Hako, who’d knocked it, and who had the fastest hands at the table, looked at it with his mouth open, and then reached out and caught it.

The cup. Out of the air. At the speed of a leaf. And then, because the wine was still hanging there in its slow red ribbon, he moved the cup under it, very carefully, and caught that too.

The tempo came back.

Everything was normal again. The rain on the window. The fire. Lucan with a purple paper squid held up to the lamp, frozen. And Hako, half out of his chair, holding a full cup of hot spiced wine that hadn’t spilled a drop.

Nobody said anything.

Newish opened her window.

[ Newish · Tier 1: Spark · Skills: Tempo ]

The seventeenth.

“Tempo,” said Lucan, very faintly, from the end of the table. He couldn’t see the window. He didn’t need to. He’d said the word himself, a year and a half ago, the first week, watching her in the Lists: tempo… a support path. “I said that. I said it the first week. I said tempo.”

“You did,” said Newish.

“I’m going to cry,” said Lucan.

“You’re always going to cry,” said Ysolde.

Lucan cried.

Chris had his notebook out before anyone else had moved. He wrote it down, 17. Newish. Spark. Tempo, in the long column he’d been keeping for a year and a half. And then he looked at her across the table, with his pen still on the page, and his beat did something it had never done before. It sped up. Just slightly. The way a heart speeds up when somebody you care about does something good.

“Seventeen,” said Chris. “That’s nearly half.”

“Seventeen out of thirty-five,” said Newish. “That’s not half. That’s nearly half. Don’t round up. You hate rounding up.”

“I’m rounding up today,” said Chris.

Ina had taken the cup from Hako. He was holding it in both hands, looking at it, with his mask pushed up on his head and his face doing something complicated.

“That was going in my lap,” he said.

“I know,” said Newish.

“That was very hot.”

“I know.”

Ina looked at her for a long moment. Then he put the cup down on the table, very carefully, well away from the edge, and reached across Hako, the way Hako always reached across everyone, and held out his fist.

Newish bumped it.

They posted it in the channel that night. Spark. Tempo. Seventeenth. I slowed down a cup of hot wine so it wouldn’t land on Ina. He’s welcome.

She scrolled back through it afterwards, because she could now, two hundred lines, with the times on every one. Maigo in capitals. Bee: a cup is a good first one. mine was a dog. Yan, from Cinderhold: I felt it go up. Congratulations. Hana, from the capital: Newish! I’m making you a pair, for you and Winter, you’ll love it. Gigichi, from Wrenhaven: finally someone useful. Thea: Seventeen. Rule three. Good news, loudly.

Higu didn’t write anything. Higu never wrote much now, in the evenings. Newish had noticed that too, in the last month. All five of them in Wrenhaven wrote less in the evenings than they used to. As if they were somewhere else, in the evenings, and only remembered the channel in the mornings.

She didn’t know what that meant. She didn’t think about it much. She just noticed it, the way she noticed beats.

The thing about the fishing rods happened the next day.

She’d gone down to the Lists with Chris, in the afternoon, when the rain stopped, to see Lucan. He’d sent a boy round to the Broken Lance in the morning, very excited, to say that the paper-makers had finished the first thousand of something and he wanted them to see. They found him in the middle of the drained sand, on his folding stool, surrounded by crates.

Crates of paper. Horns, in one. Little white masks in another. Purple squids. Penguins. Antlers with leaves on. He’d opened every crate and spread a few of each on the sand round him in a circle, and was sitting in the middle of it with his arms folded and his face shining.

“Thirty-five,” he said, as they came up. “Every one. Every single one of you. Twenty thousand people, and every one of them wearing something for one of you.” He looked round at the circle of paper on the wet sand. “Thirty-five.” He frowned. “Chris. Your list. You said thirty-five. But there was another line at the bottom, wasn’t there? An empty one. I saw it. I didn’t ask.”

Chris went very still.

Newish heard his beat. It stopped. The held note.

“What was that one for?” said Lucan.

Chris didn’t answer.

And Newish, who’d been rehearsing rude in a tin mirror for three months, and had decided a long time ago that she’d hear it when it was time, looked at Chris’s face, and at the empty line she could see from here in the open notebook in his hand, and at the circle of paper things on the sand, thirty-five of them, a crowd’s worth of love for thirty-five people. And she thought about a report in the channel, in the autumn, from Group 3. The Hero came. Across the red, on his own, with a fishing rod.

She didn’t know what Chris and Winter and Ina knew. She didn’t need to.

“Fishing rods,” said Newish.

Lucan looked at her.

Chris looked at her. His beat didn’t come back. It stayed on the held note, waiting.

“Paper fishing rods,” said Newish. She said it lightly, the way she said everything, the way she’d say rude. “For the Hero. Everybody comes to the Lists at midsummer, don’t they? Everybody in the kingdom. That’s what you said.” She shrugged. “If he comes, the crowd’ll want something to wave. And if he doesn’t come, it’s still a nice thing to have. Paper fishing rods. For the Hero of the Redmarch.” She looked at Lucan. “Group 3 said he carries one.”

Lucan stared at her.

“The Hero hasn’t been seen in eight years,” he said slowly. “Except once, on the Redmarch, this autumn. He doesn’t go anywhere. He doesn’t come to tournaments. He’s never once come to the Lists. Not even after the war, when the king held a victory tournament in his name and saved him the royal box.” He looked at the circle of paper on the sand. “He won’t come.”

“Probably not,” said Newish.

“Then why—“

“Because it’s a nice thing to have,” said Newish. “Just in case.”

Lucan looked at her for a long time.

Then he looked at Chris. And Newish watched him see Chris’s face. He couldn’t hear beats; he didn’t need to. He’d spent fifteen years reading crowds. He looked at Chris’s face, and then at the notebook in Chris’s hand, open to a page with an empty line at the bottom, and something in his own face went very quiet.

He didn’t ask. Newish saw him not ask. She’d seen a lot of people not ask things in a year and a half.

“How many?” said Lucan. Very quietly.

“How many what?”

“Fishing rods.” He looked at the circle of paper. “For the Hero. Just in case.” He swallowed. “How many?”

Newish thought about it.

“All of them,” she said. “Everyone who doesn’t already have something. Everyone in the crowd who doesn’t know which of us to cheer for.” She looked at the empty middle of the circle on the sand, where nothing was lying. “Give them a fishing rod. Then they can cheer for him.”

Lucan stood up off his stool.

“Ten thousand,” he said. To the empty north stands. His voice had gone up into the march, the one Newish had been hearing since the king’s letter. “Ten thousand paper fishing rods. Just in case.” He turned round. “I’ll go to the paper-makers tonight. They’ll think I’m mad. I don’t care.” He was already crying again. “I don’t care at all.”

Chris didn’t say anything all the way back to the Broken Lance.

They walked through the wet streets in the dusk, the two of them, with the rain starting again, soft. Newish didn’t say anything either. She just walked beside him and listened to his beat, which was still on the held note, and waited.

At the door of the inn he stopped. He took out the notebook. He opened it to the page. Thirty-five lines, in his small square hand, and Lucan’s native writing beside them. And at the bottom, the thirty-sixth. Empty.

He took out his pen.

He didn’t write a name. Newish watched him not write a name. He wrote, very small, in the space where the thing a crowd would want to wear went, at the end of the empty line:

paper fishing rods

Then he closed the notebook.

His beat started again. Normal. The steady, careful, slightly-too-fast click, like somebody typing. Except that under it, now, very faintly, Newish could hear something else. Something she hadn’t heard from Chris in a year and a half.

A tune. Under the beat. The kind you hum without knowing.

“Thank you,” said Chris. Not looking at her.

“For what?”

He didn’t answer. He went in.

That night she stood in front of the tin mirror for a long time.

“Rude,” she said. Lightly. With her eyebrows up.

Then she tried it a different way. Not to the mirror. To the window. North. Past the rain, past the city, past the whole of the kingdom, toward a lake she’d never seen and a man she’d never met in person, who’d said except Newish and laughed and not woken up.

“Rude,” she said, very quietly.

It came out wrong. It came out wet.

She stood there with her hand on the windowsill and let it.

“You’re still rehearsing,” said Winter, from the bed, without opening her eyes.

“I’ve only got one go.”

Winter was quiet for a moment. Newish heard her beat. Water dripping in a cave. Slow. Holding, very slightly, the way it held whenever anybody said north.

“You’ll get it right,” said Winter.

Newish looked out at the rain.

“How do you know?”

“Because he’ll laugh,” said Winter. “Whatever you say. He always did. You could have said anything.” She turned over. “Go to sleep.”

Newish went to sleep. She dreamed about a cup falling very slowly through the air, and a man catching it, and laughing.

She thought, when she woke up, that she knew exactly how she was going to say it.
