The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 5: The Lists

Chapter 138

Pale Light

5,717 words · 25 min read

Yan bought the hen from a woman in the poultry market at the bottom of the Street of Coopers, the way he’d bought a hen in every town between Cinderhold and Valcrest. He paid her two coppers more than she asked, the way he always did. She looked at the snake round his neck and didn’t ask what it was for.

People never asked. He’d noticed that, on the road. They looked at the white snake and the silver-haired man with the pointed ears and they took the extra coppers and handed over the hen and didn’t ask. As if they knew, somewhere, that it was a thing you didn’t want to know the answer to.

He took it round the back of the market, to the yard where the poulterer’s boy wrung necks for the cookshops. He always did it this way. He didn’t do it himself. He’d tried once, in the first month at Cinderhold, and found he couldn’t, and Aldous had found him sitting in the snow behind the barracks afterwards with a hen in his lap that was still alive and Suzu cold as ice round his neck, and had taken the hen off him very gently and done it himself, and said nothing about it ever again.

The poulterer’s boy did it quickly. He was good at it. Yan gave him a copper.

And then, in the moment after, Yan watched the light come out.

It was very small. That was the thing nobody would believe, if he told them. A hen’s soul was tiny. A little pale point of light, no bigger than a candle flame seen from across a room, lifting out of the bird the moment it stopped, the way warm breath lifts on a cold morning. Pale. Without any colour he could name. It hung there for a moment in the air of the poulterer’s yard, wavering, the way a flame wavers when a door opens, as if it were looking for something.

Suzu took it.

She didn’t move much. She never did. She lifted her head off his collarbone, very slightly, and her tongue went out, and the little pale light went to her the way water goes downhill. And was gone.

And down the cord, warmth.

He’d felt it every time since the first week. The cord: the thing between them, from his chest to wherever she was, which he’d felt the first hour in the hall and had never once since been without. When she was hungry it went cold. When she was fed it went warm, and the warmth came down it into him, and for a while afterwards he felt stronger, and sharper, and very slightly sick.

He hated it. He’d always hated it. He’d never once let her go a whole day unfed.

“She says thank you,” said Kei, beside him. Quietly. “For the hen.”

Yan didn’t look round.

Kei had come with him. He hadn’t asked to. He’d just been there at the bottom of the Street of Coopers when Yan came out of the Broken Lance, leaning on a wall with his hands in his haori sleeves and Hisui looped round his neck, the way somebody waits for a friend they know is about to do something they don’t like doing. And he’d walked down to the market with him, and not said anything, and stood in the poulterer’s yard while the boy did the thing.

“She says,” said Kei, and listened, “you don’t have to look away.”

“I always look away.”

“She knows,” said Kei. “She says she’s always known. She says you’ve looked away every time for two years and she doesn’t mind, but you don’t have to.” He listened again. “She says she’d rather you watched. She says it’s not a thing to be ashamed of. It’s just eating.”

Yan stood in the poulterer’s yard with the cord warm in his chest and the white snake round his neck and didn’t say anything for a while.

I’m going to stop guessing, he’d said, three nights ago, on a roof. He’d meant it. It was harder than he’d thought. Two years of translating for her, and four years before that on stream, and every time he heard her through Hisui now it was like finding out a song he’d been singing all his life had different words.

“Okay,” said Yan, eventually.

He made himself turn round and look at the poulterer’s boy, carrying the hen away to be plucked for somebody’s supper. A hen. Fed to a snake. That was all.

Suzu put her head back down on his collarbone, warm.

The Singles semifinals were at dusk, in the Lists, by lamplight.

Lucan had insisted. It was, he said, the old way. For three hundred years the semifinals of the midsummer singles had been fought at dusk on the second-to-last night, under torches, with the stands half full of people who’d come back after supper because they couldn’t stay away. It’s the best night of the Lists, Lucan had said, weeping. Everyone’s fed. Everyone’s tired. Everyone’s in love with someone on the sand. Drew had looked at him for a long time and then said okay.

So the great bowl was lit, at dusk, with torches on poles along the rail every ten paces, and lamps on the master’s platform and the royal box, and the paper fishing rods along every rail catching the light. The sand was raked and gold. The stands were half full and filling, people coming in with their suppers still in their hands, families, couples, old men, children asleep on shoulders.

Two bouts. Xav and PenguChi. Salty and Milli.

Yan sat on the sixth tier of the east stand with Group 3, or most of it. Salty was down in the tunnel. Maigo was beside him, in his hoodie with the hood down because his antlers were too full of leaves now to fit, talking to the man in front about trees. Niko on his other side, chill, hands in pockets. Wendy beyond Niko, with her headphones round her neck, eating a pie very slowly because it was one of Salty’s eleven and Salty had made her promise to eat one so they’d go further.

Kei was behind him, with Hisui. Hyvred beside Kei, with her glasses up in her hair and a cup of something hot.

And on the fourth tier, two tiers below and a little to the left, at the end of a bench, a man with a rod across his knees and two small bees on his head.

Yan didn’t look at him. He didn’t need to. He could feel him from anywhere now.

He reached out.

He’d been doing it since he went up a tier, in the winter, at the grave. Kin. That was the skill’s name; his window had written it in the snow on the Redmarch, the moment he reached for the Earth grain and felt all thirty-five of them across the kingdom, and a thirty-sixth. He could reach out from wherever he was and feel the candles. Every living thing had one. A small warm flame of soul, burning somewhere behind the face, leaning toward whatever it loved.

In a crowd, it was like standing in a field of candles at night.

He let himself feel it. The Lists at dusk, half full, filling. Thousands of candles. Small and warm and wavering, all through the stands, like the speckle of stars when you look long enough. Families, close together, their candles leaning in toward each other the way they always did, like plants on a windowsill. A couple in the row in front, leaning together. A child asleep on a shoulder, a tiny bright flame tucked against a bigger one. An old man on his own, burning low and steady.

And the thirty-five. He could pick them out in a crowd now, the way you pick out a voice you know. They had Earth’s grain. A different grain from this world’s: the way a piece of wood from a different tree has a different grain, even when it’s been cut and planed to look the same. Tiger’s on the eighth tier, quick and hot. Chris’s, in the stand behind the master’s platform, newly bright. Rachael’s, on the fifth tier of the east stand, among Group 4.

Rachael’s was very bright.

It always had been. He’d noticed it the first day in the hall, without knowing what he was noticing. Healers burned brighter. Something about the mana; it was purer, Aldous said. A healer’s candle in a crowd was like a lamp in a window.

That was why they wanted her.

He knew the story now. Everybody did. Three hunters, in Kingsferry, in a year. A man in a hat who’d dropped off a cliff for her in a gorge on the way upriver and been cut by her with Gabe’s knife and gone grey and stopped moving like water. And before any of that, nearly nine years ago, on a rise an hour’s walk from Cinderhold, inside a ring of fires: a healer with cold hands.

Yan let his attention move through the stands.

And found the first gap.

It was on the third tier of the north stand. A woman in a brown shawl, sitting between a family with a picnic and a man asleep with his hat over his face. Yan’s attention went to her, the way it went to everyone, a candle at a time.

There was no candle.

He stopped. He made himself go back and look again, the way you go back over a sum. The family beside her: candles, all four, leaning in toward the youngest. The man with the hat: a candle, low and dozy. Between them: a woman in a brown shawl, eating a pear, looking at the sand.

Nothing.

Not cold. That was the first thing he understood. He’d felt cold souls. The turned had cold souls, the thing that wore a person after a turning, the thing under Diwa’s grave that he’d kept a thread of since his first week on the Redmarch. Cold was something. Cold was a flame burning the wrong way. This wasn’t cold.

This was nothing at all. A gap in the field of candles the shape of a woman eating a pear.

“Kei,” said Yan. Very quietly.

“I know,” said Kei, behind him. “Hisui says. He says it tastes like nothing. He says the air round her tastes like the inside of a shell with the snail gone.”

And Suzu, round Yan’s neck, went cold.

Not hungry-cold. He knew hungry-cold. This was the other kind. The kind she’d gone on the red when the scouts came, the kind she’d gone at the grave. A coiling, tightening cold, all down the cord, the way a snake goes still in the grass when a hawk’s shadow crosses it.

“She says,” said Kei, and stopped. “She says there’s more.”

There were more.

Yan found them one at a time, the way you find the stars of a constellation once you’ve seen the first one. Moving his attention through the stands of the Lists in the torchlight, candle by candle, and every so often finding a gap. A man in a green coat on the seventh tier of the south stand. A boy in a cap, on his own, high on the west side. An old woman knitting by the master’s platform. Two men together, eating nuts, near the royal box. A woman in an apron, near the gate.

Forty.

He counted them twice. Forty gaps in the field of candles. Spread through the stands of the Lists, all the way round the bowl, the way you’d spread markers round a field. Not together. Never together. Each one alone in a crowd, between people who had candles, eating something or knitting something or sleeping, looking at the sand.

He felt sick.

“Hyvred,” he said.

Hyvred had put her cup down. She was already looking. Her left eye had gone sharp and still, the gold of it, the little red mark in it like a crosshair locked on something. The woman in the brown shawl on the third tier of the north stand.

“Oh,” said Hyvred, very quietly. “Oh, no. Oh, no, no.”

“You can see them.”

“I can see through them.” Her voice had gone very thin. “The faces. They’re not—they’re wearing them. Like a coat. The light’s wrong. It’s—“ She swallowed. “It’s like the man at the well in Coldwater. In the first month. Someone wearing someone.” She took a breath. “Except there isn’t a someone underneath. There’s just the coat. And something holding it up.”

“How many can you see?”

Hyvred turned her head slowly, all the way round the bowl, the crosshair locking and letting go. Locking and letting go.

“Forty,” she said. “I think. I can’t—there might be more on the far side. Forty.” She was very pale. “Yan. They’ve been here all week. I saw one on the first day. The opening. By the north gate. I thought it was one. I thought it was the apple woman from the downs. I told Deston. I thought it was one.” Her hands had closed into fists in her lap. “It’s forty. It’s been forty all week.”

They were counting.

Yan saw it a moment after Hyvred did. He wasn’t looking at them; he couldn’t look at them, not properly, not with his eyes. He was feeling for them. And when he held his attention on one of the gaps for long enough, the woman in the brown shawl, he felt it do something.

It turned.

Not its head. Its attention. The way a candle leans. Except it had no candle, and it leaned anyway, the way a coat on a hook leans when someone walks past it in a draught. It leaned toward the eighth tier. Toward Tiger.

Then it leaned away. And toward the stand behind the master’s platform. Chris.

Then the fifth tier. Group 4. Gabe. Deston. Dennis. Calvin. Rachael.

It stayed on Rachael a long time.

Then it moved on. Group 1, on the fifth tier of the south stand. Group 2, near the royal box. Group 7. Group 6. Group 5. Group 3. Yan felt it come to the sixth tier of the east stand and lean toward him. Toward Maigo, beside him, talking about trees. Toward Niko. Toward Wendy, eating Salty’s pie.

Toward him.

He sat very still and let it lean.

It didn’t feel like being looked at. It felt like being touched by something that had no hands. Like walking through a cobweb in the dark. It leaned toward him, and stayed, and he felt Suzu go colder round his neck, coiling, tightening, a soft hiss in her that he felt in his own chest more than heard.

Then it moved on.

To the fourth tier. Two tiers below him. A little to the left. The end of a bench.

It leaned. And stopped.

And stayed.

Yan counted with it.

He couldn’t help it. He was a counter. He’d counted thirty-six from a grave in the snow. He sat on the sixth tier of the east stand in the torchlight and followed the gap’s attention as it went round the bowl again, and again, slowly, and counted.

Tiger. Chris. Group 4, five. Group 1, five. Group 2, five. Group 7, four more. Group 6, five. Group 5, four more. Group 3, three here, and one in the tunnel, and one.

Thirty-five.

And the fourth tier.

Thirty-six.

It counted to thirty-six, and stopped on thirty-six, and stayed on thirty-six for longer than on any of the rest. And then went back to the beginning and started again. Tiger. Chris. Group 4.

All forty of them were doing it. He could feel them now that he knew what he was feeling for. Forty gaps in the field of candles round the bowl of the Lists, each one alone in a crowd, each one leaning its attention round the stands in turn, counting. Thirty-five. Thirty-six. And then the beginning again.

They’d been doing it all week.

If they’re together they’re one target. That was what Halvard had been afraid of, for two years. That was why there’d been an order, all that time: no gathering. And then a single word on cream paper, and a reply. Consider the order lifted for a week.

They were all together. For the first time since the hall. Thirty-five of them, and one more, in one bowl of pale stone, under torches.

And something was counting them.

“Rachael,” said Hyvred.

She’d gone very still beside Kei. Her crosshair had locked on something on the fifth tier and wasn’t letting go.

Yan looked.

Group 4 were on the fifth tier, a little way along. Rachael at the end of their bench, beside Dennis, with her sleeves rolled and her arms folded and her face doing the thing it did when she was watching a fight she thought was stupid. She’d been watching PenguChi come out of the tunnel with her penguin for the first semifinal. She hadn’t noticed anything.

Two rows behind her, on the seventh tier, a woman was sitting with a basket of apples on her knees.

Yan knew her. He’d never seen her. But he’d read about her, a week ago, on the road, in the channel, from Deston on the downs: a woman with apples at the crossroads, watching Rachael. She walked into the barley and the barley didn’t move. Hyvred had seen her on the first day, by the north gate, and thought she was one.

The apple-seller had no candle.

And her attention wasn’t going round the bowl. It wasn’t counting. The other thirty-nine were counting. This one had stopped. It was leaning, all of it, the whole hollow shape of it, toward the bright healer’s candle two rows in front of it on the fifth tier. Leaning the way a hand leans toward something it’s about to pick up.

Suzu went so cold round Yan’s neck that it hurt.

“Yan,” said Kei. Very calmly. The calmest voice in the kingdom, the voice people brought their grief to. It wasn’t calm. “Yan. Hisui says it’s—“

“I know,” said Yan.

He looked at the fourth tier.

He was already moving.

Yan hadn’t seen him start. Nobody had. The man with the rod had been sitting at the end of his bench on the fourth tier with two bees on his head, the way he’d been sitting there all week, very still. And then he wasn’t sitting there. He was walking. Along the fourth tier. Not fast. Not slow. At the pace of a man going to get a pie.

Nobody looked at him. Nobody ever did. He went along the fourth tier between people with candles, who moved their knees to let him past without looking up, and up the steps between the stands to the fifth tier, and along the fifth, past Group 4’s bench, past Gabe, who turned his head, and Deston, who’d gone very still, and Dennis.

He didn’t stop at Rachael.

He went past her. Up one more step. To the sixth tier, the row behind her. Between Rachael on the fifth tier and the apple-seller on the seventh. And sat down there. On the end of the sixth-tier bench, directly behind Rachael, directly in front of the apple-seller, with his rod across his knees.

Between them.

He didn’t look round. He sat with his back to the apple-seller, a foot from her basket, and looked at the sand. Two bees on his head. Very still.

Yan felt it.

He felt it the way he’d felt it once on the red, a year ago, when demon scouts came out of the grey grass and a fisherman with a rod walked into the middle of them. Not a candle. He’d never been able to feel a candle in the man with the rod; he’d felt something else, something that wasn’t a flame at all, older than the body it was in, from somewhere else. He felt that now. Not flaring. Not angry. Just there. On the sixth tier of the east stand, between a healer and a gap. The way a mountain is just there.

The apple-seller’s attention stopped leaning.

Nothing happened for a long time.

Down on the sand, PenguChi and Xav had come out into the torchlight, and the marshal had said begin, and they were standing in the chalk ring not moving, both of them, the way the dawn class stood on wet sand. The crowd had gone quiet to watch. Nobody in the stands was looking at the sixth tier of the east stand. Nobody ever looked at the man with the rod.

Yan was looking. So was Hyvred. So was Kei, and Hisui, who had gone silent round Kei’s neck for the second time in two years. So were Gabe and Deston, on the fifth tier, very carefully not turning round.

The apple-seller sat on the seventh tier with her basket on her knees, behind a man who wasn’t looking at her.

Then she got up.

She did it the way anyone does, at the Lists, in the middle of a bout, when they’ve decided they’ve seen enough. She picked up her basket, and stood, and said something polite to the man next to her that Yan couldn’t hear, and stepped past his knees into the gangway. And walked along the seventh tier, and down the steps, and along the bottom rail, and out through the gate under the north stand, into the dark.

She didn’t look back. Not once. Not at Rachael, not at the man on the sixth tier. She just went, with her basket of apples, the way a woman goes home after a long day.

The man with the rod didn’t look round either.

Yan felt her go. The gap in the field of candles moving away, out of the gate, into the city, until he couldn’t feel it any more.

The other thirty-nine stayed. Counting.

Rachael hadn’t turned round.

She’d been sitting on the fifth tier at the end of Group 4’s bench the whole time, with her arms folded, watching PenguChi and Xav stand in a chalk ring not moving. She hadn’t moved. She hadn’t looked over her shoulder. Yan had been watching her the whole time.

He was close enough to hear her. Two tiers up, a little way along. In the quiet, while the whole bowl held its breath for the two people on the sand, her voice came up to him very clearly. Dry. Flat. Not turning round.

“I saw her,” said Rachael.

Behind her, on the sixth tier, the man with the rod didn’t move.

“I saw her,” said Rachael again. “On the downs. At the crossroads. With the apples. And again on the first day, by the north gate. And today, coming in.” She didn’t uncross her arms. “I’m not an idiot, Declan.”

“I know,” said the man behind her.

“I’ve had three of them. In Kingsferry. One through a wall. One in the night market. And a man in a hat on a cliff.” Her voice was very even. “I know what they look like. I know how they sit. I know when one’s two rows behind me looking at the back of my neck.” She paused. “I was fine.”

“I know.”

“I was handling it.”

“I know you were.”

Rachael was quiet for a long moment. On the sand, PenguChi had moved, and Xav had moved a hair before her, and the crowd went oh and then went quiet again.

“Then why did you come and sit there?” said Rachael.

The man with the rod didn’t answer straight away. He looked at the sand, at the two people in the chalk ring under the torches.

“Because I could,” he said.

Rachael didn’t say anything for a long time.

Yan watched her. He had his attention on her candle without meaning to, the way you keep a hand near a lamp in the wind. It was very bright. A healer’s candle, burning like a lamp in a window. And it was leaning. Not toward anything in the crowd. Back. Over her own shoulder. Toward the man on the bench behind her.

Leaning the way candles lean toward what they love. The way Suzu’s leaned toward him.

“Sit there anyway,” said Rachael.

She said it the way she said everything. Dry. Like a woman telling off a patient for needing her. But her voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, and she didn’t uncross her arms, and she didn’t turn round, and the man behind her stayed exactly where he was, between her and the gate the apple-seller had gone out of, for the rest of the night.

Xav beat PenguChi in the end, by a hair, the way everyone that week seemed to win by a hair. Salty beat Milli, not by a hair, by about a mile, lifting him clean off the sand on the end of a blunted greatsword and setting him down again outside the chalk very gently, the way you’d set down a cat.

“Sorry,” said Salty.

“Don’t be,” said Milli, from the sand, delighted. He’d been Drawn for a day and a half and lost to a man who’d carried a black stone across the Redmarch. “That was amazing.”

Yan didn’t see much of either bout.

He sat on the sixth tier with Suzu cold round his neck and counted. Thirty-nine gaps in the field of candles, round the bowl of the Lists, in the torchlight. Counting. Thirty-five. Thirty-six. Back to the beginning. Every one of them leaning, at the end of every count, toward the sixth tier of the east stand, and the man sitting there behind Rachael, and staying on him longer than on any of the rest.

Thirty-six.

They’d counted the same number he had.

He found him afterwards, on the walk back.

The Lists had emptied into the dark. The torches were going out along the rail, one by one, as the marshals went round with their snuffers. The crowd poured out of the gates into the warm night, laughing, talking about Salty lifting the cat, about Xav, about the Trios and the girl with the horns and the north stand chanting her name. The thirty-five went out in their twos and threes toward the Broken Lance.

The thirty-nine went out too. Yan felt them go. Separately. Through different gates. Into the city. Not together. Never together.

The man with the rod was walking up the street toward the Lance with Rachael on one side of him and Gabe on the other, and Deston behind, and Dennis. Rachael was talking. Telling him off, Yan thought, from her voice. The man was listening.

Yan caught them up at the corner of the high street, under a lamp, with a paper fishing rod in the window above it.

The man stopped. The others went on, Rachael last, looking back once over her shoulder. Yan and the man with the rod and the white snake round Yan’s neck, under a lamp, on a corner.

“Forty,” said Yan.

“I know.”

“Thirty-nine now. One went.”

“I know.” The man was quiet. “How long have you been able to feel them?”

“Tonight,” said Yan. “I didn’t look before. I didn’t know to.” He breathed. “Hyvred saw one on the first day. She thought it was one.”

“It’s never one.” The man turned the rod on his shoulder. “It wasn’t one in Kingsferry. It wasn’t one at the Narrows. It’s never one.”

“They’re counting us.”

“Yes.”

“Thirty-five,” said Yan. “And then you. Thirty-six. And then they start again.” He looked at the man, at a face he couldn’t see. “They stay on you longest. Every time.”

“Good,” said the man.

Yan looked at him.

“Better they’re looking at me,” said the man, “than at her.”

They stood under the lamp for a while.

Down the street, the Broken Lance was lit up, every window, and the noise coming out of it was the noise of thirty-odd people who’d just watched a tournament and wanted to talk about it all night. Somebody was singing. Tiger, probably, badly.

“The cold one,” said Yan, eventually.

The man went still.

“Under her,” said Yan. “At the grave. I’ve had a thread of it since my first week on the red. I wrote it in the letter. Still asleep. Something’s holding it.” He looked down at Suzu, cold round his neck, slowly warming again now that they were away from the Lists. “It’s not like these. These are nothing. That’s—cold. It’s a flame burning the wrong way.” He paused. “But I think they’re the same thing. I think they’re from the same place. The ring with lines in it, at the bottom of the valley. The rise. The grave.” He swallowed. “I don’t know what’s holding it. I thought you would.”

The man was quiet for a long time.

“I don’t know either,” he said. “Not yet.”

“But you think something is.”

“I think,” said the man slowly, “that when I sat by that grave with the sword across my knees, on the anniversary, a year ago, with five of you waiting at a distance and the red going quiet wherever I walked, something under it went to sleep. And I don’t know if it went to sleep because of me, or because of something else, or because of a tree a boy with antlers planted so she wouldn’t be alone.” He turned the rod on his shoulder. “In a week we’ll go east. With all of you. On the anniversary.” A pause. “We’ll both look.”

“Together.”

“Together,” said the man. “You see souls. I see the rest. Between us we might see the whole thing.”

Yan nodded slowly.

“Okay,” he said.

Suzu lifted her head.

She’d been cold since the Lists. Coiled tight round his neck, the cord between them cold and tight as wire. Now, under the lamp on the corner of the high street, with the man with the rod standing in front of them and the thirty-nine gone off into the dark, she uncoiled. Slowly. She lifted her head off Yan’s collarbone and looked at the man for a long time, with her bright green eyes.

Then she did a thing she’d never done.

She leaned. Not her soul. Her body. The whole white length of her, off Yan’s neck, out into the air under the lamp, toward the man with the rod. Not far. A hand’s width. Her tongue went out, once, and touched the back of his hand where it rested on the rod.

The man didn’t move.

Suzu drew back. She wound herself round Yan’s neck again, once, twice. She put her head back down on his collarbone. And down the cord, into Yan’s chest, came warmth. Not hen’s warmth. Not fed warmth. Something else.

Yan didn’t know what it meant. He’d stopped guessing.

He’d ask Hisui in the morning.

“She says,” said a voice, very quietly, from the dark at the edge of the lamplight, and Yan turned and found Kei leaning on the wall at the corner, where he must have been the whole time, with his hands in his haori sleeves and Hisui round his neck. Kei looked apologetic. “Sorry. I followed. I wanted to make sure you were all right.” He listened. “She says he tastes like the red. Like the grave. Like the cold thing, except the other way round.” He frowned, listening harder. “She says he tastes like something that’s been holding something down for a very long time. And is tired.”

The man with the rod didn’t say anything.

“She says,” said Kei, and stopped, and listened, and looked at Yan very carefully. “She says thank you. For sitting there.”

The man looked at the white snake on Yan’s neck for a long moment.

“Tell her,” he said, “she’s welcome.”

Kei listened.

“She says,” said Kei, “she knows. She says she likes him. She says she’s prepared to tolerate him.”

Yan laughed. He didn’t mean to. It came out of him under the lamp on the corner of the high street, low and slow and surprised, and the man with the rod laughed too, very quietly. And they walked down the street together toward the Broken Lance, the three of them and two snakes, under a row of paper fishing rods in the dark windows, with thirty-nine gaps in the city somewhere behind them, counting.

That night, at the long table, Yan wrote in the channel.

He didn’t usually. Not since the road. He let Maigo do the capitals and Wendy do the corrections and Salty do the horseshoes, and wrote one word at the end of the night, a full stop. But tonight he opened his window at the end of the long table, with the noise of the Lance going on round him and Suzu warm round his neck, and wrote, carefully, plainly, the way he’d written a letter once from Cinderhold:

[ 23:41 · Suzuyun: Forty in the Lists tonight. Threadless. No souls. Spread round the stands, one at a time, all week.
23:41 · Suzuyun: They’re counting us. 35, then him. 36. Then again.
23:42 · Suzuyun: One left when he sat down behind Rachael. 39 stayed.
23:42 · Suzuyun: Don’t go anywhere alone. Not tonight. Not till we’re all out of the city.
23:43 · Hyvred: I saw them too. Forty. All week. I’m sorry I only saw one
23:43 · Rachael: I’m fine.
23:43 · Rachael: Thank you, Yan.
23:44 · Chris: Logging.
23:44 · Chris: 39. Noted.
23:45 · Tiger: nobody goes anywhere alone
23:45 · Tiger: IF ANYONE NEEDS SOMEBODY TO WALK WITH THEM I AM AVAILABLE
23:45 · Tiger: AT ALL TIMES
23:46 · Drew: He is.
23:47 · Maigo: me too ]

He looked at it for a while.

Then, at the bottom, because it was what he always wrote, and because it was true:

[ 23:49 · Suzuyun: Okay. ]

He closed his window.

At the end of the long table, the man with the rod had read every line. Yan saw him read them. He didn’t write anything.

He never had.
