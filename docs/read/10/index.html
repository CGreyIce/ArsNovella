The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 1: Thirty-Four Winters

Chapter 10

The Lists

2,958 words · 13 min read

The Lists at Valcrest could hold eight thousand people, and on the morning of their first exhibition bout, Chris was fairly sure about seven and a half thousand of them were there to watch Winter and Ina die.

“That’s an exaggeration,” said Lucan, beaming, as they stood in the shadowed archway under the stands and listened to the noise. “It’s at most six thousand. And they don’t want them to die. They just want them to lose humiliatingly in front of their families.” He clapped Chris on the shoulder. “Which is a completely different thing!”

“Is it?” said Hako.

“Entirely. Death is final. Humiliation is a story.” Lucan’s eyes were shining. He’d been shining for six weeks, ever since the wagon had rolled through Valcrest’s gates and he’d watched the whole street stop to stare at the girl with the black horns and the man with the demon mask on his shoulder. “And stories, my friends, can have second acts.”

Out beyond the archway, the crowd was chanting something. Chris didn’t need the translation to know what it was.

Demons. Demons. Demons.

Valcrest was a city built around a fight. That was how Lucan put it, and after six weeks Chris thought he was right. Everything in the town faced the Lists the way flowers face the sun: the inns, the armourers, the betting houses, the tailors who sold coloured sashes in the champions’ colours. There were bouts most days, and something bigger every week, and the whole city talked about nothing else.

Which meant that for six weeks, the whole city had talked about them.

It wasn’t hostile, exactly. Not at first. People stared at Winter’s horns in the street, and crossed to the other side, and whispered. Children dared each other to run up and touch Ina’s mask and then fled screaming when he turned around. An old woman spat at Winter’s feet in the market on the second day, and Ysolde, without breaking stride, stepped between them and looked at the old woman until she went away.

“They did it to me,” Ysolde said afterwards, when Winter wouldn’t talk. “For years. Goat horns, in a war against demons. Doesn’t matter that they’re nothing alike. People see horns and get frightened, and frightened people are stupid.” She touched one of her own, the curled grey ram’s horn sweeping back from her temple. “You can’t make them stop. You can only make them watch.”

Winter had looked at her for a long time, the way she looked at everyone now: guarded, measuring, giving away nothing.

“How?” she’d said.

“Win,” said Ysolde.

So they trained.

They trained in the mornings with Ysolde, in a yard behind the Lists that smelled of sawdust and horses, with wooden swords and wooden spears and blunt practice bows. They trained in the afternoons with Lucan, who didn’t teach them to fight at all but taught them to stand: where to look when the crowd was shouting, how to walk out of the archway like you owned the sand, how to bow so that it looked like mercy. And in the evenings they sat in the common room of the inn Lucan had found for them, which was called the Broken Lance and was mostly full of off-duty fighters, and compared their windows.

“Three,” said Chris, at the end of the sixth week. He had a notebook open on the table in front of him, full of tiny cramped handwriting. “Still three. But look at this.”

The others leaned in. Newish groaned.

“Oh no, he’s got graphs.”

“They’re not graphs, they’re logs.” Chris tapped the page. “Every training session. What I did, how long, what I felt, whether the number moved. I’ve been tracking it for a month. It goes up after the mornings with Ysolde, never after the afternoons with Lucan, and it goes up more on days I’m tired.” He looked up. “I think you have to be a bit worn down before the aura notices you. Like it doesn’t bother coming out unless your body’s already asking for help.”

“That’s horrible,” said Hako cheerfully. “Three, by the way. Me too. The spear helps. I don’t know why. Something about the length.”

“Four,” said Winter. She said it quietly, the way she said everything. “When I reach for it, my fingers go cold. Not cold like winter. Cold like when you put your hand in snow and it starts to burn.” She turned one hand over on the table, looking at it, one blue eye and one pink. “It’s mine. It feels like mine.”

“Four,” said Ina. “And it’s easier with the mask on.” He touched it, where it hung at his shoulder. “I don’t know if that’s magic or just… focus. When I put it on, everything else goes quiet, and there’s heat, right here—“ he touched his sternum— “like a coal that someone’s breathing on.”

“Three,” said Newish. “And I’ve got a weird one. When we spar, I can tell when people are about to move. Not what they’ll do. Just when. Like there’s a beat, and I can hear it.” She shrugged. “Is that a skill? Or am I just really good at annoying people at exactly the right moment?”

“Both,” said Chris, and wrote it down.

There had been letters, too, all through the six weeks. Chris had written to half the summoned and heard back from most of them, and the ones that mattered most were from Thea, in Wrenhaven, who wrote long, dense, wonderful letters about what she could see under the window: lines and structures and nested conditions, half of which Chris could follow and half of which kept him up at night. He couldn’t see the code himself. But he could think about it. And the more he thought about it, the more he was sure of one thing.

“Whoever built this,” he’d written back to her, “built it like someone who’d used things like it. Not invented them. Used them. It’s too comfortable. Nobody’s first try at a system is this comfortable.”

She hadn’t answered that part yet.

The bout was simple. Lucan explained it to them three times, with diagrams, as if they were children.

Two teams of five. Wooden weapons, padded. A banner on a pole at each end of the sand. You took the other team’s banner back to your own end, and you won. If you were struck on the body or the head with a weapon, you were out, and had to walk off and stand by the wall. No magic, which didn’t matter, because none of them could do any. No aura, which didn’t matter either, for the same reason.

The other team were cadets from the city guard. Native-born. Mostly Initiates, like them, but two of them were Drawn, and all of them had been training with wooden swords since they were twelve.

“So we lose,” said Hako, looking at them across the sand. The cadets were warming up in a tight, practised knot, and they were good. “We’re definitely going to lose.”

“We’re going to lose nobly,” said Lucan. “That’s all I ask. Lose nobly, and make it close, and the second act writes itself.”

Chris was looking at the cadets too. He’d been watching them practise for three days, from the stands, with his notebook.

“Actually,” he said, “I don’t think we lose.”

The noise when they walked out of the archway was like walking into a wall.

Demons. Demons. DEMONS.

Winter’s hand closed on Chris’s sleeve, the way it had in the summoning hall. He could feel it shaking. Beside her, Ina walked with his chin up and his face perfectly calm, and Chris could see the effort it cost him.

Newish stopped in the middle of the sand, turned in a full circle, looked up at six thousand screaming people, and cupped her hands around her mouth.

“IS THAT ALL YOU’VE GOT?” she bellowed. “MY NAN BOOS LOUDER THAN THIS!”

There was a moment of stunned silence from the nearest stands. Then the booing doubled.

“Newish,” said Hako, faintly.

“What? They’re louder now. That’s engagement.”

“That’s not how—“

“It’s exactly how.” She was grinning, the same grin Chris had seen on a hundred late-night calls, usually right before she said something that started a fight. “If they’re yelling at me, they’re not yelling at Winter.”

Winter’s hand loosened on Chris’s sleeve.

At the far end of the sand, the master of the bout raised a white flag. The crowd’s noise sharpened into something hungry.

And Ina, without saying anything, reached up and put on his mask.

Chris heard the gasp go round the stands like a wave. The grey face with its red-rimmed eyes and its terrible fanged grin, the red cords hanging from its mouth: on the sand, in the sun, in front of six thousand people who had come to see a demon, it looked exactly like one. For a second the booing faltered.

“Sorry,” said Ina, his voice muffled and calm behind it. “It helps.”

“Don’t be sorry,” said Ysolde, from the wall. “Let them look.”

The flag came down.

The cadets did exactly what Chris had seen them do in practice for three days.

They came forward in a wedge, fast and disciplined, the two Drawn cadets at the point. It was a good formation. It was built to punch straight through to the banner, and it would have, against anyone who stood in front of it.

So nobody stood in front of it.

“Split,” said Chris.

It wasn’t a plan, exactly. It was older than a plan. It was the thing his body remembered from a thousand matches on a glowing screen, with Winter’s voice on one side of him and Newish’s on the other and a third voice, steady and amused, calling the angles. Split. Let them commit. Punish the commit.

Winter and Ina went left. Chris and Newish went right. Hako held the middle, alone, with his long wooden spear, and the wedge came at him like a battering ram.

Hako didn’t try to stop it. He stepped back, and back again, keeping the spear’s long point in the face of the leading cadet, and the leading cadet had to slow down to get past it, and the wedge bunched up behind him.

“Now,” said Newish.

Chris had no idea how she knew. She just knew: the beat she’d talked about, the one she could hear. And she was right. The moment she said it, the cadets on the edges of the wedge had turned toward Hako, all of them, committed, their backs half-turned to the flanks.

Ina came out of the left like a nightmare, mask and all, and the cadet nearest him actually screamed. Winter was half a step behind him, silent and fast and precise, and her practice sword took the second cadet across the ribs before he’d finished turning. On the right, Chris and Newish hit the other edge. Newish shouted something so rude at the cadet in front of her that he swung wildly at her face, and missed, and Chris tapped him on the shoulder while he was still off balance.

Four cadets left. Then three.

And then, for one strange second in the noise and dust, Chris looked across the sand and saw a gap.

It was on the far side of the wedge, behind the last two cadets, where nobody was standing. And his whole body knew, with absolute certainty, that someone should be standing there. That this was the spot the third player always took: the one who’d hold the far angle, wait, and finish it. He’d played a hundred matches with that gap filled. He’d stopped noticing it was there.

It was empty. It had been empty for three years. He’d been leaving room for someone who wasn’t coming.

Then Hako stepped into it.

He’d dropped back out of the middle without anybody telling him to. He moved around the edge of the scramble and took up the far angle, the spear held low, and waited: exactly the way he’d been taught to wait, back on Earth, in his first clumsy months of a game he’d never played before, by someone who’d been very patient with him.

The last two cadets broke toward the banner.

Hako’s spear swept their legs out from under them, one after the other, neat as a lesson.

Ina picked up the banner, mask still on, and walked it back to their end of the sand without hurrying, as if he had all the time in the world.

For a moment, there was no sound at all.

Then somewhere high in the eastern stands, one person started clapping.

Then a few more. Then a section. It wasn’t everyone; it wasn’t close to everyone. There was still booing, plenty of it, and somebody threw a cabbage, which Newish caught and held up over her head like a trophy, to a roar that was at least half laughter. But it was no longer one sound. It had split, like the cadets had, and the part that was cheering was getting louder.

Winter was standing in the middle of the sand with her practice sword hanging from one hand, staring up at the stands. Chris watched her look at them, all of them, the ones still shouting demon and the ones who weren’t any more. He watched something in her face loosen, very slightly, the way her hand had loosened on his sleeve.

She didn’t smile. Not quite. But it was very close.

“Told you,” said Ysolde, at the wall.

Ina took off his mask. His face underneath was flushed and grinning, which only made the half of the crowd that was cheering cheer louder.

Hako had come back across the sand to them. He was breathing hard, and his spear was scraped where it had swept the cadets’ legs, and he looked at Chris with a strange, uncertain expression.

“Was that right?” he said. “The far side. I just… it felt like someone should be there.”

“Yeah,” said Chris, after a moment. His throat felt odd. “Yeah. That’s exactly right. That’s where someone should be.”

He didn’t explain. Hako didn’t ask. But Chris saw Newish look at Hako, and then at the empty far end of the sand, and then away, very quickly, and he knew she’d felt it too.

One down. They’d been one down for three years.

Today, for a minute, they hadn’t been.

Lucan was on the edge of the master’s platform before the cadets had even finished limping off, arms spread, cloak blazing red in the sun, and somehow his voice carried over all of it.

“People of Valcrest!” he cried. “You came to see demons!”

The crowd roared: half jeering, half laughing.

“I am delighted to inform you,” said Lucan, “that you have been robbed!” Laughter, louder. “What you have seen today are five of the summoned, the thirty-five strangers brought to Aurelan by the Advisor’s own hand! Five of them, still at Initiate, still unable to light a candle or sharpen a blade with aura, who have just taken the banner from the finest cadets of the city guard!”

The cadets, by the wall, looked pained.

“And I have more news.” Lucan reached into his cloak with a flourish and drew out a folded paper with a heavy seal, which Chris was almost certain had been in there the whole time, waiting for exactly this moment. “By decree of the crown, and with the blessing of His Majesty King Severin, when the year turns there will be held, here in the Lists at Valcrest, a tournament of the summoned! All thirty-five! Every group, from every corner of the kingdom, here, on this sand, before you!”

The noise that came back was enormous. Chris felt it in his teeth.

“A tournament,” said Hako slowly. “Like a real one.”

“Like a real one,” said Newish. She was grinning so hard it looked painful. “Oh, Drew’s going to lose his mind.”

That night, at the table in the Broken Lance, Chris wrote to Hyvred and Kei.

He told them about the bout, and the wedge, and Newish’s nan, and the cabbage. He told them that the crowd had split, and that part of it had cheered, and that Winter had almost smiled. He told them about the decree, and the tournament, all thirty-five of them in the Lists when the year turned. Tell Drew, he wrote. He’s going to want to run it. Somebody should.

He didn’t tell them about the gap on the far side of the sand.

He sat for a long time afterwards with the pen in his hand. Around him the common room was loud with off-duty fighters, half of whom had been in the stands that morning and several of whom had bought Ina a drink. Newish was teaching a table of guardsmen a card game and fleecing them. Hako was asleep in his chair. Winter was sitting by the fire with Ysolde, the two of them saying almost nothing, which seemed, for both of them, to be enough.

And Chris thought about a voice he’d heard every night for over a year, steady and amused, calling the far angle. A voice that had laughed at his bad jokes and made worse ones. A voice that would have had a nickname for Lucan in about four seconds, and would have caught Newish’s cabbage before she did.

He’d never met him in person. He’d always meant to.

For a moment, in the noise of the common room, he could have sworn he heard that voice say something. Something completely untrustworthy, finished with one small word.

Then it was gone, and there was only the fire.
