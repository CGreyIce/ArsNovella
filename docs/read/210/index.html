He packed on Thursday night.

He didn’t decide to. He found he’d done it. He came in off the jetty at dusk with the channel still open in front of his face, Maigo’s lines at the bottom of it, all in lowercase, which was wrong, which was the most wrong thing he’d seen in that channel in two years and a half, they’re at the foot of the hills. a day out maybe. a lot. step and stop like the scouts, and he put the rod in the corner and the fish on the board, and he went to the chest under the window and took out the old pack and started putting things in it.

A blanket. The flint. The water skin. Socks. He knew the list. He’d made it at nineteen and never changed it. Everything a man needed to go a long way fast and not stop.

He knew the sum, too. He’d done it at noon, on the end of the jetty, without meaning to, the way you do a sum you’ve done a hundred times. A month by ox. Less by horse. A great deal less on foot, for a man at the top of the eighth tier who didn’t sleep and didn’t eat much and didn’t stop for anything. He knew exactly how long. He’d known on Thursday at noon. If he’d left then, and run, and never once stopped, he’d have been on the red by Sunday.

It might have been close enough.

He put a knife in last. Not the good one. The good one was on a girl’s left hip. A plain one out of the drawer, that he gutted fish with. His own old soldier’s knife was at the bottom of the lake off the end of the jetty, where Diwa had thrown it the summer Alea was five.

Alea came down the ladder.

She stood at the bottom of it with her blanket round her shoulders, the way she’d stood the morning he pinned the list up, and looked at the pack on the table. And at him. She didn’t say anything. She’d stopped saying things about packs a long time ago. At eleven she’d asked to come, once, and been told when you’re older, and never asked again; and every year after, on the last night of summer, she’d put the good knife in the small pocket on the left of his pack in the dark, so he’d think he’d remembered it. They’d both been very careful about packs, in this house, for seven years.

She looked at it a long time.

Then she went back up the ladder.

He sat at the table with the pack in front of him until the lamp burned down. And on Friday morning, before she was up, he took everything out of it again, and put it back in the chest under the window, the blanket, the flint, the socks, the knife, and shut the lid. And went down to the end of the jetty and fished.

He didn’t decide that either. He just knew, on Friday morning, that he wasn’t going.

He knew why, too. It took him until Saturday to say it to himself in words, and he only did it then because Gigichi asked.

She came down on Saturday with the hens’ corn, which she’d taken to doing since the ice went out, for no reason she’d ever given, and stood at the land end of the jetty by her tree. Gigichi’s tree. The cutting from the ash on the knoll, a year and a bit old, that she’d put in the ground by the water in the spring, with a dozen leaves on it now and more coming, pointing nowhere in particular, the way a tree’s leaves should.

“You okay?” said Gigichi.

He didn’t say yes. She’d have known.

“Nine years,” he said. “I went every year. At summer’s end. Walked east, a month, on my own. Sat by her. Walked home.”

“I know.”

“Nine years it was mine. That grave. Nobody else’s. If anything came for it, I’d have known first, because I was the only one who was ever there.” He looked at the water. “And they’ve been there two years and a half. Every week. Maigo on Thursdays. Yan. The old ones. They sang over it. They planted her a tree.” He was quiet. “It’s theirs as well, now.”

Gigichi waited.

“If I go,” he said, “every time something comes for it, I’ll go. Every spring. And they’ll wait for me. And it’ll be mine again.” He stopped. “It can’t be mine any more. Not just mine. It’s too far. And I’m not—“ He stopped again.

“You’re not there,” said Gigichi.

“I’m here.”

Gigichi looked at him for a while. Not at his face. Nobody looked at his face. At his shoulders, the way they all did now, Group 1, as if they’d agreed it among themselves without telling him.

“That’s a good reason,” said Gigichi. “I don’t like it.”

“No.”

“I’ll sit up,” said Gigichi. “When it comes. I’ll sit up with you. Not down here.” She looked at the jetty. “It’s not mine to come down here.”

He thought about telling her she could come down any time she liked. He didn’t. She was right. Some things you sat up with somebody by not being next to them.

“Thank you,” he said.

She put the corn down for the hens and went back up the slope, through the gap in her hedge, and he fished.

On Sunday at a quarter to eight Wendy wrote 161.

He was on the end of the jetty. He was always on the end of the jetty at a quarter to eight. The lamp lit on the last plank, the rod across his knees, the line out in the evening water going pink and then grey. The three hens in a row along the boards behind him, the way they’d sat every evening since the first snow, for no reason anyone had ever found out. The channel open in front of his face, because it always was, at this hour.

He sat there.

He’d known it was coming since Thursday. He’d made his peace with it on Friday morning, and said it out loud on Saturday. It didn’t matter. He read 161 and the grave and it’s ours, and he felt his right hand go into his coat on its own, into the inside pocket, past the flat grey stone that was always there, and close on the other thing.

Half of a gold heart. Wire and lacquer. A cat girl in Solmere had made it, the winter she found her skill, for a man she’d never met in this world, and cut it in two, and given half to a guard, if you ever go north again. And the guard had carried it in a book for ten months and given it to him in the mist at the north gate of Valcrest. Perhaps anyway.

He’d only held it like this once before. All night. In a merlon at Cinderhold, with a letter from Halvard in his other hand. He’d found out afterwards that she’d felt it. On her wrist, in Solmere. Warm, all night. That she’d sat up with it, and not known why, and not asked.

It was the only thing he owned that went to anybody without words.

He held it.

Behind him, the red hen got up off the boards, and walked down the jetty, and sat down again against his hip. He didn’t look round. He put his other hand on her back.

The lamp went out a little before midnight.

He didn’t fill it. There was oil in the cabin, and he didn’t go and get it, and the flame went down on the last plank to a blue bead and then nothing, and the dark came in off the water all at once. He didn’t mind. He didn’t need a lamp to read the channel. It was the only light on the lake.

They all wrote. All night. He read every line.

He’d heard her come down through the field at nine, very quietly, and stop, halfway, and the briar move round her, and then nothing. She was sitting in her hedge. In the dark. Facing the water.

And at half past twelve, Bee. drawing the knoll from wendy’s numbers. thirty paces. a rock on the east side. niko’s rock.

He knew the rock.

He’d sat on it. Once. Nine years ago, in the morning, with the spade across his knees and his hands black to the wrist, when it was done. A big grey rock on the east side of the knoll, flat on top, half sunk in the ground, the only stone on that hill bigger than a man’s head. He’d sat on it for an hour, facing east, because he couldn’t face the other way yet. And then he’d got up and walked back across the red to the fort and not looked up.

Mashi was sitting against it now. In the dark. On the east side. With something the size of a cart somewhere out on the slope in front of him.

He held the heart.

Alea came down at two.

He heard the ladder. He heard the cabin door. He heard her come down the lake path in the dark, not carefully, the way she walked everywhere now, her feet knowing where the ground was, and along the jetty, and the hens moving over for her the way they did. She had a blanket over her arm and Astra on her back.

She put the blanket over his shoulders without asking. Then she sat down on the boards beside him, on the other side from the red hen, with her back against the last post, and took Astra off her back and laid it across her knees, the way she’d laid it beside a headstone once, in the autumn.

She didn’t ask what was happening. She couldn’t read it; nobody from here could. She’d watched him sit on the end of a jetty and read thirty-five people writing to each other every night of her life since she was sixteen and never once asked him to read it out.

She asked now.

“What does it say?”

“Nothing from the knoll,” he said. “Not since a quarter to eight. Everyone else.” He was quiet. “Malcolm says if nobody writes by dawn nothing’s wrong. They’re busy. He says that’s how knolls work.”

“Is he right?”

“Yes.”

She looked out at the water.

“Could you get there?” she said. “If you went now.”

He didn’t lie to her. He’d stopped, a long time ago.

“No,” he said. “Not now. On Thursday, maybe.”

“And you didn’t.”

“No.”

“I saw the pack,” said Alea.

“I know.”

She was quiet for a long time. Her hands were on Astra, on the sharkskin of the grip and the old saddle leather Gabe had put under it, and the fisherman’s knot.

“It’s my mum,” she said.

“Yes.”

“It’s my mum,” said Alea. Not loudly. She’d never been loud. “And there’s a hundred and sixty—“

“A hundred and sixty-one.”

“—a hundred and sixty-one of them walking up the riverbeds to dig her up, and four big ones, and you’re sitting here.”

“Yes.”

She didn’t say anything else for a while. He let her not say it. He’d learned that from Diwa, a long time ago: that when someone was angry with you for a good reason you sat and let them be, and didn’t explain, because the explaining was for you and not for them.

“Who’s there?” said Alea, eventually.

“Yan. Salty. Wendy. Maigo. Mashi.” He said it in order, the way he’d have called them on Earth. “Rhea. Aldous. The twenty-five old ones, every one who can still walk, Teague with them. Fifteen more, and a boy called Garth among them who salutes buckets. Hesper, with the bandages.” He was quiet. “The elves came out to watch. Liraun’s with the tree.”

Alea sat with that.

She’d been there, in the autumn. She’d walked out to the knoll on the night of the full moon beside him, with Aldous and his white cloth, and Group 3 behind her. She’d met Teague, who’d said she’s got her face. She’d met Hesper, who’d tapped his fingertips three times. She’d read the stone aloud to them all and answered her mother’s last words, line by line, with all of them standing round her in the dark.

“It’s theirs too,” said Alea. Slowly. As if she’d only just found it out.

“Yes.”

“That’s why.”

“That’s why.”

She leaned her head back against the post and shut her eyes.

“I don’t like it,” she said.

“Gigichi said that.”

“Gigichi’s right.” She was quiet. “I’ll sit up.”

She didn’t. She was asleep inside a quarter of an hour, sitting up against the last post of the jetty with her mother’s sword across her knees and her chin going down by degrees onto her chest, the way she’d gone to sleep on him at the top of every hill in the world when she was small. He pulled the blanket off his own shoulders and put it over her. She didn’t wake.

He held the heart.

At ten to five the old moon came up over the far hills.

Higu had said it would. Ten to five here. Thinnest it gets. Last one before new. She was right, the way Higu was always right about the sky; he’d stopped being surprised by it the first winter. He looked up from the channel and there it was, coming up out of the black line of the hills on the far shore, east, over the water. Not a moon at all, really. A thread. A white thread lying on its back, so thin you could only see it because the sky behind it was beginning, very faintly, to go grey.

The last of the old moon. The day before it went.

He’d seen it like that on the red once. From the wall at Cinderhold, the summer he was fifteen, when he’d stood a night watch for the first time, and a sergeant had told him it was bad luck to fight under the last of the old moon, and he’d asked why, and the sergeant hadn’t known.

It was coming up over the knoll now, too. A month east. The same thread. He knew it was. He thought about Mashi, against a rock, looking up at it.

The grey came up behind it. The hills went from black to blue. A heron he hadn’t known was there, on the shallows by the reeds, took its head out from under its wing.

At nine minutes to six, Astra rang.

He’d never heard it ring on its own.

Not once. He’d heard it ring in the autumn, on the knoll, when Alea laid it beside the headstone it had been cut from, and the two halves of one blade had lain a hand’s width apart for the first time since the king split them on Garrow’s anvil, and the note had gone up out of both of them at once and down into the ground. He’d heard it hum, sometimes, in the cabin, when she drew it, if the light was right. He’d never heard it do this.

It rang across her knees. By itself. With nothing touching it. One note.

Low. Long. The parting note. The one it had sung on the knoll in the autumn, a hand’s width from its other half. It came up out of the steel and went out over the water in the grey, and the heron went up off the shallows in a clatter, and the three hens were on their feet all at once on the boards, and Alea’s eyes came open and her hands closed on the grip before she was awake.

It went on ringing. Longer than it should have. Long enough to fill the lake.

And then it stopped. Not all at once. The way a bell stops, going down and down under the edge of hearing until you’re not sure if you’re still hearing it or remembering it.

Alea was looking at him.

“That’s the stone,” he said.

His hand had closed on the heart in his coat so hard it hurt. He hadn’t told it to.

“The other half,” said Alea. “On the knoll.” Her voice was very steady. She’d learned that from him too. “Is it good or bad?”

“I don’t know.”

He looked at the channel.
