The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 9: The Other Front

Chapter 256

Antidote

4,729 words · 21 min read

There was no such thing as a poison. There was only a dose.

Kei had believed that since before he could have said it. Since a chemistry teacher had written it on a board, probably, or since a book, or since some long night on Earth looking things up he didn’t need to know, the way he looked up everything. Foxglove would stop your heart and foxglove would start it again. The same snake that could kill you could be milked for the thing that saved you from it. It was all the same stuff. It was only ever a question of how much, and where, and when.

Here, it was a path.

Venom and medicine, his window had said in the first week, and Hisui had said, from round his neck, obviously, in a voice like somebody very bored reading a menu. Three years later his window said Spark and Dose and his room behind the guardhouse kitchen at Thornwall smelled permanently of nettles, and he’d made more medicines than anybody in the town including the apothecary, and kept nine people from walking up a valley in the spring and thirty-three in the autumn, and lost three anyway.

He’d lost three anyway.

He hadn’t been able to dose them. They’d had no window. Hyvred hadn’t seen the cold on them; nobody had; they’d walked out of the town at dusk while the rest of them were looking at the long room, and his forty-one flasks had sat in their satchels with green thread round their necks, and done nothing at all for a dyer’s girl and a trooper and a widow.

And for the thirty-three he had dosed, it hadn’t been enough. They’d stood up anyway. Smiling. Drew had held them with his chest, and broken two ribs doing it, and then the king had had to open his ninth tier on a floor.

His doses made the call quieter. That was all they’d ever done. Like putting your hands over somebody’s ears.

He needed one it couldn’t get through.

You’re going to do something stupid, said Hisui.

He was round Kei’s neck, the way he always was, in the little room behind the kitchen with the shutters closed and the brazier going and the long table covered in jars. A green tree snake the length of Kei’s arm, jade-coloured, with a pale belly and a way of looking at things that made Kei feel, every single day, slightly underdressed.

I can tell, said Hisui. You’ve got the face. The thinking face. The one you had before you made the first dose, and before you tried the foxglove on the rat.

“I’m thinking,” said Kei.

You’re thinking about testing it on yourself.

Kei didn’t say anything.

You are, said Hisui. I can taste it. You taste like a man about to drink something. His tongue went in and out against Kei’s jaw. Don’t.

“I can’t test it on them,” said Kei. Out loud; he always talked to Hisui out loud, when there was nobody there, and quite often when there was. “The called. I can’t. I’ve thought about it for a month. Corporal Dunn offered. He came to the door and said try it on me, I’ll go. And I said no.”

Good.

“Because he can’t say no to it. Not really. Not to the call. If I give him something and it doesn’t work, he walks up the valley and I can’t get him back.” Kei looked at the jars. “I don’t test on people who can’t say no.”

So you’ll test it on the one person you can’t say no to, said Hisui. Very sensible. A man of principle.

“I’m not called. I’ve never been called. It doesn’t want me.” He picked up a jar and put it down again. “So I’ll go where it’s loud. Up the road. Past the first milestone, where the swept part starts. Where it’s in the air all the time now, the way you said. And I’ll take a dose, and walk, and see how far I get before I hear it.”

Hisui was quiet. That was rare enough that Kei looked down at him.

It’s in the air all the time now, said Hisui at last. Since the twenties. Since the fourteenth place got past twenty. I taste it every morning. Like sugar in the rain. Somebody in the next room, saying names, not stopping. He was quiet again. It doesn’t want you yet. If you walk up that road far enough, it will.

“That’s the point.”

That’s the stupid part, said Hisui.

He asked the king.

He had to. He’d made Drew promise not to go up the road alone, and Tiger, and he wasn’t going to be the one who did it. He went up the tower at dusk, a hundred and twelve steps, with Hisui round his neck saying this is a mistake on every fortieth step, and found the king sitting against the north parapet beside Hyvred, the way he did, with his big hands in his lap, and told him.

All of it. The doses. The quiet. The three. One it can’t get through. The road.

The king listened without moving.

“How far?” he said.

“As far as I need to. A mile. Two. Not to the rings.” Kei swallowed. “Hild comes with me. She can hear it when I can’t. If I start walking the wrong way she turns me round.”

“Hild,” said the king.

From the trapdoor, where she’d come up behind Kei without his hearing, because nobody ever heard Hild: “Yes.”

The king looked at her for a long time. Then at Hyvred, beside him, with her eyes shut and her back to the valley.

“I’ll watch his light,” said Hyvred, without opening her eyes. “From here. Every step. I’ll see the cold before he hears it. If it comes, I’ll write it, and Hild will turn him round.”

The king was quiet for a long time.

“My father,” he said at last, “used to test his quenches on his own hand. Dip a finger in the barrel, see if it hissed. He had no feeling in three fingers of his left hand by the time he died.” He looked at his own hands. “He said you couldn’t ask a blade what it wanted. You had to find out on yourself.” He looked up at Kei. “He was wrong about a great many things. That wasn’t one of them.”

“Is that a yes, Majesty?”

“It’s a yes,” said the king. “With Hild. Never further than she says. And if Hyvred writes, you come back, whatever the dose is doing.” He paused. “And you eat. Every day. I’ll ask Cook.”

He’ll ask Cook, said Hisui, as they went down the hundred and twelve steps. The king of Aurelan is going to ask Cook whether you’ve eaten. We’ve come a long way.

The first dose was the old one. The nettle one. The one he’d given the thirty-three.

He drank it at the north gate at dawn, with Hild beside him with her staff and her one eye shut, and Hisui round his neck; and walked.

Out through the postern, past Pruett glaring at him. Along the valley road, north, in the frost, a quarter mile, over the millstream bridge where Drew had stopped Tiger. A half mile. The fields white on either side. The mountains ahead, enormous, with the notch in them where the pass road went in. A mile, at the first milestone; and past it, where the road changed.

Swept. That was what Hyvred called it, and that was what it was. The old paved road going on north up the valley, dark and clean, not a leaf on it, not a stone, not a skin of frost, while the fields on either side were white; as if somebody walked down it every night ahead of the weather with a broom. It started a furlong past the milestone now. It had started three furlongs past in the spring. It was sweeping toward them.

He stepped onto it.

Ugh, said Hisui.

Kei felt nothing. He walked. Hild walked beside him, a pace back, listening. Hisui’s tongue was going and going against his jaw, tasting.

A mile and a half.

Two.

And at two miles and a furlong, on the swept road, in the frost, with the town small behind him and the rings nine miles ahead and invisible, he heard it.

Not loud. Hisui had been right; it was like somebody in the next room. Somebody he didn’t know, in the next room of a house he’d never been in, who’d been saying names for a long time, one after another, patiently, warmly, not in a hurry. And then, among them, so ordinary he almost didn’t notice it, the way you almost don’t notice your own name in a crowd—

Kei.

He stopped walking.

He didn’t decide to. His feet decided. And then, very slowly, the way a plant turns, he felt the front of him turn, a hair, north.

A hand on his shoulder. Hild’s. Not hard.

“Back,” said Hild.

He went back.

He read it at the milestone, sitting on it, with his hands shaking. Hisui had gone very quiet round his neck.

Two miles, said Hisui, at last. With the old dose. Without it?

“I don’t know. I’ll try tomorrow.”

You won’t try without it, said Hisui. That’s not a test, that’s a walk into a ring.

“I’ll try a stronger one.”

Of course you will.

The stronger one got him to three miles and was sick on the road.

Not from the call. From the dose. He’d doubled the nettle and added a root the Solmere poison books called quietwort and the apothecary called the old man’s sleep, because it was what the old men in the villages took when they couldn’t sleep for the noise in their heads; and it did make the noise quieter. At three miles he heard nothing at all. He walked on the swept road in the frost in a kind of padded silence, as if somebody had stuffed his ears with wool, and Hild beside him listening; and at three miles and a half his knees went, and he sat down in the road and was sick on the clean dark stones.

He was sick for two days.

Not badly. Cold sweats, and his hands not working, and the brazier in his little room going round and round when he looked at it. Sister Agathe, one of the three healers who’d come up from Kingsferry in the spring and never gone back, sat with him the first night and held a bowl and said nothing about it, because Sister Agathe had been a healer for thirty years and had held a great many bowls for a great many idiots. Brother Ansgar came in once, saw him, and went out again, because Brother Ansgar fainted at blood and had decided, Agathe said, not to risk anything else either.

Hisui wasn’t round his neck.

That was the thing he noticed, in the first hour. He’d lain down on his pallet sweating, and reached up for the snake out of habit, the way you’d reach for a blanket; and Hisui had gone. Off his neck, down his arm, across the floor, and up onto the shelf over the brazier, where he lay along the top of the jars, jade-green, with his head turned away.

“Hisui.”

No, said Hisui.

“Hisui. Please.”

You taste wrong, said Hisui. From the shelf, not looking at him. You taste like them. Like the long room. Like the thirty-three when you’d dosed them, sitting there, with the quiet in them and the window still open behind it. His tongue went in and out at the air. I’m not going to sit on you while you taste like that. Get it out of you. Then ask.

Kei lay on the pallet and looked at the ceiling.

He’d never once in three years not had the snake on him. Not in the bath; Hisui liked baths. Not in his sleep. Not in the long room in the autumn with thirty-three people standing up smiling. He’d had a voice in his ear the whole time, every hour, saying things nobody else could hear, telling him he was an idiot, telling him what was in the air. He’d never known what it was like to be in a room with nothing talking.

It was very quiet.

Like the dose, he thought, lying there. That’s what it’s like. That’s what I’ve been giving them. Silence. Wool in the ears.

And the window still open behind it.

“Why isn’t Hisui here,” said Malcolm.

That was the seventh morning. Kei didn’t remember the fourth and fifth days very well; he’d tried a third dose on the fourth, against Hild’s advice and Agathe’s and Hisui’s from the shelf, a stronger one again, and got four miles up the swept road in a silence like the bottom of a well, and then not known who Hild was. Not for long. Half an hour, she said, afterwards. He’d stood in the road and looked at a tall woman in a soldier’s coat with one eye and not known her name, or his own, or which way the town was; and she’d taken his arm and walked him home like a horse.

He’d been in bed since. Hyvred and Hild had sat with him in turns. Hyvred with her eyes shut, holding his wrist, watching his light from a foot away instead of three miles. Hild with her staff across her knees, listening to him breathe.

And then on the sixth day, people he’d only ever known in the channel had come in at the south gate at dusk, in sleet: six of them, and the king had gone down the road to meet them; and he’d gone too, because he’d wanted to see them come in, and he’d sat on the mounting block by the gate because he couldn’t stand, with his satchel on his knees. And one of them had looked at him with a face like a man reading a column of figures that didn’t add up, and had made everybody eat.

Malcolm. Who was sitting on the end of his pallet now, the next morning, with a book open on his knee and a bowl of porridge in his other hand that he was obviously going to make Kei eat.

“He won’t sit on me,” said Kei. “I taste wrong.”

Malcolm looked at the shelf. At the jade snake lying along the top of the jars with his head turned away.

“Why do you taste wrong?”

So Kei told him. All of it. The doses, the road, the miles. Two and a furlong. Three and a half and sick. Four and not knowing Hild’s name. The wool. The quiet. The window still open behind it.

Malcolm listened without writing anything down, which Kei had been told by John was a very bad sign.

“So the doses work,” said Malcolm, when he’d finished. “The stronger they are, the further you get.”

“Yes.”

“And the stronger they are, the less of you there is when you get there.”

Kei didn’t say anything.

“IF the dose is strong enough to stop the call,” said Malcolm slowly, the way he wrote his lists, “THEN it’s strong enough to stop you.” He looked at the book on his knee, which he hadn’t written in. “That’s not an antidote. That’s just a different way of not being there.”

Kei looked at the ceiling.

“I know,” he said.

“I’m a pessimist,” said Malcolm. “I always assume the first one fails. So I always have a backup.” He held out the porridge. “What’s yours?”

“I don’t have one.”

“Then eat this,” said Malcolm, “and think of one. That’s mine.”

The answer came from Kingsferry.

He didn’t expect it to. He’d been writing in the channel all week, every night, every dose, every mile, because that was what you did; Higu’s rule three was for tier-ups but Chris’s rule, unwritten, was that you logged everything, and Kei had always liked Chris’s rule better. He’d written two miles and a furlong, the old dose, it came in on the left and three and a half, quietwort, sick and four, didn’t know Hild. And people had answered. Drew: stop. Tiger: stop. Yan, from Cinderhold: Suzu says the snake’s right. Thea: what does Hyvred see at the edge when it comes in. Chris: logging.

Rachael hadn’t written anything.

And then, on the seventh evening, Corvin came down from the tower with a strip of paper in his hand. He came to the door of the little room behind the kitchen and stood in it, with his surveyor’s spectacles on his forehead and his face very serious, the way it always was, and said:

“Kingsferry. By the cords. Rachael gave it to Emrys and Emrys sent it straight across to me.” He looked at the paper, and then at Kei, with a kind of wonder that hadn’t left his face in three weeks, since the night Hana fell asleep. “It came straight to me. Not through her. I’m still not used to it.”

He read it out. In his own careful voice, the way the cords wrote, in his own hand, so he could read it.

To Kei, at Thornwall, from Rachael, at Kingsferry. Read it to him exactly.

You’ve been writing it all week and I’ve been reading it at Maud’s bench with a patient’s hand in mine, and I didn’t answer, because I wanted to be sure. I’m sure.

A dose numbs. Numbing isn’t closing.

I spent twenty-six years being the patient. I know the difference better than anyone alive. When you’ve got a wound, they can give you something so you don’t feel it. You still bleed. You bleed more, sometimes, because you don’t know you’re bleeding. And everyone stands round the bed and thinks you’re fine, because you’re quiet.

Hyvred says there’s a crack at the edge of them. A window left open in another room. The cold comes in through it. That’s not a sound. That’s a wound. It’s an open place, and the call goes in through the open place, and your doses put wool in their ears so they don’t hear it going in.

It’s still going in.

You don’t stop a wound by making the patient not feel it. You close it.

Healers close it.

Corvin stopped. He looked at the strip.

“There’s more,” he said. “Under it. In a different—it feels different. Somebody else.”

“Read it.”

Sister Maud says: tell the boy with the snake to stop drinking his own poisons, or she’ll come up there herself and pour them in the millstream, and him after them.

Kei laughed.

It hurt. Everything hurt; he’d been sick for a week. He lay on his pallet in the little room behind the kitchen with Malcolm on the end of it and Corvin in the doorway and a jade snake on a shelf with his head turned away, and laughed until he had to stop and hold his ribs, the way Drew held his.

On the shelf, Hisui turned his head. Just a little. To look.

Healers close it.

He lay awake all that night thinking about it.

He wasn’t a healer. That was the first thing. He was a poisoner who made medicines; he’d always known that. Rachael could put her palm on a chest and read what was wrong in it and Mend a hand bone by bone for an hour. He could make a thing in a jar that did something to you when you drank it. That was all. He worked from outside. With doses.

But there were doses that closed things.

He knew there were. Every apothecary in the kingdom knew one or two. Not the ones that numbed; the other kind. The ones you put on a cut so it stopped bleeding. Not because you didn’t feel it; because the cut shut. Puckered up, drew together at the edges, sealed itself, the way skin seals over a scald. He’d made them in the first winter, for the soldiers, for shaving cuts and blisters and the arrow-graze Brannoch’s sergeant got on the wall. Knitwort, the apothecary called the root. Bitter as sin. You powdered it and put it on the edge of a wound and it drew the edge together.

The edge.

A crack at the edge of them, Rachael had written. A window left open.

He’d been putting his doses in the middle. In the blood, in the belly, in the head; everywhere at once, the whole person, wool in every ear. Of course they’d taken the person with them. He’d been numbing everything to quiet one crack at the edge.

What if you only put it at the edge?

He didn’t know how. He didn’t know if a dose could go to one place in a person and not the others. He didn’t know if a crack at the edge of a light was a thing a root could reach, or if it was only a thing Hyvred could see. He didn’t know anything.

But it was a different question. That was the thing. For a month he’d been asking how much quiet can I put in them before they stop being there. Now he was asking can I close one small thing at the edge.

He liked it better. It was a smaller question. Small questions had answers.

He lay in the dark and listened to the brazier tick, and to the town outside, and to the silence where Hisui wasn’t talking; and somewhere a long way north, eleven miles, through the shutters, the thing in the next room went on saying names.

He tried it on the ninth day.

Not on the road. Hild wouldn’t let him, and neither would the king, and neither would Malcolm, who’d written IF Kei goes up the road again THEN Azac sits on him and shown it to Azac, who’d nodded gravely. He tried it in his room. With the smallest amount of knitwort he could measure, the size of a grain of barley, in a spoonful of water, and nothing else. No nettle. No quietwort. Nothing to numb.

He drank it, and lay down, and Hyvred held his wrist.

And then he did the stupid thing. The last one. He opened the shutters, the north ones, the ones that faced the valley; and lay there on his pallet with the cold coming in and the thing in the next room coming in with it, faint, eleven miles off, there you are, saying names; and let himself listen.

Not walk. He couldn’t walk. Just listen. As hard as he could. For his own name.

It came. Faint. A long way off. Kei. He felt the front of him start to turn, a hair, the way it had on the road.

“Now,” said Hyvred. Very sharp. “The cold. On the left. At the edge. It’s—“

She stopped.

He felt her hand go still on his wrist.

“It flinched,” said Hyvred.

He didn’t feel anything. Nothing at all. His name in the next room, faint, and then not, and then faint again; nothing he could point to. The knitwort bitter in his mouth.

“The cold,” said Hyvred. “At the edge of you. Where it comes in. It came in, and then it—“ She was quiet for a long time. “It drew back. A hair. Like a finger off something hot. And then it came in again.” She opened her eyes. Gold, with the red mark in them, very close. “It flinched, Kei. One hair. I saw it.”

He lay there.

One hair. With a grain of barley. Not enough to stop anything; not enough to do anything at all, that he could feel. But it hadn’t taken anything from him. He knew who Hyvred was. He knew who he was. He could feel his hands. And the cold at the edge had drawn back. One hair.

Healers close it.

He started to laugh again, and then stopped, because he was going to be sick; and was.

He was sick for two more days. Knitwort was a poison too. Everything was. It was only a dose.

On the second night, toward midnight, with the shutters closed and the brazier low and Hild asleep sitting up in the corner with her staff across her knees, something cold and dry came down off the shelf over the brazier, and across the floor, and up the side of the pallet, and over his hand, and up his arm.

Round his neck.

You taste like you again, said Hisui.

Kei lay very still.

Mostly, said Hisui. A bit bitter. Like the green root. But that’s you being stupid, not you being gone. I can sit on stupid. His tongue went in and out against Kei’s jaw. I’ve been sitting on stupid for three years.

“I missed you,” said Kei.

Obviously, said Hisui. And then, after a long time, quieter: It flinched. I tasted it from the shelf. When the girl said. The thing in the next room. It stopped saying your name for a heartbeat and said it again. A pause. It didn’t like the green root.

“No.”

Do it again.

“I will.”

Smaller, said Hisui. At the edge. Not more. More is what you always do when you’re frightened, and it’s always wrong. He settled. The healers will close it. The girl in Kingsferry said. You can’t. You’re not a healer. You’re a man who knows what’s in a jar. He was quiet. But you can make it flinch. For a heartbeat. So that somebody who can close it has a heartbeat to do it in.

Kei thought about that for a long time.

A heartbeat. That was all. Not an antidote. Not a cure. A heartbeat, in which the open place drew back a hair, and a person was all there behind it; and somebody who knew how, a girl who’d been the patient for twenty-six years, a sister with thirty years of bowls, could reach in and close it.

He didn’t know how to make it last a heartbeat. He’d made it flinch for less than one. He didn’t know if he could make it work on somebody who’d been called and walked eleven miles and stood in a ring in the frost for months. He didn’t know if he’d have time to learn before it came down the pass.

It’s coming, said Hisui, as if he’d heard. The thing in the next room. It’s got louder every day you’ve been in bed. The girl on the tower says twenty-nine. He tasted the air. It tastes different. It’s stopped saying names to the town. It’s saying them to the road.

“What does that mean?”

I don’t know, said Hisui. I’m a snake.

In the corner, without opening her eye, Hild said: “It means it’s getting ready to walk.”

Kei hadn’t known she was awake. Nobody ever knew Hild was awake.

“I was on the Redmarch,” said Hild, “the night before the Brenn crossing. The whole Kharvos line sounded like that across the river. Not shouting. Talking to itself. Getting its feet under it.” She shifted the staff on her knees. “It’s getting its feet under it.”

Nobody said anything for a long time.

“How long?” said Kei.

“Not long,” said Hild. “Go to sleep. Make your root smaller in the morning.” She closed her eye again. “I’ll listen.”

He went to sleep. With a snake round his neck for the first time in eleven days, talking, softly, about the taste of the brazier and the taste of the frost through the shutters and the taste of a bitter green root that something in the next room didn’t like; and in his sleep his hand found the edge of the pallet, and a woman with one eye sat in the corner with a staff across her knees, and listened to the valley for him.
