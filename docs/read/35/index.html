The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 2: Letters

Chapter 35

Holding

2,309 words · 10 min read

Calvin had climbed every mast on the Kingsferry waterfront by the end of the first winter, and by the start of the spring he’d decided that the best one belonged to a salt barge called the Patient Widow.

It wasn’t the tallest. That was a coastal trader from the south with three masts and a captain who threw things at him. But the Patient Widow was tied up permanently at the end of the long jetty, because her owner had died two years ago and nobody had worked out who she belonged to now. And her mast had a crosstree just wide enough to sit on, and from up there you could see the whole river: the quay and the warehouses and the infirmary by the river gate, and the long brown water going south toward the sea and north toward the hills.

And the wind. From up there, you could feel all of the wind.

He sat there most mornings now, with his legs hanging over the crosstree and his coat buttoned to the chin, and let it talk to him.

That was how he thought of it, though he’d never have said so out loud to anyone but the others. The wind talked. Not in words. In pressure, and temperature, and the way it moved over his skin and through his hair and round the mast. It had moods. It changed its mind. And always, a little before it changed, there was a moment, a kind of pause, like a breath being drawn in another room, and he’d feel it coming.

He’d been feeling that since the first month. He’d been calling it since the second: standing on the quay and saying it’s going to turn east in a minute, and then it would. The bargemen had stopped laughing at him by midwinter. Now some of them asked him, before they cast off, which way it would be blowing in an hour.

But since Thea’s letter, it had been different.

You have to write. Put something back into the world. One small line.

He’d tried. For weeks. Up on the crosstree of the Patient Widow, with the whole river spread out below him, he’d felt the wind getting ready to turn, felt that breath being drawn in another room. And instead of just knowing it, he’d tried to ask it. Just a little. Turn now. Turn this way. Please.

Mostly nothing happened.

But twice, in the last week, something had. The wind had been about to turn east, and he’d asked it, politely, to turn north instead. And for about three heartbeats, at the very top of the mast, it had. A little gust, cold off the hills, just round his head and shoulders, the wrong direction entirely. And then it had gone back to doing what it wanted.

His window had gone up three percent each time.

He hadn’t told anyone yet. He was saving it. He wanted to do it a third time first, so he’d be sure.

From the crosstree of the Patient Widow, you could also see the roof of the infirmary. And on the roof of the infirmary, if you knew where to look, you could see the little window of the back room where Rachael worked in the mornings.

That was the other reason he sat up there.

They all watched her. That was the deal, since the night market. Deston, mostly, with his threads, scanning every face in every crowd for the ones with nothing tied to them. Gabe walking her to and from the river gate with his bad ribs and his big shoulders. Dennis and Sabine working the river, asking every bargemaster and ferryman and innkeeper from here to the hills if they’d seen a man in a good coat, a woman in a shawl, a man in a brown coat, people who didn’t quite walk right.

And Calvin, on the mast, watching the wind.

Because the wind had told him, at the night market. It had turned behind Rachael where nobody was looking, cold and wrong, like the air in a cellar. And he’d turned round and seen the man in the brown coat coming, and thrown himself at his knees without thinking, and skinned both of his own to the bone. They’d scabbed over by now. He liked to look at them. They were the bravest thing he’d ever done.

Nothing had come since. Not a breath. Not one wrong turn of the wind in three months.

He didn’t trust it.

“Eight weeks,” said Dennis, that evening, dropping into a chair at the table above the chandler’s with his coat still on. “Eight weeks we’ve been asking. And you know what I’ve found?”

“Nothing,” said Deston.

“Nothing.” Dennis looked personally offended. “Not one person on this whole river saw them come. Not one saw them go. Not one rented them a room or sold them a meal or took their fare across. It’s like they walked out of the river and walked back into it.”

“They did walk back into it,” said Rachael quietly.

“Yes. Well.” Dennis pulled off his gloves. “That’s the problem.”

Sabine came in behind him, shaking rain off her cloak. She’d been out all day too, and she looked tired, which Calvin had never seen before. Sabine didn’t get tired. Sabine got annoyed, and then she got paid.

“Not nothing,” she said. She hung her cloak by the fire. “Not quite.”

They all looked at her.

“Old Haskett. Runs the grain barges down from Fallow’s Reach, up in the western hills.” Sabine sat down heavily. “He came in today, first time since the autumn. Took him three hours and a bottle of the good stuff before he’d talk.” She looked round the table. “There’s villages emptying out up there. Up the western tributaries. Not just Coldwater, over by Thornwall. Here. Our side. Half a dozen of them, he says, since midwinter. People going off into the hills at night. Visiting.” She said the last word the way Hyvred had written it, in the letter from Thornwall: flat, and cold. “And coming back. Some of them. Haskett says some of them come back.”

“Come back?” said Rachael.

“Come back,” said Sabine. “And they’re fine. They’re just the same as they were. Except the dogs won’t go near them.”

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Emrys, in the corner, put down his book. His voice was still hoarse; it had been hoarse since the night market, when he’d thrown the whole of his tier into a single note of light and nearly torn something in his throat doing it. Sister Maud had told him not to sing for a month. He’d been very bad at not singing.

“Someone wearing someone,” he said quietly. “Coming home.”

Rachael burned her hand on the forge two days later.

It was nothing. A stupid accident. She’d gone down to the south wharf to bring Gabe his lunch, the way she did sometimes, because the smith liked her and it got Gabe five minutes off the bellows. Somebody had left a hot bar on the edge of the anvil, and she’d put her hand down on it without looking.

She didn’t scream. Calvin was there; he’d walked her down. She just snatched her hand back and stood there holding it, white-faced, her mouth pressed tight, while Gabe dropped his tongs and swore and the smith came running with a bucket of water.

“It’s fine,” she said. “It’s fine, it’s just a burn, it’s—“

And then she stopped.

She was looking at her hand. At the angry red welt across the palm, already starting to blister. And Calvin watched her face change: the pain going out of it, and something else coming in. Something very focused. Very still.

“Rae?” said Gabe.

“Wait,” said Rachael. “Wait. I want to try something.”

She put her other hand over the burn. Not touching it. Just a finger’s width above it, cupped, the way you’d shelter a candle from the wind. And she closed her eyes, and breathed.

Calvin felt it before he saw it. A change in the air. Not wind; something else. Warmth, steady and gold, the way the infirmary felt when Sister Maud was working.

Under Rachael’s cupped hand, the light came up.

Faint. Very faint. Gold, like the glow Hyvred had said she carried where the Advisor had touched her wrist on the first day. It sat in the space between her two palms for a heartbeat. Two. And Calvin, watching, saw the angry red edge of the burn go pink. Just at one end. Just a little. As if it had been hours old instead of moments.

Then the light went out.

Rachael opened her eyes and looked at her hand.

Most of the burn was still there: red and raw and blistering. But one end of it, the bit closest to her wrist, had faded to pink. Healing. Not healed. But healing, much faster than any burn should.

“I wrote it,” she whispered. “Like Thea said. I found the place where it hurt and I wrote… I wrote close. That’s all. Just close.” She was laughing, and crying a bit, and holding her hand out in front of her as if it belonged to someone else. “It only lasted a second. A second. But it closed, Gabe, a bit of it closed, I did that—“

Gabe didn’t say anything. He just put his big forge-blackened arms round her very carefully, avoiding the hand, and held on.

The smith stood there with his bucket of water, looking at the two of them, and then at Calvin.

“Is that,” he said slowly, “what I think it is?”

“Yeah,” said Calvin. He found he was grinning so hard it hurt. “Yeah. I think it is.”

They did their numbers that night like always, round the table above the chandler’s, with the rain on the windows.

“Thirty-one,” said Rachael, and held up her bandaged hand like a trophy. “Up four. From one second.”

“Twenty-six,” said Deston. “Still no threadless faces. Not one, in three months. I’ve looked at thousands.” He rubbed his eyes. “Either they’ve gone, or they’ve got better at hiding.”

“Twenty,” said Gabe. “Smith says I can make my own blade by summer.”

“Twenty-three,” said Dennis, “and I’d like it noted that I’m the only one of us who’s made a profit this quarter.”

They all looked at Calvin.

He’d been saving it. He’d wanted to do it a third time first, to be sure. But Rachael had done hers, and held up her bandaged hand, and laughed, and he couldn’t hold it anymore.

“Twenty-six,” he said. “And I made the wind turn. Twice. Just for a second. Just round my head, up on the Patient Widow. I asked it to go north instead of east, and it did.”

There was a moment of silence. Then Dennis said, “Show-off,” and Rachael threw a bread roll at him, and everyone was laughing.

Calvin laughed too. But under it, he was thinking.

He’d known Declan for a year. Not even. He’d met him on the trip to Malacca, the summer before. An hour after they’d met, Declan had walked him into his kitchen and pointed at a bad kettle and said this is the kettle, like it was a trophy. And Calvin had thought: I like this guy. He’d thought there’d be more time. More trips. More kettles.

There hadn’t been.

And now here he was, a year and a world away, making the wind turn. And the person he most wanted to tell, after this table, the person who’d have laughed hardest and said something completely ridiculous and finished it with trust, wasn’t anywhere.

“He’d have loved it,” said Gabe quietly, from across the table, as if he’d heard. “Declan. The wind thing. He’d have made you do it a hundred times.”

Calvin looked at him. Gabe had known Declan longer than anyone at this table. Since they were kids, in the back row of a choir. And he was smiling, a little sadly, the way he always did when he said the name.

“Yeah,” said Calvin. “Yeah. I think he would have.”

He went back up the mast the next morning, early, before anyone else was awake.

The river was grey and quiet, mist lying on it like breath on glass. The Patient Widow creaked under him. Far off to the west, the hills were just coming out of the dark, a long dim line under a pale sky. Somewhere up there, up the western tributaries, villages were emptying, and people were going visiting at night, and some of them were coming home. And the dogs wouldn’t go near them.

He sat on the crosstree and let the wind talk to him.

It was coming off the hills this morning. From the west. Cold and clean and smelling of snow, the way it should. Nothing wrong in it. No cellar-air. No breath being drawn in another room.

Not yet.

He sat there and listened to it for a long time. Then, very carefully, the way you’d ask a stranger for directions, he asked it to turn. Just a little. Just for a moment.

North, he asked. Toward the lake country. Toward wherever the river came from.

For three heartbeats, at the very top of the mast, it did.

And then it went back to the west, and Calvin sat there grinning in the cold with his scabbed knees and his wind-tangled hair, thinking: three times. That’s three. I’m sure now.

Down on the quay, a bargeman looked up at the sky, frowning at the little gust that had come from the wrong direction for no reason at all. Then he shrugged, and went back to his ropes.
