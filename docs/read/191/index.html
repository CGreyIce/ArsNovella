The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 7: Myths

Chapter 191

The White Serpent

4,505 words · 20 min read

Tell them to get up, said Suzu. Tell them he’s mine.

Yan had said it. Out loud, in the road, to Rhea, because Rhea had asked. He’d heard his own voice say it and thought, quite clearly, that’s the first thing she’s ever said to anybody but me that I haven’t made polite.

The three of them didn’t get up.

They knelt in the snow in the west gate of Cinderhold with their foreheads nearly on the road and their pale staves flat beside them, and didn’t move, and Yan stood over them with the scythe on his shoulder and the cold coming up through his boots and Suzu’s head lifted beside his face, and didn’t know what to do.

He’d never been knelt to. Nobody had. Not in two worlds. On Earth people had sent him hearts in a chat. Here, they’d stepped round him in the street, at first, because of the snake, and then they’d stopped stepping round him and started nodding, and in the autumn, after the green, the old soldiers on the wall had begun to touch their foreheads when he went by, the way you’d touch your forehead to a chaplain. Not to him. To the snake, he’d always thought. To the white.

Nobody had ever knelt in a road.

Tell them, said Suzu again. Cool and low and very slightly impatient, in his head, the way she said things when he was being slow. They will not get up for the leaf-man’s snake or the loud one with the sword. They will get up for you. Tell them.

“Please get up,” said Yan.

He said it the way he said everything. Slowly. In English. Which they’d hear in their own tongue, he supposed, the way the kingdom heard him in its own. His window did that. He’d never thought about what it would sound like to somebody whose language was older than the kingdom’s.

The old woman’s head came up.

Not all the way. Enough to look at him. With her winter eyes, from the snow, with the silver rings in her long swept-back ears catching the last of the light.

“You hear her,” she said.

In her own tongue. He heard it as English. Low and singing and slow, like water going over stones. He heard every word.

“Every day,” said Yan. “Since the morning I woke up here.”

The old woman looked at him for a long time.

Then she said something to the other two. One word. And all three of them got up out of the snow at once, the way they’d gone down: together, without looking at each other, the long grey lengths of them unfolding in the road like herons. And picked up their staves. And stood in front of him.

“Then you are the bound one,” said the old woman. “We did not know if there would be one. It has been a very long time.”

Aldous brought them to the chapel.

There wasn’t anywhere else. The commander had come down from the commander’s room by then, red in the face, saying regulations, and Aldous had looked at him over the top of his spectacles and said they’ll want a fire and they won’t want stairs, and the commander had looked at the three tall grey people standing in his gate with their staves and stopped saying regulations and gone back up.

So the chapel. Aldous’s, the small stone one by the infirmary, where he’d said the old words over every soldier who’d died at Cinderhold for nine years, and kept his book, and the folded white cloths in a chest by the wall. There was a fire. There were benches. There was the altar, which wasn’t really an altar, just a long stone table at the end with a candle on it and nothing else, because Aldous said the gods of Aurelan didn’t need much and the dead needed less.

The three of them stood inside the door and looked at it.

At the white cloths, folded in the chest, with the lid up. Yan saw the old woman’s eyes go to them and stay. Then to the candle. Then to Aldous, with his book, standing by the fire with his hands still not quite steady.

“Sit,” said Aldous. In the kingdom’s tongue. He gestured at the benches. “Please. You’ve walked a long way.”

The young one, the one who’d arm-wrestled Rhea and won, said it again in the singing tongue, for the old woman. Badly, Yan thought; he could hear it was badly, the way you can hear a man speak your language with the wrong stresses. The old woman listened. Then she inclined her head to Aldous, very slightly, and sat down on the nearest bench, and laid her stave across her knees.

The other two didn’t sit. They stood behind her. One either side.

Group 3 came in after. All of them. Salty, too big for the door, ducking. Wendy, with her bow still in her hand, because she’d forgotten to put it down. Niko, quiet, at the back, against the wall. Rhea, who’d left the gate to Teague and come in red-faced and silent, which was the most frightening thing Yan had seen all day. And Maigo. Who’d stopped crying, mostly, and stood very close to the girl with the pale hair, the one called Liraun, as if he’d decided she was his, the way he decided things.

Liraun didn’t seem to mind.

Yan sat down on the bench across the fire from the old woman, with the scythe leaning against the wall behind him, and Suzu round his neck.

Nobody said anything for a while.

He looked at their souls.

He hadn’t meant to. It came up on its own now, the other sight, since the green. He’d look at somebody and see them twice: the face, and the candle under it. Warm or cold, bright or pale. And now the colour. Green, mostly, at Cinderhold, the old ones on the wall green toward each other and toward the fort and toward a man with a rod who’d gone west. White, in Aldous. Grey, in the dying hens.

He looked at the three across the fire and didn’t have a name for what he saw.

Not green. Not white. Not any colour he’d seen in a soul. The nearest he could get, afterwards, when Wendy asked, was silver. Old silver. The kind on a candlestick in a house where somebody’s grandmother had polished it every week for fifty years, gone soft and grey at the edges and still, under the grey, if you turned it to the light, bright. Three candles of it. Burning very slowly. Slower than any candle he’d ever seen. The way a fire burns that somebody banked a long time ago and means to keep all winter.

And all three of them leaning.

Toward him. Not toward him. Toward the white snake round his neck. All three. The way the old ones’ souls leaned toward the fort, and Alea’s had leaned toward a grave she’d never seen. The way Suzu’s leaned toward him.

Like a plant on a windowsill. Toward the light.

They remember, said Suzu. Very quietly. Not me. Not yet. What I am.

“What are you?” said Yan. Under his breath. Not meaning to.

The old woman across the fire looked up.

She’d heard him. Of course she had; his window carried it to her. She looked at him, and then at the snake, and then at him again, and for the first time since the gate her long still face did something. Not a smile. The thing a face does when it’s about to tell somebody something they should already know.

“She has not told you,” said the old woman.

“She tells me everything,” said Yan.

Not this, said Suzu.

“Not this,” said Yan.

The old woman was quiet for a long moment.

“Then I will tell you what we call her,” she said. “And she can tell you if it is wrong.”

“Vael,” said the old woman.

It went through the chapel. Not loud. She said it the way you’d say a name in a church: low, with the weight on the front of it, so that the end of it went away like breath. And at the fire, Aldous put out his hand to the end of the bench and held on to it.

Yan heard it in English. That was the strange thing. His window took the word and gave it to him in his own tongue, the way it took everything. And it gave it to him as two words.

The Carrier.

“In our tongue,” said the old woman, “the white that carries. The one who takes the dying across. Not into anything. Across.” She looked at the folded white cloths in the chest by the wall. “When a thing dies, it goes out. A light. You have seen it, I think, bound one. Going out of the beasts she feeds on, small and grey. It goes out, and it looks for somewhere to go. And most of the time it finds nowhere, and thins, and is gone, and that is the end of it.” She was quiet. “The Vael carries them. So that they are not lost. So that they arrive.”

“Arrive where?” said Wendy. From the back. Very quietly. The skeptic.

The old woman turned her head and looked at her.

“We do not know,” she said. “Nobody has ever known. Not even the Vael, I think. She does not ask where. She only carries.” She looked back at the snake. “That is what she is. That is what she was, before. Before your people. Before ours, perhaps. The white, that carries.”

Before, said Suzu, in Yan’s head. Quietly. Not to him. To herself.

Yan didn’t say anything. His hand had come up and was resting on her, on the white coil over his chest, very lightly. He felt her under his hand. Cool. Dry. Still. The way she’d been the first morning on the floor of the hall.

There you are, she’d said, that morning. Yun.

As if she’d been looking for him. A long time.

“Your rite,” said the old woman. To Aldous.

The old chaplain looked up.

“The white cloth,” she said. “Over the dead. And the words.” She was looking at the chest by the wall. “We lay white over them, for the Vael, who carries. That is the last line. Is it not.”

Aldous’s mouth opened. Nothing came out.

“It is ours,” said the old woman. Not unkindly. “It was ours, a long time ago, before your kingdom. When your people and mine lived nearer. We laid white over our dead so the Vael would see them in the dark and come. Your people saw us do it. They took the rite.” She inclined her head very slightly. “We did not mind. It is a good rite. It does no harm to say it, even when she is not there.”

“Soul Eater,” said Aldous. It came out of him in a whisper. “My master said it meant Soul Eater. And his master. All the way back. I’ve said it over eleven hundred people. For the Soul Eater, who carries.”

The old woman was quiet for a moment.

“Your word is wrong,” she said. “She does not eat. Eating is for keeping. She keeps nothing.” She looked at the snake. “She carries.”

Aldous put his hand over his eyes.

Yan watched him do it. The big slow gentle man who’d made a two-fingered sign at a white snake round his throat on the first day in the castle, and every day for a month after, warding. Who’d said, the night of the green, on a fallen tree in the moonlight, that’s not something that eats, son. That’s something that carries. Who’d been right, and hadn’t known he was right, and had said a wrong word over eleven hundred people for thirty years because his master had.

Tell him he was not wrong, said Suzu.

Yan looked down at her.

Tell him, said Suzu. The old man. He was not wrong. He said the wrong word with the right meaning. That is better than the other way round. Tell him I listened. Every time. I heard him.

“She says you weren’t wrong,” said Yan. To Aldous. Out loud. In the chapel. “She says you said the wrong word with the right meaning, and that’s better than the other way round.” He swallowed. “She says she listened. Every time. She heard you.”

Aldous didn’t take his hand away from his eyes.

But his shoulders went. All the way.

The young one, Oen, had come round the end of the bench.

Yan hadn’t seen him move. They moved like that, all three of them, as if the air didn’t quite notice them go. He was crouching at the end of the bench now, at Yan’s eye level, with his pale stave across his knees and his long ears swept back and his face, which was younger than the old woman’s and not young at all, very close.

Looking at Suzu.

He’d been the one who smiled at the gate. Who’d arm-wrestled Rhea and laughed, very slightly, at one corner. He wasn’t smiling now. He was looking at the white snake round Yan’s neck the way Yan had seen children in Valcrest look at the king. At something they’d been told about all their lives and never thought they’d see.

“May I,” said Oen. In the singing tongue. Very quietly. To Yan, not to her.

Yan didn’t know what he was asking.

Tell him he may look, said Suzu. Tell him he may not touch.

“She says you can look,” said Yan. “She says you can’t touch.”

Oen didn’t move. He didn’t reach out. He stayed crouched at the end of the bench with his hands on his stave and looked, for a long time, at the snake.

Tell him, said Suzu, after a while. Something had changed in her voice. Not warm; Suzu was never warm to anybody but him. But not cold, either. Tell him I remember the smell of their forest.

Yan went still.

He didn’t pass it on. Not at once. He sat on the bench with his hand on her and the words in his head and didn’t say them, because he’d heard what was in them, and it wasn’t anything she’d ever said to him before. Remember. She’d come through the floor of the hall with him, two years and a half ago. On Earth she’d been a drawing. A line of white on a model, round his shoulders, that did a little animation when he laughed.

You couldn’t remember a forest you’d never been in.

“Suzu,” he said. Under his breath. “What forest?”

She didn’t answer.

“Suzu.”

Not yet, Yun.

It was the second time she’d said it. The first had been in the snow on the wall, three months ago, about the south. He’d thought, then, that she was keeping something from him. He thought, now, sitting in a chapel at Cinderhold with three people out of a song kneeling in his memory in the snow, that she was keeping something from both of them. From him, and from herself.

As if there were a door in her she hadn’t opened yet either.

“She says,” said Yan, slowly, to Oen, “she remembers the smell of your forest.”

Oen closed his eyes.

He knelt there, at the end of the bench, with his eyes shut. And then he said something, very softly, in the singing tongue, that Yan’s window gave him as oh, she remembers, and put his long thin hand flat on his own chest. Over his heart. The way the old soldiers had, on the wall, in the autumn, for a man who looked up.

The old woman said it at the end of the evening.

The fire had burned down. Aldous had brought water, which they drank, and bread, which they looked at politely and didn’t eat, and dried fish, which they ate, all of it, very fast, the way Salty ate pies, and Salty had watched them with enormous approval. Maigo had fallen asleep on the end of the bench against the girl with the pale hair, Liraun, who’d let him, and put her long hand on his antler again, and not moved it for an hour. Wendy had asked four questions, all of them careful, all of them sharp, and been answered three times. Rhea had asked when the arm-wrestle was, and been told tomorrow, and gone quiet again.

They hadn’t said where they’d come from. Not exactly. South, the old woman had said. Past where your maps stop. A forest your people do not go into. We have not come out of it in a long time. And when Wendy asked how long, the old woman had looked at her with her winter eyes and said since before your kingdom had a name, and Wendy had written something down very fast and then sat looking at it.

They’d said why. We heard the song.

The summoned brought an elf with ears like leaves.

“We knew it was wrong,” the old woman had said. “When it came to us. Down the south road, in a trader’s mouth, at the edge of the forest where we sometimes listen. We knew none of ours had gone. We count.” She’d looked at Yan’s ears and the corner of her mouth had moved. “And a treant. The old kind are asleep. We knew that too.” She looked at Maigo, asleep against Liraun. “So the song was wrong. And we asked ourselves why a song would be wrong in that way. Elf, and tree, and the summoned.” She’d looked at the snake. “And one of us had dreamed of white. For a year. Every night.”

Liraun hadn’t looked up. She’d kept her hand on Maigo’s antler.

“So we came,” the old woman had said. “Not for an elf. Not for a tree.” She was quiet. “For the white.”

They are coming for me, Suzu had told Hisui, through a cord, across the kingdom, in the snow. Not for him.

She’d been right. She was always right. It was, Yan thought, the most annoying thing about her, and he’d thought so for years.

And then, at the end of the evening, with the fire down to red and Maigo asleep and Aldous sitting with his book closed on his knee and his face still wet, the old woman looked at Yan across the coals for a long time.

“You are tired,” she said.

Yan didn’t say anything.

“Bound one,” said the old woman. Gently, now. For the first time all evening, gently. “You are tired. I can see it. In the light of you. You burn bright, and green, and you are tired under it, the way a lamp is tired when the oil is low.” She looked at the snake. “She has not told you this either.”

Enough, said Suzu.

Yan felt it go through her. Down the cord, into his chest. Cold. Not hunger-cold, not threadless-cold. The other kind. The kind she’d gone on the red when the scouts came at the cart. Every scale of her tightening round his neck.

“The Vael takes nothing for herself,” said the old woman. Quietly. Steadily. Not looking at the snake now. At him. “That is what we know. That is what our songs say, which are older than yours and less wrong. She takes nothing. She carries. And the one who carries her—“

ENOUGH.

Yan had never heard her shout.

Not in two years and a half. Not on the red, not at the grave, not on the floor of the hall. Suzu didn’t shout. She was cool and low and patient and very slightly impatient and she said exactly what she meant in exactly as few words as it took. She’d never once raised her voice in his head.

She shouted it. And he heard it. And so, he understood, did the three across the fire. Not the words. The thing under the words. All three of them went still at once, the way they’d gone still in the gate. The old woman’s mouth closed.

The old woman looked at the snake for a long moment.

Then she inclined her head. Very slightly. The way you’d bow to somebody who’d told you, quite correctly, that you’d said too much in their house.

“It is hers to tell,” she said. To Yan. “Not mine. I am sorry, Vael.”

Tell her, said Suzu, in his head. Not shouting now. Low, and cold, and perfectly clear. Tell her it is not my name.

Yan sat on the bench in the chapel at Cinderhold with his hand on a white snake who’d just shouted in his head for the first time in his life, and a sentence half said hanging in the air in front of him. And the one who carries her. He knew how it ended. He didn’t know the words. He knew how it ended the way you know how a song ends when somebody stops singing it on the last line.

He didn’t ask.

“She says,” said Yan. Slowly. To the old woman. “She says it’s not her name.”

The old woman looked at him.

Tell her, said Suzu. They will call me by an old name. It is not my name. My name is the one he gave me.

“She says you’ll call her by an old name,” said Yan. “She says it isn’t hers.” He swallowed. “She says her name’s the one I gave her.”

There was a long silence in the chapel.

Then the old woman looked at the white snake round his neck. And said, very carefully, in her own singing tongue, the way you’d say a word in a language you’d only just learned and wanted to get right:

“Suzu.”

Suzu put her head back down on Yan’s collarbone.

Better, she said.

They slept in the chapel. The three of them. Not on the benches; on the floor, in front of the fire, in their lichen coats, the way Yan imagined deer slept. Aldous gave them blankets and they folded them very neatly and put them to one side and didn’t use them. Rhea put two soldiers on the chapel door, and then took them off again, because the old woman had looked at the soldiers for a long moment and then at Rhea, and Rhea had gone red and sent them away. Maigo wouldn’t leave. He slept on the end of the bench, under three of Aldous’s white cloths, which Aldous said was all right, because he wasn’t dead.

Yan went up on the wall.

He couldn’t sleep. That was strange, too. He’d slept, every night since the green, an hour longer than he used to. Wendy knew; Wendy kept a column. Maigo knew; Maigo noticed things on Thursdays. Kei had a slate, on a shelf, in a little room behind a kitchen in Thornwall, with his name on it. He slept, every night now, like a man who’d walked a long way.

He couldn’t sleep tonight.

He stood at the parapet of the north wall, a little way along from the gap in the coping where the stone had cracked under two hands, with the scythe leaning beside him and Suzu round his neck, and looked south. At the downs. White, going away in their long low folds into the dark, under a sky with the stars coming out. And on the road over the first hill, under the snow, a line of something that only Maigo could feel, going all the way back south to the edge of the world.

Green.

“Suzu,” he said.

She didn’t answer.

“What did she mean,” said Yan. “The one who carries her.” He was quiet. “She was going to say I pay. Wasn’t she.”

Nothing.

“Is it the sleeping?” said Yan. “Is that it? Is that what I’m paying?”

Nothing. Just the cold, and the stars, and the weight of her round his neck, cool and dry and still.

“Suzu.”

Not yet, Yun.

He’d known she’d say it. He stood there with his hands on the cold stone of the parapet and let it sit in him, the way you let a stone sit in your hand when somebody’s given you one and you don’t know yet what it’s for.

Then she said something else. Very quietly. So quietly he nearly didn’t hear it, under the wind.

I will tell you, said Suzu. When I know how to say it so you will not be frightened. I do not know how yet. I have never had to. A pause. I have never had anyone I did not want frightened.

Yan didn’t say anything for a long time.

He put his hand up and rested it on her. On the white coil over his heart. And felt the cord between them, warm, from his chest to wherever she was, the way he’d felt it every hour of every day since the floor of the hall.

“Okay,” said Yan.

That was all. It was what he said. It was what he’d always said. To Salty, at the gate, when the man left. To the channel, at the end of the night. Okay. It meant I heard you. It meant I’m still here. It meant I’ll wait.

Okay, said Suzu.

She’d never said it before. Not once. He’d said it to her a thousand times.

She said it back.

He wrote it to Kei before he went down.

Not in the channel. On the strip, round the knot of the green-and-white cord in his coat, by the light of the gatehouse lamp. In small careful English. The very small channel for two people. And two snakes.

They came. Three. They call her Vael. The Carrier. The rite’s theirs. Aldous’s word was wrong.

He held the cord. The letters went.

They said I’m tired. They started to say why. She shouted. She’s never shouted.

He held it again.

She says she’ll tell me when she knows how to say it so I won’t be frightened.

He held the cord a long time, after that, on the wall in the cold, before it went warm in his hand. Somebody a kingdom away, in a little room behind a kitchen, holding the other half.

The strip came back. Kei’s hand. Small and square and careful. Four words.

I’m here tomorrow too.

Yan read it twice. Then he put the cord back in his coat, against his chest, beside the snake, and went down off the wall, and slept.

Not an hour longer, that night. Not an hour shorter. Just the way he always had, before. As if somebody had, very briefly, taken something off him and was holding it, and would give it back in the morning, and wanted him to have one night without it first.
