The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 10: The Sword's Country

Chapter 272

When the Wood Dries

5,441 words · 24 min read

Twenty-three.

She counted them at dawn from the north tower, the way she had every dawn and every dusk since the snow: a hundred and twelve steps up, the leads white with frost, her hands in her sleeves and her back against the parapet and her eyes open on a valley eleven miles off that she could have drawn with her eyes shut. Thirteen rings of people standing in a field under snow, so still the snow lay on their shoulders. The curve on the left of them, twenty-three holes the size of people, with the snow not lying on them at all. And at the top of everything, at the foot of a pass that was shut, a round place where the snow didn’t settle, facing the town.

Twenty-three. The same. It had been the same for twenty-two days.

She said it to Corvin, who was waiting at the top of the stair with his board and his chalk and his nose red with cold, and he wrote it down under yesterday’s, and the day before’s, a column of 23s going down the board like stitches. Then he looked at her over the top of it, the way he did every morning, and didn’t say anything, the way he didn’t every morning, and went down.

She stayed.

She always stayed, for a bit. Not to look. She’d counted; she didn’t need to look. She stayed because there wasn’t anywhere to go that the valley wasn’t. That was the thing nobody could see, and she’d stopped trying to tell them. It wasn’t up here. It wasn’t eleven miles off, at the top of a white valley at the foot of the Greyspine. It was everywhere. It lay over everything like a reflection lies over a window: over the town below her, the roofs and the smoke and the long street; over the faces of the people she talked to; over the inside of her own eyelids when she shut them, and the inside of her sleep when she slept, which wasn’t often. She’d look at Bee’s face across the long room and see, under it, through it, a ring of people standing in the snow. She’d look at a bowl of Malcolm’s porridge and see the absence sitting at the bottom of it like a thumbprint.

It’s the eye, Kei had said, on the well, the second day. Hisui says it’s stuck open. Like a door that’s swelled in the wet.

It’ll go down, Hisui had said. When the wood dries. It always does.

Twenty-two days. The wood hadn’t dried.

Thornwall had gone into the winter the way a man goes into a long sleep after a fever: slowly, and all at once.

The pass was shut. That was the first thing, and it was the thing that let everyone else breathe. The snow had come down on the last dawn and kept coming for three days, and when it stopped, the swept road that had run north out of the gate and up the valley for a year and more, swept clean by nobody, by the feet of people who’d walked it in the night, was gone under four feet of white like every other road; and beyond it the switchbacks up to the pass were gone, and the pass itself was a white notch in a white wall, and nothing was going to come down it until the spring. Fenwick said so. Brannoch said so. Hild, who could hear the mountains, said the mountains had gone quiet, the way a dog goes quiet when it’s lain down for the night.

So the town lay down too.

The bars came off the doors, the way the king had said in the square. The children came out of the granary and went back to their own beds, and then, in the second week, back to the schoolroom, where the schoolmaster had chalked the names of the seven valley dead on the board in a corner and left them there. The Bell went back to being an inn with a healers’ room at the back instead of the other way round. Agnes banked her forge. The long room, where forty people had held hands every night for fifteen nights against something that said their names, went back to being a long room: a place where the Watch ate, and Corvin kept his maps, and Group 5 and Group 6 and Petra slept on pallets along the north wall because none of them had wanted to go anywhere else.

Hyvred could see it all happening. That was the trouble. She could see the town’s lights settling, night by night, the way a pond settles after somebody’s thrown a stone: the leaning at the six places easing a little, not gone, never gone, but less; the grey in Wyn Alder going a shade less grey; Joss’s light at the farrier’s door going out each night at a sensible hour instead of burning till dawn. She could see the town getting better. She could see it, and under it, over it, through it, she could see the valley; and she didn’t know how to tell anyone that she was glad and couldn’t feel it, because there was a white field with people standing in it between her and everything she was glad about.

The king hadn’t gone home.

He’d said he would, in the square, on the day of the count. Spring, he’d said; I’ll go home in the spring and tell the Council to its face. Then the snow had come, and the pass had shut, and the road south over the downs had gone hard as iron, and he’d stayed anyway.

He sat in Agnes’s forge, mostly.

Agnes had banked the big hearth for the winter, the way you did, with the fire pushed back and covered and breathing slowly under its ash, so that it would come up in a quarter of an hour any morning she wanted it and wouldn’t eat a cartload of coal a week while she didn’t. The king sat in front of it in the evenings in her big chair, the one they’d carried out of the Bell for the count, with his hands on his knees and his feet to the warm stones, and made small things. Hinges. A new pin for the granary door. A latch for the Pells’ back gate, which had always stuck. He didn’t ask anyone’s leave. He’d bring the fire up a little, and put a bit of bar in it, and sit, and when it was ready he’d take it out and do something to it on the small anvil, three or four blows, very precise, and put it back, and sit again.

Kestra stood by the door. She always stood by the door.

Hyvred went and sat with him most evenings. She didn’t know why, at first. She’d go down the tower stair at dusk after the count and find her feet going down the long street instead of back to the long room, and in at Agnes’s side door, and onto the bench by the cold quench trough; and the king would look up from the fire and nod, and not say anything, and she wouldn’t either.

It was the light, she thought, after a week. The king’s light was the one thing in Thornwall she could look at and see only it. Everybody else had the valley over them. But the king was a forge with both doors open; he was so bright, close up, that the reflection on the window couldn’t get a grip on him. She could sit on the bench and look at a big plain grey man of sixty-six with soot on his knuckles making a latch for somebody’s gate, and see only that.

“You’re not sleeping,” he said, the fifth night.

“I sleep.”

“Sitting up.”

She didn’t say anything. Petra must have told him. Petra told him things; she’d come at the start of the winter and sat on a cask and said them, in that flat grey voice, and gone away again.

“I did that,” said the king. He turned the bit of bar in the fire. “After the Redmarch. The first winter. Sat up in a chair by the bed every night with my sword across my knees, waiting for it to come back over the wall.” He took the bar out, looked at it, put it back. “Kestra said I’d wear a groove in the floor.”

“Did it stop?”

“It did.”

“How?”

He thought about it, honestly, the way he thought about everything. She could see him do it: his light going round the question like a smith walking round a bar to see where to strike.

“I don’t remember,” he said at last. “That’s the truth. One night I was in the chair, and one night I was in the bed, and I don’t remember the night in between.” He looked at her. “That’s not much use to you.”

“No.”

“No. I’m sorry.” He went back to the fire.

Kei sat with her on the well the next noon, with Hisui round his neck and his notebook on his knee. He had a page in it for her now. He had a page for everybody; she’d seen it over his shoulder. HYVRED, at the top, in capitals, and under it a column of days and a word for each. Same. Same. Same, slept two hours. Same. Ate (Malcolm).

“Hisui wants to know if you’re trying to make it go down,” Kei said.

“Yes.”

“How?”

She showed him. She’d been doing it for a week. She shut her eyes and pushed. Not with her hands; with whatever it was in her that looked. She pushed the valley away, the way you’d push a door shut against a wind, and held it, and pushed, and held.

It came brighter.

It always did. That was the thing. The harder she pushed, the brighter it came: the rings sharper, the holes blacker, the round place at the top of the valley more there, until she could see the frost on the shoulders of the people in the third ring and count the shawls. She stopped pushing and opened her eyes and the valley lay over Kei’s face, over his notebook, over the white snake round his neck, sharper than it had been when she started.

“It does that,” she said.

Kei listened to his collar for a moment.

“He says of course it does,” said Kei. “He says you’re pushing a door that’s swelled. You push a swelled door, it sticks worse.” He listened again. “He says the wood doesn’t dry because you push it. It dries because you stop putting water on it.”

“I’m not putting water on it.”

Kei didn’t say anything. Hisui didn’t either, for a while. Then Kei said, carefully, the way he said things he wasn’t sure Hisui wanted said out loud: “He says what’s the water.”

She didn’t know.

She went up the tower at dusk and counted, and it was twenty-three, and she said so to Corvin; and she stayed, and looked, and thought about it. What’s the water. What was she doing, every day, that kept the wood wet.

Looking.

That was the stupid, obvious answer, and it took her until the stars came out to see it, because it was the thing she’d done every waking hour of her life since a stone hall three years ago when she’d lain on her back on the floor and seen a whole ceiling for the first time, all of it, edge to edge, and cried. She looked. It was what she was. A girl who’d seen the world through a hole all her life, and then didn’t have to. She’d never once, since the hall, not looked at something she could see. Why would you? Why would anyone who’d lived in a tunnel ever close their eyes again?

And the valley was the thing she could see. The thing nobody else could. The thing that, if she didn’t watch it, would move, and nobody would know until it was in the long street.

So she watched it. Every minute. Asleep. Sitting up.

That was the water.

“You can’t stop,” said Hild.

She’d found Hyvred on the parapet with her knees up, an hour after the count, and sat down beside her on the frozen leads without asking, the way she did, with the great sword across her lap and her one eye on the dark and her good ear to the north.

“That’s what I said,” said Hyvred.

“No. I mean you’re right. You can’t.” Hild’s breath went up white. “Somebody has to watch it. It’s not a silly thing to want. It’s the only thing that matters about it. If it moves and nobody sees, there’s a long street and nobody in front of it.” She was quiet. “That’s not the water. Wanting it watched isn’t the water.”

“Then what—“

“Wanting it watched by you.”

Hyvred didn’t say anything.

“I lost the eye on the Redmarch,” said Hild. “You know that. The morning after the Brenn crossing, a thing on the bank with too many arms. I’ve told you.” She turned the sword over on her knees. “I didn’t look east for a year, after. I’ve told you that too. Not looking was worse.” She stopped. “I didn’t tell you the other half.”

“What other half?”

“The year after. When I could look again.” Hild’s eye was on the dark. “I couldn’t stop. I sat on the wall at Cinderhold every night for a winter with the eye I had left on the red, and I didn’t sleep, and I didn’t come down, because the last time I’d stopped looking a thing with too many arms had come up the bank while I was turned away. And I was the only one on that wall who’d seen what it looked like. So I watched.” She was quiet a while. “The old commander had the fort, then. Not this one’s; the one before. He came up one night in the snow and stood behind me for a long time, and then he said Hild, who’s on watch?, and I said I am, and he said no. Fenner is. You were relieved at the second bell.” Something moved at the corner of her mouth. “I didn’t know there was a watch. I’d stopped knowing there was anybody on the wall but me.”

“What did you do?”

“Went down. Because it was an order.” Hild shrugged. “Slept two days. And when I came back up, the red was still there, and the wall was still there, and Fenner was on it. Fenner, who couldn’t see in the dark and talked to himself and was afraid of owls. Fenner had watched it for two days and nothing had come.” She turned her head and looked at Hyvred with the eye she had. “You stand a watch. That’s what it is. You stand it, and then somebody relieves you, and you go down. It isn’t less yours because you’re not on it. It’s yours because you go down and come back. The ones who never go down aren’t watching any more. They’re just awake.”

Hyvred looked at the valley. At the rings in the snow, and the holes, and the thumbprint at the top of it all, lying over Hild’s face like a reflection on a window.

“There isn’t anyone,” she said. “Nobody else can see it.”

“No,” said Hild. “Not the way you can.”

She got up, with the sword, the way old soldiers get up, all at once and then slowly, and stood for a moment on the leads with her good ear to the north.

“But you lot always find a way round,” she said. “That’s the thing I’ve learned about you, in three years. Something can’t be done the way it’s done, and one of you finds a way round it that nobody here would’ve thought of.” She went to the trapdoor. “Ask the boy with the lights.”

John had been thinking about it already. That was the thing about John; he thought about things for a long time without saying anything, and then said them all at once, slightly too fast, as if he’d been holding his breath.

“It holds while it’s true,” he said.

They were in the long room, at the end of the north wall where Group 6 had their pallets, with the lamps low and most of the Watch asleep and Azac in the doorway narrating softly to nobody in particular, the way he did every night now. Hyvred’s awake. John’s talking. Malcolm’s writing it down. Malcolm was. He had his book open on his knee and his pencil going.

“The step,” said John. “In the snow. The king, and Declan, and everybody. I sent it at dusk and it held all night, because it was true all night. Nobody moved. And then he stood up, a bit before dawn, and it went out, because it wasn’t true any more.” He was turning a mote round his finger, a little point of light no bigger than a firefly, the way he did when he was thinking. “Every picture I ever sent before that went out in a heartbeat, because they were pictures of things happening. And now’s a heartbeat long. But if it’s a picture of something that isn’t happening—“

“It holds,” said Malcolm, writing.

“It holds as long as it isn’t happening.” John looked at Hyvred. “The valley isn’t happening. Is it. Not now. It’s just sitting there.”

“It’s just sitting there,” said Hyvred.

“So if I sent a picture of it,” said John, “from the tower, the way it is now, with the snow on it and the rings and the twenty-three and the thing at the top, sitting. It’d hold.” He swallowed. “All winter, maybe. As long as nothing moved.” He stopped turning the mote. “And the moment something moved, it’d go out.”

Nobody said anything.

Malcolm had stopped writing. He looked at his own page for a long moment, and then turned the book round so that Hyvred could see it, in the lamplight, in his small square capitals.

IF the picture holds while it’s true THEN it goes out when it isn’t.

“It’s a bell,” said Rui Xiang, from his pallet, where Hyvred had thought he was asleep. He wasn’t; she could see the cup of him from here, the hard little edge round the light, sitting up against the wall in the dark. “Like the ones on a shop door. You don’t have to watch the door. It rings.”

“In every window,” said Xav. He was sitting with his back against the end of Drew’s pallet, the way he’d sat every night since the snow; Drew was asleep, for once, with his ribs bound and his mouth open. “All of ours. Everybody here. And you, Hyvred.” He looked at her. “If you want.”

She didn’t say yes. She couldn’t, for a moment. She looked at the five of them, and at Azac in the doorway, and at the long room with its low lamps and the sleeping Watch, and the valley lay over all of it like a reflection on a window, the way it had for twenty-three days; and she thought about Hild on a wall at Cinderhold in the snow, with an eye she didn’t have any more and one she couldn’t close, and an old man behind her saying you were relieved at the second bell.

“Can you do it tonight?” she said.

They went up together. All of them except Drew, who was asleep and needed to be, and Tiger, who was asleep beside him and needed it more; Bee came, though she hadn’t been invited, because Bee went where Hyvred went these days, and sat on the top step of the tower stair with her wings folded and her chalk in her fist.

It was very cold on the leads. The stars were out, hard and bright and in the wrong places, and there was no moon. John went to the north parapet and stood where Hyvred stood every dawn and every dusk, and opened his hand, and the mote went up off it into the dark like a spark going up a chimney, very small, out over the wall and the white fields and away north until even Hyvred couldn’t follow it, which was saying something.

“I can’t see it the way you do,” he said. “I can’t see them. The rings. To me they’re just people in a field.” He had his eyes shut. “The holes look like people too. I told you. Furniture. And the thing at the top’s a burn. A brown edge and nothing.” He breathed out. “But I can see the field. And the snow on the field. And nothing moving on it.”

“That’s it,” said Hyvred. “That’s all it is. Nothing moving.”

“Okay,” said John. “Then that’s what I’ll send.”

He didn’t knock. He’d told her, after the step, that he didn’t knock any more; that he just put the picture where it could be found, it’s here, if you want it, and let the window open by itself if it wanted to. She felt it arrive. Not in her eyes. In her window, the way the channel arrived: a page turning over, very quietly, in the place where she kept her name.

She opened it.

The valley. At night, from the tower, eleven miles off, the way a mote saw it: a long white floor going up between black hills to a white wall, with stars over it, and, very small, far off, a field with people in it, standing; and a curve of more people, standing; and at the top of it all a dark smudge at the foot of the pass, like the mark a thumb leaves on a page. Nothing moving. Nothing at all. Snow on everything, and the stars, and the stillness.

It was the worst picture she’d ever seen. It was the valley, the thing that had been over everything for twenty-three days. And it was in her window, held, there, a page she could close and open.

She looked at it for a long time.

“It’s holding,” said John, after a while. He sounded surprised. He always did.

“It’ll hold,” said Malcolm. He had his book open on the parapet, writing by starlight, which shouldn’t have been possible. “It’s true.”

She didn’t sleep sitting up that night.

She didn’t decide not to. She went down the tower stair with the others and along the long street in the cold and in at the long room door, past Azac, who said Hyvred’s back. Bee’s back. They’re all back, very softly, to the dark; and she went to her pallet at the end of the north wall, between Bee’s and the wall, where she’d sat every night for twenty-three nights with her back to the stones and her knees up and her eyes open on a valley; and she lay down.

It was a strange thing to do. Lying down. She’d forgotten how. She lay on her back on the pallet with the blanket up to her chin and Bee curled up beside her with one wing over both of them, the way Bee had slept in her bunk every night since the summer, and looked up.

The valley was there. Over the beams of the roof, the way it was over everything. Thirteen rings in the snow. The holes. The thumbprint.

And in her window, a page she could open, with the same thing in it, held.

She didn’t open it. She didn’t need to. She knew it was there. She knew that if anything on that white floor eleven miles off so much as turned its head, the page would go out, in her window and John’s and Xav’s and Malcolm’s and Rui Xiang’s and Azac’s and Bee’s and Kei’s and Tiger’s and Drew’s; and ten people would sit up in the dark at once, and Malcolm would wake her, and Hild would be on the stair before any of them. She didn’t have to watch it.

She’d been relieved.

She didn’t know when she stopped looking. She’d think afterwards, the way the king had said, that she couldn’t remember. One minute she was lying on her back under Bee’s wing with the valley over the roof-beams, telling herself she’d only shut her eyes for a moment. And then there was a moment she didn’t remember. And then she opened them.

And there was a ceiling.

Just a ceiling. The roof of the long room, in the grey before dawn: old black beams, and the boards between them, and the smoke-stain over the place where the stove pipe went through, and a crack in one of the boards near the wall that let in a thread of cold air and a thread of grey light. Cobwebs in the corner. A nail somebody had driven in and bent over. That was all. That was all there was.

No valley.

She lay there and looked at it.

She looked at it for a long time, the way she’d looked at the ceiling of a stone hall on the first morning with the painted suns on it and soldiers round the walls and thirty-four of her friends on the floor around her, waking up. She’d cried then, because she could see all of it, edge to edge, for the first time in her life, and nobody had understood why she was crying at a ceiling.

She cried now because she could see only it.

Quietly. So as not to wake Bee. The tears went down sideways into her hair and she let them. She looked at the crack in the board, and the thread of grey light coming through it, and the bent nail, and the cobweb, and none of it had anything lying over it at all; and she thought she’d never in her life seen anything as beautiful as a long room’s ceiling with nothing on it.

Bee woke up anyway. Bee always did.

“What,” said Bee, into the blanket.

“I can’t see it,” said Hyvred.

Bee went very still.

“No,” said Hyvred. “No, I mean, it’s all right. It’s not gone. It’s not me.” She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. “I’m just not looking. I’m looking at the ceiling.”

Bee lifted her head and looked at the ceiling too, for a while, with her head on one side, as if trying to see what was so good about it.

“It’s a ceiling,” said Bee.

“I know.”

“It’s a bad one. There’s a crack.”

“I know,” said Hyvred, and laughed, and cried a bit more, and Bee put her head back down and her wing back over and said you’re very strange into the blanket, and went back to sleep.

She counted at dawn.

She went up the hundred and twelve steps in the grey with Corvin behind her and his board under his arm, and stood at the north parapet in the place she always stood, and for a moment there was nothing there at all. The fields. The white floor of the valley going up between the black hills. The white wall at the end of it. She couldn’t see a ring, or a hole, or a thumbprint. She couldn’t see anything but snow and hills, the way Corvin saw it, the way the Watch on the wall saw it; the way anybody would.

For a heartbeat she was more frightened than she’d been on the night it came in under the arch.

Then she looked.

Not the way she’d been looking for twenty-three days, without stopping, the way you’d hold your breath. On purpose. She opened whatever it was in her that saw, and reached, eleven miles, with Range, the way she’d learned to reach in the summer; and it was there. All of it. The rings. The holes. The round place at the foot of the pass where the snow didn’t settle. Exactly where it had been, exactly as it had been, sitting.

She counted.

“Twenty-three,” she said.

Corvin wrote it down.

And then she did the thing she hadn’t done in twenty-three days. She stopped. She let go of whatever it was she’d reached with, the way you’d let go of a rope you’d been hanging on, slowly, a finger at a time; and the valley went down.

Not away. Down. The way a fire goes down when you bank it. It was still there; she knew exactly where it was. She could have reached it again in a breath. But it wasn’t over everything any more. It was eleven miles off, at the top of a white valley at the foot of the Greyspine, where it belonged.

And Corvin was just Corvin. Red-nosed, with his board, looking at her over the top of it.

“You all right?” he said. It was the first time in twenty-three days he’d said anything at the top of the stair.

“Yes,” said Hyvred. “I’m on watch.”

He looked at her for a moment longer. Then he nodded, as if that made perfect sense, which it did, and went down.

Forty-seven.

It had said forty-five since the night the absence came out of the mouth of the pass, when she’d looked at it with the red mark open and all thirty-four of them looking with her. Two points. She didn’t know what for, exactly. For putting it down, maybe. For lying on her back under a bad ceiling and letting ten people and a picture keep a watch she’d been keeping on her own. For finding out, three years late, that sight wasn’t the same thing as watching; and that watching wasn’t the same thing as never stopping.

She closed it.

Below her, at the foot of the tower, in the grey, a boy with a tiger’s tail came out of the long room door and stood for a moment with his breath going up white, the way he did every dawn. Then he ran. Down the long street, not fast, not Bolt, just a boy running in the snow; through the arch, past the place on the cobbles where a sergeant with three daughters had been crushed against the gatepost, past the step where a king and a fisherman had sat for four nights not talking; to the black oak post inside the gate with its cap of new snow.

He slapped it. Flat hand on the wood.

“Tomorrow,” said Tiger. She heard him from the tower. She didn’t need Range for it; the whole town was still enough to hear.

And then he stood there for a moment with his hand on the post, beside the other mark in the wood, the one at the same height that a man with a rod had left there on his way out of the gate at first light; and turned round, and walked back.

Hyvred watched him come all the way up the long street. A boy, and a street, and snow.

That was all she saw. That was all there was.
