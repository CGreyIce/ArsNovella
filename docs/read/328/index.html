The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 11

Chapter 328

Eleven

3,126 words · 14 min read

He woke before it was light, the way he always did on the day, and lay in the dark and listened to the lake.

Eleven years.

He didn’t have to count. He’d never had to count. Some years he’d woken on this morning in the cabin with the frost on the inside of the window and some years on the road east with his coat over him in a ditch, and once, the first year, he hadn’t slept at all; but he’d always known, before he was properly awake, what morning it was. The body knew. It knew the way it knew where the jetty ended in the dark.

And four years since the hall. The same night. Four years ago tonight, a long way south, thirty-five people had come through a floor with a black sword lying in the middle of the circle, and stood up, and looked at each other, and started to find their way to a lake.

He’d always gone east.

Every year. Ten of them. For seven he’d walked out of the lake road with his pack and the rod a month before and gone east alone, twenty days, to a knoll above an old river, and sat by a grave with his hand on the grass for one night, and walked home. Nobody came. He didn’t ask anybody to. Then the summoned came, and the eighth year Group 3 carried a stone out of the fort for him, and the ninth Alea walked east beside him, and the tenth twenty of them came out of the east gate behind him without being asked. But he’d always gone. The way you’d walk to the end of a jetty, because that was where it was.

He hadn’t gone this year.

He’d sat in a seat at the near end of his own jetty a month ago, on the night he would have had to leave, and thought about the road east, and the rise, and a line in the red forty miles round with two hundred of the Marshal’s foot standing on it every night; and Yan on the knoll, who’d sat with her every night since the spring and said in the channel I said I’d tell her. And he’d sat there with his hands on his knees until it was too late to start.

He hadn’t decided it. That was the strange thing. He’d just not got up.

Alea was on the step when he came out. Of course she was. With two cups. She’d been home eleven days and she’d been on the step before him every morning, which she’d never been before in her life; he thought she’d learned it in Gullhaven, from a woman who got up at four for the herring.

She held one out. He took it.

She didn’t say anything. She didn’t say it’s today. She didn’t ask if he was going anywhere. She sat on the step in the grey with her cup and Astra across her knees and the paper rod back in the box on the shelf with her mother’s things, where he’d told her to put it, and let him stand in the door.

“I’m not going east,” he said.

“I know,” said Alea.

“Yan’s there.”

“I know.” She looked at the lake. “He’s been there every night since the spring.”

He drank the tea. It was hot. It was always hot now; she made it too hot, the way her mother had, so that you had to hold it a while before you could drink it.

“I didn’t decide,” he said. “I just didn’t get up.”

Alea didn’t say anything for a while.

“That’s a sort of deciding,” she said.

He went down to the jetty at first light.

Not to the seat. He’d sat in the seat every day for nine weeks, at the near end, by the ash, facing south; and today he walked past it. Past Gigi’s bench, empty this early, three paces nearer the water than it had been in the spring. Down the thirty planks to the end, with the rod over his shoulder and the hook on the end of it, and stood on the last plank, where he’d stood every morning for nineteen years until the night of the steps.

He cast.

Once. The old way. The line going out over the grey water in a long slow curve with the hook at the end of it, the hook Gabe had made at Harl’s forge in the winter and cut a word into at two in the morning: RESURGAM. Off a gravestone, Gabe had said. I shall rise again. The hook went out and went in with a small sound, and the rings went out from it, and the lake took them.

He stood and watched the float.

He didn’t want a fish. He’d never wanted a fish on this morning. He’d cast on this morning every year since the first, wherever he was, at a river or a pond or a ditch full of rain on the road east; once, the fourth year, into a horse trough at an inn at Ashby with the ostler watching. Because it was the thing his hands did when they didn’t know what to do. Because she’d laughed at him, the first summer, for fishing every morning of their lives, and he’d said somebody’s got to, and she’d said nobody’s got to, you just like standing still.

He did like standing still.

The float sat on the water. It didn’t move.

After a while he reeled in, slowly, and looked at the hook. Bright. Wet. The word cut into the shank, small, in Latin, a language nobody in this world had ever spoken but him and a few of the thirty-five who’d known him when he was somebody else.

Then he walked back down the jetty to the near end, and sat down in the seat.

The channel was quiet.

He noticed at noon. He’d opened it the way he did, on the seat, with the rod across his knees and the sun high on the lake; not to write anything, he never wrote anything; just to read. To see who was where. Hako in Valcrest, Higu at Wrenhaven, Kei at Thornwall with something about Ossie’s legs. Every day for four years there’d been something. Thirty-five people in six towns, talking.

There was nothing.

He looked at it for a long time. The last line was from the night before, very late, from Chris: a count, the way Chris logged everything. Nothing after. Not Higu’s Everybody’s fine. Not Gabe in capitals. Not Thea, or Xola, or Malcolm’s IF and THEN. Not a word from any of them since midnight.

He shut it. He opened it again at two. Nothing.

He understood, about three.

He’d sat in the seat in the afternoon with the sun on the water and thought about it, slowly, the way he thought about everything, and understood that they hadn’t forgotten. That every one of them knew exactly what day it was; they’d all known, this year, for the first time, all of it. The rise and the ring and the healer half-turned and the man who went through a host all afternoon and got there half. Rae had put it in the channel in the spring in her own plain words, he said I could, and every one of them had read it, and every one of them knew that today was the day.

And they’d said nothing.

Thirty-four people, holding a door open all day on an empty stair. The way they’d held it on the night of the steps, so that whatever needed to come up it could come up without anybody in the way. Not saying we’re thinking of you. Not saying are you all right. Not saying anything at all, from Thornwall and Valcrest and Solmere and Cinderhold and Kingsferry and Wrenhaven, so that the only thing in the channel all day was the quiet.

So he’d know they were there.

He sat in the seat at the near end of his jetty in the afternoon sun with the rod across his knees and his hand over his face, for a long time.

Gigi came at four. She didn’t come to the stones; Alea was on the stones. She sat on her bench, three paces nearer, and didn’t say you okay.

He looked at her. She looked at the lake.

“No,” he said. Because she hadn’t asked. Because it seemed like somebody should say it.

Gigi didn’t look round.

“Me neither,” she said.

He wrote to Halvard at five.

At the table in the cabin, with the window open and the lamp not lit yet and Halvard’s letter in front of him where it had been for three weeks, under the salt jar. He took it out and unfolded it for the last time. The hens at the top. The question under them, in the kingdom’s hand, small and very neat. The H. And under that, a fish with a fin.

What do you need?

He’d sat with it three weeks. He’d sat with it in the seat, and on the step with Alea, and on the last plank this morning with the hook going out. He’d thought he would know, today. Today of all days. He’d thought it would come to him at the end of the jetty with the float on the water the way the answer to why did you go had come to him in the spring, all at once, a thing he’d always known and never said.

It hadn’t.

He got out a sheet of the cream paper. One sheet. He didn’t draft.

How are the hens.

Laying. The brown one saw Alea home first and announced her to the door. She’s insufferable about it.

What do you need?

He stopped there a long time.

Then he wrote what was true. Which was the only thing he’d ever been able to write, in any language, to anybody.

I don’t know. Nobody’s asked before. I’ve been sitting with it three weeks and I don’t know yet. I think that means there is something, because a year ago I’d have known straight off and the answer would have been nothing.

I’m finding out. When I know, I’ll tell you first. Before anybody. That’s a promise.

It’s eleven years today. I didn’t go east. Yan’s with her. I’m sat at the near end of the jetty in a seat a tree grew. I didn’t decide that either.

Thank you for asking.

A.

And under it, because Halvard had practised since the spring and it showed, a fish. He did it slowly. He gave it a fin. He gave it an eye in the right place. He looked at it when he’d finished, and it was nearly as good as Halvard’s, and he thought Halvard would be very pleased about that and pretend not to be.

He folded it once. He didn’t seal it. Halvard. Solmere, the west tower.

He walked it up the lake road to Wrenhaven himself, at six, with the sun getting low, and gave it to Mistress Hennet in the post-house yard. She looked at the outside, and at his face, and didn’t say anything, and put it in the bag for the morning rider with her own hands, at the top, flat.

“It’s the day,” she said, at the gate. Not a question. Everybody in Wrenhaven over forty knew what day it was. They’d known for ten years that he went east on it.

“Yes,” he said.

“You didn’t go.”

“No.”

Mistress Hennet looked at him for a long time over the gate.

“Good,” she said, and went in.

He lit the lamp at dusk.

Alea had the matches. She’d lit it every night for the eleven nights she’d been home, because she said it was still hers till the autumn and he’d said so himself. She was standing at the table by the window with the matches in her hand and the wick trimmed a hair too high, the way he always trimmed it, when he came in.

She looked at him.

She held the matches out.

He took them. He struck one. The small blue flame. He held it to the wick and it caught and went yellow, and he put the chimney down over it, and picked the lamp up and put it on the table in the window, where you could see it from the end of the jetty, and from the lake road, and from the top of the last hill on the way home.

And he stood and looked at it.

“Who’s it for,” said Alea. Very quietly. The way he’d asked her, in the spring, whose is it.

He didn’t answer for a long time.

“Her,” he said.

Alea didn’t move.

“I used to light it for her,” he said. “Before. When she was late at the infirmary. Every night for eight years, so she’d see it coming over the hill and know.” He looked at the flame. “And then she wasn’t coming over the hill. And I went on lighting it. For you, mostly. For whoever. For anybody who wasn’t home.” He was quiet. “I never once lit it for her again. On purpose. In ten years. Because she wasn’t going to see it.”

The kettle ticked on the side of the stove.

“She’s not going to see it now,” he said. “She’s not anywhere she could. She’s safe; I made sure. The king and I made sure. She’s not cold under that grave; that’s not her, that’s the other thing, the thing they poured in, and Yan’s sitting with it tonight so I don’t have to.” He looked at the lamp. “But it’s the day. And everybody knows. And there’s a seat at the end of the jetty and thirty-four people not saying anything.” He stopped. “So I thought I’d light it for her. Once. Even if she can’t see it.”

Alea came and stood beside him at the window.

She didn’t touch him. She stood beside him, the way her mother had stood beside him at a window once, in another cabin, a long time ago, looking at the same lake; and they looked at the lamp together, and past it, at the dark coming down on the water.

“She’d have liked Rae,” said Alea, after a while.

He thought about it.

“She’d have told Maud off,” he said. “For the hook. For the four days.”

“Maud would have told her off back.”

“Yes,” he said. “They’d have been unbearable.”

Alea laughed. It came out of her the way it did now, wet, all at once. And he stood beside her at the window and felt the corner of his mouth go, the way it had on the step when she said forty thousand rods went up; and didn’t stop it.

He sat at the near end till late.

In the seat, with Alea on the stones beside him and Gigi on her bench three paces up, and the lamp lit behind them in the window, and the stars out in the lake upside down. Nobody said anything for a long time. Up at Wrenhaven, he could see, every window in the militia hall was lit. He didn’t know if that meant anything. He thought it probably did.

“It’s the first one,” he said, at last, “where I’m not carrying it on my own.”

Alea didn’t say anything. Gigi didn’t.

“Ten years,” he said, “I walked east with it. The whole thing. Nobody knew. The king knew some. Halvard knew a bit and didn’t know he knew. You knew.” He looked at the lake. “And I’d sit by her with my hand on the grass and it’d all be there, the rise and the ring and the fires and what she asked me. And I’d carry it home again. Twenty days. On my own.”

He was quiet.

“Now everybody’s got a bit,” he said. “Rae’s got the rise. She put it in the channel in her own words, and she went into a ring on a customs house and did what nobody did for her. Yan’s got the cold. He’s sitting with it now. Halvard’s got the gate. Thea’s got the word for it. Gigi’s got the end of the jetty.” He looked at the seat under him. “And thirty-four people have got the quiet.”

“And me,” said Alea.

“You’ve always had it,” he said. “You had it first.”

The lake was very still.

“It’s lighter,” he said. Wonderingly. As if he’d only just noticed. “Leaka said that. In the spring, about Halvard. It’s lighter.” He put his hand on the arm of the seat, the living wood, warm. “I didn’t know you could put it down a bit at a time.”

Nobody said anything for a long time.

The channel lit at nine minutes to midnight.

He’d had it open on his knee, not reading, the way you’d keep a door open on a dark stair without looking at it. One line. The first all day.

He read it. He read it twice.

Salty came up the hill. He knew what that meant. Yan had said it, in the channel, in the summer, not as a complaint: that Salty walked him out every dusk and stopped at the foot of the slope and never came up, because it wasn’t his to walk up without being asked. And nobody had asked.

Somebody had asked.

He sat very still in the seat at the near end of his jetty with his daughter on the stones and a girl on a bench and a lamp lit for somebody who couldn’t see it.

He didn’t write anything. He never had.

He put his hand on Alea’s head, between her ears, and held it there. And looked east, which you couldn’t see from here, over the far shore and the hills and twenty days of road, to a knoll above an old river where an ash tree stood over a grave with its leaves turned toward him in no wind; and a man with a white snake round his neck knelt by a headstone with his hand on the grass, and another man stood behind him on a hill he’d never once been up, because he’d been asked.

Try, he thought. To nobody. To Yan. Go on. Carry it.

He didn’t count, this time.

He waited.
