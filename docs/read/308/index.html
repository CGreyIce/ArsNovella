The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 11

Chapter 308

Ask Me Another

4,131 words · 18 min read

The rider had been in Wrenhaven for nine days, waiting for him to answer, and Ars had not yet worked out how to tell her she could go home.

She was staying at the mayor’s. She’d come down the lake road on the bay horse on a wet afternoon with mud to her thighs and asked for him at the post-house, and Hennet had walked her out to the cabin herself, which Hennet had never done for anybody, and stood in the yard with her hands folded while the girl took the letter out of her bag and put it in his hand. By hand, the girl had said. To you. The Advisor said nobody else. And then, red to the ears: He said I was to wait. For an answer. If there was one. He said there’d be no hurry.

So she was waiting. Rowan was feeding her. Rowan Hale had taken one look at a post-rider in the king’s own stockings who’d been told to wait for the fisherman and decided she was the most important guest Wrenhaven had ever had, and she was sleeping in the good room over the mayor’s yard and eating three dinners a day and, Rowan reported, had been taught to play knucklebones by the children in the market square and was losing to all of them.

Ars had not written.

He had the letter inside his coat, where he kept things. Folded once, cream paper, the good kind. On the outside, in a hand he had known for twenty years, A., and The lake. By hand. And inside:

How are the hens.

Why did you go?

H.

And a fish. A very bad fish. Worse than the first one. It looked, Ars had thought on the jetty the night it came, like a boot that had been given the news and taken it badly, and he’d stood on the end of the last plank with the letter in his hand in the dusk and laughed so hard that Gigi, on her bench at the top of the shingle, had called out what and he hadn’t been able to tell her.

That was nine days ago.

He’d known what he’d say since the first night. That wasn’t why he hadn’t written.

He’d spent six of the nine days in a seat made of tree.

He hadn’t meant to. He’d heard on the evening of the ninth day before, from Alea, coming back up the road from the town in the rain with her face set: a fisherman’s girl from the huts at the bottom of the road with a hook in her hand and the grey in it. The same grey as the boy in Kingsferry, in the channel, in Rachael’s plain short lines. A man on the river bank with a box of hooks. If anyone sees a cut go grey, don’t close it. Tell me first.

He’d gone down at first light. He hadn’t known why. He’d stood in the gate of Odile’s yard under the elder and looked at the family asleep in the roots Gigi had grown for them, and Thea asleep against Gigi’s shoulder, and the shut door; and there’d been a seat. A plain flat one, a little apart, under the elder, facing the gate and the door at once. Nobody had sat in it.

He’d sat in it. Every day. Six days. From first light to dark, with his elbows on his knees, facing the door.

He couldn’t have said what he was doing there. He wasn’t a healer. He couldn’t read the grey; he couldn’t touch it; there wasn’t anything a man with a sword could do for an eight-year-old’s hand, and he didn’t carry a sword any more. But he couldn’t be anywhere else. He’d tried, on the second afternoon. He’d gone back up the road to the cabin, and stood on the jetty with the rod, and found he’d walked back down again without deciding to.

He knew what it was. He’d known it since the first night, sitting there with the rain coming off the elder.

He’d watched a door like that once before. Ten years ago, on the east wall at Cinderhold, with the fires lit round a rise so he’d be able to see. He hadn’t been allowed near that door either.

He’d sat in Gigi’s seat for six days and watched the infirmary door and not let himself think about it. And on the evening of the sixth, Maren had come out with her hands wrapped to the knuckles, and stood in front of him in the rain, and said it’s out. It was a word. I burned the word. She’s warm.

And he’d said you aimed, because that was the only true thing he could think of that wasn’t about him. And got up, and walked home, and stood on the end of the jetty till midnight.

The other letter had come the day after Halvard’s.

By the post road, not by hand. A post-boy on a mule, the ordinary kind, with a bag of ordinary letters for Wrenhaven, and in among them a scrap of rough grey paper, the kind they wrote doses on in an infirmary, folded twice. On the outside, in pencil, in a hand he knew from four years of the channel: Declan, the lake, by the post road.

Hennet had brought that one out herself as well. She’d looked at it in her hand, and at him, and not said anything, which was a thing Hennet had learned to do since the spring.

Inside:

Can I?

R.

He’d read it standing in the yard with Hennet watching, and his face had done whatever his face did, and Hennet had looked away.

He knew what it meant. He’d known before he unfolded it. He’d been watching her for two years, since the first time Group 4 came up the river and she’d stood on the shingle with her arms folded and let him look at her for as long as he needed to; and she’d known he was watching, and why, and never once asked him about it. She didn’t ask people things. She didn’t touch them without asking either. Can I? That was what she said, before she put her hands on anybody. He’d heard her say it at the lake in the spring, to Gabe, with her hand an inch from a cracked rib.

She was asking if she could tell them.

All of it. The rise. The ring. Diwa in the middle of it. What they’d done, and why it was a healer, and what he’d done. She was asking if she could put it in the channel, so that thirty other people in seven towns would know why a grey line on a child’s hand was the worst thing they’d ever read.

And she’d asked it in two words, on the back of a dose slip, because she’d known he’d rather be asked a small thing straight than a big thing sideways.

He’d put it in his coat beside Halvard’s.

The channel had been very loud all week.

He read it every night. He always had; he’d read every line of it for four years and never written one, and nobody had ever asked him to. He read it on the step after dark with the lamp behind him, the way other men read a book.

Drew, on a bridge, with his hands down. I stood there and didn’t catch anybody. That’s what it was measuring. And a boy who’d been turned four years ago running across that bridge by himself, and putting a flat hand on Drew’s back, and going home.

Gigi. Briar’s “not here”. This is “here”.

And Hyvred. On the morning of the seventh day, from the bridge at Thornwall, in short broken lines with her eye bleeding, he could tell, from the shape of them.

There’s a seam.

Round Ossie’s face. Where the thing was put on.

And he’s under it. Flat. Like a pressed flower. His own face.

There’s somebody inside.

He’d read that on the step. He’d read it four times. And then he’d put the channel away and gone down the shingle in the dark and out along the jetty to the last plank and sat down on it, with no rod, and looked at the black water, and not thought about anything at all for a very long time, very hard, the way you hold a door shut.

Half-turned. That was what he’d told them, three of them, two springs ago. She was half-turned, and she asked me to end it before she stopped being herself. And he had. That was the whole of it; that was what he had carried for ten years, and he had never once in ten years let himself ask the next question, because there wasn’t one. There’d been nobody in the kingdom who could have done anything else. Not a healer. Not a court mage. Not the king. She’d known that. She’d been a healer; she’d known better than anybody. She’d asked him because he was the only one there.

Somebody inside.

A seam you’d want to open, Rachael had written, under it. Not close. You’d want to get the top off without tearing what’s under it. I’ve never opened anything on purpose in my life.

He hadn’t let himself think it. He sat on the last plank and didn’t think it, with both hands, until the sky went grey over the far shore; and then Alea came down the jetty with two cups and sat beside him and put one in his hands and didn’t say anything, and he drank it, and it was the first time in his life she’d made him tea that he hadn’t noticed was too strong.

That had been three nights ago. He’d stopped not thinking it, since. He’d just let it sit there, beside him, the way you let a dog sit beside you that you haven’t decided to keep.

It didn’t change anything. That was what he’d found, on the second night. It didn’t change the rise. It didn’t change what she’d asked him or what he’d done. There’d been no seam that anybody could have opened, ten years ago, on a hill in a ring of fires with the whole of Kharvos between him and her, because there’d been nobody in the world who knew there was a seam to open. There was now. A girl with a red mark in her eye had walked out barefoot onto a bridge and looked.

It didn’t change anything. It just meant that the next time, it might be different.

He found that he could think that, sitting on the step, and not break.

Alea found him at the table on the tenth morning with both letters out in front of him and a pen.

She stood in the door of the cabin with Astra on her back and her ears up and looked at him, and at the letters, and then at the pen.

“Oh,” she said.

“Don’t.”

“I didn’t say anything.” She came in and sat down on the other side of the table, on the stool, with Astra across her knees, the way she did. She looked at the two letters, the cream one and the grey one, and didn’t touch either. “You’ve had them ten days.”

“Nine and a half.”

“The rider’s learned knucklebones.”

“I heard.”

“She’s losing to a boy of five.” Alea looked at him. “You know what you’re going to say. You’ve known since the night it came. I saw you on the jetty. You laughed at the fish.”

“It’s a very bad fish.”

“It’s the worst fish I’ve ever seen.” She was quiet a moment. “So why haven’t you?”

He looked at the letters.

He thought about lying to her, and didn’t. He’d stopped doing that, mostly, since the spring. Since a list in Diwa’s slate hand on the back of the cabin door that said Write first and Not an answer.

“Because I’m not going,” he said.

Alea waited.

“To Kingsferry.” He turned the pen in his fingers. “Rachael’s asking me if she can tell them. That’s what the two words are. And if I say yes, and she tells them, then they’ll all know what a healer’s for, to the thing that made the hook. And then every one of them is going to look at Kingsferry and look at me and wonder why I’m sitting at a lake.” He put the pen down. “And I want to. I want to go down that river tomorrow and stand on the customs-house steps with my back to the infirmary and dare anything to come up them. I’ve wanted to every day for nine days. I sat in that seat at Odile’s for six of them wanting to.”

“But you’re not going.”

“No.”

“Why?”

He looked at her. At his daughter, nineteen, twenty in the autumn, Honed, with half a black sword on her back and her mother’s ears, asking him the way you ask.

“Because they did it where I could see,” he said.

Alea went very still.

“That was the whole of it,” he said. “On the rise. They didn’t need to do it there. They could have taken her anywhere. They lit fires round a hill an hour from the wall so I’d see, because it wasn’t her they wanted. It was me. A man who watches that either breaks, or comes running on his own into whatever’s waiting.” He looked at the grey slip of paper. “If I go and stand in front of Rachael, I bring that with me. I am that. Whatever’s watching her is watching for me to come; it has been for two years. I’d be carrying the rise down the river and setting it down on the customs-house steps and saying here.” He was quiet. “She’s got Gabe. And Dest, and Sabine, and Emrys, and Calvin with his ears and Dennis with his eyes. She’s got a city. She’s got people standing in front of her who aren’t what they’re waiting for.”

Alea didn’t say anything for a long time.

“Does she know that?” she said at last. “Why you’re not coming?”

“Not yet.”

“Then tell her,” said Alea. “Write it down. That’s an answer she didn’t ask for, but it’s one she needs.” She looked at the cream letter. “And tell Halvard why you went. He’s waited eighteen years.” She stood up. “I’m going to go and get the rider her stockings back off the boy of five. He’s been wearing them as a hat.”

He wrote Halvard’s first, because it was easier, which he hadn’t expected.

He didn’t burn anything this time. He didn’t fill four sheets. He sat at the table with one sheet of Alea’s cream paper and Thea’s pen and the lamp unlit, because it was morning, and thought about a young man on a wall. Not young. Forty-two; he’d worked that out on the jetty, in the spring, counting back. A man of forty-two, who’d sat down on a wall walk one midnight beside a boy of fifteen and stared at his window until dawn with his hands over his mouth, and then counted him, for four years, very carefully, and been afraid of him once at a gate.

Why did you go?

He’d been asked that before. By Alea, on the ice, in the winter. He’d told her he didn’t know, and then he’d told her what he thought, and it had been the truest thing he’d said about it in eighteen years. He’d never said it to anybody else.

He wrote the hens first, because Halvard always asked.

How are the hens.

Laying. The brown one has opinions about the yard. The other two have stopped listening to her.

Then he wrote the answer. It took him one line. He’d thought it might take more; he’d thought, on the jetty, that he’d want to explain. He found he didn’t.

Because I was happy, and I didn’t know what to do with it.

He looked at that for a long time.

It was true. That was the thing about it. It wasn’t the whole of it; nothing ever was. But it was the part that Halvard had been missing for eighteen years, and he could see, looking at it, what Halvard had been putting there instead. He’d known Halvard twenty years. He knew what a man who counted things would have counted, in the space where the reason should have been.

He wrote one more line under it. He didn’t plan it. It came out the way You can ask me now had come out, from under the deciding.

It wasn’t you.

Then, under that, smaller:

Ask me another.

He signed it A. And under the A., because fair was fair, a fish. A good one. Better than last time. Five quick lines and a dot, swimming left, with a fin on its back that he was rather pleased with.

Rachael’s he wrote in English.

He hadn’t written English in a long time. Not to anybody; he wrote fish and hooks and saddles to the natives in the kingdom’s hand and never wrote anything to the thirty-five at all, because he didn’t write in the channel and there’d never been anything that needed a letter. His hand had forgotten some of it. The letters came out a little stiff, a little too careful, like a man walking in boots he hasn’t worn for years.

He wrote at the top, Rae, because that was what Gabe called her, and Gabe had called her it in front of him on the jetty, and she hadn’t minded.

Then:

Yes.

He looked at that.

Then, because Alea was right and it was an answer she needed and hadn’t asked for:

All of it. You don’t have to make it small. Ask Gabe for anything you haven’t got; he has it. Dest has the rest.

I’m not coming down the river. They did it on the rise where I could see, so I’d break or come running. If I come, I bring that with me, and they’ve been watching you to see if I will. You’ve got people in front of you who aren’t what they’re waiting for. That’s better than me.

He stopped, and looked at it. It was the longest thing he’d written in English in thirty-eight years.

Tell Dest the other thing can go up too. Both of them. They should have it all at once.

He thought about writing stay off the steps. He thought about it for a long time. And then he didn’t, because it wasn’t his to say, and because she’d been going down to the window to look at those steps every hour for a week, and she knew more about them than he did.

He signed it D.

Not A. Not to her. He looked at the D. for a moment; he hadn’t signed anything with it since he was twenty-six, in another world. Then he folded the dose slip she’d sent him inside it, so she’d have her own two words back, and folded the whole thing twice, and wrote on the outside, in the kingdom’s hand, so the post would know where it went: Rachael. The infirmary. Kingsferry.

He walked them both down to Hennet.

The rider was in the post-house yard, with her bay horse saddled and her stockings back on and the boy of five from the market sitting on the mounting block beside her in a paper crown, explaining knucklebones. She stood up very fast when she saw who it was, and went red.

“By hand,” said Ars. He gave her the cream one. “To him. Nobody else.”

“Yes, sir.” She put it in her bag, under the oilcloth, against her body. She looked at him. Then she said, all in a rush, the way people do when they’ve been told not to say a thing and are going to say it anyway, “He walked it down himself, sir. The Advisor. All hundred and forty steps of the west tower and across the court in the rain, and put it in my hand by the well. I thought you’d want to know.”

Ars stood in the post-house yard.

“Thank you,” he said.

She went. He heard the bay on the cobbles of the market, and then on the lake road going up over the ridge, and then not.

Hennet took the other one through the window. She looked at the outside, the kingdom’s hand, and turned it over, and saw the English inside through the fold, the strange straight letters no native in the kingdom could read; and looked at him over it.

“Five days,” said Hennet. “If the road’s dry.”

“Fine.”

“It’s in the other writing.” She held it a moment longer. “The one only you lot have.”

“Yes.”

Hennet put it in the Kingsferry bag, very carefully, at the top, where it wouldn’t get crushed.

“I’ll not tell anybody,” she said, the way she always did now.

“You can,” said Ars. “It’s not a secret. It’s a yes.”

Gigi was on her bench when he came back up the lake road.

She’d been at Odile’s every evening that week; she’d come home at dusk with roots under her fingernails and her silver hair coming down and her face doing nothing at all, very carefully, the way it did when she’d done something she didn’t want to be told she’d done. The seats were still there. Odile had said they could stay. Lissa’s brother had carved his initials into the wall one with Chi’s good knife and been made to sand them out again by his grandmother.

She watched him come down the road.

“You okay?”

He thought about it.

“Nearer,” he said.

She looked at his face for a long moment with her red-brown eyes. Then she moved over on the bench. Not much. A hand’s width. And the bench, which had grown a back at midwinter and leaves at the ice-out and been, for most of a year, exactly the size of one stubborn woman waiting for nobody, was very slightly longer at the end where he stood than it had been that morning.

He looked at it.

“Oh, shut up,” said Gigi, to the bench.

He sat down. Not close. The length of the new bit. They sat at the top of the shingle and looked at the lake together, and didn’t say anything, and after a while she started humming something with a tune, which she’d been doing since the spring and pretended she wasn’t.

He read the channel that night on the step, with the lamp behind him.

It was quiet. The quiet it went sometimes, between things. Hako in Valcrest asking whether anybody had ever ridden a horse backwards on purpose. Chris logging Malcolm’s ninety-six. Kei, from Thornwall: the third ring hadn’t come down today, and Hisui said the valley still tasted full. Thea at Wrenhaven, very short: Lissa’s hand was itchy and warm and she’d been sent home with it in a sling she kept taking off to show people, and Maren had gone up the hill to the militia hall with both hands in a bucket of cold water and slept for fourteen hours.

And from Kingsferry, from Rachael, at the end of the day:

Ned’s grey is still under my seam. past his wrist now. Emrys is going to try. He’s been burning his name off his hand all afternoon. Maud thinks he’s an idiot. He’s getting better at it.

He read it twice. He thought about a man past fifty with a voice for every song in the kingdom, sitting at a table in the long ward with ink on the back of his hand, learning to mean that instead of there. He thought about Rachael at the window with the notebook on her knee.

Five days, if the road was dry.

He thought about the dose slip, folded inside his own letter, going back down the river to her. Her own two words, in her own pencil. Can I? And under them, in English he hadn’t written in thirty-eight years, the answer.

He put the channel away.

Out on the end of the jetty, the lamp Alea had handed back to him in the spring was lit. He’d lit it himself at dusk. He still did, every night. He looked at it for a while from the step, a small yellow light on the black water at the end of the boards, lit for whoever wasn’t home yet.

He found, sitting there, that for once he didn’t feel like it was him.
