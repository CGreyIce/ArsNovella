The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 11

Chapter 311

The Cup

4,418 words · 19 min read

Yan read it on the wall at Cinderhold, in the dark, with Suzu round his neck, and when he got to the end he found he’d known it for two years and never once let himself say it.

Pouring.

He’d been sitting on the knoll beside a man with a rod, the summer before last, at the ninth anniversary, with the red going dark all round them and the cold lying under the grave like a stone at the bottom of a well. And the man had said it, after a long time, very quietly, looking at the empty air over the headstone. Poured. That’s what it looked like. From the wall. Like pouring out a cup and pouring something else in.

And Yan had said nothing, because he hadn’t understood. And then he had sat with that cold every night for two years, on the knoll, at dusk, with his back against the stone, and felt it be small and dense and patient under the iron, and never once asked it what it was.

He read Rachael’s lines twice on the wall. Emptying her out and putting something else in. Something cold. He read Calvin’s song. Pour it out and pour it in. He read Hyvred, the seam, that’s what it was, on her too, and Rachael, there wasn’t anybody. Don’t.

Then he put the channel away and sat on the parapet of the east wall with his legs over the drop and the red in front of him, black now, all the way to the edge of the world, and Suzu so still round his neck he could feel her not breathing.

“I know,” he said to her. “I know.”

She didn’t say anything. She couldn’t, to him; only to Hisui, and Hisui was the whole kingdom away at Thornwall. But he felt her press her head against the side of his jaw, cool and dry, the way she did when she was frightened and didn’t want him to know.

He didn’t go out to the knoll that night. He wanted to, very badly, and he didn’t, because he didn’t trust himself to sit there with it in the dark and only sit.

He went the next evening, at dusk, the way he always went.

Out of the west gate and along the old dry river and up the knoll. Salty walked out with him as far as the bottom of the slope, the way he had all spring, and stopped there, the way he always stopped, and stood with his hands in his coat and the greatsword on his back looking at the red, while Yan went up alone. Salty had never once come up. Salty said it wasn’t his to come up to. Yan had never told him how much that meant.

The headstone stood where it always stood, on the top of the knoll, black, with the light going over it. Half a sword. The broad back of a blade nobody had made, cut and set upright in the ground by a king and a fisherman with a smith’s chisel, and the ash tree that Maigo had planted in the first spring standing a little way off with its leaves just out. And under the headstone, under the ground, down through the grave: the point.

He sat down with his back against the stone.

He put his hand flat on the earth.

He’d done this every night for two years. He knew exactly what he’d feel, in what order, the way you know the steps of your own stair in the dark.

The iron first. The old point, the spike, driven down through her grave into the old hole, saying its word into the dark. He’d felt the word as a note for two winters before Thea read it in the folds and gave it a name. Stay. A hand flat on a lid. It was still saying it. It was tired; Suzu said so, and he could feel it himself now, a little: a note held too long, beginning to waver at the edges the way a singer’s voice wavers at the end of a breath. But it held.

The warm thing next. Behind the iron, behind the stone, a little to the side, the way it always was. He didn’t reach for it. He’d never reached for it, not once, not in two years. It wasn’t his. It wasn’t anybody’s but the man at the lake’s and the girl with the black half-sword on her back and the king’s, who’d made it so that nobody in the world could take it or touch it, and Yan had felt the shape of that making once, long ago, like a hand round a candle in the wind, and gone away and never come back to it. He sat near it, every night. The way you’d sit near a fire somebody else had built.

And then the cold.

Under the iron. Down in the old hole, where the point went in. Small and dense and very still. Colder than the hunters on the red; colder than anything on two legs he’d ever felt come across that ground at night. It lay there under the spike the way it had lain for ten years, pressed flat, held, listening. Since the spring it had been listening east. He’d felt it lift its head on the night of the probe, and turn, and look past Kharvos, past the edge of the world, toward something further than Suzu could taste.

It was listening now.

He’d always called it the cold. To Suzu, to himself, in the channel. The cold under the grave. As if it were weather.

What they poured in.

He sat with his hand on the earth and his back against half a sword and thought: I’ve never once looked at it.

He’d never looked because looking was reaching, and he’d promised.

Not about this. About the dead under the red. He’d been on the wall at Cinderhold for four years, with the red in front of him and fourteen hundred markers going out across it, and under every one of them, and under the ground between them where nobody had put a marker, the lights of the dead. Grey, most of them. Faint. Going out slowly the way the light goes out of embers in a grate overnight. He could feel all of them from the wall if he let himself. He’d felt them the first week and been sick over the parapet.

And Aldous, who’d walked him out to the grave in the first month with flowers, had said: Those aren’t yours.

So he’d promised. Not mine. He took from the dying, sometimes, the ones who were going anyway, and let Suzu take from them, a little; he took from the dead men on the field after a probe, the hunters’ victims, the ones still warm, because Suzu had to eat and they didn’t need it any more. But not the red. Not the ones who’d been there ten years and fifty and three hundred. Those were Aldous’s, and the garrison’s, and the ground’s. He’d sat on top of them for four years and never once put his hand down.

And the cold under her grave was under the red, so he’d counted it with them. Not mine. And never looked.

But it wasn’t under the red. Not really. It was under her. It had been put there. Poured. It wasn’t the dead of a battle, lying where they’d fallen. It was something somebody had done, ten years ago, on a rise an hour from here, to a woman in a ring of fires; and when she’d gone, it had stayed. In the ground under where she lay. Held there by a word in an old iron point because a fisherman and a king had driven it there without knowing what they were holding down.

It wasn’t his.

It wasn’t anybody’s. That was the thing he’d never let himself think. Everything else on that knoll belonged to somebody. The warm thing was hers, and theirs. The iron was Aldemar’s two, three hundred years dead. The headstone was the man’s. The tree was Maigo’s.

The cold was nobody’s. Nobody had ever claimed it. Nobody had ever even looked.

He looked.

He did it the way he’d learned to do everything: slowly, with Hue, the way you’d lean over a well with a candle to see how far down the water is.

Hue was colour. It had come to him two summers ago at the grave, when he’d seen, for the first time, that a soul’s light wasn’t just bright or dim but a colour: grey for the plain ones, the ordinary people living ordinary lives; green, the rare green of something given willingly; and white, which he’d felt only once, and never forgotten. He could hold a colour in his palm now. Not take it. Hold it, the way you’d hold a moth in your cupped hands to look at it before you let it out of the window. He’d done it in the infirmary at Cinderhold for a year, with the dying, with their leave, and let them go.

He reached down with Hue past the iron, into the cold.

It was like putting your hand into a cellar in midwinter. It went up his arm at once, through the earth, through his palm, a cold that wasn’t cold the way frost is cold but the way an empty room is cold, a room where nobody has lit a fire in a hundred years. Suzu went rigid round his neck. He felt her hiss, very quietly, into his collar.

“I know,” he said. “I’m only looking.”

He looked.

It wasn’t one thing.

He’d always felt it as one thing. A stone. A lump. Small and dense. But Hue didn’t see shapes; it saw lights; and down in the old hole under the spike, pressed together so close that from up here they’d always felt like one lump of cold, there weren’t one. There were—

He didn’t know how many. He couldn’t count them. They were packed too close, the way coal’s packed in a scuttle, one against the next against the next, a great many, more than a great many, a knot of them as tight as a fist; and every one of them was a light.

A light that had been put out.

He’d felt lights go out before. Everybody who’d ever sat with the dying had. The light goes dim, and dimmer, and grey, and then it’s an ember, and then it’s going, the way a candle goes when the wick drowns in its own wax: not snapped, just done. And then it goes up. Away. Somewhere he couldn’t follow and had never tried to. That was what happened. That was the shape of it.

These hadn’t gone up.

They’d been put out. Not gone out; put out. Snuffed, the way you’d snuff a candle between a wet finger and thumb, so fast the wick doesn’t even smoke. And then kept. Held. Packed down into the dark, cold, unlit, not able to go up and not able to burn, the way you’d keep candles in a drawer. Hundreds of them. More. A drawer full of put-out candles that somebody had poured into a woman, ten years ago, in the middle of a ring of fire.

That was what the cold was.

Not a thing. Not a demon. People.

He sat on the knoll with his hand in it to the wrist and couldn’t breathe.

He’d always wondered why Suzu couldn’t eat demons. He’d sat on the wall after the probes with the dead hunters on the red below, cold points going out one by one, and felt Suzu reach for them and pull back, every time, as if she’d put her mouth to an empty cup. Nothing to eat, Hisui had relayed once, from her. There’s nothing lit in them.

He understood it now. There was nothing lit in a demon because a demon was this. Put-out lights, kept. Whatever a demon was, inside, whatever it was made of, it was made of this: a drawer full of snuffed candles, held cold, that couldn’t go up.

And they’d poured a drawer of it into a healer.

He reached for one.

He didn’t decide to. He’d tell Aldous afterwards that he’d decided; it wasn’t true. He was sitting with his hand in the cold and Hue down in the dark among all those put-out lights, and one of them was nearer than the others. Right at the edge of the knot. Pressed against the iron, where the point went in, as if it had been pushed there by all the others and was being held up against the word. Very faint. Very cold.

And under the cold, so faint that he’d never have seen it if he hadn’t been looking with everything he had, a colour.

Grey. The plain grey. The ordinary colour of an ordinary person living an ordinary life. A grey like a hundred thousand others he’d felt on the road and in the market and on the wall at night. Somebody. A person. Who’d been alive once and lit and had been snuffed and kept in the dark for longer than he could tell, pressed against an iron point saying stay.

He cupped it.

The way he cupped a colour in his palm with the dying. Very gently, with Hue, so that it lay in the hollow of his attention like a moth in his hands. It didn’t resist. It didn’t do anything. It was too cold to do anything. It lay there, grey under black, and he held it, and he felt the smallest thing about it, the way he always felt the smallest thing about every light he’d ever held: a smell. Bread. Somebody’s bread, baking, a long time ago, in an oven with a cracked door.

And he wanted, more than he’d ever wanted anything, to carry it out.

Up. Out of the knot, out of the hole, out of the cold, past the iron, up through the ground into the dusk, and open his hands, and let it go. Let it go up, the way they were supposed to go. Away. Wherever that was. Not his. Not anybody’s. Just—out.

He knew the word for that. It had been sitting in his window for a year, grey, under Kin and Hue, behind a lock.

Carry.

He tried.

It was like picking up an ember with bare hands.

No. It was like picking up the opposite of one. The cold came up off the light he was holding and into him all at once, up through his palm, through his wrist, a cold so deep it wasn’t a feeling at all, it was an absence of feeling, a place in his arm where his arm stopped being there. He felt it climb. His wrist. His forearm. White, he thought afterwards; his skin had gone white, and then a blue like a drowned man’s, and frost had come up on it, actual frost, in a fine fur, on the hair of his forearm in the warm dusk of early summer.

And the light didn’t move.

He had it in his cupped attention, grey and faint and smelling of bread, and he pulled with everything he had, and it didn’t come. Not because it was stuck. It was too cold to come. It had been put out, and kept put out, for so long that it didn’t know any more how to go anywhere at all. And the thing that would have warmed it enough to move, the thing in him that would have lit it just enough to carry, wasn’t there. It was behind the lock. Requires Tier 3.

He pulled anyway.

The frost went past his elbow.

Suzu came off his neck.

He felt her go, the whole cool weight of her sliding off his shoulders and down his chest and onto his arm, onto the white frozen forearm, coiling round it, tight, tight, her whole body wound round the frost like a bandage, hissing. Not at him. At the cold. A sound he’d only ever heard her make once before, at the hunter on the wall in the spring, the night she’d hissed a demon to a stop until Rhea cut it in half. And he felt her pull.

Not on the light. On him. Pulling him back. Out of the cold. Up.

He let go.

He was lying on his back on the knoll in the dark when Salty found him.

He didn’t remember lying down. He remembered letting go: the light slipping out of his cupped attention, down, back against the iron, back into the knot with all the others, cold; and the cold going out of his arm all at once like water out of a tipped bucket, and the pain coming in behind it. He was lying on the grass with his right arm across his chest and Suzu wound round it from the wrist to the shoulder, and he couldn’t feel his hand, and the stars were out.

“Yan.” Salty, kneeling over him, big and dark against the sky. “Yan. You were yelling.”

“Was I?”

“You don’t yell. You’ve never once yelled.” Salty’s hands, on his arm, on the snake, not knowing where to go. “Your arm’s frozen. It’s summer. Your arm’s got frost on it—“

“I know.”

“What did you do?”

Yan looked at the stars.

“I looked,” he said.

They carried him down between them, Salty and Rhea, who’d come out of the west gate at a run when Salty shouted, with Niko behind her with his blade drawn and nothing to use it on. Maigo met them at the gate with his antlers full of leaves and took one look at the arm and said oh, no, no, no, very gently, and went to get things. Wendy counted his breaths. He heard her do it, under hers, all the way across the yard.

Aldous came to the infirmary at midnight.

He was old, older than he’d been when Yan came, and slow on the stair, with his stick and his Bonfire burning low and steady in him like a hearth at night. He came and sat on the stool by Yan’s cot and looked at the arm, which Maigo had wrapped to the shoulder in leaves and linen and something warm that smelled of resin, and at Suzu, who was still wound round it on top of the bandages and wouldn’t come off, and at Yan’s face.

“The grave,” said Aldous.

“Yes.”

“You reached.”

“I looked,” said Yan. “And then I reached.” He didn’t lie to Aldous. He never had. “I’m sorry. I promised. Not the red. Not the ones under the red.”

Aldous was quiet for a long time.

“Tell me what you saw,” he said.

So Yan told him. The knot. The packed cold, the drawer of candles. The lights, put out and kept. The one against the iron, grey under the black, that smelled of bread. Carry, and the lock, and the frost up his arm, and Suzu pulling him back.

When he’d finished, Aldous sat with both hands on the top of his stick and his chin on his hands and looked at nothing.

“People,” he said at last.

“People.”

“Put out. And kept.” Aldous’s voice was very low. “Not the dead of the red. Not lying where they fell. Kept. Like coin in a box.”

“Yes.”

“Poured into her.” The old man shut his eyes. “I’ve stood on that wall a long time, Yan. I’ve heard the men who were there tell it. The fires on the rise. I’ve wondered every year what they did to her that he couldn’t bear. I thought—“ He stopped. “I thought it was something done to her. I never once thought it was something done with other people.”

Yan didn’t say anything.

“Those aren’t the red,” said Aldous. He opened his eyes. “You were right to look. And wrong to reach. Both. You know that.”

“I know.”

“Those aren’t the dead under the red. Those were never ours.” He looked at Yan’s bandaged arm, at the snake wound round it. “Not mine was a promise about the ones who’d gone. These didn’t go. They were never let.” He was quiet a long moment. “I don’t know whose they are. But they aren’t mine to keep you from.”

Yan lay on the cot and looked at the ceiling.

“I couldn’t carry it,” he said. “I tried. It was too cold to move. It’s been put out so long it’s forgotten how to go anywhere.” He swallowed. “There’s hundreds, Aldous. More. And I couldn’t move one.”

“Not yet,” said Aldous.

He got up, slowly, with his stick. At the door he stopped.

“The point’s tired,” he said, without turning round. “You told me. Suzu says. It’s been saying its word for ten years into a drawer of that, and it’s tired.” He was quiet. “And on the edge of a ring forty miles across, the black stood all night in the spring facing in toward something we can’t see. And the cold under her listens east.” He looked back at Yan. “If it ever stops holding them, Yan, they’ll go somewhere. Not up. I don’t think they can go up. They’ll go where they’re called.”

He went out.

He didn’t, for a long time.

He lay on the cot in the dark with Suzu wound round his arm, cool and heavy, and the arm itself a long dull ache from the shoulder to the fingertips as the cold came out of it, slowly, the way the cold comes out of a stone that’s been in the river. He could feel his fingers again. A little. They were burning, the way your fingers burn when you come in from snow.

He thought about the light against the iron. Grey under black. Bread, in an oven with a cracked door.

Somebody had baked that bread. Somebody had stood at an oven and opened the door and smelled it and been, for one moment, ordinary and lit and alive, in a kitchen somewhere; and then something had come and put them out between a wet finger and thumb, and kept them. For how long, he didn’t know. Since before the rise. Since before the man at the lake was born, maybe. Since before Aldemar.

And then poured them into a healer, so she’d hold them.

And when she’d gone, they’d stayed.

He thought about Ars, on a jetty at a lake, with the lamp lit, reading Rachael’s lines. He thought about the man knowing, now, all of it, that every one of them knew. So it’s us. He thought about whether Ars knew what was under her. Whether he’d want to know. Whether Yan should tell him.

He thought he probably would. Not tonight. Not in the channel. On the knoll, at the anniversary, at the end of the summer, the way the man had told him poured: quietly, looking at the empty air over the stone.

He opened his window, in the dark, with his left hand.

Carry. Grey. Behind its lock.

He’d thought, for a year, that it was about him. Carrying souls in himself, the way the old stories said soul-eaters did, that he’d always been half afraid of. Keeping.

It wasn’t about keeping.

You carried a thing so you could put it down somewhere else. That was all carrying was. You picked it up where it was stuck and you took it where it was going and you opened your hands.

He’d felt one of them, tonight, pressed against the iron, too cold to move, waiting. Not for the ring. Not for the east. Just waiting, the way everything in that knot was waiting, for something to come and lift it a hand’s width out of the cold, and warm it just enough to remember which way was up.

Ninety-two.

“Not yet,” he said to the dark. To Suzu. To the knot under the grave a mile away, and the light that smelled of bread. “But nearer.”

Suzu tightened round his arm, once, and was still.
