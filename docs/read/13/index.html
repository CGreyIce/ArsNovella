The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 1: Thirty-Four Winters

Chapter 13

Reports

2,696 words · 12 min read

The reports came in on the first of every month, and the Advisor read them at night, alone, in the order he had trained their writers.

It was a small vanity. He knew that. But he had chosen those seven, and taught them, and sat up with each of them in turn the night they first saw their own windows open, and there was something in reading their reports in that same order that felt like keeping a promise. So he lit the lamp in his study, and poured a cup of wine he would not finish, and broke the first seal.

Maren, from Wrenhaven. Neat, small, exact handwriting, every line ruled. He would have known it anywhere.

The group continues to train daily. All five remain at Initiate. Progress is slow and steady. The one called Althea is significantly ahead, at approximately sixty percent; she reports that she can perceive structure beneath the status window, which I cannot verify.

On the seventh day, the group obstructed the lake road during a quarrel. The fisherman came upon them. The guard and I removed them from his path and showed him the courtesy due. He passed without incident and spoke one sentence.

He looked at each of them before he passed. I have seen him at the market twice since, and there he looks at no one for longer than it takes to pay. I do not know what it means. I record it because you asked me to record everything.

The Advisor read that paragraph twice.

A girl has begun training with the group. Alea, sixteen, a fox demihuman, copper rank with the adventurers’ guild, on the aura path at the first tier. She arrives most days and brings fish. I recognised her from the road. I have permitted her to stay, as she is not the one I was ordered not to disturb.

I believe she is his.

I await your instruction.

He sat back in his chair. Outside his window the castle was dark, and below it the city, and far beyond the city, beyond the hills and the long north road, a cabin on a lake where a man he had once called his friend was presumably asleep.

He picked up his pen and wrote on the bottom of Maren’s report, in the space she had left for his reply, because she always left a space.

Let her.

Oswin, from the archive. Long, enthusiastic, digressive, stained with ink. Two pages on the summoned’s progress, which was slow. Three pages on Cursiffe’s ink, which held on skin when written in the summoned’s own script and nowhere else, and which Oswin found quite extraordinary, Advisor, quite without precedent. One page on a cat-eared girl who had talked the Keeper into opening the restricted shelves, which I mention only because in fifteen years I have never seen him say yes to anyone.

And then, on the last page, the thing the Advisor had told him to leave.

I know you have asked me to set aside the matter of the summoned’s connections to one another. I have tried. But I would be failing in my duty if I did not tell you that the more I learn of them, the more certain I become. They did not merely know each other on their own world. They are connected, all thirty-five, through a single web of acquaintance. Every one of them can name someone in another group. Some can name a dozen.

A summoning is a net, Advisor. It catches what is tangled in it. Something tied these people together before we ever reached for them.

I ask only to be permitted to find out what.

The Advisor folded the last page in half, and then in half again, and set it to one side.

He did not write a reply on that one.

Aldous, from Cinderhold. Slow and careful and kind, the hand of a man who had written a thousand names on a thousand grave markers.

The group was well. The guard, Rhea, was loud. The large one, Salty, was strong and stubborn and would lift the blade, Aldous thought, when the time came. The archer was a marvel. The small tree-person had begun to feel the ground, and he says the red earth hurts him, which I have long suspected it does.

The one called Yan has begun to feel the dead.

I took the group to the healer’s grave. You know the one. I wanted them to understand what they would be carrying, when the anniversary comes. The boy knelt there of his own accord. His serpent hissed at the earth. When he stood up, Advisor, his face was that of a man who had heard something he would not repeat.

I do not know what he felt. I did not ask. But I have stood at that grave for seven years and I have always felt that something was done there, beneath the grief, that should not have been done. I think the boy felt it too.

I ask again, as I have asked before, to be the one who carries the blade home.

The Advisor wrote, under it: Yes.

He sat for a while afterward, not reading anything.

Garrick, from Duskhollow. Blunt, misspelled, furious.

One of mine nearly died. The big one, Azac. Something came out of a new tunnel in the first ring that had no business being in the first ring. Split his shield like a biscuit. He’ll live. Petra cut it in half before it could finish him, or I’d be writing you a different letter.

I want you to know they did everything right. Everything. I’ve never seen Initiates move like that. The one who calls for them, Xavier, called it before I did. It didn’t matter. Nothing at their tier could have done anything about what came out of that wall.

Something’s changing in the Hollow. Things are coming up from deeper than they should. I’ve sent word to the guild. I’m sending it to you as well.

Folded inside Garrick’s report was a second, much smaller piece of paper, in a different hand. Petra’s. A guard’s report, sent through the guide, as was proper. It was one line long.

The one called Xavier fights like a man I saw on the Redmarch.

The Advisor looked at that line for a very long time.

Emrys, from Kingsferry. Elegant, musical, faintly amused. The group was well. The healer-girl had found the infirmary and was the happiest he’d ever seen anyone. The big one had found a forge. The quick one had found a bargain, and Emrys suspected the Crown would be happier not knowing which one.

And Deston sees bonds.

I don’t know what else to call it. He says that every person he looks at is joined to others by threads of light, thin for strangers and thick for those they love. He says the thickness is closeness. He has learned, in a month, to read them like a book. He says he felt something go wrong at the far end of one of them, three days before the letter came telling him what it was.

I have taught magic for twenty years and I have never heard of anything like it. I record it so that someone wiser than me can decide what it means.

The Advisor did not feel wiser than anyone.

He sat very still in the lamplight and thought: a boy who sees what ties people together. He thought of Oswin’s letter, folded on the corner of the desk. Something tied these people together before we ever reached for them. He thought of thirty-five windows opening in a stone hall, every one of them exactly like a window he had first seen nearly twenty years ago, on a young swordsman at a border garrison, who had laughed at his astonishment and said, you’ve never seen one of these? Here. Let me show you how to look.

He thought of what a boy who saw bonds might see, if he ever looked north.

Corvin, from Thornwall. Eleven pages. The Advisor had expected that. Corvin’s reports were always long, and always frightened, and for two years he had read every one and believed every word and been unable to make anybody else believe them.

This one was different.

This one had a witness.

The girl Hyvred sees light. Not as a mage sees mana; she sees it in everything, in people, in animals, in stone. She says every living thing carries it, and she can see its shape.

At Coldwater she saw a man whose light did not fit his body. Her words were “someone wearing someone.” He saw her see him, Advisor. He looked directly at her, and then he walked away, and by the next morning he was gone.

That night, from the wall of the fortress, she saw a figure on the north ridge that had no light at all. Hild was beside her and heard nothing. I believe the girl completely.

You sent her to me because she could see. You were right. I am frightened of what else she is going to see.

There were eight more pages. Maps, tracks, sightings, dates. The Advisor read every word. Then he went to the cabinet against the wall, where he kept two years of Corvin’s reports in a locked drawer that nobody else had a key to, and put this one on top of the others, and stood with his hand on it.

Someone wearing someone.

Seven years ago, on the Redmarch, the enemy general had taken a healer and begun to turn her into something else in full view of the man who loved her. Nobody had ever known how. Demons did not do that. They had never done it before, and they had not done it since.

Unless they had, and nobody had been able to see.

The last report was not from a guide at all.

It came from the king, as Kestra’s always did, with a single line from Severin himself scrawled across the top in his big, rough hand.

Halvard. The cat girl. Read this.

Kestra’s report was short, the way Kestra was short. The capital group was well. The two swordsmen were progressing; the red-haired one stood where she would stand, and the cat one was learning to wait. The fox one tamed things without trying. The scribe wrote in a script that would not wash off.

The one called Hanarisu made the archive Keeper say yes. I watched her do it. I do not believe she knew she was going to. I do not believe the Keeper knows that he did.

She is at the bottom of the ladder, as they all are. But a charm that works at the bottom of the ladder will be a dangerous thing at the top.

I will continue to watch her.

The Advisor set it down, and pressed his fingers to his eyes.

The king trained at dawn. He always had. He was sixty-three years old, and he had reached the ninth tier before he was forty, and the ninth tier doubled a man’s years. By any reckoning he had decades left in him, and he moved through his forms in the cold of the private yard like a man half that age.

The Advisor waited by the wall until he’d finished.

“Corvin’s right,” said Severin, without preamble, towelling his face. “Isn’t he.”

“Yes.”

“Two years he’s been writing to you. Two years I’ve been reading them over your shoulder, and two years the council’s been laughing at him.” The king hung the towel on a peg. “What does it mean, someone wearing someone?”

“I don’t know. I think it means whatever was done to the healer on the Redmarch is being done again. Quietly. In the west, where nobody’s looking.”

“The external power.”

“Perhaps.”

Severin was quiet for a long time, looking at the grey sky.

“How strong are they?” he said finally. “Your thirty-five.”

“Eight percent,” said the Advisor. “Ten. Twelve. After two months. The fastest of them is at sixty, and she had a head start.” He made himself say it. “At this pace, the first of them will reach the first tier before winter. The tenth tier…”

“We don’t have that long,” said Severin.

“No.”

The king turned and looked at him. He had a way of looking at people, Severin did, that made them feel the full weight of the ninth tier without his raising a finger. The Advisor had known him forty years and still felt it.

“Does he know they’re here?” said the king.

There was no need to ask who.

“He knows,” said the Advisor. “Maren’s girls blocked his road. He looked at every one of them. And his daughter has been training with them for a month.”

“Will he come?”

“He has given enough.”

“That isn’t for you to decide, Halvard.” Severin’s voice was quiet, which was worse than if it had been loud. “Nor me. You took his sword off his woman’s grave and used it to pull thirty-five strangers out of the sky, and you didn’t ask him. I let you.” He paused. “When the time comes, I’ll go to that lake myself. And I’ll ask him. And he can say no to my face.”

The Advisor didn’t answer. There was nothing to answer with.

“The cat girl,” said the king, turning back to his forms. “Watch her. Kestra’s right. A charm like that is a gift if she’s honest and a war if she isn’t.”

“Yes, Majesty.”

“And Halvard.”

“Majesty?”

Severin didn’t turn around. “The boy who fights like the Black Blade. Petra’s line. I read that one too.” He lifted his sword into the first form, slow and perfect. “Find out why.”

That night, the Advisor wrote a letter to Ars.

He did it at the desk in his study, with the lamp turned low, the way he had written perhaps thirty of them over seven years. He wrote it slowly, crossing out more than he kept.

Ars.

They have windows like yours. All of them. Every one of the thirty-five opened their eyes in that hall with a window exactly like the one you showed me at the garrison, nearly twenty years ago, when I was too proud to admit I’d never seen one. You laughed at me. Do you remember? You said, here, let me show you how to look.

I have never asked you where you came from. You never offered. You had the window before any of us, and you taught it to me, and I taught it to seven others, and that was all I ever needed to know.

I think I need to know now.

I took your sword from her grave. I told myself she would understand. I told myself you would never know, that the blade would be home again before the anniversary, that it was only borrowed. Aldous will carry it back. I have given him leave.

They are yours, aren’t they. Somehow. Oswin says something tied them together before we reached for them. Emrys has a boy who sees the ties. Petra has a boy who fights like you. Maren says you looked at every one of them.

I brought you thirty-five strangers, I said to the empty hall. I don’t think they were strangers at all.

Forgive me. Or don’t. But

He stopped there. He sat with the pen in his hand for a long time.

Then he folded the letter, unsealed, and opened the bottom drawer of his desk, where the others were: thirty letters, more or less, in seven years. Letters about the war, and the grave, and the girl, and the long silence afterwards. Letters he had never sent, because a man who had given enough did not need an old friend’s guilt arriving at his door in a courier’s satchel.

He put this one on top, and closed the drawer, and turned out the lamp.

Outside, very far to the north, there was a lake. He didn’t need to see it to know exactly how still it would be.
