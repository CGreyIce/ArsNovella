The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 10: The Sword's Country

Chapter 294

The Empty Middle

5,245 words · 23 min read

Every frame had a middle. Thea had known that for a year, and she’d never once thought about it, and that was the thing she couldn’t forgive herself for, afterwards, sitting on the cabin step at midnight with Higu beside her.

She’d thought about everything else. She’d spent most of last year with Oswin’s copies spread on Maren’s table in the militia hall, fourteen leaves of them, packed in the king’s oak case with felt between them and Edwy’s red ticks down every margin, two by two, every line checked twice. Fifty-six frames. Fifty-six times the circle in the summoning hall had run, in however many hundreds of years since somebody had first cut it into the stone; fifty-six times it had brought somebody, or some people, out of a floor in Solmere into a world that didn’t want them. And every time it had added a frame to the list, by itself, in its own hand. The register. It kept itself.

She’d read the shape of it. A ring. One line going out from the ring for every person caught. A short arc on the right, with ticks on it, one tick for every line, for the first thirty-seven frames, and none after. Frame fifty-five: two lines. Frame fifty-six: thirty-five.

She’d read the lines. She’d read the arc, as far as she could, which wasn’t far; she still didn’t know what a tick meant, and she wasn’t going to guess. She’d read the rings and found them all the same ring, the same word written round, come, the oldest hand in the leaves.

And in the middle of every frame, inside the ring, where the lines started from: a mark. Small and dense and black, like a knot. A different one in every frame. Fifty-six of them, no two alike.

She’d looked at them, in the first month. She’d tried to Parse one and got nowhere; it was too dense, too much written in too small a space, like a word said so many times over itself it had gone solid. Oswin had called it the middle, in his notes, because he called things what they were. She’d called it the knot, in hers. And then she’d moved on to the lines, because the lines were people, and the people were what mattered, and the knots were just where the lines started from.

Fifty-six frames. A middle in every one.

She’d never once looked for it in the valley.

John’s new picture came on the third day after the balance.

He’d made it from the tower at Thornwall at noon, the way he’d made the last one, very carefully, sitting still on the leads for an hour with a mote held out over the parapet until the valley was so still in his window it could have been painted. And sent it, without knocking, to the ten windows he always sent it to. And then, because Thea had asked him to, in the winter, and Higu had said not before, to one more.

It opened in her window at the militia hall while she was copying a corner mark for Maren, and she put the pen down, and looked at it for a long time.

The valley at noon in the spring. Brown and green now, not white; the snow gone off the floor of it and the swept road clean up the middle like a line drawn with a ruler. The rings. All thirteen, standing in their rings, four hundred and some people in coats in a field, facing down the valley toward the town. The round dark place at the top, at the foot of the pass, like a burn in the picture, brown at the edges and nothing in the middle. And outside the rings, the curve.

She counted. She couldn’t not.

Twenty-six.

Three more than in the winter. A comma, moving along a line.

“Is that it?” said Maren, across the table. She couldn’t see it. Natives couldn’t. But she’d learned Thea’s face in three years better than Thea had, and she’d seen it change.

“That’s it,” said Thea.

“Are you going to read it?”

Thea looked at the picture.

“Yes,” she said. “Tonight. At the cabin.” She shut the window. “Higu said not before there was a new one. There’s a new one.”

Maren didn’t say anything for a moment. She was sitting very straight on the other side of the table with her hands flat on it either side of the corner sheet, the way she sat now when she was reading: not pushing at the page, not leaning into it. Just there. Her hands had healed in the winter, mostly. There were shiny places on both palms where the blisters had been, from the autumn when she’d pushed at Torch so hard she’d burned herself. She didn’t push any more. She read slowly, word by word, aiming, the way Thea did.

“Seventy-nine,” said Maren.

Thea looked up.

“This morning.” Maren didn’t look up from the corner sheet. “I opened it before you came. Seventy-nine.” She turned the sheet a little, toward the window, so the light came across it. “It was seventy-one at midwinter. Seventy-four at the thaw.” She was quiet. “I’m not trying to make it go. I’m just reading. Every word. And it goes.”

“That’s how it works,” said Thea.

“I know,” said Maren. “You told me. I didn’t believe you.” She picked up her pen. “I’m coming tonight. I’d like to watch you stop.”

They did it the way they’d done it in the winter. At the cabin. By the lamp.

It was still Alea’s lamp. Thea didn’t know why that mattered; it was just a lamp, a plain brass one with a glass chimney and a wick that was always a hair too high, the one Declan had lit at dusk every night of Alea’s life. Alea had taken it in the autumn when he went west, and lit it every night until he came over the hill at midwinter, and kept it after, and said she’d give it back at the thaw. The thaw had come. She hadn’t. Nobody had said anything. It sat on the cabin table in the dusk, lit, and Thea sat on Declan’s stool in front of it, because that was where she’d sat the last time she read the valley, in the winter, when he was in the west; and it had worked.

They were all there. They always were.

Higu at her elbow with her notebook open on her knee and the pencil ready, the box already drawn at the top of a clean page. DOOR, in capitals, with nothing in it yet. Gigi on the floor at her feet with two fingers on Thea’s wrist, finding the pulse, counting it under her breath. Xola on the end of the bed with the blanket, folded, ready, and Squiddles on her shoulder. Chi with Waddles in her lap. Maren on the crate by the door. Bram in the doorway, filling it, the way he did, with his back to the dark and his face to the room. I’ll be the door. Again.

And outside: Deston and Gabe on the step, because Group 4 were still at the lake, and Deston had asked, and Thea had said yes. Alea on the step beside them, her shoulder against the doorframe, where she could see in.

And Declan on the jetty.

He’d been on the jetty when they came, and he hadn’t come up. He’d looked round once, at the lamp coming on in the cabin window, and Thea had seen him see it, and then he’d turned back to the lake. It wasn’t like him. When she’d read the folds in the winter he’d been right there on his stool the whole time, with his cup going cold. Tonight he was out on the last plank with his legs over the edge and his back to them.

Thea didn’t ask. She’d learned not to. Alea was on the step, and Alea knew, and if it was a thing Thea needed to know Alea would have said.

“Ready,” said Higu.

Thea opened the picture.

Handshake was the easiest part, now. She’d done it in the winter for the first time on something that wasn’t a person, and it had been like stepping into a window. It was like that again. She put her hand out, not to the mote, not to John, but to the picture itself, sitting still in the corner of her window like a painted thing; and she asked it, the way she’d learned to ask, can I, and it was only a picture, and it didn’t look back.

She went in.

The valley came up round her. Not like seeing it. Like reading it. She’d tried to explain that to Maren a hundred times and never managed. When she Parsed a thing she didn’t see it any more; she saw what it was written in. And the valley was written in the oldest hand in the leaves, the same hand as the circle in the floor of the summoning hall, the same as the rings on the register, the same as every frame. The rings first. Thirteen of them, round, every one the same word written round and round, come, in four hundred and some people standing in their places like letters on a line. She read them the way she’d read them in the winter. The seven blanks in the inner rings, rubbed out, the rings still reading round them, a hair quieter. The thirteenth, full. The fourteenth.

She went along the fourteenth. The curve. The one that wasn’t people, that was holes.

It was further than it had been. The comma had moved. Twenty-six. The line she’d read in the winter, nearly a sentence at thirty-one and cut square at twenty-three like a reed a hand past a blade, was growing back toward the cut. Three words more. Open. Waiting. Five more to the cut, and then two or three past it, and then it would be a sentence, and she didn’t know what it would say.

She didn’t follow it. She’d promised Higu. She came back along the curve to the rings, and stopped there, and held, and felt the house round her, Gigi’s thumb and the lamp and Higu’s pencil not moving yet.

And then she did what she’d never done.

She went in.

Not along the rings. Across them. In, toward the middle. Through the thirteenth ring and the twelfth and the eleventh, the way you’d walk in through the rings of a tree stump toward the heart of it, every ring the same word, come, come, come, closer and closer together; through the seventh where the blanks were, and the third where a boy with freckles stood turned toward the town, and the second, and the first. And on to where all the lines started from. To where, in every frame she’d ever read, in fifty-six frames in fourteen leaves in the king’s oak case, there was a knot.

To the middle.

There wasn’t one.

She stood in the middle of the valley circle, inside the first ring, in the oldest hand in the world, and there was nothing there.

Not nothing. She’d read nothing before. She’d read the star-iron in Harl’s tongs, which wasn’t written in anything at all; that had been nothing, plain, like a blank page, like a clean floor. This wasn’t that. This was written round. Every line in the inner ring turned toward it. Every word of come in thirteen rings pointed at it, the way the spokes of a wheel point at the hub. The whole circle was built to have something here. The grammar of it wanted something here so badly it ached.

And where the something should be, there was a hole.

Round. Small. She didn’t know how big; there were no sizes, in reading. Small, the way a word is small. And the edges of it weren’t clean. The writing round it, the last of the first ring, didn’t stop at the edge of the hole the way writing stops at the edge of a page. It was torn. Pulled outward, all round, the strokes bent and lifted at the rim, the way the grain of a plank lifts round a hole when something’s been pulled out of it hard.

Like a nail hole.

She thought it, just like that, in those words, standing in the middle of the valley in the oldest hand in the world. Like a nail hole. Like where a nail’s been pulled out of old wood. And then, because she was Thea and she couldn’t help it, she Parsed the hole.

Its grammar.

It was a word, too. Even a hole was a word, in this. It had a shape, and the shape meant something. And she read it the way she’d read Astra’s folds on the cabin floor in the winter, a hand flat on a lid, stay: she read what the hole was.

It was the same kind of word. A holding word. The word you’d put in the middle of a circle so everything round it stayed where it was.

But it wasn’t there.

The hole was the shape of that word, with nothing in it. As if somebody had written stay very hard into the middle of a circle, a long time ago, and then somebody else had come along and pulled it out, and left the shape of where it had been, torn at the edges. And the circle had been written round the hole anyway. Thirteen rings. All of them saying come. All of them pointing at a middle that had nothing in it to say anything back.

A mouth. With nothing in it.

She felt it pull.

Not like the call. Not like the folds in Astra pulling her down a stair. It pulled the way an empty thing pulls, the way a drain pulls water. Everything in the circle went toward the hole, all the words, all the rings, and she was standing in the circle reading it, and she was going too. Toward the middle. Toward the torn edge. Not because it wanted her. Because it was empty, and everything went toward it, and she was a thing.

She didn’t see it. She felt the pencil go, very far off, a scratch on a page in another world.

Five, Higu had said in the winter. Then learn how to stop first.

She stopped.

She didn’t know how. She found out afterwards, from Gigi, who’d had two fingers on her wrist the whole time, that her pulse had gone to a hundred and thirty at three hundred heartbeats and then dropped, all at once, to seventy, like somebody setting down a bucket. She didn’t remember deciding. She remembered the pull, and the torn edge of the hole coming toward her like the edge of a well when you lean over it in the dark, and a very small clear thought, in her own voice, not the circle’s:

I don’t have to go in to know it’s empty.

And she stopped.

Not out. Not back along the rings. She just stopped going toward it. She stood where she was, in the middle of the valley circle, a step from the torn edge, and let it pull, and didn’t go. The way you’d stand on a riverbank and let the water go past your feet. It was very hard. It was the hardest thing she’d ever done in a reading. And then it wasn’t, because she was standing still, and the hole was just a hole, and a hole couldn’t make you do anything if you didn’t move.

She looked at it from there. A long time.

Then she turned round, and walked back out through the rings, the first and the second and the third, past a boy with freckles turned toward a town, through the seventh with its blanks, out through the thirteenth to the curve, the twenty-six; and out of the picture, and out of the valley, and into the cabin, on Declan’s stool, with the lamp in front of her and Gigi’s thumb on her wrist and Higu’s pencil stopped on the page.

“Four hundred and two,” said Higu.

Thea sat on the stool and breathed.

No nosebleed. She touched her lip to be sure. Nothing. Her hands were cold. She was cold all over, the way you’re cold after you’ve been leaning over something deep for a long time; and Xola came off the end of the bed with the blanket before anybody asked and put it round her shoulders, and Squiddles put a tentacle on her ear.

“You stopped,” said Higu. Her voice was very flat, the way it went when she was trying not to make it anything else. “At three hundred. You were going in, and you stopped, and you stood there for a hundred heartbeats, and then you came out on your own.” She looked at her notebook. “I’ve never seen you do that.”

“I’ve never done it,” said Thea.

“Pulse a hundred and thirty,” said Gigi, from the floor. “Then seventy. Like that.” She snapped her fingers. “Then eighty for a hundred heartbeats. Steady. Then you came out.” She let go of the wrist. “That’s not a door. That’s somebody who knows where the door is and isn’t using it.”

Maren, on the crate, had her hands flat on her knees, and was looking at Thea with an expression Thea had never seen on her: as if she were reading something, very slowly, word by word.

“Tell us,” said Higu.

Thea told them.

She told them the curve first, because it was easy. Twenty-six. Five more to the cut, two or three past it. A sentence coming, a comma moving. They knew that.

Then she told them about the frames.

She had to, first, or the rest wouldn’t mean anything. Fifty-six frames in fourteen leaves in the king’s oak case. A ring and lines and an arc. And in the middle of every one, a knot. A different knot every time, dense and black and too much written in too small a space. The middle, Oswin called it. She’d never been able to read one. She’d never tried very hard. She’d thought it was just where the lines started from.

“The hall’s circle has one,” she said. “The real one. In the floor in Solmere. I’ve thought about it all day; I’ve been trying to remember. When we woke up. When we were all lying on it.” She shut her eyes. She could see it. The cold stone, and the soldiers, and the Advisor, and thirty-four people she loved getting up off a floor. “There was a mark in the middle of the floor. Under the lamps. Black. I was lying with my hand on it. I thought it was a stain.” She opened her eyes. “It was the middle. Every circle that’s ever run has had one.”

“And the valley,” said Higu.

“The valley’s built round one,” said Thea. “Every ring. Every line. Everything in it points in, to the middle, the way every frame does. It’s built to have a middle so badly it hurts to read.” She pulled the blanket round her. “And there isn’t one. There’s a hole. A small round hole with the writing torn up all round the edges, like—“

She stopped.

She’d felt it, before she said it. A change in the room. She didn’t know where it came from. Not from Higu or Gigi or Xola or Chi, who were looking at her the way they always looked at her after a reading, waiting. From the door. From the step.

She looked.

Deston was on the step, and he wasn’t looking at her. He was looking past her, out of the cabin door, down the shingle at the jetty. Gabe beside him had gone very still. And Alea, at the doorframe, had her hand flat on the wood, and her face had gone the way it went when she was standing between her father and something.

Thea looked down the shingle.

On the end of the jetty, in the dark, Declan had stood up.

He was standing on the last plank with his back to the cabin, very still, with his arms at his sides. Not looking round. Thea could see the shape of him against the lake, which was black now, with a thin grey line along the far shore where the last of the light was. She couldn’t see his face. She wouldn’t have been able to read it if she could.

She hadn’t said it. She’d been going to say like a nail hole. She hadn’t got there.

Nobody said anything.

“Thea,” said Alea, quietly, from the door. “Finish.”

Thea looked at her.

“Say all of it,” said Alea. “Please. He’s listening. He can hear from there. He always can.” Her hand on the doorframe tightened. “Say what you were going to say.”

Thea looked at the jetty. At the man standing on the last plank with his back to them.

“Like a nail hole,” she said. “Like the hole in a plank where a nail’s been pulled out of old wood. Torn up round the edge. That’s what it looked like.” She swallowed. “And I read the hole. Its grammar. It’s the same kind of word as the one in Astra. A holding word. The kind you’d put in the middle of a circle to make everything stay.” She was quiet. “But it’s not there. The hole’s the shape of where it was. And the circle’s written round the hole anyway, all thirteen rings, all saying come, all pointing in at a middle that’s got nothing in it.”

The cabin was very quiet.

“It’s a mouth,” said Thea. “That’s what I thought, at the end. Standing there. It’s a mouth with nothing in it. And everything in the circle goes toward it, the way water goes toward a drain. Not because it wants anything. Because it’s empty.” She pulled the blanket tighter. “I didn’t go in. I stopped. I didn’t have to go in to know.”

On the jetty, Declan didn’t move.

He didn’t come up.

Thea sat on the stool with the blanket round her and watched him not come up. A long time. Long enough that Xola put the kettle on, and it boiled, two notes, the way that kettle always did, and nobody poured it. Long enough that Higu wrote something in her notebook, under the box, and turned the notebook face down on her knee so nobody could see it.

Then Gigi got up off the floor.

She didn’t say anything. She went out past Bram, who moved, and past Deston and Gabe on the step, and past Alea at the doorframe, who looked at her and didn’t stop her. Down the shingle in the dark, not fast. Not to the jetty. To the top of the lake road, where her bench was, the roots that had grown up into a seat and then a back and then, at ice-out, leaves. She sat down on it.

Facing the jetty. Not going out to it. Just sitting there, where he’d see her if he turned round.

He didn’t turn round. But after a while Thea saw him sit down again, on the last plank, with his legs over the edge, the way he always did. As if something that had been standing up in him had sat down.

Alea let go of the doorframe.

“Thank you,” she said. To Thea.

“Did I say something,” said Thea. She hadn’t meant to ask. It came out of her. “Alea. Did I say something I shouldn’t have.”

Alea looked at her for a long time. She had her father’s way of looking at you, all at once, with nothing held back.

“Yes,” she said. “No. You said something true.” She looked at Deston, on the step. Deston looked at his hands. “It isn’t yours to know yet, what it means. It isn’t mine to tell you. It’s his.” She was quiet. “He’ll tell you. He’s going to. Not tonight.”

Thea sat with that.

She was a reader. She’d read the window in the first spring, all the way down, and found it was written, every line, in English, by somebody who’d once been a boy in the other world. She’d read a scroll in a vault and a circle in a valley and fifteen folds of a black sword. She knew what it felt like when a thing was being held back from her on purpose. It felt like this: a room full of people she loved, looking at their hands.

Rule five. Group 1’s. We never write Alea. We never write the fish. We never write Wrenhaven. Alea’s rule, from the first winter, when she’d stood between her father and all of them. He watched us not tell anyone.

“Okay,” said Thea.

Higu turned her notebook back over. Thea saw what she’d written, under the box and the 402 and the four lines.

Don’t ask. (Rule five.) He’s got a bench.

She posted it at midnight, from the step, with Higu beside her, because Higu said she wanted to see it before it went and Thea didn’t believe her.

She didn’t post all of it. She thought about it for a long time first. She was the one who posted things; it was what she was for. Higu’s rules said post everything you read, so nobody else had to read it, so nobody got hurt. And she was going to. Just not the part that wasn’t hers.

She shut the window.

She knew what she’d done. She’d seen Deston’s line come up, not yet, Cursiffe. please, and Cursiffe’s ok. sorry, and she’d known. Somebody had known what the middles were for, once. Cursiffe had found it, somewhere in Solmere, and it was tied up with whatever had made Declan stand up on the end of the jetty, and Group 4 had it, and Cursiffe had it, and Alea had it. And Thea, who read everything, didn’t.

She sat on the step and let it be that.

It was very hard. It was nearly as hard as stopping at the edge of the hole. She was the one who read things; she’d always been. Here she’d read the window and the circle and the sword. And there was a thing a hundred paces away on the end of a jetty that she could have walked down to and asked, and he’d have told her, probably, because he never lied to anybody.

She didn’t.

I don’t have to go in to know it’s empty.

She’d learned it tonight, in the middle of a circle. She thought it might be true of people too. She thought you could stand at the edge of a thing somebody was carrying and know it was heavy, and not reach in. You could stop first.

She opened her window, because it was the only thing left to look at.

Thirteen. Four points, in one night. It had sat at nine since the folds.

She looked at it a long time.

Then she shut it, and sat on the step with the blanket round her, beside Higu, who’d shut her notebook at last and was just sitting, and looked down the shingle at the jetty.

He was still there. Sitting on the last plank with his legs over the edge and his back to them, the way he’d sat every night of her life here. The lake black in front of him. The thin grey line along the far shore gone now. And at the top of the lake road, on a bench made of roots, a small figure in the dark, facing him, not going any closer.

Behind her, in the cabin, the lamp on the table hissed a little, the way it always did, the wick a hair too high.

“Higu,” said Thea.

“Mm.”

“What’s Cursiffe not saying?”

Higu didn’t answer for a long time.

“I don’t know,” she said at last. “I didn’t ask.” She leaned her shoulder against Thea’s. “He’s got a bench.”

On the jetty, Declan lifted his hand. Not to them. Not turning round. Just lifted it, a little, the way you’d lift a hand to somebody across a field to say I know you’re there.

At the top of the lake road, on the bench, Gigi lifted hers back.

Thea watched them do it. She didn’t know what it meant. She didn’t Parse it.

She let it be what it was.
