The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 4: Threads

Chapter 95

Chalk

3,078 words · 13 min read

Bee’s wings still didn’t work.

She’d stopped mentioning it a long time ago. She’d stopped mentioning it the way you stop mentioning a limp: not because it stops being there, but because everybody’s already seen it, and saying it again just makes them look at you with that face. They were bee’s wings. Thin and clear and veined, folded flat against her back under her coat, a gift from a drawing of herself she’d made years ago on Earth and never once thought would be real. They twitched when she was angry. They buzzed, very faintly, when she was happy, which wasn’t often. They didn’t lift her off the ground.

I’ll learn, she’d told Hyvred, in the summer, before the deep patrol. Out of spite.

She hadn’t learned yet. But she hadn’t stopped being spiteful, either, so she counted it as ongoing.

The Pollen Puffs flew for her. That was something. Two round fuzzy bees the size of plums, yellow and black, who’d come through the floor on her shoulders the first morning and had been the only creatures in the summoning hall not crying. They flew everywhere. They flew round her head when she laughed, which was less often than they’d have liked. They flew to Tiger, mostly, these days. They seemed to think he needed it.

He did.

Thornwall was buried.

The snow had come early to the Greyspines, the way the soldiers said it always did, and it had kept coming. By the middle of the winter it was up to the sills of the barracks windows, and the paths across the courtyard were dug out like trenches, and the walls had a crust of white along the top that the soldiers on watch had to kick off every morning. The pass was shut. Hild said so. Fenwick said so, with charts. Nothing was going to come over the top of the Greyspines until the thaw, and nothing was going to go up, either.

Which meant the patrols had stopped. Which meant Hyvred couldn’t go up to the top every week and stand on the old paved road and look.

She looked anyway. From the top of the north-west tower, on clear days, with her glasses on and her face doing the hawk thing. She couldn’t see over the mountains. Nobody could. But she could see the mouth of the pass, high up, a notch of white against the white, and she said she could feel, sometimes, at the very edge of the Scope, the absence on the other side of it. Not see. Feel. The way you feel somebody standing behind a door.

“Quiet,” she said, every time she came down. “It’s quiet.”

“Asleep?” said Kei.

“Not asleep.” Hyvred took her glasses off and rubbed her eyes. “Waiting. The same as before. Just under more snow.”

Hisui, round Kei’s neck, tasted the air coming down off the mountains with his green head up, and for the first time since the summer didn’t say it tasted like the bottom of a well.

“Just snow,” said Kei, translating. “He says it just tastes of snow.” He scratched the snake under the chin. “He says he doesn’t trust it.”

Bee drew every day.

She’d always drawn every day. On Earth, on a tablet, for hours, for streams, for herself. Here, with charcoal from the fire and chalk from Corvin’s stores and anything she could find to draw on: the walls, the backs of maps, the whitewashed section of the old granary that Corvin had given her in the first summer, please, Bee, just this one wall, please stop drawing on my maps.

She drew dogs, mostly. Since the spring. Since the thing with Ossie’s face had come in through the gate and smiled at everyone and she’d drawn a dog on the gatehouse and written Tell him under it, and it had barked, three heartbeats, and every dog in Thornwall had answered. She’d drawn one on every wall after that. The gatehouse, the barracks, the granary, the shed. A dog on every wall, with Tell him underneath.

They barked at night, sometimes. When the absence looked at them. They’d barked all together in the autumn, the night it turned round and faced Thornwall for the length of time it took to count to fifty, and then they’d stopped.

They hadn’t barked since the snow came.

They’d faded, some of them. Chalk didn’t last, in the weather. She went round every week and drew them again.

She’d been at ninety-eight percent for a month.

She knew why. Everybody did, by now. Thea’s letter, the first winter. You have to write. One small line, in its language, not yours. She’d made a sparrow move once with a single charcoal line, before she even knew what that meant. Three heartbeats, then four. She’d made a dog bark by writing words under it. Tell him.

But that was words. Words under a picture. And she’d been sitting at ninety-eight for a month, writing words under pictures, Go and Run and Stay and Here, and nothing.

She’d said it herself, in the first summer, to Hyvred and Kei, after the sparrow. Drawing’s writing. Before there were letters, there were pictures. The first writing anyone ever did was drawing.

She’d said it. She’d never actually listened to it.

She listened to it on the morning of the long dark.

The shortest day of the winter. The sun barely came over the mountains at all, just a smear of grey-gold on the edge of the world for a few hours, and then went down again. Bee went out to the old granary in the middle of the morning with a fresh stick of chalk, to draw the dog again, because it had faded almost to nothing.

Tiger was on the shed roof.

She could see him from the granary. Across the courtyard, over the trenches in the snow. Ossie’s shed, the one he’d built three times, with its roof that had stood through the summer storms and now stood under a foot of snow. Tiger was sitting on the ridge of it, with his knees up and his short blades across his lap and his tiger ears flat, looking north-west at the white notch of the pass.

He went up there every day now. Since the snow closed the pass. He sat on the roof of Ossie’s shed and looked at the place where Ossie wasn’t, for an hour, two, and came down when Drew came and stood at the bottom of the ladder.

Drew wasn’t there yet.

The Pollen Puffs were. Both of them, sitting on Tiger’s shoulders in the snow, pressed against his neck.

Bee stood in front of the granary wall with the chalk in her hand and looked at him for a long time.

She didn’t write anything.

She didn’t think about it. She was too tired of thinking about it. She just put the chalk on the whitewash, at the place where the old dog’s nose had been, and drew.

One line.

She didn’t lift the chalk. That was the only thing she decided. Once she’d put it down, she didn’t lift it. She let it go where it wanted. Nose, and the long line of the muzzle, and up over the brow, and the ear, pricked, and down the neck and along the back, a long low dog, running, and the tail, out straight behind, and back under along the belly to the back legs, stretched out, and the front legs, reaching. All of it. One line. Never once lifting the chalk off the wall. The way she’d drawn the sparrow the first summer, by accident, with a single line of charcoal from her finger.

It was the best dog she’d ever drawn. She knew it the moment she finished.

She’d drawn it running.

Not standing on a wall, the way the others stood, with Tell him underneath. Running. Flat out. Ears back. Legs reaching. Going somewhere, as fast as it could, with everything it had.

She found the edge.

It was just there. At the end of the line. Where the chalk stopped. She didn’t need to write anything under the dog. She’d already written it. The whole dog was the line, in the oldest language there was, the one people had written before they’d ever made a letter.

Go, said the line. Without saying it.

The dog went.

It came off the wall.

Bee saw it happen. She’d remember it for the rest of her life. The chalk line lifted. Off the whitewash, all at once, the whole long unbroken line of it, white, in the grey air, the shape of a running dog drawn in light. And it hung there for a heartbeat in front of her face, a hand’s width from the wall.

And then it hit the snow.

Running. Its chalk legs reaching and folding, reaching and folding, the way she’d drawn them, the way a real dog’s go, and paw prints, there were paw prints in the snow behind it, small and clean, one after another. It went across the courtyard in long low bounds, over the trenches, a running line of white light in the grey morning, with its ears back and its tail out straight. Soldiers on the wall turned round. Somebody shouted. A real dog by the barracks door stood up and stared and didn’t bark, because it didn’t know what it was looking at either.

It went straight to the shed.

It went up the ladder.

Bee hadn’t drawn it able to climb. It climbed anyway. Up the rungs of the ladder that leaned against Ossie’s shed, in bounds, the way a dog goes up a steep bank, and onto the snow on the roof, and along the ridge, leaving tiny paw prints in the white.

Tiger turned his head.

Bee saw his face. Across the courtyard, from the granary wall, with the chalk still in her hand. She saw him see it: a dog made of one white line, running along the ridge of the roof toward him through the snow, with its ears back.

It stopped beside him.

It sat down. The way the Pollen Puffs sat down on his shoulders. Right beside him, on the ridge of Ossie’s roof, pressed against his side. Its chalk line head came up and turned and looked at him. And then it put its chalk nose against his arm.

Tiger didn’t move.

Then, very slowly, he put his hand out, and put it on the dog’s back. On the white line. It didn’t break. His hand rested on it as if it were fur.

Bee heard him laugh.

From across the courtyard. Over the snow. Short and surprised and cracked down the middle, the way he hadn’t laughed since the summer. Since before the pass. Since before a voice with Ossie’s face had called up the mountain, Tiger! Come down! It’s warm down here!

“Good boy,” said Tiger.

It lasted a minute.

A whole minute. Bee counted, without meaning to, standing at the granary wall with the chalk in her hand. Sixty heartbeats, and more. The sparrow had lasted four. The dogs had barked three. This one sat on a shed roof in the snow beside her friend for a whole minute, with his hand on its back, while he laughed, and then stopped laughing, and just sat there with it.

Then it faded.

Not all at once. It went the way chalk goes in rain: soft at the edges first, the line blurring, the white going grey, until it was the ghost of a dog sitting on a roof, and then the ghost of a ghost. And then it was just Tiger, with his hand resting on the snow on the ridge where a dog had been, and a line of small clean paw prints going away behind him across the roof and down the ladder and back across the courtyard to a granary wall.

He didn’t move his hand for a long time.

Bee opened her window.

She did it standing at the granary wall, with the chalk still in her fingers and the white dust on her hand. She knew what it would say. She looked anyway.

[ Bee · Tier 1: Spark · Skills: Sketch ]

The sixteenth.

Sketch. Not drawing. Not anything grand. Sketch. The quick thing. The one line you put down before you’ve thought about it, to catch something before it goes. The thing she’d done ten thousand times on a tablet, at two in the morning, for nobody.

She stood there and looked at it, and her wings buzzed.

Not twitched. Buzzed. Under her coat, against her back, a sound like a bee in a jar, very loud in the silent courtyard. She felt them. Both of them, the thin veined wings she’d stopped mentioning, lifting off her back under the wool, beating, once, twice, faster than she’d ever felt them go. Her heels came up off the snow. Just her heels. Just for a heartbeat.

Then they settled again, and folded flat, and she was standing on the ground in the snow with her mouth open.

“Out of spite,” said Bee, out loud, to nobody, and burst out laughing.

Drew was at the bottom of the ladder when she got to the shed.

He’d come out of the barracks at the shout. He was standing in the trench at the foot of Ossie’s shed, looking up at the roof, at Tiger sitting there with his hand on the snow and the line of paw prints. He turned his head when Bee came across the courtyard. He looked at the chalk dust on her hands.

“Was that you?”

“That was me.”

Drew looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked back up at the roof.

“He laughed,” said Drew.

“I know. I heard.”

“I haven’t heard him laugh like that since the summer.” Drew’s voice was very quiet, the calming voice, the one that made you listen. “Not since the pass.” He was quiet a moment. “Twelve years, Bee. I’ve been trying to make him laugh like that since the summer. I tell him I’m slow. I tell him he’d have to wait for me. I get a laugh, sometimes. Not that one.” He looked at her. “You drew a dog.”

“I drew a dog,” said Bee.

“Thank you,” said Drew.

Bee looked at the ground. She’d never been good with thank you. She didn’t know where to put it.

“It was a good dog,” she said, gruffly.

“It was a very good dog,” said Drew.

Tiger came down the ladder a while later. He didn’t say anything. He came down and stood in the trench in the snow, with the Pollen Puffs on his shoulders, and looked at Bee, and his tiger ears were up for the first time in a month.

Then he hugged her. Hard. Without a word. The way you’d hug someone at an airport.

Bee stood there and let him, with her arms at her sides and chalk dust on her hands and her wings buzzing very faintly against her back, and didn’t know where to put that either.

“Can it come back?” said Tiger, into her hair.

“I can draw another one.”

“Tomorrow?”

“Every day,” said Bee. “If you want.”

Tiger let go. He looked at her. His face did something complicated.

“Every day,” he said.

She wrote it in the channel that night. Spark. Sketch. Sixteenth. I drew a dog in one line and it ran for a minute. And my wings worked. For one heartbeat. Out of spite.

The channel went the way it always went. Two hundred lines now, so she could scroll back and watch it happen: Maigo in capitals, SIXTEENTH, and then A DOG, and then WINGS???. Kei, from across the courtyard, who’d seen it: Hisui says it was a very good dog. Yan, from Cinderhold, who’d gone up a tier himself the week before, the fourteenth, with a skill called Kin: I felt your candle go up. Congratulations, Bee. Hana, from the capital, the fifteenth, who’d started making cords that remembered each other: Bee! I’m going to make you a pair for Tiger. Rachael. Newish. Chris with his column.

And Hyvred, from the next bunk, who didn’t write in the channel at all. She just reached over in the dark and squeezed Bee’s hand.

She went back to the granary in the morning.

The old dog was gone. Not faded. Gone. Where it had been, on the whitewash, there was nothing. Just the clean white wall, and, on the snow at the foot of it, a line of small clean paw prints going away across the courtyard toward the shed, half filled with new snow.

She stood there and looked at the empty wall for a long time.

Then she took out a fresh stick of chalk.

She didn’t draw a dog. Not yet. She’d draw one later, for Tiger, the way she’d promised. Every day. First she drew something else. Something small, low on the wall, near the bottom, where the dog’s front paws had been.

A heart.

A small one, quick, in one line, the way you’d draw it at the bottom of a message to someone without thinking. The way somebody had once drawn one, in a chat window, years ago, under a picture she’d posted of the Pollen Puffs, when she’d known him for four months and they’d never really talked and she’d thought nothing of it.

She hadn’t cried when he died. She’d said so. She’d been sorry for Hyvred, and for everybody else, and she’d been sorry for herself, a bit, in the way you’re sorry about someone you meant to get to know better. She hadn’t cried.

She stood at the granary wall in the snow and looked at the small chalk heart for a long time.

She didn’t cry now, either.

But she left it there. She didn’t draw over it. Every day for the rest of the winter, when she came out to draw Tiger’s dog, she drew it a little higher on the wall, so as not to touch it.

And every day, on the snow at the foot of the wall, there was a new line of small clean paw prints going away across the courtyard to the shed. And every day, from the roof of the shed, for a minute, Bee could hear somebody laugh.
