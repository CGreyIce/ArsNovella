The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 10: The Sword's Country

Chapter 276

Folds

5,053 words · 22 min read

Thea had never read a sword.

She’d thought about it, on and off, for three years. Not this one; any of them. She’d sat in the militia hall in the first winter with Bram’s old sword across her knees, when she was still at Spark and everything was new, and looked at it with Parse the way she looked at everything, and seen nothing. Steel. A grain in it, the way there’s a grain in wood, and a little of Bram’s aura gone into it over twenty years like oil into a board. No writing. No lattice. A sword wasn’t a spell; it was a thing. You read things the ordinary way, with your eyes.

She’d never once tried it on the black one. On either half. She’d sat next to Astra at supper for two years and watched it hang on Alea’s back on the shingle and on the jetty and in the stands at the Lists, and never looked at it with anything but her eyes. She didn’t know why, exactly. It had felt like reading somebody’s letters.

And then Gabe had done sums on an anvil in Kingsferry, at midnight, and written it’s not thousands. it’s fifteen. it doubles. And somebody from home made it. And she’d sat up in bed in the room over the militia hall with her window open in front of her face and Higu asleep in the next bed, and read it four times, and thought: a smith from home. Who folded it fifteen times. Who knew how to do that.

What else did they know how to do?

Alea brought it down herself.

Not to the militia hall; to the cabin. That had been Alea’s one condition, and Thea hadn’t argued with it, because it was the right one. At the table, Alea had said, on the shingle, two days ago, with her ears very still. Where he can see. He said you can read it. I want him there when you do.

So it was the cabin, again, at dusk, the way it had been the night she read the valley through John’s picture; and it was small again with all of them in it. Higu at the end of the table with her notebook open and three pencils, her coat buttoned wrong. Gigichi on the bench under the window with her knees up and her eyes on Thea’s wrist, waiting to put her thumb on it. Xola with a blanket. PenguChi in the corner by the shelf, slow and enormous and calm, with Waddles asleep in her lap. Maren in the other stool’s place, on a crate, with her new pink hands folded in her lap the way she held them now, as if they were somebody else’s that she’d been lent. And Bram in the doorway with his shoulder against the frame and the cold coming in round him, because he’d said I’ll be the door the last time and seemed to think it was his job now.

And Declan, on his own stool by the stove, with a cup of tea in his hands that he wasn’t drinking.

He hadn’t said anything when they came down the lake road with their lanterns. He’d opened the door and stood aside to let them in, the way he always did, and put the kettle on, and sat down. He looked the way he’d looked for the last week, since the day on the ice that Alea had come up the lake road afterwards and told Thea about, in pieces, on the shingle, with her face very calm and her ears flat. A hill. Rings in the grass. A cold breath out of a hole he’d never looked into. It was somebody’s. He looked like a man who’d taken something heavy off a high shelf after a long time and found it was heavier than he remembered.

Alea had lit the lamp at the end of the jetty at dusk, the way she always did now, and come in, and taken Astra off her back.

She laid it on the table in front of Thea. On a folded blanket, so it wouldn’t mark the wood. The black blade, narrow, the length of Thea’s arm and a bit; the king had drawn it narrow for a fox girl of seventeen out of a sword built for a grown man, and Declan had struck it. The grip was old brown leather, dark with years of hands, with a sharkskin underlay showing at the edges where Gabe had rewrapped it. A strip of an army saddle, cut off with a knife by a boy of nineteen in the middle of Kharvos.

Alea sat down on the bench beside Thea. Not across from her. Beside.

“It’s yours,” Thea said. “If you want me to stop—“

“I know.” Alea didn’t look at her. She looked at the sword. “Read it.”

“How long,” said Higu.

“Three hundred.” Thea had thought about it all day. “It’s not a picture. It’s not far off. It’s right here, on a table. But it’s dense; Gabe says it’s dense, and Gabe’s never wrong about iron.” She looked at Higu. “Three hundred, and door.”

Higu wrote it down. 300. DOOR. She underlined door twice and drew a box round it, and then, after a moment’s thought, drew another box round the box.

“And if it looks at you,” said Higu.

“It won’t. It’s a sword.” Thea put her hands flat on the table either side of the blanket. “Swords don’t look.”

“That’s a theory,” said Higu.

“Everything’s a theory.”

Gigichi leaned forward and put her thumb on the inside of Thea’s left wrist, on the pulse, and held it there. Her thumb was cold. It always was. “Seventy-eight,” she said. “Go on, then.”

Thea looked at Declan.

He nodded. Once. He didn’t say anything.

She put her right hand flat on the black of the blade, halfway down, where the edge didn’t reach; and opened Parse.

At first there was nothing.

That was what she’d expected; it was what Bram’s sword had done, in the first winter. Steel. Black, under her palm, cold, heavier than it looked. A grain. She could see the grain the way Gabe must have felt it, with Parse laid over her eyes like a second pair of lenses: fine lines in the black, running the length of the blade, side by side, closer than the lines on a fingertip. She tried to count them and couldn’t. They were finer than hair. Finer than the threads in a spider’s web. A field of them, all the way through, edge to edge, packed together like the pages of a closed book seen from the side.

Pages.

She hadn’t meant to think it. It was just what it looked like. A closed book, seen edge on.

She leaned in. Not with her body. With whatever it was in her that read. She leaned in toward one of the lines, the nearest, at the very surface of the black under her palm, the way you’d lean toward one word on a crowded page; and the line opened.

It was writing.

Not like a window. Not the lattice, the code under the words that she’d been reading every morning for three years. This was the other thing, the thing she’d been learning to read for two years: the circle’s language. The thing the hall in Solmere was written in, ring on ring, and the valley at Thornwall, written in people. She couldn’t read what it said. She’d never been able to; nobody could. But she could read what it was, the shape of it and the grammar, the way you can tell a question from an order in a language you don’t speak by where the voice goes up.

And this was one word.

One. Written along the whole length of the line, from the tang to the point, in one stroke, so fine she could only see it because she was leaning right into it. One word, said once. And then, folded over, lying on itself, the same word again. And again. The line wasn’t one line. It was a word written, and the bar folded so the word lay on itself, and beaten out, and folded again, so that two lay on two; and again, and again; and every fold doubled it; and the black under her palm was nothing but that word, said over and over and over, down and down and down through the blade like the floors of a house with no ground floor.

Fifteen folds, Gabe had written. Thirty-two thousand, seven hundred and sixty-eight.

She was reading the first of them.

“Thirty,” said Higu, very far away.

The word was a holding word.

She could tell that much at once. It was the grammar of it; it was the first thing you saw. She’d spent the autumn learning the shape of a different word, the one written down the left arc of the fourteenth ring in the valley at Thornwall in twenty-three people with nothing in them: Come. A pulling word. A word you say to something, that leans toward it and draws. She’d read it through Hyvred’s eyes on a night in the autumn and felt it pull at her down the line like a hand on a sleeve, and cut herself off from it, and bled for three days.

This was that kind of word. The same kind. A word you say to something.

But it was the other way round.

It didn’t lean. It didn’t draw. It was said downward, the way you say something to a dog that’s lying at your feet; it pointed down, into whatever the sword had been standing in, and it pressed, steadily, without any hurry at all. It was what you’d say to a thing you didn’t want to come. Not go. Not away. It wasn’t pushing anything off. It was holding it where it was.

“Sixty,” said Higu.

Stay.

She didn’t hear it. There wasn’t any sound; there never was, in the circle’s language. She just knew it, the way you know what a face means. If Come was a hand on a sleeve, this was a hand laid flat on a lid. And it had been written by somebody who knew exactly what they were doing, and meant it with everything they had, and then folded it into itself fifteen times so it would never, ever wear out.

She thought: it isn’t the hall’s hand.

That was the second thing. She knew the hall’s hand. She’d read it through seven people at once at One Pour; she’d read it in the valley through Hyvred and again through John’s picture. It was careful. Old and very careful, every stroke the same weight, like somebody copying a thing out exactly because they were afraid of getting it wrong. This wasn’t that. This was the same language, written by somebody else. Somebody who’d learned it. You could see the learning in it, the way you can see in somebody’s handwriting that they came to the alphabet late. A stroke a little heavier than it needed to be, here. A curve a little too round, there, like a beginner’s. And then, at the end of the word, a little flourish where the hall’s hand would have stopped clean: a tail, a twist, a thing you’d do if you’d learned a language from a book and then got good at it and started, very slightly, showing off.

She knew that flourish. She’d done it herself. In the margin of Maren’s chant book, in the second winter, when she’d first understood the shape of the word for kindle and written it out twenty times in a row to see if she could, and on the twentieth she’d put a little tail on the end, without meaning to, because it was hers now.

Somebody from home, Gabe had said.

Somebody who’d learned to read it.

“A hundred,” said Higu.

She went down.

She hadn’t decided to. That was the thing she’d tell them, afterwards, and they’d believe her, because it was true. She’d been leaning toward the first line, the top one, the word said once; and when she’d read it, there was the next one under it, folded onto it, the same word; and it was so easy to read the next one. It was the same. She already knew it. You just leaned a little further and there it was, Stay, said again in the same learned hand with the same little flourish at the end, and under it the next, and the next, like going down a stair you know so well you don’t have to look at your feet.

Stay.

Stay.

It wasn’t frightening. That was what made it dangerous, and she knew it was dangerous, and couldn’t make herself mind. It was the quietest thing she’d ever read. Every word she’d read in three years had wanted something: a window wanting to be looked at, a spell wanting to be cast, a circle wanting to pull thirty-five people out of their lives. This didn’t want anything. It just said what it said, steadily, over and over, a hand laid flat on a lid; and every time it said it, a little more of her stopped wanting to be anywhere else.

Stay.

She was very deep. She knew that, the way you know you’ve been asleep a long time without knowing how long. The lines had stopped being lines. They’d stopped being anything except the word, said again and again, closer and closer, so that she couldn’t see the top any more, or the table, or the cabin; just the word, and the next one under it, and the next.

“Door,” said Higu.

She heard it. A long way up. A voice, saying a word she’d asked it to say. She knew what it meant. It meant come out. It meant three hundred heartbeats, and she’d said three hundred, and Higu was keeping her promise.

She thought about coming out. It seemed like a good idea. She thought she probably would, in a while.

Stay.

“Door,” said Higu again, louder. And then, in a different voice, not to her: “She’s not coming.”

She heard Gigichi say something. A number, sharp. She heard a stool go over. None of it was very interesting. She was reading. She’d never in her life read anything so easy, or so quiet, or so sure; and it was telling her the only thing it had ever told anyone, and it had been telling it for longer than the kingdom had had a name, and it was very, very good at it.

Stay.

And then there was a hand on hers.

Not on her wrist; Gigichi was on her wrist, she could feel that now, very far off, a cold thumb pressing. On her hand. The right one, the one on the blade. Somebody’s hand had come down over hers and closed on it, warm, and moved it.

Not off the sword. Along it. Up the black, toward the grip, a hand’s breadth at a time, the way you’d guide a child’s hand along a line of print; until Thea’s fingers came off the end of the blade onto something that wasn’t black and wasn’t cold and wasn’t folded.

Leather.

Old leather. Soft, and dark with years of hands, and warm from the room. A strip of an army saddle cut off with a knife on a black rock in the middle of Kharvos, by a boy who didn’t know why he wanted something of it. It wasn’t written. It didn’t say anything. It didn’t hold anything down. It was just a thing a person had wrapped round a sword so they could carry it.

She came up.

All at once, the way you come up out of water: the cabin, and the stove, and the lamp, and the noise of everybody breathing. Her face was wet. She didn’t know with what. Gigichi had her wrist in both hands and was saying a number, very loudly, a hundred and forty, Thea, a hundred and forty, and Higu was standing up with her stool on its side behind her and her notebook crushed in one fist, and Xola was making the kettle sound, and Bram had come in off the doorframe and was standing in the middle of the cabin floor with his big hands open, as if he’d been about to catch something.

And Alea was beside her on the bench with her hand over Thea’s hand on the grip of the sword, holding it there, on the leather. Holding it hard.

“You’re out,” said Alea.

“I’m out.”

“You didn’t come when Higu said.”

“No.” Thea looked at their two hands on the old brown leather. Alea’s was shaking. So was hers. “No. I couldn’t. It said—“ She stopped. She breathed. “Can you let go? Not of me. Of— I want to take my hand off it.”

Alea let go. Thea took her hand off the sword and put it in her lap, and looked at it, and it was just a hand.

“Five hundred and nine,” said Higu. Her voice was perfectly flat, the way it went when she’d been more frightened than she was going to admit. “You said three hundred. I said door at three hundred. I said it again at three hundred and forty. You didn’t come.” She put the notebook down on the table, very carefully, and smoothed the crushed page with her palm. “You didn’t even flinch. Every other time you’ve gone too far you’ve flinched at door. You smiled.”

“I’m sorry.”

“I don’t want sorry.” Higu sat down. She picked up the stool first, and set it on its feet, and sat on it, and put both hands flat on the table. “I want to know what it did.”

Thea told them.

She told it in order, the way she’d tell a bug: the lines, the pages seen edge on, the first word. The writing. The circle’s language, one word, said once and folded onto itself and folded again, all the way down through the blade, thirty-two thousand times. The grammar of it. The same kind of word as Come, the left arc at Thornwall, a word you say to something. Turned round. Said downward, onto a lid.

“I can’t read what it says,” she said. “I’ve never been able to read what any of it says. Only what it is.” She looked at the black blade on the blanket. “But if I had to put it in English. If you made me.”

“Say it,” said Gigichi.

“Stay.”

Nobody said anything.

“It holds,” said Thea. “That’s all it does. It doesn’t push anything away and it doesn’t pull anything in. It says stay, to whatever’s under it, and it presses. Steadily. And it’s been folded fifteen times so it says it thirty-two thousand times at once, and it’ll never wear out.” She swallowed. “And if you read it, it says it to you. That’s why I didn’t come out. Not because it was holding me. Because I didn’t want to come. It’s the most…” She looked for the word. “It’s the most sure thing I’ve ever read. Everything else I’ve ever read wanted something. That doesn’t. It’s just sure.”

“And it’s not the hall’s hand,” said Maren.

They looked at her. She’d been sitting on the crate with her pink hands in her lap the whole time, not saying anything, the way she sat in the militia hall when Thea read; and she’d been watching Thea’s face, Thea understood, and reading it, the way she’d learned to read the language, slowly, word by word.

“No,” said Thea. “How did you—“

“You frowned. At the end. The way you frown at my handwriting.” Maren almost smiled. “You said once that the hall was written by somebody frightened of getting it wrong. You didn’t frown like that.”

“It’s not frightened,” said Thea. “It’s somebody who learned it. The same language. But you can see the learning. A stroke too heavy. A curve too round. And at the end there’s a…” She drew it in the air with one finger, a little twist. “A tail. Where the hall would stop clean. You do that when you’ve learned to write something and you’re good at it and it’s yours. I did it in your chant book, the second winter. On kindle.”

“I remember,” said Maren. “I thought it was a blot.”

“It wasn’t a blot.”

“No,” said Maren. “I know that now.”

Declan hadn’t moved.

He was still on his stool by the stove, with the cup in his hands. He’d been there the whole time; she’d seen him, she thought, when she first came up, a shape by the stove in the edge of the lamplight, not standing up when everybody else did. He wasn’t looking at the sword. He was looking at the floor in front of his feet.

“Stay,” he said.

Very quietly. To nobody.

Thea didn’t say anything. None of them did. Alea had gone very still beside her on the bench, with her ears flat and her hands in her lap, looking at her father.

“I carried it eight years,” he said. “On my back. Through all of it. And I put it in the ground over her and walked away.” He turned the cup in his hands. “And the whole time it was saying stay.”

“To what’s under it,” said Thea. “Not to you. It’s said downward. To whatever it’s standing in.”

“I know.” He was quiet. “I know what you said.” He looked up, then, at last; not at her. At the sword on the blanket. “I stood it over her. In the ground. Point down. And it said stay. Ten years. To her.” Something happened in his face that she couldn’t read; nobody could read his face, it was the one thing about him they’d all stopped trying to do. But she could read his hands, and his hands had gone very tight on the cup. “And to the cold under her. Yan says it holds the cold down. The headstone. The point of it.” He breathed out. “I didn’t know it was saying anything. I just put it there because it was the only thing I had.”

“It’s a good word,” said Alea.

He looked at her.

“It’s a good word, Dad.” Her voice wasn’t steady. “If you were going to put something over her. If you could have chosen. You’d have chosen that one.” She put her hand on the leather of the grip, flat, the way she’d put it on Thea’s. “Somebody made it to say that. Somebody from home. And you found it, and carried it, and put it where it was needed most, and it said the right thing the whole time. Even when you didn’t know.”

He didn’t say anything for a long time.

Then he put the cup down on the floor beside the stool, and got up, and came across the cabin, and put his hand on the top of his daughter’s head, between her ears, for a moment. He didn’t say anything. He didn’t need to. Then he went out of the door past Bram, onto the step, and sat down on it in the cold with his back to them all, looking at the lamp on the end of the jetty.

Nobody went after him. That was the rule, in this cabin. You didn’t.

Her window was open. She hadn’t opened it.

Nine. It had been seven since the night she read the valley at this table through a picture nobody was holding. Two points. For reading a sword, she thought, at first. And then, no: she hadn’t read it. Not really. She’d gone down into it like a stone into a well and been brought out by somebody else’s hand on a strip of saddle leather. You didn’t get points for that.

For the flourish, maybe. For seeing a little twist at the end of a word in a dead language, three hundred years old or six hundred or more, and knowing it, because she’d put the same twist on the end of kindle in the margin of a chant book in the second winter, and Maren had thought it was a blot.

For finding out that somebody had been here before her. Not in the hall; everybody had been in the hall. Here. At a table, with the language, learning to read it a stroke at a time the way she had, from nothing, until it was theirs. And had written one word with it, the most sure word she’d ever read, and folded it into a star fifteen times, and given it to the ground.

She closed the window.

Higu was writing. Gigichi still had her wrist, though there was no need any more; she’d stopped saying numbers, and just held it. PenguChi had Waddles awake in her lap, blinking. Xola had put the blanket round Thea’s shoulders at some point and Thea hadn’t noticed. Maren was looking at her own pink hands. Bram had gone back to the doorway.

And Alea was sitting beside her on the bench with the black sword across her knees again, her hand flat on the old leather of the grip; looking at the door, where her father was sitting on the step in the cold with his back to all of them, looking at a lamp she’d lit.

“Will he be all right?” said Thea, very quietly.

Alea thought about it, seriously, the way she thought about everything; with her ears forward and her chin down.

“Yes,” she said. “Not tonight.” She turned the sword a little on her knees, so the lamplight went along the black. “He just found out the thing he put over Mum was saying something kind. For ten years. And he didn’t know.” She was quiet. “That takes a night.”

He was still on the step when they went up the lake road with their lanterns, an hour later.

Thea looked back once, from the corner of the field wall, where the road turned. She could see the cabin, small and dark against the white of the lake, with one window lit and the smoke going straight up. She could see the jetty going out over the ice, thirty-four planks, white with frost. She could see the lamp on its post at the end, small and yellow and steady, that a fox girl had lit at dusk.

And she could see him, on the step. A shape in an old coat with the elbows gone, sitting with his hands between his knees, the way he sat, looking at the light.

She didn’t open Parse. She didn’t need to. Some things you read the ordinary way, with your eyes.

She thought she knew what he was doing. She’d sat with Higu on the edge of her bed in the room over the militia hall, in the first winter, a long time ago, on a night when she’d missed her mum so badly she couldn’t speak, and not said anything, and been sat with; and she thought it was the same thing. Not grieving, exactly. Not any more. Just sitting with somebody for a while, who wasn’t there; and telling them, in a language with no words in it, the thing you’d only just found out.

It was saying stay.

The whole time.

Higu’s hand found hers in the dark, on the lake road, not quite holding it, the way Higu did. They walked up to the town together under the stars, with the lanterns swinging, and didn’t say anything; and behind them, on a step at the end of a lake road, a man sat on in the cold with his tea going cold beside him, keeping somebody company.
