The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 6: Nine

Chapter 156

The Third Line

4,395 words · 19 min read

Niko had worked it out from a frying pan.

He’d never tell anybody that. It was too stupid. Two years on the edge of the red, two candles in his chest that wouldn’t stay together, a nosebleed a week and a grey line in his window that nobody in two worlds had ever seen, and the thing that finally showed him how to get past the wall was the Black Blade making noodles.

But it was true. He’d been on the step.

He’d been on the step outside Oakes’s forge every night of the eleven, with Maigo, eating what came out of the pot and watching. That was what he did. He watched. He’d always watched, on Earth, in games, for years: other people’s hands, other people’s habits, where they went when they were frightened and where they went when they were sure. It was how he’d got good. Not by having the fastest hands. By watching somebody long enough that he knew where they’d be before they did.

He’d been watching the man for thirty-eight days now. Since the east gate of Valcrest. Every day, all day, from the tailboard. The way he carried the buckets. The way he stood on a stone over a stream for an hour and then moved once. The way he arm-wrestled Rhea, which wasn’t strength at all, Niko had noticed by the third night; it was the opposite of strength; he just wasn’t there when she pushed, and then he was. The way he cut shallots. The way he slept, on his back, with one hand on the rod.

And on the seventh night he’d watched him put a skillet into a forge fire bare-handed and toss noodles through the edge of the flame, twelve times, while Salty counted on the bellows, and Niko on the step had stopped eating with the fork halfway to his mouth.

Because the skillet didn’t burn him.

It should have. It was the colour iron goes before you hit it. And Niko had seen why, if he looked the way Thea had taught him once in a letter, with his mana-sight let down, past the light, into the way things moved. The man’s aura had gone out of his hand into the iron handle, the way aura went into a sword. Out. Like breath. And at the top of every toss, when the noodles went up into the smoke and caught the flame and came down, it had come back. Into his hand. Not stopping. Not pushing. Going out into the fire and coming round and coming home again, like the noodles, in one movement, through the hottest part, without a seam.

He hadn’t fought the fire. He’d thrown the food through it.

Twelve times. Salty had counted.

Niko had put his fork down on the step, and sat there with Maigo talking beside him about how good the noodles were, and thought: oh.

Oh, I’ve been doing it wrong.

He went up on the east wall before dawn on the hundred-and-eighth day, a week after the eleventh dinner, to try.

He’d been waiting. He was good at waiting. He’d waited a week because a week was what it took, sometimes, for a thing to settle, the way you don’t play a new strat in a match the morning after you’ve thought of it. You sleep on it. You run it in your head. You let it get boring. And then you try it.

The wall was empty, except for a sentry at the far end by the north tower, and Old Wat going off duty, very slowly, along the walk with his spear. Wat nodded to him. Niko nodded back. Wat went round the gap at the eleventh merlon north of the gate without looking at it, the way he always did, and on down the stairs.

Niko didn’t go near the gap. He went the other way, south of the gatehouse, to the place he’d sat every dawn for two years, with his back against a merlon and his face to the red. It was cold. The end of summer, nearly, and the nights were drawing in. The moon was going down in the west behind the fort, nine days past full, half of it gone. The red was dark. The sky over Kharvos was grey, going pale.

He sat down. He put his hands loose on his knees.

He breathed.

It went the way it always went, at first.

In. Mana, from outside, from the cold air on the wall, the way water goes toward a drain, gathering at his hands, outside his skin. And on the in-breath, without stopping at the top, without the seam, he let it turn: in becoming out, mana becoming aura, the bruise of it coming up in him from underneath to meet what was coming in. Two candles. That was what Yan called them. Side by side in his chest, almost touching, the way they’d been since the first spring.

He let them touch.

It never got easier. Two years, and it never once got easier. Like holding two magnets the wrong way round and pushing them together with your bare hands. Everything in him wanting them apart. Aura goes out. Mana comes in. That was the rule. Every mage in the kingdom knew it. Every swordsman. Aldous had sat on the chapel floor with him in the first winter with his face very stern and very frightened and said nobody has ever done both. Their hearts stopped.

His heart didn’t stop. It went hard and fast against his ribs and he counted it.

One. Two. Three.

The circle closed. Out like a spell, in like breath, round and round, no seam.

Four. Five. Six. Seven.

He felt the grey line come up in his window, under the other two, the way it always did. He didn’t look. Not yet.

Eight. Nine. Ten.

And there it was. The wall.

He’d been hitting it for six weeks.

Since a morning in the empty Lists, when he’d gone from five to eleven in one go with the man watching from the bottom tier. Eleven. And then nothing. Every dawn since, on the wall at Cinderhold and on the tailboard of the cart all the way across the kingdom, with Wendy counting: eleven. Ten, once. Eleven. Never twelve.

It wasn’t a wall, exactly. That was the word he used, in his head, because he didn’t have a better one. It was more like the moment in a game when you’ve held an angle as long as you can hold it and your hand starts to shake on the mouse, and you know, you know, that if you don’t move now you’ll move wrong. At eleven the two candles stopped wanting to go round. They wanted to stop. Everything in him wanted them to stop, all at once, to come apart and go back to their own sides and be two things again, and the wanting got so big and so loud that he couldn’t hold the circle against it, and he’d push, and push, harder, holding them together by main force, with everything he had—

And his nose would bleed, and the circle would break, and Wendy would say eleven.

He’d been pushing. That was the thing. For six weeks. Every time he got to the wall he’d pushed harder against it, the way you’d push a door that was stuck.

The man in the forge hadn’t pushed.

He’d held a skillet at the mouth of a fire hot enough to soften iron, and at the moment when anybody else would have pulled back, when the heat got too much and everything in you said stop, he’d thrown it up. Into the fire. Through it. And let it come back down.

Eleven.

Niko didn’t push.

He let go.

Not all the way. Not of the circle. He kept it going, round, out and in, out and in. But at eleven, when the two candles wanted to come apart, he didn’t hold them together. He let them go up. Out. Away from each other, the way they wanted, out past the edge of him, the aura going out into the cold air on the wall and the mana going out with it, both of them, apart, into the dark, the way the noodles went up out of the skillet into the smoke.

It was the most frightening thing he’d ever done.

It felt like falling. It felt like taking his hand off the mouse in the middle of a round. For half a heartbeat there was nothing in his chest at all, nothing, the two candles gone out of him into the air over the red, and he thought that’s it, that’s what Aldous meant, that’s how the heart stops—

And they came back.

Down. Into him. The way the food came down into the pan. Not apart. Together. Through the edge of something, the way the noodles had gone through the edge of the flame, and come back down into the iron changed. Aura and mana, coming back into his chest at the same moment from outside, through the same place, already round each other, already going round, as if being thrown up into the cold had done the thing he’d been trying to do by force for two years.

Twelve.

His heart hit his ribs like a hammer.

Thirteen.

He didn’t push. He threw it again. Up, and out, into the air. Down, and in, and round.

Fourteen. Fifteen.

Toss it, he thought, with something that might have been laughing, somewhere very far down, under everything. You just toss it.

Sixteen.

The third line lit.

It had lit once before. In the Lists, at dawn, for a few heartbeats. He’d seen it go from grey to white, like a lamp coming on in a window, and seen the shapes on it, faint, like frost on the inside of a glass. Letters. In English, he’d been almost sure. A word, or two words, that he couldn’t read.

It lit now. Under the other two, in his window, in the grey light on the wall.

And this time he looked.

He looked at it the way he’d learned to look at things in two years on the red: not hard. Not trying. Letting his eyes go soft and down, past the light, the way Thea had written in her letter. Watching it, the way you’d watch somebody’s hands across a table.

The shapes were there. The frost-letters. Very faint, under the light.

And they were moving.

He hadn’t seen that in the Lists. He’d only had a few heartbeats, then, and he’d been trying so hard to read them that he hadn’t seen what they were doing. He saw it now. They weren’t sitting still. They weren’t a word he couldn’t make out. They were a word that kept changing its mind. A shape that came up, faint, for a heartbeat, and almost made sense, and then went, and another one came up in its place. And went. And another. The way the letters go, on a phone, when you’re typing and the thing at the top keeps guessing what you mean and getting it wrong.

Seventeen.

And behind the shapes, in the line, under them, something else. He nearly missed it. A very small, very faint mark, at the start of the line, before the shapes. A thin upright stroke of light.

It went on. And off. And on.

Niko stared at it.

He knew what that was. He’d seen it ten thousand times. He’d seen it on the first screen of every game he’d ever started, when he was twelve and fifteen and seventeen and twenty-five: a box, on a black screen, with a word above it, and nothing in the box, and that little upright line at the start of it going on and off. On and off. Waiting.

Enter name.

It wasn’t locked.

It had never been locked. Two years he’d thought it was locked, grey and nameless, a door with no handle. It wasn’t a door. It was a box. A box with nothing in it and a cursor at the start, blinking, waiting for somebody to type.

IT’S A FORM WITH A BLANK BOX, Cursiffe had written on the circle, in the archive, in blue ink. Thea had put it in the channel. Niko had laughed at it at the time.

Eighteen.

His nose started to bleed.

He felt it go, warm, over his lip. He didn’t stop. He held it. He threw it up one more time, out into the cold, and it came back round, and the cursor blinked, and the frost-letters changed their mind again, and—

Nineteen.

The circle broke.

He came back sitting against the merlon with his chin wet and his hands shaking on his knees and the sun just coming up over Kharvos, the first edge of it, red, very bright.

He was laughing. He didn’t know when he’d started. He wiped his nose on the back of his hand and looked at the blood on it in the red light and laughed, quietly, so as not to wake anybody, with his head back against the stone.

Nineteen.

Not eleven. Nineteen.

He opened his window. It opened in front of him, plain and white.

[ Nikorasu · Tier 0: Initiate · Aura 48% · Mana 48% ]

And under them, the third line. Grey again. Unlit. Nameless. With the faintest shape on it, if he let his eyes go down, of frost on a window from the inside.

And at the start of it, so faint he’d never have seen it if he hadn’t known it was there, something that might have been a mark, or might have been nothing.

He looked at it for a long time.

You haven’t finished writing it, the man had said, in the Lists, at dawn. It’s yours. And you haven’t finished writing it.

He’d thought it was a way of talking.

“Nineteen, Mashi,” said somebody, below him.

Niko turned his head. He didn’t need to. Only one person in two worlds had ever called him that.

The man was sitting on the steps. The stone stair that came up from the courtyard to the wall walk beside the gatehouse, about ten steps down, where you couldn’t see him from the walk unless you looked over the inside rail. Sitting the way he always sat, on the end of things: on the bottom tier of the Lists, on the end of a jetty, on the edge of the firelight. With the rod across his knees. Looking up.

Niko hadn’t heard him come. Nobody ever did.

“How long have you been there?”

“Since before the moon went down.”

“You were watching.”

“I was watching.”

“You didn’t help.”

“You said don’t,” said the man.

Niko looked at him. He couldn’t see his face. Nobody could. It slid off your eyes like rain off a window, and he’d stopped trying on a road outside Valcrest. But he didn’t need it. He’d never needed it. He’d spent four hundred rounds learning this man from a name in a kill feed and the way a rifle sat on an angle, and the way he waited, and he knew him from the shoulders the way you’d know a friend’s handwriting.

The shoulders were very still.

Not the ordinary still. Not the jetty still, the waiting still, the man on a stone over a stream. The other kind. The kind Niko had seen exactly twice. Once on the red a year ago, when Salty had said who was she? And once in the empty Lists at dawn, when Niko had said what if in tandem doesn’t mean two people.

“You saw it,” said Niko.

“I saw you get past eleven.”

“Not that.” Niko wiped his nose again. “The line. Something about the line.” He looked at the man. “You went still. When I looked at it. I saw you go still from up here.”

The man didn’t say anything.

“It’s not locked,” said Niko. He heard his own voice. It was perfectly level. It always was; it was the thing everyone said about him, chill, put together. He was neither. His hands were shaking on his knees. “The third line. I thought it was locked. For two years. It isn’t. It’s a box.” He swallowed. “There’s a cursor in it.”

The man was still.

“Like a name box,” said Niko. “At the start of a game. Enter name. And the letters in it keep changing. Like it’s guessing. Like it’s trying things.” He looked at the grey line in his window. “Like it’s waiting for somebody to type.”

The man didn’t answer for a long time.

The sun came up. The red went from dark to rust to gold, the way it did every morning, all the way out to the mountains, and the markers caught the light on their tops in their long rows, and a long way out past them, on a knoll above a dry riverbed, a small tree with green leaves stood up very bright beside a dark stone.

“You know what it is,” said Niko.

“I think I might,” said the man. “I’ve been wrong before.”

“Once.”

“Twice,” said the man. “Once was you.”

Niko looked at him. Something in his chest did a thing he didn’t have a name for, and he let it.

“Are you going to tell me?”

“No.”

“Because it’s mine.”

“Because it’s yours,” said the man. “And you haven’t finished writing it.”

Niko looked at the line.

He thought about it, the way he thought about everything: slowly, from the outside in, the way you’d walk round a map you didn’t know before you played on it. A box. A cursor. Letters that kept trying things. A line in a window that nobody in two worlds had ever had, because nobody in two worlds had ever been both. Every other line in every other window had a word on it already. Drawn. Spark. Tempered. Candle. Words that were there before anybody got to them, that the window had been given by somebody, once, a long time ago, and copied into every window since.

Nobody had ever given it a word for this.

So it didn’t have one.

So it was waiting.

He didn’t say any of that. He didn’t know if it was right. He held it in his head, very carefully, the way you’d hold a smoke grenade with the pin out, and didn’t throw it.

“Okay,” said Niko.

“I’m going to be last,” he said, after a while.

The man looked at him.

“Of the thirty-five.” Niko looked at the grey line. “John got there in Valcrest. That’s twenty-five. There’s ten of us left. Gigichi, Xola, Aleaka, Cursiffe, Tiger, Drew, Kei, Malcolm, Azac. Me.” He counted them off on his fingers. He didn’t need to. He’d counted them every night for a month. “They’re all close. You can see it in the channel. Kei’s at ninety-something. Aleaka’s got that hawk that does what she says before she says it. Azac’s barrier’s been showing up on its own since the Lists.” He let his hand fall. “They’ll go. One at a time. This year, next year. And I’ll be at nineteen heartbeats on a wall.”

“Probably,” said the man.

“I’ve been slowest since the hall. Since the first day. Aldous said it would be like this. Two paths, half the speed of either. Maybe less.” Niko looked out at the red. “Everyone said it was a waste. Doing both. Rhea said it. The soldiers said it. I said it, some nights.”

“It isn’t.”

“I know it isn’t.” Niko was quiet. “I worked that out from a frying pan.” He wiped his chin. “I’m just saying. I’m going to be last. Of thirty-five people. Out of everyone. The very last one.”

The man waited.

“I don’t mind,” said Niko.

He found, saying it, that it was true. He sat on the wall at Cinderhold in the sunrise with blood on his chin and a grey line in his window and nineteen heartbeats behind him, and turned it over, and looked at it from every side, and it was true. He didn’t mind.

“Somebody has to be last,” he said. “Maigo says last is just first but later.” He nearly smiled. “Later. He shouted it. In the channel, in the first winter, when Higu got there before him. THIRD IS JUST FIRST BUT LATER BUT LATER.” He shook his head. “It doesn’t make any sense.”

“It makes a bit of sense.”

“It makes a bit of sense,” Niko admitted. He looked at the line. “If I’m going to be last, I want to be last at this. Not at one of them. Not Drawn. Not Spark. Whatever this is.” He was quiet. “Both. At once. The first time. Anywhere.”

The man didn’t say anything. But the shoulders moved. Just slightly. The way they’d moved on the bottom tier of the Lists, when Niko said I lost to you four hundred times and meant something else.

“Then be last,” said the man.

The others found him at breakfast.

Wendy first, because Wendy always saw things first. She came into the mess with her headphones round her neck and looked at him across the room, at his face, at the blood he hadn’t got all of off his chin, and stopped.

“How many,” said Wendy.

“Nineteen.”

Wendy put her bowl down on the table. Very carefully. The way she’d put down a bow.

“NINETEEN,” said Maigo, from the bench beside him, at a volume that made the cooks drop something. “NIKO SAID NINETEEN. NINETEEN. That’s EIGHT MORE. That’s—Salty, what’s nineteen take away eleven—“

“Eight,” said Salty, with his mouth full.

“EIGHT MORE,” said Maigo, to the mess. “HE DID EIGHT MORE.” He grabbed Niko by both shoulders and shook him. “How? How did you do eight more? You’ve been stuck for six weeks. You’ve been stuck since the Lists. What did you do?”

Niko looked across the mess. At the far end, by the kitchen hatch, the man with the rod was sitting at the end of a bench with his daughter, eating porridge, with his back to them. Very ordinary. A water-carrier, eating his breakfast.

“I watched somebody make noodles,” said Niko.

Maigo stared at him.

Salty, beside him, stopped chewing. He looked at Niko for a long moment. Then down the mess at the man’s back. Then at his own hands, on the table, which had pumped a forge bellows twelve times while a skillet went up into the fire and came down again.

“Huh,” said Salty.

“Yeah,” said Niko.

Yan, at the end of the table, with Suzu on the scythe propped against the wall behind him, hadn’t said anything. Niko looked at him. Yan was looking back, with his half-lidded pale eyes, very calm, at the middle of Niko’s chest. At the two candles. At whatever Yan saw there that nobody else could.

“Closer,” said Yan. Very quietly. Just to him. “They’re closer than I’ve ever seen them.”

“Touching?”

“Not touching.” Yan was quiet. “Going round. Even now. Even sitting still.” He paused. “Slowly. But going round.”

He didn’t put it in the channel.

He thought about it, that night, on his bunk, with his window open and the channel scrolling. Twenty-five of thirty-five, at the top, in Chris’s evening numbers. Higu in Wrenhaven: the basket’s still on the step. nobody touches it. Tiger in capitals about a shed. Hana, from Solmere, something careful about a door under the castle that Niko read twice and didn’t understand. Kei: Hisui says the wind off the valley tastes different this week. like rain that hasn’t happened yet.

He thought about writing nineteen. Everybody would cheer. Maigo would write it in capitals eleven more times. Thea would ask what had changed.

He didn’t. He wrote what he always wrote, the thing he’d written every night for two years, the last thing at the bottom so it was the first thing anyone saw in the morning.

Then, after a moment, under it:

He watched it go up.

Maigo answered first, of course. NINETEEN!!!!!! And then a lot of other people. And Thea, after a while, from a militia hall at the top of a road beside a lake, one line, very small: Locked or empty?

Niko looked at that for a long time.

He didn’t answer it. Not yet. He closed his window and lay on his bunk in the dark with Salty snoring in the next one and Maigo talking in his sleep about noodles, and held his hands up in front of his face in the dark, and looked at them, even though he couldn’t see them.

Enter name, he thought.

Not yet.

He’d know it when he saw it. The way the man knew a fish was there before the float went. The way an AWPer knows the angle before anyone peeks. He didn’t have to guess. He just had to wait, and keep going round, and watch, and not be in a hurry.

He was very good at not being in a hurry.

He went to sleep with the two candles in his chest going round, very slowly, on their own, for the first time in his life, without him holding them. Like something being stirred in a pan over a low fire. Like noodles, he thought, very nearly asleep, and laughed, once, in the dark, at how stupid that was.

On the far side of the fort, on the east wall, at the twelfth merlon north of the gate, a man with a rod stood for a long time looking out at the red under the moon, with his hands on the parapet beside the cracked stone, not on it. Thinking about a boy of six under a blanket in a cottage, a long time ago, in another life, whispering a word because it was the only one he had, and a door opening in the dark in front of his face.

And all the words he’d given it after that, one at a time, because there had been nobody else to give it any.
