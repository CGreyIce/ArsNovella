The letter came on the hundred-and-eleventh day, in the afternoon, while he was drawing water.

He’d been at Cinderhold three weeks. He’d carried the water every day. Aldous still told him so every morning at the tail of the line, water, though there was no cart now and no line, only Group 3 in the courtyard at dawn with the chaplain’s book under the chaplain’s arm, because Aldous said a day was still a thing you carried from one end to the other, and you’d better know before you picked it up. So he carried the water. From the well to the kitchen, from the well to the stables, from the well to the forge for Oakes’s quenching trough. With the blue bucket. Pim had been very clear about the blue bucket.

He’d cooked eleven dinners. He’d paid. Salty had said twelve, and he’d laughed until he couldn’t breathe, on a forge floor, in the dark, for the first time in he didn’t know how long. He’d fished the stream half a mile west of the fort every dawn and caught perch and chub and once a trout that Oakes said nobody had caught in that stream in twenty years. He’d watched Rhea hold him for a count, once, in front of the whole fort, and watched the whole fort lose its mind about it. He’d sat on the gatehouse steps before dawn and watched a boy he’d killed four hundred times on a server get to nineteen without any help at all.

And every morning and every evening, he’d gone up on the east wall and stood at the tenth merlon north of the gate, and his daughter had stood at the twelfth, and they’d looked at a tree a long way out on the red, beside a dark stone. And neither of them had stepped into the gap.

Sixteen days to the full moon. Higu’s chart. He’d been counting too.

He let the blue bucket down the well. He heard it hit the water, far down, cold. He began to draw it up.

And somebody said, behind him, “Are you the one carrying the water?”

She was small and brown and forty, in a riding coat that had been grey before the road and was now the colour of every road between here and Solmere. Mud to the thighs. A horse behind her in the gateway of the yard, a tall bay, blown and steaming, with its head down. She stood by the well-head with her riding gloves still on and looked at him.

Not at his face. He saw her not look at it. Her eyes went to it and slid off, the way everybody’s did, and she didn’t try again; she looked at his hands on the rope, and the blue bucket coming up out of the dark, and the rod leaning against the well-head. A rider’s look. Quick. Taking the whole of a thing in at once, the way you’d take in a road ahead to see where the holes were.

“I am,” he said.

She nodded, as if that settled something. She didn’t say her name. She unbuttoned the top of her shirt, under the coat, and took something out from against her skin, where the king’s riders carried the things that mattered. A letter. Folded. Sealed in grey wax with a seal he knew. Warm from her.

She held it out.

“From the Advisor,” she said. “Into your hand. His words. Your hand and no other.” She paused. “He said you’d be the one carrying the water.”

He looked at the letter.

He’d known it would come. He’d told Halvard to send one, on a roof in Valcrest, by a chimney, with the city going to sleep under them. Send one. And I’ll answer it. He’d known for three weeks, and every day at the well he’d thought, today, maybe. And now it was here, in a small brown hand in a riding glove, three weeks out of Solmere, warm from somebody’s skin, and his own hands were wet from the rope.

He wiped them on his coat. Then he took it.

On the outside, in a plain upright hand he’d have known anywhere, the hand of a court mage who’d learned his letters at a garrison school, one word.

Ars.

And under it, smaller, Cinderhold. By the hand of the king’s fastest rider. To be put into his hand and no other.

“How fast?” he said.

“Twenty-one days,” said the rider. “Downs were dry.”

“That’s very fast.”

“I know,” said the rider, with some satisfaction. And then, after a moment, because she was looking at the letter in his hand and not at his face: “He was on the gatehouse wall when I went out. At dawn. In his shirtsleeves. He watched me till I was gone.” She was quiet. “The king came and stood beside him. I saw them in the road, when I looked back. On the wall. Both of them.”

He didn’t say anything.

“I’ll wait for the answer,” said the rider. “He said there’d be one.” She turned back toward her horse. “I’ll be in the stables. Sleeping. Wake me when it’s written.”

“It might be a while.”

“I’ve got a while,” said the rider, and went to see to her horse.

He didn’t open it at the well.

He finished drawing the bucket, and carried it to the kitchen, because it was the kitchen’s, and Cook was waiting. Then he went back across the courtyard to the gatehouse, and up the stair, the hollow-worn stone stair he’d gone up ten thousand times at fifteen and twenty and twenty-seven, with the letter in his hand. Onto the east wall. And north along it, past the gatehouse, past the horns hanging on the outside of the gate, which he didn’t look at. Counting, the way Wat counted, not counting the door.

Ten. He stopped at the tenth merlon, where he’d stood every evening for three weeks, beside the gap.

And looked at the gap.

The eleventh merlon north of the gate. Two worn stones and the embrasure between them. The coping split across the top with cracks running out from two places the size of a man’s hands, like ice when you stand on it too fast. And the sentries’ path, the long shallow trough nine years of boots had worn down the middle of the wall walk, bending away from it toward the inside rail for the width of two men, and coming back. Nobody had stood in it in nine years. Wat had told Alea. He’d known before Wat told her. He’d seen the path bend the first morning, and known what it was going round.

It hadn’t been the place of the hour, first.

That was the thing nobody alive knew except one man, and that man was in Solmere in his shirtsleeves on a gatehouse wall, watching a rider go. Before it was the place of the hour, it had been his place. At fifteen. A garrison recruit with a borrowed sword too long for him and a temper too short, who came up here at night when he couldn’t sleep, which was most nights, and sat on the parapet in the gap with his legs over the drop and his window open in the air in front of his face, because it was the only place in the fort where nobody came. Where nobody would see a boy looking at nothing.

Until a young court mage came along the wall with a lantern on his fourth night at Cinderhold, and found him.

You’ve got one too, he’d said. You just can’t see it.

He stood at the tenth merlon with the letter in his hand and looked at the gap for a long time.

Then he stepped into it.

It felt like nothing.

He’d thought it might. He’d thought, for three weeks, standing beside it, that it would be like stepping into cold water, or into a room where somebody had died. It wasn’t. It was two stones and a gap, with the sun on them, and the red going away below to the mountains, and the wind. He stood in it. He put his hands on the coping. On the cracked stone, in the two places that were the shape of his hands, because they were the shape of his hands, and his hands went into them the way your feet go into old boots.

Then he sat down on the parapet with his legs over the drop, the way he’d sat at fifteen.

Somebody on the wall, a long way south, by the gatehouse, made a small sound. A sentry. Young. He didn’t look round. He heard boots go along the wall at a run, away, toward the stair. He heard, a while after, other boots come up the stair, slower, heavier, and stop. Teague, probably. He didn’t look round at that either.

He broke the seal with his thumbnail.

He read it.

Ars.

This is the one I never finished. I have started it eleven times. They are in the drawer, and they all stop at the same place, which is the wall.

He read it slowly. Once. The way he’d read a pike’s water, or a line of the host on a slope: all of it, the whole thing, at once, and then each part of it, one at a time.

On the second night, a little after the middle watch, the south line was attacked in strength.

He knew. He’d been there. He’d been on the wall that night when the commander came running along it with the scouts’ reports in his fist, white, and gave them to the court mage at the king’s elbow, and the court mage read them by lantern-light with his thin grey face going still. And looked up. And said the south. Ars. The south line. Now. And he’d gone. Down the stairs and across the yard and out of the west gate at a run, with the reserve behind him and the stretcher-bearers behind them, out into the dark toward a sound of screaming. To a ditch. To forty soldiers holding a broken line with nobody on either side of them. To a girl of twenty-three at the end of it with her arm broken and her sword in the other hand, who wouldn’t put it down.

I was wrong. It was the other way round.

He’d known that by midnight. In the ditch. With the line holding. Too easily. He’d stood in the ditch with the scouts falling back in front of him, not pressing, not dying, just falling back, and listened past them, east, toward the fort, and heard nothing. And known. The way you know, in the middle of a round, that the noise on your side of the map is the noise they want you to hear.

He’d stayed. He’d held the ditch till dawn. There’d been forty soldiers in it, and they’d have died, and he’d known that too.

At dawn the wall of it was open. You know what was gone.

He knew.

The second is the hour.

He stopped reading, there. Just for a moment. He sat on the parapet in the gap with the letter on his knee and his legs over the drop, and looked out at the red.

East. And a little south. A long low hump in the plain, an hour off, with grey grass on it. And in the afternoon light, if you knew, a ring.

He’d stood here, that afternoon. Here. In the gap. He’d come up the stair at a run, because somebody had shouted from the wall fires, on the rise, and he’d known before he got to the top what he’d see, because everything they’d done for two days had been to put him in a place where he’d see it. And he’d come along the wall to his place, because his feet went there, because it was where he went. And put his hands on the stone. And looked.

Fires. A ring of them, round the top of the rise, an hour’s walk out. And something in the middle. And between the wall and the rise, the whole of the host, waiting, on the slope, in the dark of the afternoon smoke, for one man to come over a wall.

And a thin grey court mage beside him. Not touching him. Standing very close, at his left shoulder, the way you’d stand by a horse you weren’t sure of.

Ars, Halvard had said. The gate’s barred. On my word. It’s a trap. Look at it. You go over that wall now, and you die on that slope, and she’s still there.

He’d known. He’d known all of it. He’d known it before Halvard opened his mouth. He’d been a soldier for twelve years and he could read a host on a slope the way he could read water.

That’s an order, Halvard had said.

And then nothing else.

For an hour.

He’d stayed.

That was the thing he’d never once said to anyone. Not to Alea. Not to Gabe and Deston and Xav on a jetty at midnight with the whole of it coming out of him like a river through a broken dam. Not to the king, in a tent, in the war, or by a grave, or on the shingle, or in the empty Lists. He’d told them all of it. Not that.

That he’d stood on this wall, at this merlon, for an hour, with his hands on this stone, and watched the woman he loved in the middle of a ring of fires, and not gone.

Not because he’d been told. Because it was right. Because he could see that it was right. Because he’d been a soldier since he was fifteen and Halvard was the best strategist the crown had ever sent to the border, and he’d never once in twelve years seen Halvard wrong about a host. Because if he’d gone over the wall the way every part of him wanted to go, he’d have gone the way they wanted. Running. Alone. Out of his head. Into the middle of everything they had.

So he’d stood. And the stone had gone under his hands. He’d heard it. Crack. And then another. He hadn’t meant it to. He hadn’t been able to stop it. All of him had been going into his hands, everything he had, all of the sixth tier and whatever was under it, into a piece of grey stone on a parapet, because it had to go somewhere and it couldn’t go over the wall.

And Halvard had stood beside him. The whole hour. Not saying anything. Not leaving. A thin grey man who could have gone down the stair at any moment and given the order from the gatehouse and not had to watch. Who stood at his shoulder in the smoke for an hour, close enough to touch, and watched.

He’d seen Halvard’s face, that hour. He’d never told him that either. He’d looked round, once, about halfway, and seen it. Halvard had been able to see his; Halvard had been one of the very few who could, for years by then, and nobody knew why. But he’d seen Halvard’s, too, and it had been the face of a man watching somebody be burned alive by his own order, and not looking away, because looking away would have been easier.

At the end of the hour, Halvard had said one more word.

Go.

And his voice had broken on it. Like a stick. In the middle. A thin grey court mage who never raised his voice, and never let it do anything, and it had broken on one word like a boy’s.

He’d gone.

He picked the letter up off his knee and read the rest.

So I held you. For an hour. You are the only man I have ever known who would obey an order of mine with his hands breaking the stone.

At the end of the hour I said go. Nothing had changed. The trap was still a trap. I said it because I could not stand beside you one minute longer and watch you obey me.

He read that twice.

An hour sooner, and it might have been less than half. I have done that sum every night for nine years.

He lowered the letter.

He thought about a sum. Done every night for nine years. In a tower in Solmere, by a lamp, at a desk with a drawer in it that stuck in wet weather. A strategist’s sum. An hour sooner. Done every night until it wore a groove.

He’d done it too. Every night for the first year, at the end of the jetty, with a nine-year-old girl asleep in a half-rebuilt cabin behind him. And then less. And then, one night in the third year, he’d done it all the way to the end for the first time, slowly, the way you’d walk a ditch you’d fought in to see where everyone had fallen. And come out somewhere he hadn’t expected. And not done it since.

Both orders were right.

He knew.

That I was right, and she is under a stone, and you have a lake.

He sat on the parapet with his legs over the drop and the letter in his hands, and read the last line.

I have missed you every day for nine years. That is the last thing in this letter, and the only part of it I never once had to work out.

He didn’t move for a long time.

Alea found him there at dusk.

He heard her on the stair. He always heard her. He heard her come up it and along the wall the way she’d come every evening for three weeks, quick and light, with Astra on her back, toward the twelfth merlon. And stop. Two merlons off. Where she could see him.

Sitting in the gap.

She didn’t say anything for a long time. He heard her not say it. He felt her standing on the wall walk behind him, very still, with her ears up, looking at her father sitting on the parapet in the place nobody had stood for nine years, with his legs over the drop and a letter on his knee.

“Come and sit,” he said.

She came.

She didn’t go round the path. She walked straight along the wall walk, through the place where the trough in the stone bent away, the way nobody had walked in nine years, and stepped into the gap beside him. And sat down on the parapet, with her legs over the drop, beside his, with her shoulder against his arm. And Astra on her back, the black iron of it against the black iron of nothing, against the cracked grey stone.

They sat there. The two of them. In the gap. Looking at the red going dark, and the markers, and a tree a long way out beside a dark stone, and further out, and a little south, a rise with a ring on it.

“Wat told you,” he said. “That I stood here an hour.”

“Wat told me.”

“And that Halvard stood here too. And said something at the start and something at the end. And nobody knows what.”

“Yes.”

“You didn’t ask me.”

“It was yours,” said Alea.

He looked down at the letter on his knee.

“It’s not just mine any more,” he said.

He told her.

All of it. The second night. The scouts’ reports by lantern-light. The south, Ars, now. The ditch, and the line holding too easily, and the silence from the east. Dawn, and the infirmary wall. And then the afternoon, and the fires, and the order. That’s an order. And the hour. And go, and Halvard’s voice breaking on it.

She listened the way her mother had listened, which was all the way, without interrupting, with her ears flat and then up and then flat again.

When he’d finished, she didn’t say anything for a while.

“Did you know?” she said. “That it was him. Both times.”

“Always.”

“On the wall?”

“On the wall,” he said. “In the ditch, the first one. I knew by midnight it was the wrong line. I knew who’d read the reports.” He looked at the letter. “And the second one, I was standing next to him.”

“Were you angry?”

He thought about it. He wanted to give her a true answer. She’d always been able to tell when he didn’t.

“For about a week,” he said. “Nine years ago. At everything. At the general. At the sky. At myself.” He was quiet. “At him, for a day. Maybe two.” He turned the letter over on his knee. “And then I did the sum.”

“What sum?”

“His sum,” he said. “The one in the letter. An hour sooner.” He looked at the rise, going dark. “I did it all the way to the end, once. In the third year. On the jetty. And it came out somewhere he hasn’t got to yet.” He was quiet. “He’s been doing it wrong for nine years. Every night. Getting the same answer.” He breathed. “I’m going to tell him.”

Alea looked at him sideways, in the dusk, with her mother’s eyes.

“Tell him slower,” she said.

He nearly laughed.

“Slower,” he agreed.

He wrote it in the infirmary.

He hadn’t meant to. He’d meant to write it on the wall, in the gap, by lamplight, the way you’d write a thing in the place it was about. And then at full dark Alea had gone down to supper and he’d sat in the gap a while longer with the paper on his knee, and the pen, and the lamp, and not written anything. And looked down into the courtyard. And seen the lamp lit over the infirmary door, the way somebody had lit it every night for longer than she’d been alive.

And thought about a table, with a drawing pinned over it, that he’d seen once, the first night, nine years ago, with the whole infirmary watching and Diwa standing beside him with her arms folded saying well? Say something nice about the planks.

And never gone into that room again.

He went down the stairs.

Hesper was sweeping.

Of course he was. The big old man with the soft hands, in the long room with the beds folded square, sweeping the boards in the lamplight the way some people pray. He looked up when the door opened. He saw who it was. He stopped.

He didn’t say anything. He looked at the man in the doorway with the letter and the pen and the paper, for a long moment, with his big soft face very still.

“Can I use the table?” said Ars.

Hesper looked at him a moment longer.

Then he leaned the broom against the end of the nearest bed, and went to the far end of the room, to the table where the jars were, under the place on the wall where a pin had been for nine years with nothing on it. And moved the jars, very carefully, one at a time, to the shelf. And wiped the table with his sleeve. And pulled out the chair.

And put the kettle on.

“You can’t write anything in an infirmary without tea,” said Hesper, without turning round. “It’s a rule.” He was quiet. “Hers.”

“I know,” said Ars. “She made it.”

He sat down at the table.

It took him all night.

Not because it was long. It wasn’t long. He’d never in his life written anything long. Because he’d never written to Halvard. Not once. In twenty years. Not from the border, not from the north, not from the lake. Tell Halvard I’ve taken it, to Aldous, on the red. Let them, on a scrap of market paper, by a trader. Two lines in twenty years, to the man who’d sat down on a wall walk at Cinderhold at thirty-eight with his hands over his mouth and stared at a window until dawn because a boy of fifteen had shown him how to open it.

He sat at the table under the empty pin with the paper in front of him and the pen in his hand, and didn’t know how to start.

Hesper brought him tea. In a cup with a handle. And put it down at his elbow, and went back to his sweeping, and didn’t say anything.

He wrapped his hands round the cup.

They weren’t cold. They’d never been cold. That had been hers. But he wrapped them round it anyway, the way she used to wrap hers round Hesper’s cup in the mornings, to warm them, before she touched anyone. And sat there.

At some point, near midnight, something in his coat went warm.

He felt it before he knew what it was. A small warmth, against his chest, on the left, where the inside pocket was. Where the good knife was, and the drawing, folded in four. And beside them, a short purple cord with half a gold heart on it, that a woman with a guard’s coat and a book had given him at the north gate of Valcrest, in the mist. Perhaps anyway.

He took it out.

Half a gold heart. Warm. The warmth of somebody’s hand. Somebody in Solmere, at midnight, awake, with the other half on her wrist and her hand over it. He didn’t know why she was awake. He thought he could guess. The channel had gone strange an hour ago: Hyvred and Bee and Tiger, a few lines about Thornwall and the wind and Drew at a window, that had made him put the pen down and sit very still for a long time. And then a line from Kei, plain, at the bottom: Spark. Dose. Twenty-seven. Drew’s okay.

He’d read that one three times.

He held the half-heart in his closed fist. It was warm. Somebody in Solmere was holding the other half. Not saying anything. Just awake.

He didn’t put it back. He held it in his left hand, closed, on the table, while he wrote with his right. All night. It stayed warm.

This is what he wrote.

Halvard.

Tamsin put it in my hand at the well. I was carrying the water.

I know. I knew on the wall. I’d have given the same orders. Both of them.

I knew about the ditch by midnight. The line held too easily and there was nothing from the east, and I knew who’d read the reports, because I’d watched you read them. I stayed in the ditch anyway, because there were forty of them in it and they’d have died. I’ve never been sorry for that either.

And the hour. You said it was a trap and it was an order. You were right twice. I could see the host on the slope better than you could. I knew. I stood there because you were right, not because you said so. You’ve thought for nine years that I obeyed you. I didn’t. I agreed with you. It’s not the same.

You stood next to me the whole hour. You didn’t go down to the gatehouse and give the order from there, where you wouldn’t have to watch. I looked round once and saw your face. I’ve never told you that. You were watching somebody burn by your own order and you didn’t look away. I’ve thought about that more than I’ve thought about the stone.

You’ve done a sum every night for nine years. I did it too. Every night for a year, and then less, and then once all the way to the end, in the third year, on the jetty. Here’s where it comes out.

If I’d gone at once, I’d have gone the way they wanted. Running. Alone. Out of my head. Straight down the slope into the middle of everything they had. I was Shaped. Not a fifth of the way to the next tier. I’d have died on the slope, or worse, and she’d have been all the way gone by morning with nobody there to hear her.

I went an hour late. And by then the first of it had burned off, standing on that wall with my hands in the stone. I didn’t run. I walked. All afternoon, through the whole of the host, like a man walking, one at a time, the way I’d go through anything. That’s the only reason I got there at all.

Half was what there was when I got there. Half was enough for her to know me, and ask me, and give me the words for Alea. An hour sooner and you think there might have been more of her. I think there’d have been none of me.

So it wasn’t your hour. It was ours. Mostly it was hers. She got to say goodbye. That was the hour.

You didn’t break me. I said that in Valcrest. Here it is in ink, so you’ve got it: you didn’t break me. You held me.

I’ve missed you too. Every day. Nine years. I’ve never said that to anyone. I didn’t know how. I’m saying it badly now.

Come to the lake in the frost. Bring the king. He’ll want to see the hens.

Ars.

He read it once.

Then he folded it, and sealed it with a drop of wax off the infirmary candle, and pressed it with his thumb, because he had no seal and never had. And on the outside, in the plain narrow hand he’d written eight years of anonymous books in, he wrote:

Halvard.

And then nothing else, because the rider would know where.

He sat at the table a while longer with the letter in front of him and the half-heart still warm in his left hand. Hesper had stopped sweeping, some time ago. He was asleep on the end of one of the beds, sitting up, with his back against the wall and his big soft hands folded in his lap, the way an old orderly sleeps who has sat up with a great many people in his life.

The window over the table was going grey.

He looked up at the wall above the table. At the pin. A small iron pin, driven into the plaster, where a child’s drawing of a lake had hung for two weeks, nine years ago, and nothing had hung since. Nobody had taken the pin out. He supposed nobody had thought to. Or somebody had thought to, every day, and not done it.

He took the drawing out of his coat.

He unfolded it. Along the old soft folds. A lake, in blue wax pressed on very hard. A jetty, with too many planks. Three people on the end of it holding hands, and a fish with a smile.

He didn’t pin it up. It was going home. Alea had said.

He just held it up, for a moment, against the wall, under the pin, where it had been. And looked at it there. In her room. Over her table. Where she’d made everybody say something nice about the planks.

“They’re very good planks,” he said. Quietly. To the wall.

Then he folded it again, and put it back in his coat with the good knife and the half-heart, which had gone cool at last, about an hour after dawn, the way a hand goes cool when somebody a long way off finally lets go of something and goes to sleep.

He woke Tamsin Vell in the stables at sunrise.

She was asleep in the straw beside her horse, with her boots on. She opened her eyes the moment he came in, the way a rider wakes, all at once, and looked at the letter in his hand.

“Into his hand,” he said.

“Into his hand,” said Tamsin. She sat up and took it and put it inside her shirt, against her skin, and buttoned it. “His and no other.” She looked at him. Not at his face. At the rest. “Was it a long one?”

“No.”

“His was long,” said Tamsin. “I could tell from the weight.” She stood up and picked up her saddle. “He’ll want to know if you’re well. When I give it him. He’ll ask.”

He thought about it.

“Tell him I’m carrying the water,” he said.

Tamsin looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded, as if that answered something completely, and went to saddle her horse.

He watched her go from the east wall.

Not from the gap. From beside it. The tenth merlon. He stood there with his hands on the parapet in the sunrise and watched a single rider go out of the west gate of Cinderhold below and behind him and down the road at a canter and then a gallop. West. Toward the downs, and the river, and three weeks of road, and a tower in Solmere, and a thin grey man who’d watched her go from a gatehouse wall and was going to watch her come back.

Somebody came along the wall behind him.

Slowly. With a lantern, though it was light, and a spear he was mostly leaning on. Old Wat, coming off the night watch on the north tower, the way he’d come off it every morning for longer than Ars had been alive.

Wat came along the wall walk toward the gap.

Ars watched him, out of the side of his eye. Watched him come to the place where the trough in the stone bent away toward the inside rail, the way it had bent for nine years. Where Wat always went round. Without looking. The way you’d step round a grave.

Wat didn’t go round.

He walked straight on. Along the middle of the wall walk. Through the gap, past the cracked stone, the eleventh merlon, slowly, with his lantern and his spear, without bending, without looking. As if it were any other merlon on any other wall. As if the path had always gone straight.

He stopped beside Ars, at the tenth.

“Morning,” said Wat.

“Morning, Wat.”

Wat looked at him for a long moment with his old wet eyes. Then out at the red. Then down at the gap he’d just walked through.

“Saw you sitting in it,” said Wat. “Last night. From the north tower.” He leaned on his spear. “And the girl.” He was quiet. “Thought, well. That’s that, then.”

He patted Ars’s arm, twice, with his shaking hand, the way you’d pat a dog. And went on along the wall to the stair, and down, and to his bed.

He opened his window, after.

Out of habit. Standing on the wall at the tenth merlon in the sunrise, with the rider gone and Wat gone and the red going gold all the way to the mountains, and a tree a long way out beside a dark stone, and further out, and a little south, a rise. Sixteen days.

[ Ars · Tier 8: Dominion · Progress to Ascendant: 61% ]

He looked at it.

Sixty-one. It had been sixty since the closing in Valcrest. Since forty thousand paper fishing rods, and a girl’s box, and daughter of Diwa, a healer. And of Ars.

He thought about what had moved it. Not the dinners. Not the fishing. Not nineteen heartbeats on a wall, or a post coming up out of the gravel at Duskhollow, or a girl on a jetty saying hello to the oldest thing in the world. Those had all been good. The bar hadn’t moved for any of them.

It had moved for a letter.

The first he’d ever sent. To a man who’d held him for an hour, nine years ago, on a wall, with his hands in the stone, and had thought every night since that he’d held him back. And who would read, in three weeks, in a tower, by a lamp, at a desk with a drawer that was standing open, that he hadn’t.

Put things down, he’d told the king once, on the shingle, when the king asked him how. Let people help.

He’d never once in nine years let anyone help him carry that hour.

He closed the window.

Down in the courtyard, at the tail of no line at all, Aldous was standing by the well with his book under his arm, looking up at him. Waiting. The way he waited every morning.

“Water?” called Ars, from the wall.

Aldous looked at him for a long time with his grave old chaplain’s face.

“Water,” said Aldous.

Ars went down to get the bucket.
