The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 5: The Lists

Chapter 140

Two Hooks

6,169 words · 27 min read

Alea’s father fought with a hook.

Nobody knew. That was the joke of it, the one she’d been keeping all her life the way you keep a stone in your pocket. Everybody in the kingdom knew about the black sword. The songs were all about the black sword: seven feet tall, eyes like coals, a horse called Midnight, a slab of iron that took six men and a winch. Nobody sang about the rod.

The rod was a blade. The shaft of it, all the way down, folded into the wood so finely you’d never see it unless he let you. He fished with it. Really fished, every night, off the end of the jetty, for pike and perch. And when something came that wasn’t a fish, he cast it, and the hook went out, and caught, and he reeled it in. Fast. Faster than anything should move. And whatever was on the end of the line came in to him, and he cut it.

She’d seen him do it only a handful of times in her life. Once at night, from her window, the year before last, when a thing with too many joints had crouched in the orchard on the brow of the hill above Wrenhaven watching the militia hall, and he’d cast up the slope, a mile and more, and she’d seen the line go out silver in the moonlight. The rest of the times she’d been small, and hadn’t understood what she was seeing.

A hook, and a pull, and whatever was on the end of it came to him.

That was how her father fought. Not with the sword. With the hook.

She was standing in the tunnel under the east stand at noon on the last day of the Lists, with her mother’s half of a black sword on her back, waiting to walk out and fight a girl who fought exactly the same way.

“You’re thinking,” said Petra.

“I’m not.”

“Your ears are thinking.”

Alea put her hand up to her ears. They were flat. She made them not be. They went flat again.

The tunnel was dark and cool and smelled of sand and old oil and horses, the way it always did, and at the end of it, through the arch, the light of the great sand was so bright it was white. She could hear the crowd. Not a roar, yet. A hum. The sound of the whole bowl of the Lists, full, every seat, more people than had been there for the opening, more than for the Banner or the Gate or the Trios, waiting. For two girls. For a fledgling final.

She’d never thought anyone would come to watch her.

“She’s going to hook it,” said Alea. “Astra. She hooks the blade and pulls. And Astra’s so heavy, if she pulls it the wrong way it’ll take me with it. Like the boy in round three. Like the Pemberton girl.”

“Probably,” said Petra.

“And I let it fall. That’s all I do. I lift it and let it fall.” Alea looked at her hands. “If I let it fall and she hooks it on the way down, she’ll have it. She’ll have all the weight. She’ll pull me straight off my feet.”

“Probably,” said Petra again.

Alea looked at her.

Petra was leaning on the wall of the tunnel with her arms folded and her sword on, the way she always had it on, like a soldier. Thirty-three. Reaching. She’d been a recruit once on the Redmarch, in a ditch, with her unit overrun, and a man with a black sword had cut his way through to them, and she’d watched him fight for less than a minute and spent the rest of her life trying to fight like that.

“You’re not helping,” said Alea.

“I’m not meant to,” said Petra. “I’m meant to stand in the corner. That’s all a corner’s for.” She looked at Alea for a long moment. “You’ve beaten everyone this week by doing what he taught you. Every round. Waiting. Moving early. Letting it fall.”

“I know.”

“She’s going to be the first one where that isn’t enough.”

Alea didn’t say anything.

“So you’re going to have to do something he didn’t teach you,” said Petra. “That’s what a final is.”

Xav was at the arch.

He’d come down from the near ring after the semifinal and stood there, at the end of the tunnel, with his back to the light, and hadn’t said anything. He never said anything. But when Alea came up to the arch with Astra on her back and her ears flat, he held out his fist, low, the way he had after every round, and she bumped it with hers.

“Okay,” said Xav.

“Okay,” said Alea.

“Again,” said Xav, very slightly, and something at the corner of his mouth moved.

She laughed. She couldn’t help it. She went out through the arch into the white light laughing, with her ears, for a moment, not flat at all.

The Lists went up.

She’d heard them roar all week. For the summoned. For paper horns and kettles and an oni writing fire. For a gate that fell over. For her, in round one, when a lord’s son’s sword went three yards outside the ring. She’d thought she knew what it sounded like.

She hadn’t. It came down on her from every side as she walked out onto the sand, a wall of it, forty thousand people and more, and it had words in it. She could hear them. From the east stand, from the near stand where she’d fought all week: LET IT FALL. LET IT FALL. Somebody had a bedsheet with it painted on. From the far end, from the west stand, where Sorrel had been fighting: HOOKS. HOOKS. HOOKS.

And in between, from everywhere, the paper.

She hadn’t expected that. Paper fishing rods. Thousands of them. Held up over the heads of the crowd all round the bowl, the way the horns had been held up for Ina. Nobody had told them to. They didn’t know what the rods were for. But they had them in their hands, and somebody had started holding them up when she walked out, and then everybody had, and now the whole of the Lists was a field of paper fishing rods swaying in the noon sun.

For her. Because she was the lake girl. Because her sword went round like a cast line. Because a man had given her a paper rod on the second day and she’d held it up on her shoulder like a real one.

She looked up at the fourth tier of the east stand.

He was there. On the end of his bench, where nobody looked. He had the rod across his knees. The real one. And he was holding a paper one in his hand, that somebody next to him must have given him without looking at him, the way everybody gave everybody things this week. He wasn’t holding it up. He was holding it in his lap, in both hands, very carefully, as if it were something that might break.

His shoulders were going.

Alea looked away very fast, before her face could do anything.

Sorrel came out of the west tunnel at the far end.

She walked across the sand the way she’d walked away from the well, three nights ago, in the dusk: whistling, with her hooks over her shoulders like oars, in her fisherman’s smock with the sleeves cut off and her brown arms bare and a strip of sailcloth round her hair. Barefoot. She’d been barefoot all week. She said she couldn’t feel the sand in boots.

She stopped in the near ring, across the chalk from Alea, and grinned like a gap in a fence.

“Lake girl,” she said.

“Coast girl.”

“Everyone’s holding up fishing rods.” Sorrel looked round at the stands with frank delight. “For you. I’m jealous. Nobody’s holding up a boat-hook.”

“There aren’t any paper boat-hooks.”

“There should be,” said Sorrel. “I’m going to tell Lucan.” She looked back at Alea. Her grin didn’t go anywhere, but something under it changed. “Your box.”

“Still blank.”

“If you win, you tell it.”

“If I win.”

Sorrel nodded slowly. She took one hook off her shoulder, and then the other, and held them loosely, one in each hand, the way you’d hold a pair of oars before you put them in the water. Her father’s. Cut down for her when she was ten, so she could pull herself back onto the boat.

“Then I’ll have to make sure you don’t,” she said. “Or I’ll never find out.” And then, more quietly, so that only Alea could hear it under the noise: “I hope you do, though. I really want to know.”

Alea looked at her.

“Me too,” she said.

The marshal said begin.

Sorrel didn’t wait.

Alea had known she wouldn’t. She’d watched Sorrel fight all week from across the sand. Sorrel didn’t stand still, didn’t hold an angle, didn’t wait for anything. She came in, the way the sea comes in, all at once and from the side, with one hook high and one low, cheerful and quick and barefoot on the sand.

Alea waited.

It was what she did. She stood in the ring with Astra’s point on the sand and watched the whole thing: the feet, the hooks, the breath, the grin. And in the moment before, while Sorrel was still deciding where to put the first hook, she moved. Early. The black sword came up off the sand in its long easy line, like a rod lifting, like a cast going out, and at the top of the line she let it fall.

And Sorrel caught it.

Alea felt it happen before she understood it.

Astra was falling. Half a slab of black iron, all its weight, coming down through the air the way it had come down on a lord’s son’s sword and a cadet’s and a mage’s staff, the way it came down on everything. And halfway down, the curve of a boat-hook went round the back of it. The broad flat back of the blade, where it was widest. The iron head of the hook sliding round it the way a hook goes round a mooring line.

And Sorrel pulled.

Not hard. That was the terrible thing. The way you pull a boat in. Steady, with her whole weight going back and her bare feet dug into the sand. She didn’t fight the sword’s weight. She just took it, all of it, the whole fall of it, and turned it. Sideways. Away from where Alea had meant it to go, toward where Sorrel wanted it.

Astra went sideways in Alea’s hands.

She felt the whole weight of it go. All of it at once, wrenching, half a ton of iron that wasn’t falling down any more but falling away, toward the coast girl, and taking her hands with it. Her arms. Her shoulders. She felt her feet leave the place where she’d put them. One step. Two. Pulled.

The grip held.

She’d never been so grateful for anything in her life. Gabe’s grip. New slabs of wood cut to the shape of her closed hand, from the way she held a cup. Sharkskin underneath, for cold hands. The old leather from a saddle wrapped over it, tighter, more turns, finished with a fisherman’s knot. It held in her hands as if it had grown there. Anyone else’s grip would have gone. Hers held.

She got the sword back. Just. Wrenched it round, out of the hook, with everything she had, and stumbled, and stood.

Two paces from where she’d been. Toward the line.

The far stand went HOOKS.

Sorrel was grinning.

She’d stepped back, out of reach, with both hooks loose again, the way you’d lift your oars out of the water between strokes. She was breathing hard. She looked delighted.

“Heavy,” said Sorrel.

“Yes.”

“It’s like hooking a whale.” Sorrel shook out her arms, one and then the other. “I nearly had it. Did you feel that? I nearly had it.” She laughed. “What’s it made of?”

“My mother,” said Alea.

She hadn’t meant to say it. It came out. Sorrel looked at her, and the grin went still for a moment, the way it had at the well when she said mine was. Then she nodded, slowly, as if that explained everything about the sword.

“Okay,” said Sorrel. “Then I’ll be careful with it.”

She came in again.

The second time was worse.

Alea didn’t let it fall. She couldn’t. If she let it fall, Sorrel would catch it. So she waited, the way she’d waited for Corran Tolley in round five, the way her father waited on the end of the jetty. She stood with Astra’s point on the sand and didn’t move and let Sorrel come.

Sorrel didn’t come.

She stood at the edge of the ring with her hooks loose and her bare feet in the sand and grinned at Alea, and didn’t come. And Alea realised, standing there, that Sorrel had been watching her all week too. From across the sand. Every round. She’d watched Alea wait for people and go first while they were deciding. She knew.

So she wasn’t deciding.

She stood there not deciding anything, grinning, swinging one hook very gently from her wrist, the way you’d swing a weight on a line. Back and forth. Back and forth. Until Alea’s eyes went to it.

It was the oldest trick in the world. Her father would have laughed. You swing something in front of a fish and the fish looks at it.

Alea looked at it.

And the other hook came round from the side, low, out of nowhere, and caught Astra just above the grip. Not the back of the blade. The guard. The place where the blade met the handle, where there was a little iron lip. The hook went round it like a hand round a wrist.

And Sorrel pulled.

Alea’s heels hit the chalk.

She felt it. The chalk line, behind her, under her back foot. She’d been pulled straight across the ring. Not off her feet; she’d kept her feet, just. But all the way across, two, three, four paces, the whole weight of Astra going toward Sorrel and Alea going with it, and then Sorrel had let go and stepped aside, the way you let a boat go when it’s coming in too fast, and Alea had gone past her. Past her and on, toward the other side of the ring, toward the line, and stopped.

With her heels on the chalk.

One more step. One more pull. And she’d be over. Out. The way the boy in round five had been out, when she’d swung Astra on purpose and he’d gone back over the line. The way the cadet in Sorrel’s round five had been out, pulled by his belt.

The far stand was going HOOKS. HOOKS. HOOKS.

The near stand had gone quiet.

Alea stood with her heels on the chalk and Astra in her hands and her breath coming hard, and felt her ears go flat.

She was losing.

She was frightened.

Not the good kind. Not the kind you could sit next to, the way she’d sat next to Sorrel on the well. The other kind. The cold kind, in her stomach, coming up into her chest. The kind from round five, before she’d counted.

She’d never once, in four years, lost to anyone she didn’t know.

She’d lost to her father every morning of her life. She’d lost to Petra on wet verges. She’d lost to Xav once on the shingle. She’d never lost to a stranger. She’d never lost in front of anyone but Wrenhaven. And she was going to lose in front of the whole kingdom, on the last day of the Lists, with forty thousand people holding up paper fishing rods for her, to a girl with her dead father’s boat-hooks who’d hooked her mother’s sword twice and pulled her across a ring like a boat.

She looked up. She couldn’t help it.

The fourth tier.

He’d stood up. He was standing at the end of his bench, very still, with the real rod in one hand and the paper one in the other. Nobody near him was looking at him. They were all looking at her. At the girl with her heels on the chalk.

He wasn’t calling anything. He wasn’t doing anything. He never did, from the stands. He just stood there, the way he’d stood at the end of the jetty every night of her life, with a rod in his hand, watching the water.

A hook, and a pull, and whatever was on the end of it came to him.

Alea looked at him for a long moment.

And then she thought about pike.

She’d fished with him since she was four.

Every night. Off the end of the jetty, with a little rod he’d cut her out of willow, and then a better one, and then, when she was nine, one of his own. She’d watched him catch a thousand fish. She’d caught hundreds. She knew how a hook worked better than she knew how a sword did.

A hook only works if the fish pulls back.

That was the thing. That was the whole thing. Her father had told her when she was five, on the jetty, when she’d hooked her first big perch and hauled on the line and the line had gone slack and the perch had gone. You pulled, he’d said. It pulled. The hook held because you were both pulling. Then you pulled harder and it turned and came toward you, and the line went slack, and the hook fell out. He’d shown her. A fish that swims away from you is caught. A fish that swims toward you is free.

Most fish swam away. That was what made fishing work. You hooked them and they pulled and you pulled and the hook held.

But there’d been a pike. One pike. This spring. A big old cow pike in the reeds at the north end of the lake that had taken her father’s line on a misty morning and, instead of running away from him the way every pike in the world ran, had turned. And come straight at him. Straight at the jetty. Fast. So fast the line went slack in his hands, and the hook came loose, and the pike went under the jetty and through his good net and out the other side and was gone.

He’d stood on the end of the jetty with a slack line and a hole in his good net, and laughed until he had to sit down.

Clever girl, he’d said, to the water.

And then, to Alea, who’d been sitting on the jetty with her knees up watching: You know who that was? That was your mother. That’s exactly what your mother would have done.

Sorrel was coming in.

Alea saw her come. Grinning, barefoot, one hook high and one low, cheerful, quick. Coming to finish it. One more hook on that heavy black blade, one more steady pull, the way you pull a boat in, and the lake girl would go over the line.

Alea didn’t wait.

She did a thing her father had never taught her. A thing her father never did. Her father never let anything pull him anywhere. He was the one with the hook. He pulled.

She lifted Astra.

Not to let it fall. To offer it. She brought the black sword up off the sand and out, in front of her, broad side turned toward Sorrel, high and slow and easy, the way you’d hold out a big fat worm on a hook in front of a pike. Here. Take it.

Sorrel’s eyes went to it. Of course they did. She was a fisherman’s daughter too.

And the hook came round, and caught the back of the blade, the broad flat back where it was widest, and bit.

And Sorrel pulled.

And Alea went.

Not back. Not away. Not against the pull, the way you were supposed to, the way every boy and girl Sorrel had fought all week had done: hauling back on their own sword, fighting the hook, giving her the weight to work with. Alea didn’t fight it.

She went with it.

Toward Sorrel. As hard as she could. All of her. Off the chalk line with her back foot driving into the sand, every bit of her Tempered body and her whole frightened weight going forward, the way the pike had gone at the jetty. Faster than Sorrel was pulling. Faster than the line.

The line went slack.

She felt it go. The hook on Astra’s back, that had been biting, suddenly holding nothing, because the thing it was hooked on was coming toward it faster than it was being pulled. Sorrel had braced her whole body to haul in a whale. She’d dug her bare feet into the sand and leaned back with everything she had, ready to take the weight of half a ton of black iron and a girl hanging on the end of it.

And the whale came at her.

Half a slab of black iron, that had taken six soldiers and a winch to move, coming in faster than she’d braced for, with all of Alea’s weight behind it and all of Sorrel’s own pull adding to it. Like a boat she’d hooked to bring in gently that had suddenly caught a wave and come straight at the quay.

Sorrel’s arms weren’t ready. Nobody’s would have been.

She tried. She was good; she was so good. Her other hook came round, low and fast, the way it had come round on every opponent all week, for the back of Alea’s knee, for her neck, for anything. And Astra came in, heavy and fast, and Alea didn’t let it fall. She didn’t swing it. She brought it in on Sorrel’s own pull and stopped it.

A finger’s width from Sorrel’s ribs.

And felt the iron head of a boat-hook stop, very lightly, against the side of her own neck.

Nobody moved.

Two girls in the near ring of the Lists, on the last day, at noon. One with a black sword stopped a finger from the other’s ribs. One with a boat-hook stopped against the other’s neck. Both of them breathing very hard. Both of them very still.

Alea looked at Sorrel. Sorrel looked at Alea.

Neither of them knew.

Alea didn’t know. That was the honest truth, and she’d think about it for a long time afterwards. She didn’t know which had come first. She’d felt Astra stop and she’d felt the hook touch her neck and she couldn’t have told you, if her life depended on it, which had been first. They’d been the same moment. Or two moments so close together that the space between them was smaller than a breath.

Sorrel’s face was doing something Alea had never seen it do. Not grinning. Just very surprised.

“You came at me,” said Sorrel.

“Yes.”

“Nobody comes at me. When I’ve got them. Everybody pulls back.” She was breathing hard. “You came at me. You—“ She stopped. “You made it go slack.”

“A fish that swims toward you is free,” said Alea.

Sorrel stared at her.

Then, very slowly, the grin came back. All the way. Like a gap in a fence.

“Oh,” said Sorrel. “Oh, that’s good. That’s so good. My dad would have loved that.” She didn’t move the hook. “Who taught you that?”

Alea thought about it.

“Nobody,” she said. “A pike.”

The marshal’s flag was half up. It wasn’t moving.

He was standing at the edge of the near ring with his flag in his hand and his mouth open, looking from Astra to the hook and back. Then he lowered the flag. Then he raised it a little. Then he looked up at the master’s platform, very helplessly.

The Lists had gone completely silent.

Alea could hear it. Forty thousand people not making a sound. The paper fishing rods had stopped swaying. Somewhere very high up, a child asked a question and was hushed.

Two more marshals came across the sand at a run. And behind them, from the bottom tier of the east stand, where he’d been sitting all week with the old champions, Ser Garret Holm. Three times champion of the Lists. The man who’d bowed to her after round one.

The four of them stood at the edge of the near ring, very close, and looked.

Not at the girls. At the sword and the hook. At exactly where they were. The black iron a finger’s width from Sorrel’s ribs. The boat-hook resting against the side of Alea’s neck. They looked for a long time. Ser Garret knelt down on the sand to see better, his old champion’s knees cracking, and looked at Astra’s edge from below, and then at the hook from below, and then stood up.

They talked. Very quietly. Alea couldn’t hear.

Sorrel didn’t move the hook. Alea didn’t move the sword. They stood there, the two of them, holding still, the way you hold still when somebody is taking a portrait.

“If it’s you,” said Sorrel, very quietly, “I want to hear the story.”

“And if it’s you?”

Sorrel thought about it.

“Then I’ll wait till next year,” she said. “I’m good at waiting. When it’s for something.”

Lucan came down off the master’s platform.

Alea had never seen him do that. All week he’d been up there, in his red cloak, weeping, beside Drew in the rocking chair. He’d read out names from up there and announced trials from up there and cried from up there. He came down now. The steps from the platform to the sand. Across the sand, in his red cloak with the gold on the hem, not quickly, to the near ring, where the marshals and Ser Garret were standing.

He listened to them. Ser Garret said something. Lucan looked at the sword and the hook for a long time.

Then he turned to the stands.

He didn’t need to shout. He’d been doing this for fifteen years. He stood on the sand at the edge of the near ring and breathed the way he breathed, and let his voice go out over the Lists like a kite.

“The marshals have looked,” said Lucan. “And Ser Garret Holm, three times champion of these Lists, has looked.”

The silence was total.

“They tell me the blade stopped first,” said Lucan. “By a heartbeat. Less than a heartbeat.” His voice went, just slightly. “They tell me it is the closest final any of them has ever seen. In this sand. In three hundred years.” He looked at Sorrel. “They tell me the hook was there. A breath behind. It was there.”

Sorrel didn’t move.

“The Fledgling Lists,” said Lucan, “to Alea, of Wrenhaven.”

The Lists came apart.

Alea didn’t hear it, at first. She heard it later, in pieces, the way you remember a dream. At the time she just stood in the near ring with Astra a finger’s width from Sorrel’s ribs and the hook against her neck, and didn’t move, and didn’t breathe, and didn’t understand.

Then Sorrel took the hook away.

Very gently. She lifted it off Alea’s neck and stepped back and lowered it, and lowered the other one, and stood there barefoot on the sand with both hooks hanging from her hands like oars out of the water. And she was grinning. All the way. With her eyes wet.

“Next year,” said Sorrel.

Alea lowered Astra. Her arms were shaking so hard she nearly dropped it. She put the point on the sand and leaned on it.

“I nearly lost,” she said.

“You nearly lost,” agreed Sorrel, delighted. “I nearly had you. Twice. Three times.” She laughed, wet. “I’m going to tell the whole quay. I nearly had the lake girl three times and she came at me like a pike.” She shook her head. “My dad’s going to hear about it. Wherever he is. Everyone on the Gullhaven quay’s going to tell him.”

And then she dropped both her hooks on the sand, and stepped forward, and hugged Alea. Hard. Round the neck, with the black sword between them, the way you’d hug somebody you’d met at a well three nights ago and decided, there and then, without discussing it, was going to be your friend.

Alea held on.

That was when she heard the Lists.

It wasn’t a roar. It was bigger than a roar. It was forty thousand people on their feet, and paper fishing rods held up over every head in every stand, swaying, and the bedsheet in the near stand going up and down, LET IT FALL, and the far stand, where Sorrel had fought all week, chanting HOOKS, HOOKS, HOOKS, louder than they had all week, for the girl who’d lost.

And the thirty-five on the bottom tiers. She could hear them separately. She knew their voices. Tiger, screaming. Maigo, in capitals, without needing a channel. Gigichi saying you okay? to nobody. PenguChi saying something very calm. Aleaka, in her slow British voice, stumbling over a word and saying it twice. Calvin’s kettle on the rail. Newish, cutting through everything: ALEA. ALEA. ALEA.

And from the royal box, a sound she’d heard once before, in the king’s yard, when she cut a post in half. One short, deep laugh, like a hammer landing once on an anvil.

She looked up at the fourth tier.

He wasn’t there.

He was on the sand.

She didn’t see him come down. Nobody did; nobody ever saw him. He was just there, at the edge of the near ring, beside Petra and Xav, with the rod over his shoulder and two bees on his head and a paper fishing rod in his hand. Standing still. The way he’d stood at the end of the jetty, every night of her life.

Sorrel let go of her. She looked at the man at the edge of the ring, and then at Alea, and then back at the man, and her grin went curious.

“Is that him?” said Sorrel. Very quietly. “Your dad? The fisherman?”

“Yes.”

Sorrel looked at him for a long time. Her eyes went to his face, and slid off, the way everybody’s did. She frowned. She tried again. They slid off again.

“Huh,” said Sorrel. “He’s very hard to look at.”

“Everyone says that.”

“Is he good?”

“Yes,” said Alea. “He’s very good.”

Sorrel thought about it. Then she picked up her hooks off the sand, and put them over her shoulders like oars, and went across to the edge of the ring, barefoot, and stopped in front of the man nobody could look at.

She held out her hand.

“Your girl beat me,” said Sorrel. “With a pike.”

The man looked at her hand. Then he shook it.

“I saw,” he said.

“Did you teach her that?”

The man was quiet for a moment.

“No,” he said.

Sorrel looked at him. Then she grinned, and let go, and went off across the sand toward the west tunnel, whistling, with her hooks on her shoulders, and the far stand chanting HOOKS at her all the way, and she lifted one hook to them as she went, like somebody on a quay lifting a hand to a boat.

Alea came to the edge of the ring.

She stood in front of him. She was shaking. Astra was on her shoulder and her hands were shaking on Gabe’s grip and her ears were doing something she couldn’t control and she didn’t care. She looked up at him.

“Did you see?” she said.

“I saw.”

“I was losing.”

“You were.”

“I was going over the line. My heels were on the chalk. I looked up at you and I thought, I’m going to lose in front of everyone, I’m going to lose to a stranger, I’ve never—“ She stopped. “And then I thought about the pike.”

“The cow pike,” said her father. “In the reeds. This spring.”

“You remember.”

“She went through my good net.”

“You laughed till you sat down.”

“I did,” said her father.

Alea looked at him. At a face she couldn’t see, that she’d been looking at all her life.

“You said it was Mum,” she said. “That pike. You said that’s exactly what your mother would have done.”

Her father didn’t say anything for a long moment.

“It is,” he said. “It’s exactly what she would have done.” He was quiet. “I’d never have thought of it. I never let anything pull me anywhere. I’m the one with the hook.” A pause. “She used to do it to me all the time. I’d have her on the line, in an argument, and I’d pull, and she’d just come at me, all at once, and the line would go slack, and I’d lose.” Something in his voice went. “Every time. For ten years. I never once worked out how she did it.”

Alea’s ears went flat. Then up. Then flat.

“I didn’t learn it from you,” she said.

“No.”

“I learned it from a pike.”

“You learned it from your mother,” said her father. “The pike just reminded you.”

Behind him, Xav made a very small sound. Petra had turned round and was looking very hard at the east stand.

Lucan came over.

He came across the sand from where he’d been standing with the marshals, in his red cloak, slowly, with something in his hands. Not a crown. Not a prize. A roll. The great leather-bound roll of the Lists, that he’d had on the master’s platform all week, with every entrant’s name in it, in the guild hands of every sponsor, and a pen.

He stopped in front of Alea.

He looked at her for a long time. He’d been weeping all week, Alea knew. At everything. He wasn’t weeping now. His face was very still, for once, the way a showman’s face goes still at the one moment in a show that isn’t a show.

He opened the roll. To a page near the end. And held it out to her, so she could see.

Her own name. In Maren’s small careful guild hand, from a fire on the shingle at the lake, in the spring. Alea, of Wrenhaven. Copper. Aura. Tempered. Sponsor: Maren, guild guide.

And the box. For the father’s name. Blank.

“At the closing,” said Lucan. Quietly. Not to the stands. To her. “At sunset. On the platform. In front of everyone.” He looked at the box. “I read out the champions’ names for the roll. Full names. The way they’re written. It’s tradition.” He paused. “Three hundred years. Every champion of the Fledgling Lists. Their name, and where they’re from, and who their people are. Read out to the whole city, so the city knows.”

Alea looked at the box.

“I left it blank,” she said.

“I know,” said Lucan. “I’ve been looking at it all week.” He held the roll a little further out. “A showman knows what a blank is.”

“What is it?”

“A story that hasn’t been told yet,” said Lucan.

He held out the pen.

Alea didn’t take it. Not yet. She looked at the blank box in the roll of the Lists, in Maren’s careful hand. She looked at Sorrel, at the far end of the sand, going into the west tunnel with her hooks on her shoulders. She looked at Petra and Xav. At the bottom tier of the east stand, where thirty-five people had gone very quiet all at once, because they’d seen Lucan bring the roll.

She looked at her father.

He was standing very still beside her, with the rod over his shoulder and a paper fishing rod in his hand, and two bees on his head, and a face she couldn’t see, the way she’d never been able to see it. He didn’t say anything. He didn’t nod. He didn’t tell her to.

He’d told her once, by a fire on the shingle, in the spring. Across the flames, without a word. When she’d said I want to win something that’s mine first.

Let her.

“At sunset,” said Alea.

Lucan closed the roll, very gently, with the pen inside it, and held it against his chest.

“At sunset,” he agreed.

And then, because he was Lucan, and it was the last day of the Lists, and there were forty thousand people holding up paper fishing rods round him and a girl in front of him with a sword the size of a door who’d just won the closest final in three hundred years with a pike, he burst into tears.
