The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 6: Nine

Chapter 168

Green

5,600 words · 24 min read

Yan had decided not to look.

He’d decided it on the walk back from the rise, through the markers, with the man beside him saying it doesn’t ask whose night it is. He’d decided it again at supper. And again at the gate, at sunset, with the whole of Cinderhold on the wall behind him and a girl with a black sword on her back walking out ahead onto the red beside her father. Not tonight. Tonight’s hers.

He kept it. He sat on the fallen tree at the foot of the knoll with the others, in a row, the way they’d sat a year ago when the man came across the red with the sword on his shoulder, with their backs nearly to the grave. Aldous at the end with his book. Rhea next, very quiet, for Rhea. Salty and Wendy. Niko with his hands in his pockets. Maigo, who hadn’t said a single word since the gate, which was the most frightening thing Yan had seen all year. And Yan, at the other end, with the scythe across his knees and Suzu round his neck.

And he kept the other sight shut.

It wasn’t easy. It never had been. Since the first week, in the hall, it had been there whether he wanted it or not: the living as candles, small and warm, leaning toward whatever they loved. He’d learned to close it the way you learn to close your eyes in bright sun. Not all the way. Never all the way. But enough. Enough that he could sit on a fallen tree at the foot of a knoll and not see a girl’s candle at the top of it, leaning, after fifty-seven days, into the place it had been leaning toward the whole way from Valcrest.

He sat with his eyes open and the other sight shut, and looked at the red going gold, and didn’t turn round.

Round his neck, Suzu was warm. She’d been warm all day. She’d eaten well last night, a hen, in the stable yard, with Wendy kneeling beside him. And she’d been warm in the other way too, the way she’d been warm on the first morning out of Valcrest when he felt his own name come down the cord. She lay with her head on his collarbone and her eyes half shut, and didn’t move.

She didn’t turn round either. He loved her for it.

The note came at sunset.

He felt it before he heard it. Everyone did; he saw them all go still along the trunk at the same moment, the way you go still when a bell rings in a quiet house. Then the sound. High and clear and long, from the top of the knoll behind them, going out over the red in every direction at once. Like a bell. Like a glass with a wet finger round the rim, only enormous, only going on and on.

Salty’s head came up.

“That’s the sword,” said Salty. Very low. He knew iron. He’d carried both halves of it, one on his shoulder for half a day with two ribs broken, the other on a folded blanket the whole last stretch in the snow. “Both of them. That’s both of them at once.”

“She put it in the ground,” said Wendy. “Next to the stone.”

Yan didn’t hear the rest.

Because under the note, under the knoll, under the cairn, the cold went.

He felt it the way he’d felt it every night for two years: faint, deep, a coal of ice under the place where a woman lay, pinned through by a long black spike like a nail through a moth, held round by roots like fingers round a wrist. Asleep. Not turning over. Holding very still.

And when the note came, it went down.

Not up. Down. Deeper. Away from the cairn, away from the green, away from the roots, down into the red the way a stone goes down into a well, slowly, turning a little. Further than he’d ever felt it. So far he nearly lost it. And then it stopped, a long way below, and lay there, smaller, quieter, further off, the way a sound goes quiet when a door shuts between you and it.

Like a dog lying down.

The note stopped.

“It went down,” said Yan.

They all looked at him along the trunk.

“The cold,” said Yan. “Under her. When they rang.” He was quiet. “It went down. A long way. Further than it’s ever been.” He looked at the red. “Not up.”

Nobody said anything for a long time.

“Is that good?” said Maigo. It was the first thing he’d said since the gate. His voice was very small.

Yan thought about it. Properly. From the bottom up.

“Yes,” he said. “I think so. I think it’s good.”

Maigo nodded slowly behind his sunglasses. And then, for the first time since the gate, the leaves on his antlers moved. All of them. Not toward the grave. Just moved, the way leaves move when a breath comes back into a tree.

Aldous went up at sunset with the cloth.

They watched him go. Nobody said anything. The old chaplain got up off the end of the trunk, slowly, the way he got up from everything these days, with his book under one arm and the white linen folded over the other, and went up the slope of the knoll toward the green. He didn’t look back. He stopped at the edge of the green, where the red ended. Yan saw him stop. And wait. And bow his head, a little, to somebody they couldn’t see from the trunk.

And then go on, onto the green.

Then the words.

Yan had heard them before. Once. The first year, on the red, after a scout raid in the spring, over a soldier called Lowry who’d died on the wall in the night. Aldous had laid the white cloth over him for an hour on the morning after and said the old words in the old tongue, low and slow and going round and round, and Yan had stood at the edge with Suzu round his neck and felt her go very still at the white of the cloth. As if she knew it.

She went still now.

He felt it down the cord. She lifted her head off his collarbone. Not toward the grave; she turned her head the other way, up the slope, toward the top of the knoll where they couldn’t see, and the old voice saying the old words. Toward the white.

“What is it?” said Yan. Very quietly. To her.

She didn’t answer. She couldn’t; nobody in this world could hear her but a green snake twenty days west round a calm man’s neck. But down the cord he felt something he’d never felt from her before. Not warmth. Not hunger. Not the cold-wire tightness of the threadless.

Recognition.

The way you feel when you hear a song from your childhood in a market a long way from home, and don’t know the name of it, and know every word.

He sat on the fallen tree with the white snake looking up the slope toward an old man saying words nobody understood any more over a grave, and felt her know them, and didn’t know what she knew.

The hour went by.

The moon came up an hour after sunset.

Higu’s chart. Exactly. Yan had seen the chart, once, on the road east, when Alea took it out by the fire and looked at it the way you’d look at a letter. An hour after sunset. East, over the red. And he watched it come up now over the mountains of Kharvos behind the rise, gold and enormous and full, lifting out of the black line of the peaks like something being raised very carefully out of water.

The whole plain went silver.

Behind them, at the top of the knoll, a voice. Not Aldous; Aldous had come back down at the end of the hour and sat at the end of the trunk again with the folded cloth on his knees and his eyes shut. A girl’s voice. Plain and clear and a little bit small, carrying down the slope on the still air, the way voices carry over water at night.

They couldn’t hear the words. Yan was glad of it. He heard the sound of her: talking, steady, for a long time. Stopping. Going on. Once, laughing, a short wet surprised laugh, and then talking again. And under her voice, sometimes, very low, a man’s.

He didn’t turn round.

None of them did. He looked along the trunk in the moonlight at the six of them and saw that every single one of them was sitting with their back to the knoll and their eyes on the red and their faces doing the thing faces do when you’re keeping very still so as not to hear something that isn’t yours. Rhea had tears running down her face and wasn’t wiping them. Wendy had her head on Salty’s shoulder. Niko had taken his hands out of his pockets and was holding them in his lap, very still, looking at them. Maigo had pulled his hood up, over his antlers, for the first time in a month, and had his arms round his knees.

They sat there in the moonlight in a row on a fallen tree, seven people who’d carried a sword and a stone across this plain, and kept their backs to a girl talking to her mother.

And then she stopped.

Yan felt the silence come down the slope. The long kind. Not the kind between sentences. The kind that comes before something you’ve been carrying a long time and are about to put down.

And then her voice again. Different. Clearer. Not talking now. Saying. A few words, and a gap, and a few more. Like somebody reading something out, line by line, and answering each line.

Yan didn’t hear the words.

He didn’t need to. Because the other sight came up.

He hadn’t opened it. He’d kept it shut, all evening, all through the note and the cloth and the moon, with everything he had. He hadn’t decided anything. It just came up, on its own, all at once, the way your eyes open on their own when somebody says your name in your sleep. As if something at the top of the knoll had said his name.

And he saw her candle.

He’d seen it before. Every night for fifty-seven days. Quick and hot and steady at the same time, like a sword that’s just found out it fits. Leaning east, all the way from Valcrest, the way Suzu leaned toward him: toward a grave she’d never seen.

It wasn’t leaning now.

It had got there. It was standing at the top of the knoll, at the head of a cairn, in the moonlight, and it wasn’t leaning toward anything any more, because it had arrived. It was standing up straight. Very bright. Brighter than he’d ever seen it, brighter than he’d seen any candle except the man’s sea on the red. And it was—

It was green.

Yan had never seen a colour.

Not in a soul. Not once. Two years. Candles were candles: warm, or cold, or pale, or bright, leaning or still. Light. Just light. He’d told Suzu once, in the first winter, on the cairn in the snow, that he thought souls might have colours and he just couldn’t see them yet, the way a baby can’t see colours for the first weeks. She hadn’t answered. She never could.

He saw one now.

Green. The colour of new grass. The colour of a leaf held up to the sun. The colour of the bank of the Wren in the spring. Not a green you could paint. A green that was alive, that moved, that went from pale to deep and back like light on water, standing up at the top of the knoll in the moonlight in the shape of a girl’s candle, so bright and so green that Yan put his hand up in front of his face without meaning to, the way you’d put it up against the sun.

He knew what it was.

He didn’t know how he knew. He’d never been told. Nobody had ever told him what the colours of souls meant; nobody in this world had ever seen them. But he knew, the way you know what a smell means the first time you smell smoke.

It was the colour of loving.

Not being loved. Loving. Pouring out. The way water pours out of a jug that’s been tipped. A girl standing at her mother’s grave saying the last words her mother ever gave her, back, out loud, one at a time, and loving her mother with everything she had, so much and so all at once that it had colour, that it had the colour of every living green thing in the world.

He heard the last of it come down the slope. Three words. Very clear.

He didn’t hear what they were. He saw them. He saw the green go up. All of it. At once. Like a lamp turned up all the way.

And then he saw everything.

It went out from the girl like ripples out from a stone dropped in a pond, and wherever it went, Yan saw colour.

Her father. Beside her, at the head of the cairn. The man. The place where a flame had been, very large and very quiet, the thing Yan had looked at for a year and never been able to name. It had no colour. It was too big, or too old, or too far from home; he didn’t know. It was just the place. But in the middle of it, very small, the spark that had caught on the rise yesterday when the man knelt in the ring and said that’s done.

The spark was green.

The same green. Exactly. A point of it, in the middle of all that quiet, leaning toward the girl beside him with everything it had.

Down the slope. Along the trunk. His friends.

Salty: a forge. He’d always been a forge. And the forge was green, deep green, the green of the inside of a wood, and leaning toward the woman with her head on his shoulder. Wendy: a candle behind glass. Green behind the glass, a cool clear green like the sea in a bay, leaning back. Two greens under one blanket, so close they were nearly one.

Maigo: a tree in the sun. Green, of course, Maigo was always going to be green, and gold at the edges, the way his leaves were going gold. Rhea: green and hot and bright as a bonfire, leaning toward everything at once. Niko: two candles. Two greens. Side by side, going round each other, slowly, the way they’d gone round since a frying pan in a forge, so close Yan couldn’t see the space between them.

All of them green. Every person he loved.

And at the end of the trunk, Aldous.

Aldous wasn’t green.

Aldous was white.

Yan stared.

Not grey. Grey was what he’d always half-seen in the hens and the rabbits and the sheep, the small pale lights going out of dying things to Suzu; he understood that now, in the same moment, the way you understand a word in a language you’ve been hearing all your life: grey was empty. Grey was a light with nobody in it any more.

This wasn’t grey.

It was white. Clean, and very bright, and very quiet. The white of the cloth folded on the old man’s knees. The white of snow on the lake in the first week before anybody’s walked on it. The white of a snake. An old chaplain’s candle, sitting at the end of a fallen tree with its eyes shut, burning white, the way a candle burns in a chapel at night with nobody watching, steady and plain and asking nothing.

Pure, thought Yan. He didn’t know where the word came from. That’s what that is.

And round his own neck, Suzu.

He’d never seen her soul. Two years. She’d been the one thing he couldn’t look at with the other sight, the way you can’t see the back of your own head. She’d always just been there, down the cord, warmth and cold and hunger and the thing he’d felt every day since the hall and never put into words until a green snake on a roof made him.

He saw her now.

She was green.

The same green. Exactly. The green of the girl at the top of the knoll, and the spark in the man, and Salty’s forge, and every person on that trunk he loved. The green of loving. Coiled round his neck in the moonlight, close, warm, every inch of her, the whole white length of her burning that colour from the inside like a lamp behind white silk.

Leaning toward him.

Not like a plant on a windowsill. He’d said that, once, in a chapel, to the others. Her soul leans toward me like a plant on a windowsill. It wasn’t like that. It was like the girl. It had arrived. It wasn’t leaning toward anything any more, because it was already where it was going.

It was him.

His window opened.

He hadn’t opened it. It opened itself, in the air in front of his face, in the moonlight, plain and white, the way it had opened on the cairn in the snow when he counted thirty-six.

He looked at it.

Hue.

Not tomorrow, he’d said. Walking back through the markers yesterday with the man beside him. Tomorrow’s hers. I’m not going to do anything tomorrow except stand at the back with the others and keep quiet.

It doesn’t ask whose night it is, the man had said.

I’m deciding anyway.

He hadn’t decided anything. He’d kept his back to the grave and the other sight shut with everything he had. And it had come anyway. Not from him. From her. From a girl at the top of a knoll saying three words to a black stone, so hard, so green, that the colour of it had gone out over the whole of the red and opened his eyes.

It was hers. That was the thing. He’d been right and the man had been right. It was her night. And this had come out of her night the way light comes out of a lamp.

He was crying. He didn’t know when he’d started. He sat on the fallen tree with the scythe across his knees and his window open and tears running down his face in the moonlight, quietly, the way he did everything, and couldn’t stop.

It came out of him.

He didn’t feel it start. He felt it already happening: something going out of him, from his chest, from the place where the cord went in, from Suzu. Out. Into the air round him. Not much. A little. The way breath goes out of you on a cold morning and you see it.

Only it wasn’t breath. It was green.

He looked down at his hands on the scythe. They were glowing. Faintly. Softly. The green of the girl’s candle, of Suzu’s eyes, of a leaf held up to the sun. Not burning. Not hot. Just light, coming off his skin the way light comes off a lamp that somebody’s shaded with a leaf. Off his hands, and his arms, and the long silver-white hair falling over his shoulders, and the white snake round his neck, so that the two of them sat there at the end of the fallen tree in the moonlight lit green from the inside, very softly, like a lantern in a wood.

Along the trunk, everybody had turned round.

Not to the grave. To him.

Wendy saw it first. She always did. She’d lifted her head off Salty’s shoulder and was staring at him with her mismatched eyes very wide.

“Yan,” said Wendy.

Salty had half stood. Maigo had pushed his hood back. Niko was very still, with his hands in his lap, looking at Yan the way he looked at things he was going to learn. Rhea had stopped crying.

And Aldous.

The old chaplain had opened his eyes. He was sitting at the far end of the trunk with the folded white cloth on his knees, looking at Yan. At the green light coming off him. At the white snake round his neck, lit green from inside.

And his face went white.

Yan saw it happen. All the colour going out of the old man’s long grave face at once, the way it went out of the old soldiers on the wall at Cinderhold when they saw Alea’s face. As if he’d seen somebody he buried. Aldous sat at the end of the trunk with his hands on the white cloth and stared at Yan, and his lips moved, and no sound came out.

Then his hand came up. Two fingers. The sign. The one he’d made under his sleeve on the first day in the castle, when he saw a white snake round a young man’s throat, and on every day after for a month. Small and quick. Warding.

And then he said it. Not loudly. Not to anyone. The way you’d say a word you’d read in a book a long time ago and never once heard out loud and never thought you’d need.

“Soul Eater,” said Aldous.

Nobody moved.

The green light came off Yan’s hands, very softly, in the moonlight. Round his neck, Suzu had lifted her head and was looking at Aldous with her two green eyes. Not cold. Not hissing. Just looking. The way you’d look at somebody who’d said your name in a crowd.

“Father,” said Yan. His voice came out very even. It always did. “What did you say?”

Aldous didn’t answer at once. He was still looking at the green. At the snake. His two fingers were still up, half raised, in the sign. And then, slowly, Yan watched them change. The way they’d changed once before, at a waystation fire, in the first week, when Yan told him Suzu would never eat people. The fingers didn’t go down. They turned. Less like warding something off.

More like a blessing.

“It’s a name,” said Aldous. His voice wasn’t steady. “In the rites. The oldest part of them. The part we say over the dead in the old tongue that nobody speaks any more and nobody understands. I’ve said it a thousand times. I said it an hour ago, over her.” He looked at the folded cloth on his knees. “There’s one line in it that no chaplain alive knows the meaning of. We say it because it’s always been said. I asked my own master once, when I was a boy, and he didn’t know, and his master didn’t know.”

He looked at Yan.

“We lay white over them,” said Aldous, slowly, translating, as if he were dragging each word up out of a deep well, “for the Soul Eater.” He was quiet. “Who carries.”

Yan sat very still.

“That’s all it says,” said Aldous. “That’s the whole of it. One line. We lay white over them for the Soul Eater, who carries.” His hand was shaking on the cloth. “Nobody knows who the Soul Eater is. Nobody knows why the cloth is white. It’s older than the church. It’s older than the kingdom.” He looked at the white snake round Yan’s neck, lit green. “I thought it was a story. I’ve laid white cloth over eleven hundred people and I thought it was a story.”

Round Yan’s neck, Suzu put her head down on his collarbone.

And down the cord, he felt it again. What he’d felt when the old man said the words over the grave. The song from your childhood, in a market a long way from home.

Recognition.

“I’m not going to eat anyone,” said Yan. Quietly. “Father. I need you to know that. I never have. She never has. Never people.” He looked at his own hands, glowing. “She’d rather.”

“I know,” said Aldous.

“Do you?”

“I know,” said Aldous again, and his voice steadied, the way an old man’s voice steadies when he’s decided something. “I’ve watched you two years. I watched you kneel in a poulterer’s yard in Valcrest and turn your head away. I watched you kneel in the grass on the first night out with the girl beside you and not turn it.” He looked at the green on Yan’s hands. “And it says carries. Not eats. Who carries.” He was quiet. “I’ve been laying white cloth over the dead for thirty years for somebody who carries them. I never once thought I’d meet them.”

Yan didn’t say anything.

“I don’t know what it means,” said Aldous. “I don’t know what you are. I don’t know what she is.” He looked at Suzu. “I know that you sat on this tree with your back to that girl all night so you wouldn’t take anything of hers that wasn’t yours. And the first colour you ever saw came from her anyway.” He lowered his hand, at last. “That’s not something that eats, son. That’s something that carries.”

The green went out.

Slowly. Not all at once. The way a lamp goes out when the oil’s gone: dimmer, and dimmer, and then just a faint shine on his hands, and then his hands. Ordinary. Pale in the moonlight. Shaking a little.

He felt tired. Very tired, suddenly, the way you feel at the end of a long day’s walking, all at once, in the legs and the back and somewhere deeper. Older. Just for a breath. Then it passed.

He didn’t think about it. He’d think about it later, a long time later, when somebody with pink and violet hair looked at his window with a sight he didn’t have and went very quiet. Not now.

Now he closed his window, and looked along the trunk.

They were all looking at him. Six faces in the moonlight. And the other sight was still up, a little, the way your eyes stay open after a bright light, and he could still see it, faintly, fading: green. All of them. Leaning toward him. Every one.

“Okay,” said Yan.

It was the only word he had. It always was.

Maigo made a sound like a tree in a high wind and threw both arms round him, scythe and snake and all. And Yan let him. And Salty, after a moment, put one big square hand flat on Yan’s back, between the shoulder blades, the way you’d steady somebody going up a slope. And Wendy said you idiot, you absolute idiot, on her night, very fondly, with her eyes wet. And Rhea said CANDLE, at the top of her voice, and then put both hands over her mouth, appalled, and looked up the knoll.

Nobody at the top said anything. Nobody came down.

But Yan, with the last of the other sight still up, saw the spark. In the place where a flame had been, at the top of the knoll, at the head of a cairn. The green one. The new one. It had turned. Not toward the grave. Not toward the girl, though she was beside it.

Toward him. Down the slope. For a moment.

And then away again, deliberately, the way you’d turn away from a window to let somebody inside have the room to themselves.

Don’t look, Yan thought he heard, very faintly, on the still air, from the top of the knoll. It’s his. Let him have it.

He looked down at his hands.

He didn’t sleep.

The others did, eventually. Rolled in their blankets along the foot of the fallen tree, in a row, the way they’d slept round fires on the road for twenty days. Salty and Wendy under one. Maigo with his antlers against the trunk, snoring like a tree in the wind. Niko flat on his back with his hands folded on his chest and his candles going round, Yan could still see faintly, very slowly, in his sleep. Rhea with her arms out as if she’d been knocked down. Aldous sitting up against the end of the trunk with the white cloth folded on his chest and his book on top of it, asleep, his white candle steady.

Yan sat at the other end with the scythe across his knees and Suzu round his neck, and watched the moon go over.

He let the other sight come up, a little. Carefully. He was afraid it would be too much, now: colours everywhere, everything, all the time. It wasn’t. It was like the first time you put on glasses after years of not knowing you needed them. Everything the same, and everything sharper. The candles were still candles. They just had colours now, the way they must always have had, and he’d never been able to see.

He looked up the knoll.

Two of them. At the top. Lying on a blanket at the edge of the green, by the cairn, under the moon. A place where a flame had been, very large and very quiet, with a green spark in the middle of it. And on its arm, a candle. Asleep. Not leaning any more. Green all the way through, steady and deep and quiet, like a forest at night.

And between them and the black stone, the two halves of a sword, standing a hand’s width apart in the green, shining in the moonlight. And under the stone, a long way down now, further than it had ever been, the cold. Lying still. Like something that had been told to lie down by somebody it had to listen to.

Ten, thought Yan.

He’d thought it last night, at the gate, looking at the knoll under a moon a night short of full. Tomorrow, ten. Five of Group 3. Aldous and Rhea. A man. A girl. And one more.

He looked at the cairn.

He couldn’t see her. You couldn’t see the dead; he’d told the man that yesterday on the rise. He only saw what was leaving, and she’d left nine years ago. There was nothing under the cairn for the other sight but stones and earth and, far below, a cold that had gone to sleep.

But on top of the cairn, catching the moonlight, there was a small white stone. And at the foot, another. And round the foot, a ring of children’s stones from a town a month away. And beside the black stone, a tree that leaned toward it the way a person leans toward somebody they’re talking to. And on the blanket at the edge of the green, a girl, green all the way through, asleep on her father’s arm, who’d said three words tonight to her mother that had been nine years coming.

Ten, Yan thought again. We’re ten.

He didn’t need the other sight for that.

Near dawn, Suzu lifted her head.

He felt it down the cord before she moved. Warmth. Not fed warmth. The other kind. And under it, something new. Something he’d never felt from her in two years, not even on the roof in Valcrest with a green snake translating every word.

Not a word. A colour.

She turned her head and looked at him, very close, in the grey light, with her two green eyes. And he saw her soul with the new sight, the whole white length of her round his neck, burning green from the inside like a lamp behind silk. The same green as the girl. The same green as him, when it came out of his hands. The colour of loving, the colour the old rites didn’t have a word for, the colour a chaplain had been laying white cloth over for thirty years without knowing why.

Yun.

He felt it. Down the cord. Into his chest. One syllable. The name only she had ever called him, in two worlds.

He didn’t guess what it meant. He’d stopped guessing on a roof in Valcrest. He didn’t need to guess now. He could see it.

“I know,” said Yan. Very quietly. To her. “Green.”

Suzu put her head back down on his collarbone, and closed her eyes, satisfied, as if she’d been waiting two years for him to see something that had been right in front of him the whole time, and wasn’t going to discuss it.

The sky went grey over Kharvos. Then pale. Then gold. And the sun came up over the mountains behind the rise, and the red went gold all the way to the fort, and at the top of the knoll, a girl woke up on her father’s arm, and lay still a moment, and looked at a black stone in the morning light.

And at the foot of the knoll, on the fallen tree, Maigo sat up, and pushed his sunglasses up on his antlers, and looked at the young ash at the top of the slope with its leaves going gold at the edges.

“Okay,” said Maigo, to the tree, very quietly. “It’s morning.” He took a breath. “I’ve got so much to tell you.”
