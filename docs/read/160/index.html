Thea had been keeping a bug list since Valcrest.

It was in the back of her notebook, behind the pages of Parse notes and the copy of the circle’s fourteen rings and the page where she’d worked out, on the road north, how old she’d be at the top if the tiers kept coming one a year, and then, underneath, how old if they didn’t. A column of numbered lines in pencil, the way she’d kept bug lists on Earth for every project she’d ever touched, because if you wrote a thing down with a number next to it, it stopped being a ghost and started being a problem.

BUG 1. Timestamps skip a minute. Day 68, about 23:10. Every window, all at once.

BUG 2. One line appears twice. Day 71. Maigo’s. (Unfortunately.)

BUG 3. Names flicker grey for half a breath. Day 74, 21:40-ish. Hyvred, Kei, Drew. Then fine.

BUG 4. Timestamps skip again. Day 79.

Small things. Nobody else had noticed, or if they had, they’d thought it was them. A line that came up twice; well, Maigo did write the same thing twice, sometimes, in capitals, on purpose. A minute missing off the timestamps; who looked at the timestamps? Thea looked at the timestamps. Thea looked at everything in the channel, all the time, because she’d built it, out of a thing she didn’t understand, on a night in the first spring with Higu writing the rules beside her. Nobody else could see what it was made of. She could. And for two years it had run like a river: smooth, steady, two hundred lines going up and up and away. She’d never once seen it stutter.

And then Valcrest. And then, on the sixty-eighth night, it stuttered.

She’d known at once what it was. Not how. What. Because the sixty-eighth night was the night Hana gave the guides their halves.

She’d asked Maren on the road north.

She’d had to. She’d had a list of times, and a feeling, and she was a coder; you don’t trust a feeling, you check it against the logs. So on the fourth day out of Valcrest she’d fallen into step beside Maren’s horse and said, as casually as she could, which wasn’t very, Maren, would you tell me every time your half goes warm?

Maren had looked down at her from the saddle for a long moment. Maren always looked at things for a long moment.

Why?

I think it’s talking to the channel, Thea had said. And I don’t think it means to.

Maren had said yes. Of course she had; Maren said yes to anything that could be written down. And for twenty days on the road north, every evening at the fire, Maren had handed her a slip of paper in her small exact hand. Warm at the second hour after sunset. Warm briefly near midnight; words: the Kettle has a cold, from Lucan, about Ina (I do not understand). Warm at dawn. And Thea had laid them beside her bug list, line by line.

They matched. Every one.

Every time a word went along one of Hana’s cords, from a guide to Hana or from Hana to a guide, somewhere in the channel a timestamp skipped, or a name flickered, or a line came up twice. Small. A hiccup. The way a lamp dips when somebody opens a door in another room.

She hadn’t told anyone yet. She’d wanted to understand it first. That was the thing about her; Higu said it, fondly, in the channel once, Thea won’t tell you a bug exists till she’s got the fix. It was true. It was a bad habit. She’d had it on Earth too.

She was going to understand it tonight, she thought. On the jetty. Where she thought best.

They’d come home the day before.

She didn’t think she’d ever forget it. Coming down the river road into Wrenhaven in the afternoon with Gigichi carrying the water in front of her, scowling, not spilling a drop, the way she’d carried it every day for twenty-two days because a man in Valcrest had said ask Gigichi, she’ll say no and do it anyway. The town coming down to the gate. The mayor at the river gate in his chain, looking past all of them at the back of the dray, at the place where nobody with two buckets was walking. And then looking anyway.

And the militia hall. The yard, the step, the shutters. Their beds. The smell of the place, which was woodsmoke and Maren’s lamp-oil and the damp of the lake at the bottom of the hill, and which Thea hadn’t known was the smell of home until she walked into it and her knees nearly went.

And this morning, the barge. Ten people from another world and a musician and two hard women with swords, going away downriver on a tarred black boat, and Gabe in the stern waving until the reeds took him. And Higu, after, walking up from the quay with an empty basket on her arm, and putting it down on the step of the militia hall, with a stone in it.

Thea had stood in the road with the others and looked at it.

She hadn’t cried. Xola had, beside her, the way Xola did everything, openly and with her whole face. Thea hadn’t. She’d looked at the empty basket on the step and thought, very clearly, the way you think something at three in the morning when the code finally compiles: I want to be good at something before he comes home.

Not better than him. Nobody was better than him. Just good. Good at the thing she was for. So that when he came down the lake road in the frost with his daughter and the rod and knocked on the mayor’s door like a stranger, and came up the hill to the militia hall and filled the basket, she’d have something to show him that she’d done on her own.

She hadn’t known, at the time, what the thing would be.

She went down the lake road after supper, in the dark, with a lantern.

“Nobody alone,” said Higu, at the door of the hall. Not stopping her. Just saying it.

“I know.”

“I’ll send a moth.”

“You always do,” said Thea.

Higu smiled, very slightly, under her hood. And when Thea was halfway down the lake road, between the hawthorn hedges and the wall, she felt it come: a small soft weight on her shoulder, the size of a thumbnail, pale in the lantern light. One of Higu’s. It sat on her collar and folded its wings and didn’t move, and Thea knew that at the top of the hill, in the window of the militia hall, Higu was sitting with her eyes shut and her counting book on her knee, seeing what the moth saw.

“Hi, Gu,” said Thea, to her shoulder.

The moth didn’t say anything. Higu’s moths never did.

The cabin was dark. The hens were asleep in their run, thank goodness; she’d heard about the hens. The jetty went out into the black water, silver in the lantern light, and at the end of it, nothing. Nobody. The place where the boards were worn pale in a hollow the shape of a man.

She went out to the end.

She didn’t sit in the hollow. She sat beside it, with her legs over the water, and put the lantern down on the boards beside her, and her notebook on her knee. The lake was very still. It always was, at night, by the north shore. Rowan said it hadn’t been, the summer the man was gone; Rowan said the lake had been just a lake. Thea had thought that was a mayor being sentimental. She sat on the end of the jetty with the dark water under her feet, and felt the stillness of it, and wasn’t sure any more.

She opened her window.

It opened plain and white in front of her face, the way it had opened every day for two years.

And under it, scrolling, the channel. Two hundred lines, going up and away. Higu’s home, from yesterday. Chris’s evening numbers, from Valcrest: 25 of 35. Everybody’s somewhere they know. Tiger in capitals about a shed. Kei: Hisui says the wind’s wrong over the pass. Not bad. Just wrong. Gabe, from the barge, two hours ago: Duskhollow tomorrow. The river’s very fast going this way. Calvin says it’s not him. It’s him.

She let her eyes go down.

That was how Parse worked. You didn’t look at the window. You looked through it, the way you’d look through the glass of a window at the room behind it. And the room behind the channel was the most beautiful thing Thea had ever seen, and she’d seen it every night for two years and still wasn’t used to it.

Code.

Not code the way it had been on a screen, in a language with brackets. Code the way the circle was code, the way the windows were: layers of it, written in light, in English, all the way down. Going round. Running. She could see the channel the way you’d see a river from a bridge: the lines going along it, two hundred of them, in their order. And under the river, holding it, the thing it ran in. Slow and vast and old and warm, turning, the way a mill wheel turns in the dark under a mill. The binding. The seventh and eighth rings of the circle, still running, two years after it brought them. Holding all thirty-five. Writing the Medium line in every window. And carrying, in the scope of its hands, the channel, because that was what Thea had hooked the channel into, on that night in the first spring, without knowing what it was.

And far off, east, very faint, at the very edge of everything, warmth.

The first window. The root. The one every other window had been copied from. She could feel it from here the way you can feel the sun through a closed eyelid. A long way east. Further than it had been in the summer. At Cinderhold, on the edge of the red. Asleep, she thought. It had the feeling of a thing asleep.

She sat on the end of the jetty and looked at all of it, and waited for the bug.

It came at 23:41.

She saw the time on the channel. She’d remember it. She’d write it in the bug list afterwards, in pencil, under BUG 11, and then cross out the word BUG, and write something else.

It didn’t come as a hiccup.

It came as seven at once.

Seven points of light, all over the kingdom, all in the same heartbeat. She felt them before she saw them. Purple, and gold, and very small, like seven sparks struck off seven flints at the same moment. One in Solmere, bright, the brightest. And six more, scattered: one at the top of the hill behind her, in the militia hall, where Maren slept. One far east, at Cinderhold, near the warmth. One south, in Valcrest. One west, Thornwall. Two on the river, going downstream, close together, on a tarred black barge.

Hana’s cords. All seven halves at once.

Hana had written something. On every half on her wrist, at the same time. Thea could feel the words go out from Solmere along the seven thin threads of the cords, like seven arrows loosed off one string.

And the binding saw them.

She understood it, then. All at once. The way you understand a bug in the moment it finally happens in front of you instead of in the logs.

Hana’s cords weren’t just cords. Hana had made them with her knots and her Keepsake and Cursiffe’s ink, and Cursiffe’s ink was the one thing in this world that the summoned could write that natives couldn’t read. And the guides were natives. They couldn’t read English. So the cords didn’t carry English. They carried what the words meant. And on the other half, they wrote it again, in whatever the holder could read. Hana’s English came out in Maren’s half in the plain script of Aurelan. Aldous’s careful hand came out on Hana’s in English.

The cords translated.

The way the windows translated. The way every window in the world had translated, since the hall in Solmere, the speech and the print of a strange world into something thirty-five strangers could read.

Hana had built seven tiny windows. Without knowing it. Seven little pieces of the same thing the binding was, running outside it, on threads the binding had never made.

And the binding, which had been running for two years and held thirty-five people and wrote the Medium line and carried the channel in its hands, and which had never once in four hundred years seen anything else in the world that did what it did, saw seven of them light up at once.

And reached for them.

It was like watching a hand close on smoke.

The binding reached. It was slow and vast and very old, and it didn’t think, Thea was sure; it just ran. And it ran the way it had been written to run: anything that carried the words of the people it held, it carried. That was its whole job. And here were seven bright things, all over the kingdom, carrying words, translating, the way windows did. So it reached for them, to carry them.

And couldn’t.

Because they weren’t its. They didn’t belong to anyone it had brought. Six of the seven halves were held by natives: by Maren, by Aldous, by Lucan, by Corvin, by Emrys, by Garrick’s half in Xav’s pack. Natives weren’t in the binding’s scope. They had no Medium line. They had nothing the binding knew how to hold.

So it tried to put them where it put everything it carried. In the channel.

Thea saw the first one come up.

A line in the channel with no name.

Then another one, the same, a breath later. Then another. Seven of them, one after another, each one the same four words, each one with nothing before the colon. A blank where a name should have been. Ghost lines. Lines from no window.

And the channel bucked.

The top of it went first.

Thea watched it happen. The two hundred lines, which had gone up and away smoothly for two years, suddenly started going up fast. Too fast. Lines she’d been reading a moment ago vanished off the top in a stream. Higu’s home. Gone. Tiger’s shed. Gone. Chris’s numbers. Gone. As if somebody had grabbed the bottom of the channel and was pulling it down through a hole.

And the names started to go grey.

Not one or two. All of them. Flickering. Maigo, grey, then white, then grey. Hyvred. Suzuyun. Xavier. Hanarisu. Going out, and coming back, and going out, like lamps in a wind. And under it, in the river, the binding turning, the great slow wheel of it, and something in the turning that wasn’t right. A catch. A judder. Like a mill wheel with a stone caught in it, grinding.

Seven ghost lines that it couldn’t hold and couldn’t drop. Trying to make them channel. Failing. Trying again.

And every time it tried, the channel lost ten more lines off the top.

Thea sat on the end of the jetty with her window open and her hands gone cold.

It’s going to crash, she thought.

Not the binding. The binding was too big and too old to crash; she could feel that. It would grind and grind and keep turning. But the channel was hers. It was small. It was a thing she’d hooked onto the binding on a night in the first spring with a borrowed idea and Higu’s four rules. And it couldn’t take this. She could see it not taking it. Two hundred lines, going down a hole ten at a time. Names going grey. If it lost all two hundred, if every name went grey at once, it would go. Just go. The way a program goes when it’s asked to do a thing it was never built to do and keeps trying.

And then there’d be no channel.

Thirty-five people, in seven places, in the dark, with no lines. Like the first year. Letters, by rider. A month to Cinderhold. Nobody is alone, Higu had written, rule four, the night they built it, and thirty-four people had written it back, one after another. Nobody is alone.

The ghost lines came up again. The door is open. The door is open. Twenty more lines went off the top.

She tried Patch first.

Of course she did. It was what she had. Patch was her second skill, from the midwinter fire; it mended small things. A flickering bar. A skill page drawn wrong. An ordering. She’d patched a hundred small things in the channel, quietly, at night, without telling anybody.

She reached into the place where the ghost lines came in, and tried to mend it.

It was like trying to mend a waterfall with a needle.

She felt Patch go into it, small and neat and careful, the way it always went, and find the break, and start to close it. And the binding turned, vast and slow, and the break opened again under her hands, wider, and something hit her through Patch like a wave hits a swimmer, and she was on her back on the jetty with the lantern rolling and her ears ringing and Higu’s moth clinging to her collar with all six legs.

She lay there for a moment, looking up at the stars.

Fifty lines left. She could feel it, without looking. Fifty, out of two hundred.

It’s too big, she thought. Patch is for small things. This isn’t small.

She thought, lying on her back, about the warmth in the east.

He was there. The root. The first window. A long way off, asleep, at Cinderhold. He’d almost certainly written the two hundred lines into the channel himself, one night in the winter, without asking anyone, and never said; and she and Higu had never asked. He could fix this. He’d built the thing the binding copied. He was the admin of the whole server. If anybody in two worlds could reach into the binding and tell it to stop, it was him.

She could feel the shape of how to wake him. She thought she could. Something to do with the root. A tug on the oldest thread. He’d feel it. He’d come.

He was at Cinderhold. With his daughter. Five weeks before the night he’d been walking toward, alone, for eight years.

No, thought Thea.

She sat up.

I want to be good at something before he comes home.

She’d thought it that morning, looking at an empty basket on a step. She thought it again now. Not as a wish. As a fact. Like a line at the top of a file. Something you put there so you’d remember what the whole thing was for.

Forty lines.

She didn’t try to fix it.

That was the thing, she’d think afterwards. That was the whole of it. She’d spent two years fixing things. Parsing them, understanding them, finding the break, mending the break. That was what she was. That was what Patch was. And this wasn’t a break. Nothing was broken. The binding was doing exactly what it had been written to do. The cords were doing exactly what Hana had made them to do. They were both right. They just didn’t know about each other.

It wasn’t a bug.

It was two programs that had never been introduced.

Thirty lines.

She put her hands up in front of her. Not to Patch. Not to mend. She put them up the way you’d put your hands up to steady something that was swaying: a ladder, a tall jar, a child on a wall. One on each side. One on the channel, and the great slow binding under it. One on the seven thin purple threads coming in from all over the kingdom with their four words.

And she held them.

She’d never held anything.

Not like this. Candle was reliable spells; Spark was small ones. She’d never in her life held a spell still. That was the third tier. A Lantern holds a spell steady; she’d read it in the guides’ book in the first week, in the list of tiers, and thought, that sounds boring. Lanterns were village mages. Lanterns kept a ward on a well, or a light on a gate, or a fire banked in a smithy all night. Holding. Keeping. Not doing anything clever. Just not letting go.

She didn’t let go.

It was the hardest thing she had ever done. Harder than Parse, the first night, when the whole window had opened under her eyes and she’d nearly fainted. Harder than Candle, at the midwinter fire. The binding was so big. It turned under her right hand like a mill wheel under a child’s palm, slow and huge and grinding on the stone caught in it, and she couldn’t stop it turning and she didn’t try. She just held it steady. Held the channel on top of it still, so it stopped losing lines off the top. Twenty-six lines. Twenty-five. Twenty-five. Twenty-five. Holding. And with her left hand she held the seven threads, seven purple sparks with their four words, still, in the air, so they stopped knocking at the binding’s door and just hung there, waiting.

Everything stopped.

The channel stopped scrolling. The names stopped flickering; they hung in the middle of it, half grey, half white, like lamps caught in the moment of going out. The ghost lines hung. The binding still turned, under everything, slow and vast, but it turned without grinding, because she was holding the stone off its teeth.

And she sat on the end of a jetty on Lake Ilse in the dark, with her hands up, holding two things that had never met, and felt her whole body shake with it.

Now what, she thought.

It came to her the way the best fixes always came: stupidly.

She thought about the first time. On Earth. A long time ago, when she was fourteen, at a desk in her bedroom, at two in the morning, with a book open on the bed and a screen in front of her with nothing on it. The first program. The one everybody wrote first. The one every language had, the one every book started with, the one that did nothing at all except prove that the thing on the other side of the screen was listening.

You wrote two words. And you ran it. And it said them back.

And that was how you knew it worked. That was how two things that had never met found out they could talk. Not by fixing anything. By saying hello.

She took her right hand off the binding.

She felt it start to grind. She didn’t have long. She lifted one finger, the way they all did, the way Ina wrote catch and Winter had written still and John had written look, and in the air between her two hands, between the channel and the cords, in the place where they’d been knocking at each other and missing, she wrote one word. In English. In the plain lowercase letters of a fourteen-year-old at two in the morning.

hello

The binding answered.

She felt it. She’d remember it for the rest of her life, the way Alea said her father said you remembered your first tier. The first time the world answers back. Under her, in the river, the great slow wheel turned, and in the turning, for the first time in two years of her watching it, it did something that wasn’t just running.

It noticed.

It noticed her word, there in the gap. And it answered, the way the first window must have answered a boy under a blanket at six, whispering a word because it was the only one he had. Not in words. In warmth. A warmth came up out of the binding, through the channel, into her right hand, the way the warmth of a hand comes through a glove.

And the cords answered.

The seven sparks, hanging in her left hand. Purple and gold. Hana’s knots. Hana’s whole careful, lovely, frightened heart, the one that had pushed a charm down for two years and made something to let other people talk instead. The cords heard the word in the gap, and they did what they’d been made to do. They carried it. They translated it. Hello, they said back, in seven scripts at once.

And in the gap between Thea’s hands, the two things met.

Not merging. She’d been afraid of that, a little. They didn’t become one thing. The binding stayed the binding, vast and old and slow. The cords stayed the cords, small and bright and Hana’s. They just met, the way two people meet in a doorway: and looked at each other: and knew, now, what the other one was.

Not mine, the binding said. Not in words. Not yours, the cords said.

Pass.

And the seven purple sparks went on. Past the binding. Not into it. Alongside it, the way a small boat goes past a mill wheel in a river, close, and not caught. Along their seven thin threads, out of Solmere, to the top of the hill behind her, and east, and south, and west, and downriver. Carrying their four words. Translating. Arriving.

And the ghost lines went out of the channel, one after another, softly, the way breath goes off a window. Seven lines with no names, gone. Nothing broken. Nothing lost.

And the names came back.

Maren came down the lake road at midnight with a lamp.

The cord. The short purple-and-gold knotted half, with its half-bead and its strip of thin blue paper wound round the knot.

“Yes,” said Thea. “It could.”

Maren nodded slowly. She looked at the cord in her hand, with its two lines on its strip of blue paper, one in a princess’s careful English turned into Aurelan, one in a programmer’s lowercase turned into the same. Then she wound it carefully round her fingers and put it in the pocket of her dressing gown.

“Come up,” she said. “It’s midnight. You’re shaking. Higu’s been sitting in the window with her eyes shut for an hour and Xola’s crying and Gigichi says if you don’t come up the hill in the next quarter of an hour she’s coming down it, and she means with the lamp.”

Thea laughed.

She picked up the lantern from where it had rolled against the post, and stood, which took two tries because her knees weren’t working, and looked once, before she went, east. Over the lake. Over the hill on the far shore and the dark country beyond it, toward a fort a month away, and a warmth at the very edge of everything that she’d felt all night, asleep.

It wasn’t asleep.

She felt it, just for a moment, with her Parse let down and her hands still tingling with the binding. The first window. The root. A long way east. And something in it, very faint, very quiet, the way somebody in another room looks up from a book at a sound they weren’t expecting, and listens, and goes back to the book, smiling.

He’d felt it.

He hadn’t come. He hadn’t needed to. He’d felt the binding grind, and then stop grinding, and he’d known whose hands were on it. And he’d stayed where he was. At Cinderhold, with his daughter, five weeks from the night he’d been walking toward for eight years. And let her hold it.

Still here, Thea thought, east, to nobody, the way she’d written it once in the channel after TODO.

She went up the hill to the militia hall with Maren and the lamp and the moth, past an empty basket on the step with a stone in it, and Gigichi was standing in the door with the lamp after all, scowling, and said you okay?

“Yes,” said Thea. “Yes. I’m okay.”
