The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 5: The Lists

Chapter 128

Bring the Girl

6,789 words · 30 min read

Maren kept lists.

She had kept them all her life. As a girl in a guildhouse in the capital, copying out contracts for a copper a page. As a guide-in-training in the Advisor’s tower, where she’d been the only one of the seven who wrote down everything he said, and the only one he’d ever caught doing it and not told to stop. As a guide, these two years, in a militia hall in Wrenhaven and on a lake road and on the long road south: what the girls ate, and what they learned, and what percentage their windows said, and who had cried and who had pretended not to.

Today’s list was short. She had written it on the back of yesterday’s, at dawn, at the end of the long table in the Broken Lance, while thirty people slept round her on benches and floors and each other.

Day 60. Midsummer eve.

1. Take the girl to the king’s yard. Second hour.

2. Advisor.

3. The king to the Lance (he has said noon; assume the third hour).

4. Group 3, east gate, sunset.

She’d looked at the second line for some time after she wrote it. Then she’d left it as it was. One word. It didn’t need any more. She had been writing to him every second day for two years, and he had been writing back, and every letter he wrote had ended with the same three words at the bottom, and every report she sent had answered them on its last line. She had not been in a room with him since the morning of the summoning.

She did not know what she would say. That was not like her. She had decided not to think about it.

The water-carrier had asked her to take the girl.

He’d asked her last night, at the long table, quietly, while Tiger was asleep on the floor and Drew was explaining to Lucan for the fourth time why the Committee was funny. He’d turned to her on the bench and said, “Will you take her up to the yard in the morning? To Severin.”

“He asked for you both.”

“He asked for her,” said the water-carrier. “Bring the girl. It was in the letter. I’m just the one who was meant to bring her.” He turned the cup in his hands. “If I go, he’ll watch me. He’ll want to talk about the war, and the sword, and Halvard. He’ll stand there with his arms folded and look at me and she’ll do her forms for a man who isn’t watching her.” He paused. “She deserves a king who’s watching her.”

Maren had looked at him for a moment.

“Very well,” she’d said.

“Thank you, Maren.”

She had written it on the list. 1. Take the girl to the king’s yard. And under it, very small, because she was honest in her lists if nowhere else: He is frightened that if he goes he will be proud of her in front of someone, and won’t know where to put it.

She’d crossed that out. Then she’d left it crossed out, where she could still read it.

Alea was waiting at the door of the Broken Lance at the end of the first hour, with Astra on her back and her hair tied up and her tail held very carefully still.

She’d been at the dawn class. Maren could see it on her: the sand on her boots, the flush still in her face, the particular brightness of a girl who has just been told earlier, not faster by her father and done it. She looked, Maren thought, very much as she had looked at sixteen at the door of the militia hall in Wrenhaven, the first time she’d come to stand in front of five summoned girls and pretend she didn’t know who they were. Older. Taller. A great deal stronger. The same ears.

“Ready?” said Maren.

“No,” said Alea.

“Good,” said Maren. “Your father says that’s correct.”

Alea looked at her, startled. Then she laughed, short and surprised, and some of the stillness went out of her tail.

They walked up the hill.

It was already hot. Midsummer eve, the longest day but one, and the sun had come up over the plain like a lid being taken off a pot. The streets were full. Valcrest at midsummer was a city that had doubled overnight: carts on every road in, tents in every square, stalls in every gap between two houses. Every second child in the street had a paper horn on, or a paper squid, or a little white mask. There were paper fishing rods in every window going up the hill, ten thousand of them, swaying in the hot wind. People had started asking what they were for again. Nobody knew. Lucan wasn’t saying.

Alea looked at the rods all the way up.

“They don’t know,” she said, at one point. “Do they. Nobody in the whole city knows who they’re for.”

“Lucan doesn’t know.”

“Lucan ordered them.”

“Lucan ordered them because a girl with a ragebait voice told him to, and he thought it was a beautiful idea, and he has never once asked himself what it meant.” Maren stepped round a stall selling paper penguins. “He’s a showman. Showmen don’t ask what a thing means. They ask whether people will hold it up.”

Alea thought about that.

“Will they?”

“Yes,” said Maren. “When they know.”

The king’s yard was behind the house on the hill, a long walled court of beaten earth with a well at one end and a row of practice posts at the other and an old fig tree in the corner that had been there, Maren suspected, longer than the house. Somebody had been watering it. It was the only green thing in the yard.

The king was in his shirt.

He was sitting on the edge of the well with a cup of water in his hand, a big plain grey man of sixty-five with a smith’s shoulders and a smith’s forearms and soot on one of them still, and he looked up when they came through the gate, and stood. Not quickly. He stood the way a mountain would stand, if a mountain could, with the whole weight of the ninth tier coming up with him. Maren felt it, the way you feel a change in the air before a storm. She’d felt it once before, in the autumn, on a lake road, when he came down it in a plain coat with a roll of hammers on his back and the hens went mad.

Alea stopped in the gateway.

“Majesty,” said Maren, and bowed.

“Maren,” said the king. He looked past her. “Girl.”

“Majesty,” said Alea.

“You can stop that,” said the king. “You worked my bellows for five days in the autumn. You called me a hen-thief.” He put the cup down on the edge of the well. “Where’s your father?”

“At the east gate,” said Alea. “Waiting for Group 3. Since the dawn class. He says he wants to be standing there when they come over the hill this time, instead of them coming over the hill at him.” She paused. “And he said you’d watch him instead of me.”

The king looked at her for a long moment.

“He’s right,” he said. “I would have.” He nodded at the open middle of the yard. “Go on, then. Show me.”

Maren had seen her do it a hundred times.

On the shingle at the lake, at dawn, with her father watching from the jetty. On the ice in the winter, the whole town on the shore. On a wet verge on the road south, against Petra, moving first. She had a page for it in her lists, a page that went back two years: Alea, forms, with dates, and percentages, and notes in Maren’s small hand. Weight on heels. Better. Better. Let it fall: the second swing holds, the third sings.

She had never seen her do it for a king.

Alea stood in the middle of the yard and drew Astra off her back.

It was still a buster sword. That was the thing nobody who hadn’t seen it could ever quite believe. Half of a slab of black iron that had taken six soldiers and a winch to move, drawn down narrower by a king at a lakeside forge and given a new axe-like point and a grip a hand’s width shorter, and it was still the biggest sword Maren had ever seen anyone under twenty hold. It came off Alea’s back and into her two hands and she stood there with it, a seventeen-year-old fox girl in the morning sun, and it didn’t look too big for her any more.

It had looked too big for her in the autumn. You’ll be cursing it for a year, the king had said.

It was nine months.

She went through the forms.

Maren didn’t watch the sword. She’d learned that, on the shingle; you didn’t watch the blade, you watched the feet, and then you watched the face. Alea’s feet were where they should be. Not on her heels. Never on her heels any more. And her face had gone still the way it went still when she stopped thinking about being watched, the way her father’s went still on the end of the jetty when the line went taut.

The first form went round like a cast line. That was the only way Maren had ever been able to put it in her lists. Not a swing. A cast. The sword going out and round and back on a line you couldn’t see, heavy and easy at once, and coming to rest exactly where it had started, as if it had never left.

The second held.

The third sang.

Maren had heard it sing on the lake, at the quench, the day the king named it. A clean high note, like a wet finger on the rim of a glass, ringing out across the water. She heard it again now, in a walled yard in the king’s city, faint and true, and saw the king hear it.

He didn’t move. He stood by the well with his arms folded and watched the whole thing, every form, the way he must once have watched a blade come out of a quench to see if it would crack. When Alea finished and came back to stillness in the middle of the yard with Astra’s point resting on the earth and her breath coming hard, he didn’t say anything at all for a while.

Then he came across the yard.

“Give me your hands,” said the king.

He’d done it in the autumn too. Maren had been there, at the lake, by the new forge. He’d taken the girl’s hands in his and turned them over and looked at them for a long time, the way he’d look at a piece of iron to see what it wanted to be, and then he’d drawn the sword to fit them.

He did it again now. He took Alea’s hands off the grip, one and then the other, and turned them palm up in his own enormous scarred ones, and looked at them. Then he took the sword out of her hands, very carefully, the way you’d take something from a child that might hurt you if you took it wrong, and looked at the grip.

Gabe’s grip. New slabs of wood, cut to the shape of her closed hand from the way she held a cup. A rough grey underlay of sharkskin for cold hands. And over it all, wrapped tighter, more turns, the old leather from a saddle a nineteen-year-old swordsman had cut up once in a country past the border, finished with a fisherman’s knot.

The king ran his thumb over the knot.

“Who did this?”

“Gabe,” said Alea. “Dad’s—Gabe. In the spring. At the lake.”

“The big one. From Kingsferry.”

“Yes.”

The king turned the sword in his hands. He looked at the grip for a long time. Then he handed it back to her, and took her hands again, and closed them round it himself, and stood back.

“Better than I did,” he said.

Alea’s ears went straight up.

“I drew it to fit your hands as they were in the autumn,” said the king. “That boy drew it to fit how you hold a cup.” He shook his head slowly, and Maren saw, under the grey beard, something that on a man less like a mountain she would have called delight. “Your father’s always had good people round him. He just never let them stand close enough to see.” He looked at Alea’s hands round the grip. “Who’s first? On the morning?”

“A boy from the capital. With a private master. Lucan says his master was a champion once.”

“Hm,” said the king. “His master’s good.” He thought about it. “The boy isn’t. He stands on his heels.” He looked at her. “You used to stand on your heels.”

“I know.”

“He taught you better than he stood,” said the king. “I said that in the autumn. It’s still true.” He was quiet for a moment. “You’ve left a box blank. On Lucan’s roll. I saw it at breakfast.”

Alea went very still.

Maren had made that guild mark herself, in the spring, by the fire at the lake. Alea, of Wrenhaven, copper, aura, Tempered; sponsor Maren. And then the box for the father’s name, and the girl looking at it for a long time, and saying I want to win something that’s mine first, and her father nodding across the fire. Let her.

“I’ll fill it in,” said Alea. “At the end. If I win.”

“And if you don’t?”

“Then it’ll stay blank,” said Alea, “and I’ll fill it in next year.”

The king looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed. Not much. One short, deep sound, like a hammer landing once on an anvil to see if the anvil was sound.

“Good,” he said. And then, quite gently: “Your mother stitched my arm once. At Cinderhold. She told me to hold still or she’d stitch my mouth shut too.” He looked at the girl’s face. “You’ve got her eyes. I said that in the autumn as well. You’re deciding right now whether I’m worth the trouble.”

“I decided in the autumn,” said Alea.

“And?”

“You stole Dad’s hens,” said Alea. “They love you. It’s very annoying.” She paused. “You’re worth it.”

Maren had not seen the Advisor come into the yard.

That was not like her either. She noticed doors. But she’d been watching the girl, and the king, and the sword, and somewhere in the middle of the third form, when it sang, a thin grey man had come out of the house by the side door and stood under the fig tree in the corner, in the shade, very still, watching. And she hadn’t seen him.

She saw him now. The king had turned his head toward the fig tree, and so she turned hers.

He looked older. That was the first thing. Two years. She’d known his hand on paper every second day for two years and had not thought about his face, and his face was older than she remembered: thinner, and greyer, and with something new round the eyes that she couldn’t name and didn’t try to. He was standing under the fig tree with his hands behind his back. He was looking at her.

“Maren,” said the Advisor.

“My lord.”

The king looked from one of them to the other. Then he put his hand on Alea’s shoulder, and turned her round toward the practice posts at the far end of the yard, and said, “Show me how you cut a post. That one. The thick one. Don’t hold back; it’s mine and I’ve got more.” And walked her away down the yard, talking, without looking back.

Maren went to the fig tree.

She stood in front of him in the shade. She had been one of his seven. She had stood in front of him in a tower in Solmere for a year, before any of this, writing down every word, while he taught her to say a short flat strange word in her head and opened a door in front of her face that had never been there before. She had stood in front of him on the morning of the summoning, in the anteroom outside the hall, while thirty-five people who did not yet exist in this world were about to be pulled into it, and he had given her a description of a man by a lake and a single order. The fisherman by the lake is not to be disturbed.

She had not disturbed him. She had kept the order for two years. She had kept it through a road being blocked, and a fight in a market, and fish in a basket every day, and a supper in the snow, and a song on a jetty, and a king at a forge. She had kept it until a girl came into the militia hall one evening with four fish and said Dad says come for supper, and the order had stopped meaning anything, and she’d kept it anyway, because it was hers to keep.

“You got my reports,” said Maren.

“Every one.”

“The last line.”

“Every one,” said the Advisor. His voice was quiet. It had always been quiet. “He fishes. He reads. The hens hate him. He laughed today; I do not know at what.” He paused. “He carries the water.”

“It was on the list,” said Maren.

“I know.”

“He asked for something to carry. On the first morning. I had a blank line.” She heard her own voice go very slightly stiff, the way it went when she was saying a thing she had not planned to say. “I wrote water. He carried it for sixteen days. He wouldn’t let anyone else take it. Not once. The tall one tried. The tiger boy tried. The big one from Kingsferry tried and he gave him a look.” She stopped. “The red-haired one from the capital took it off him at the north gate and carried it into the city and he let him. I have never seen him let anyone take anything off him in two years.”

The Advisor didn’t say anything.

“I thought you should know that,” said Maren. “It isn’t in a report.”

They stood under the fig tree. Down at the far end of the yard there was a great dull thock and a cracking sound and the king saying again, lower, and another thock, and the king laughing.

“You wrote I think he is happy,” said the Advisor.

“Yes.”

“I’ve read it a great many times.”

“I know,” said Maren. “I wrote it so you would.” She looked out at the yard, at the girl with the enormous sword and the king beside her. “I did not write it to be kind, my lord. I’d like you to know that. I don’t write anything to be kind. It was the truest thing I’d written in two years, and I thought somebody ought to have it.”

The Advisor was quiet for a long time.

“In the first month,” he said eventually, “you wrote I believe she is his. About the girl. In the margin. You had no proof.”

“I had her ears,” said Maren. “And how she stood. And how he looked at the five of them on the road. I had no proof.” She paused. “You wrote back let her.”

“I did.”

“It was the only order you ever gave me that I didn’t understand,” said Maren. “I understood all the others. Watch them. Train them. Report. Don’t disturb the fisherman. I understood every one.” She looked at him. “I didn’t understand let her. I thought you were being careless. I thought you’d misread the margin.” She stopped. “I understood it in the winter. At a supper. When she brought the fish.”

“It was the best order I ever gave,” said the Advisor. Very quietly. “It might be the only good one.”

Maren didn’t answer that. It wasn’t hers to answer.

“How is he?” said the Advisor.

She looked at him. A thin grey man under a fig tree, who’d sat across a table from the answer to that question two nights ago in the dark, and still wanted to hear it said in a voice he’d been reading for two years.

“You’ve seen him, my lord.”

“I’d like to hear you say it.”

Maren took a breath. She had a report for this. She had a hundred reports for this. She had two years of last lines. She found, standing in the shade of a fig tree in the king’s yard in Valcrest on midsummer eve, that she didn’t want any of them.

“He is well,” she said. “He is happy. He gets up before it’s light and teaches seven people to stand still on wet sand. He lets the tiger boy hug him. He eats whatever Lucan puts in front of him and says it is good. He has not written one word in their channel in two years, and he reads all of it, every night, and laughs.” She paused. “He’s at the east gate. He’s been there since the end of the dawn class. He wants to be standing there when they come over the hill.”

The Advisor put his hand over his eyes.

Maren looked away from him, out at the yard, so that he would not have to be looked at. That was a thing she had learned from the water-carrier, too. Down at the far end, the thick post the king had chosen was in two pieces on the ground, and the girl was standing over it with Astra resting on her shoulder looking very surprised, and the king was laughing so hard he’d had to sit down on the well.

The king went down to the summoned at the third hour.

Maren had been right about the time. She was usually right about the time. He’d said noon, at breakfast, to his steward, who’d written it down; and at the third hour, with the girl sent back down the hill with two pieces of post under one arm to show her father, he put on a plain coat over his shirt and walked out of the front door of the king’s house without a single guard and down the hill toward the Broken Lance. Kestra went three paces behind him, at exactly the speed that kept her three paces behind. Maren went with them because it was on her list.

People in the street didn’t know him. That was the strange thing. They knew the king; there were coins with his face on, and a statue in the square at Solmere. But they didn’t know the big grey man in a plain coat walking down the hill with soot on his arm, and they stepped round him the way they’d step round anyone, and a boy selling paper penguins tried to sell him one.

He bought it.

He was holding it when he walked into the common room of the Broken Lance.

The room was full. It always was now. Thirty of the summoned at the long table and on the benches and the floor, and their guides and guards round the edges, and Lucan, and the landlord, and half the street looking in through the open windows. It was very loud. Calvin was arguing with Dennis about the Kettle. Tiger was explaining the Gate to Gabe, who wasn’t listening. Bee was drawing on the hearthstone. Winter was sitting very still next to Ina, who had his mask on because of the windows.

The king stood in the doorway with a paper penguin in his hand.

The room didn’t notice him at first. Then Lucan did. Maren watched it happen: Lucan in the kitchen doorway, with a cheese, turning round; seeing the man in the door; going white, then red, then white again; and dropping the cheese. It hit the floor and rolled. It rolled the whole length of the common room, under the long table, between a great many feet, and came to rest against the king’s boot.

The room went silent.

Half of them had never seen him. Groups 5 and 7 hadn’t. Groups 4 and 6 hadn’t. They’d heard about him; they’d read about him in the channel, the king came to the lake and the hens adore him; they knew Hana had stood in front of him in the Council once and he’d said you may listen. They had never once been in a room with him.

They felt him now. Maren saw them feel it. Thirty people at a long table going still, one after another, the way grass goes still when a big shadow passes over it. The ninth tier, coming in at a door.

The king bent down and picked up the cheese.

“Lucan,” he said. “You dropped this.”

Lucan made a sound.

“There’s a rule,” said the king. He put the cheese on the end of the long table. He didn’t come any further into the room. He stood just inside the door, holding the paper penguin, and looked round at them all. “It’s older than me. Older than my father. Only the strong speak to the king. You’ll have heard it.” He looked at Milli, at the top of the table, who had gone very still. “One of you has heard it a great deal.”

Milli didn’t say anything.

“I hate that rule,” said the king. “I’ve always hated it. It means I never get to talk to anyone interesting.” He looked down at the penguin in his hand. “So I thought I’d get round it. You’re not strong enough to speak to the king yet. Most of you. So I’ll come to you.” He pulled out the end of the nearest bench, and sat down on it, and put the penguin on the table. “I’m not the king in here. I’m a man who bought a penguin. Is there any of that pie left? I’m told it’s good.”

There was pie left. Lucan, trembling, made sure of it.

The king stayed two hours. Maren stood by the wall with her list and watched, and did not write anything down, because some things were not for lists.

He went round them. Not the way the water-carrier had gone round them at the gate, one thing each. The king did it the way a smith goes round a forge at the start of the day: picking things up, looking at them, putting them down. He asked Chris what he was writing, and Chris showed him the notebook, and the king read the thirty-sixth line and was quiet for a while. He asked Hyvred what she’d seen at the pass, and listened to all of it, and asked about the ruts. He asked Bee what the dog on the hearth was, and Bee said it’s a wolf, and the king said it’s a dog and Bee looked at him with something like respect.

He stopped in front of Winter and Ina.

Ina had the mask on. Winter was sitting beside him with her long white hair and her two black horns and her face doing nothing. The windows were full of faces.

The king looked at them both for a long moment. Then he turned his head and looked at Ysolde, by the kitchen door, with her curled ram’s horns.

“Ysolde,” he said.

“Majesty.”

“They spat at you,” said the king. “In the war. In this city. Because of your horns.” He was quiet. “I was on the Redmarch. I heard about it afterwards, in a report, and I put the report down and didn’t do anything. I had a war. I didn’t think it was mine to do anything about.” He looked back at Winter and Ina. “It’s mine now. Anybody spits at either of you in this city, they’ll be speaking to the king. Whether they’re strong enough or not.”

Winter’s face did something very small.

Ina reached up, slowly, and pushed the mask to the side of his head. Underneath he was smiling: his own fanged grin, much less frightening than the oni’s. “Thank you, Majesty,” he said, in his warm British voice. “That’s very kind. They mostly hold up horns now, though.”

“I heard,” said the king. “The boy with the helmet. Lucan told me.” He nodded. “Good.”

Milli asked near the end.

Maren had been waiting for it. She’d watched him the whole two hours, at the top of the table, beside his wife, with his tail very still and his hands on his knees, being rational. Every time the king came near him she’d seen him draw a breath. Every time, he’d let it out again.

At the end, when the king stood up and picked up his penguin, Milli stood up too.

“Majesty,” said Milli.

The room went quiet again.

The king looked at him. At the cat-eared, impatient, rational man who had stood at the briefing two years ago in the hall in Solmere and asked for proof that the way home existed; who had wanted, the whole time since, to hear it from the king himself; who had not been strong enough. Maren had read every word of Kestra’s reports on him. Learning to wait. Better. Again.

“I’m not strong enough,” said Milli. His voice was very calm. “I know. I’m Initiate. I’m still at nothing. I’ve got no right to ask you anything.” He swallowed. “I want to ask you about the way home.”

The king looked at him for a long time.

“Not here,” he said.

Milli’s face did something.

“Not here,” said the king again, gently. “Not over a pie in an inn, with half of Valcrest at the windows. It’s too big for here. And you’ll want all of it, and I’ll want to give you all of it, and some of it’s mine and some of it’s my father’s and some of it’s behind a door I’ve never opened.” He put his hand on the back of the bench. “After the Lists. At my table. All of you. I’ll feed you properly. And then you ask me, and I’ll answer you as well as I can.” He paused. “That’s a promise. You can hold me to it.”

Milli stood very still. Then he nodded. Once.

“Okay,” he said.

“Good,” said the king. “And Milli.”

“Majesty?”

“Learning to wait,” said the king. “Kestra wrote that about you in the first month. Every month since. I read all of hers twice.” He picked up the penguin. “Keep learning. You’re closer than you think.”

He went out. Kestra went three paces behind him. And Maren, who was watching Milli, saw him sit down very slowly on the bench beside his wife, and put his face in his hands, and Hana put her hand on the back of his neck, and the room started talking again all at once, very loudly, the way rooms do.

The king didn’t go back up the hill.

He went down a side street instead, toward the Street of Armourers, where the smiths were, and Maren followed him because it was not on her list and she wanted to know where it went. Halfway down the street he stopped, and turned round, and looked back at the Lance, and said, without raising his voice:

“The big one. From Kingsferry. Bring him. And whatever he forged.”

Maren went back for Gabe.

The forge was a small one at the bottom of the Street of Armourers, run by a woman called Hesk who made horseshoes and nails and, once a year at midsummer, repaired the armour of men who’d been hit by lances. She took one look at the man in the plain coat and went out of her own forge and sat in the street on an upturned bucket and did not come back in.

Gabe came down the street with the blade wrapped in a piece of sacking under his arm and his face set, the way it set when he was frightened and had decided to be a wall about it.

The king was standing at Hesk’s anvil with his coat off, looking at her hammers.

“Show me,” he said.

Gabe unwrapped it.

Maren had seen it once before, at the lake, in the spring. The first blade Gabe had ever finished: short, heavy, single-edged, broad at the end, a thing the tall one had called a butcher’s cleaver married a gate. Gabe had made it at the Kingsferry forges, under a smith with no eyebrows, through the winter. He’d brought it upriver to the lake and asked the man he’d come upriver to find to name it.

The king took it in his hands.

He did what he’d done with the black sword in the autumn. Maren had been there for that, too. He didn’t look at it, at first. He closed his eyes and ran his fingers along it, very slowly, from the tang to the point, the way a man reads a thing in the dark. The weight. The edge. The spine. The place where the fuller ran out. He stopped, here and there, and went back, and went on.

“You quenched it in oil,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Cold oil. Too cold. Your first one cracked.”

Gabe stared at him. “How do you—“

“There’s a place by the tang where you went back and drew it again because you were frightened it’d crack too.” The king’s fingers had stopped there. “You didn’t need to. It was sound. But you were right to be frightened. Most first blades crack.” He opened his eyes. “Most smiths’ first blades are rubbish. This isn’t rubbish. This is a wall.”

Gabe didn’t say anything. He’d gone very red.

Then the king turned the blade over and found the letters.

They were cut into the flat near the hilt, small and plain, by somebody’s knife-point, gone over twice where the steel was hard. The king knew that hand. Maren saw him know it. He’d seen it on the black sword in the autumn, crooked and old, AD ASTRA PER ASPERA, and on the headstone he’d helped cut, TO DIWA, MY LOVE. He looked at these letters for a long time.

STO.

And under it, smaller, round the curve of the flat: AUDENTES FORTUNA JUVAT.

“He cut these,” said the king.

“He named it,” said Gabe. “I asked him to. Sto. It means I stand.” He swallowed. “The other bit’s a thing he used to say. Back home. Fortune favours the bold.”

The king read it again. He couldn’t read it, Maren knew. Nobody in this world could read the letters, except thirty-five people and the man who’d cut them. But he ran his thumb over them the way he’d run his thumb over the fisherman’s knot on Alea’s grip, and Maren saw something go across his face, quick, like a fish under water.

“My father would have liked that,” said the king.

He handed the blade back to Gabe.

“He was a smith,” he said. “In Brakewater, on the river, in the south. Before any of this. He had a hammer he’d had from his own master. A small cross-peen, with a burn on the haft where he’d left it in the coals once as a boy, and a nick in the face the shape of a fingernail that left a little crescent in everything it struck. He wouldn’t use any other. I learned on it. I carried it fifty years.” He looked at Hesk’s hammers on the wall. “I gave it away in the autumn. To the smith in Wrenhaven. Garrow. He worked my bellows for three days at the lake and never once asked me for anything, and I thought: a good hammer ought to be working, not riding about in a king’s saddle roll. My father used to say that.” He shrugged. “It went east with Garrow and the headstone, to Cinderhold. I don’t know where it is now. Working, I hope.”

Gabe was holding Sto against his chest.

“You’ll feel the metal,” said the king. “At Honed. Your guide’s book says you will; I read that too. You’ll put your hand on a blade and feel what the aura’s doing in it, the way a smith feels heat.” He looked at Gabe. “When you can, come and strike for me. I’ve had one good striker in my life, and he’s busy fishing.”

Gabe opened his mouth. Nothing came out.

“That’s a yes,” said Maren, from the door.

“I know,” said the king.

Maren went back up the hill on her own at the end of the afternoon, with the sun going down over the plain and the city starting to light its lamps, and the paper rods in every window swaying in the hot wind.

She had the list in her pocket. She took it out at the top of the hill, outside the king’s house, and looked at it.

1. Take the girl to the king’s yard. She had. The girl had cut a post in half and the king had laughed until he sat down.

2. Advisor. She had. She did not cross it out. It did not feel like a thing you crossed out.

3. The king to the Lance. He had gone. He had bought a penguin. He had made a promise to a cat. He had said a thing to two people with horns that Maren intended to remember for the rest of her life, and had not written down.

4. Group 3, east gate, sunset.

She looked east, down the long slope of the city, past the Lists, to where the east road came in over a low hill through the market gardens. It was going gold. The whole east side of the city was going gold in the late sun.

She could see the gate from here. Small and far. And on the road outside it, a little way out, just past the last of the market gardens, where you’d stand if you wanted to be the first thing somebody saw when they came over the hill, there was a man. On his own. With a rod over his shoulder. Standing very still.

He’d been there all day. Since the end of the dawn class. Alea had taken him two pieces of post at the third hour and he’d looked at them and said good and gone on standing there. Petra had taken him bread at noon. Tiger had gone out and stood with him for an hour and then been sent back. He was still there.

Maren went into the king’s house, and up the stair, and along the passage to the small room on the first floor that faced north, and knocked. Two knuckles, twice.

“Come,” said the Advisor.

He was at the warped oak table. The lamp was lit. He had a page in front of him, and a pen, and he wasn’t writing anything with it.

Maren took a piece of paper out of her coat. She had written it on the way up the hill, against a wall, out of habit. Two years of habit. A report. She’d thought, writing it, that it was absurd: he was in the same city; he was four streets away from the man in it; he could walk to a window.

She’d written it anyway. It was what she did.

She put it on the table in front of him, folded small, the way she’d folded every report for two years. Then she stepped back.

The Advisor unfolded it.

It was one line. In her small exact hand, the last line, the one that answered the three words at the bottom of every letter he’d ever sent her.

He is at the east gate. He has been there since the second hour.

The Advisor read it. Then he folded it again, very carefully, along her folds, and put it in his coat, beside another one.

“Thank you, Maren,” he said.

“My lord,” said Maren.

She went back down the stair and out of the king’s house and down the hill toward the east gate, in the gold light, with the list in her pocket and one line on it not crossed out, because it was on the list, and because she wanted to be standing there too.
