The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 5: The Lists

Chapter 143

Ink and Code

5,136 words · 22 min read

Thea had always read things the way other people listened to music.

Not words. Words were easy. Words were just the surface. She meant the thing underneath: the way a sentence was put together, the way a program was put together, the way a spell was. Structure. On Earth she’d been the kind of person who took things apart to see how they worked, and the girl at two in the morning on a call with a boy in Malaysia, singing to him while he played, because he said it helped him think and she said it helped her not to.

Here, it was spells.

She’d seen it the first week, in the militia hall at Wrenhaven. Maren had put a candle on the table and said watch, and lit it with a word, and Thea had watched, and seen. Not the flame. The thing under the flame. A shape. Lines and loops and nested rings of something, very fine, like the inside of a clock, like a page of code with every bracket in the right place, hanging in the air round the wick for the moment the word took. She’d said oh, out loud, and Maren had looked at her strangely, and that had been the start.

Two years. She read magic like code. She could take a spell apart in the air and see where it would break. She could take one apart and put it back together better. Her window called it Parse. Then, at the second tier, at midwinter, a year to the night after her first: Patch.

She’d been waiting two years to read one thing.

“It’s very big,” said Oswin.

“How big?”

“Very,” said Oswin, and unrolled it.

They were in the archive room at the king’s house on the hill, the morning after the king’s supper, the sixty-seventh day. A long narrow room at the back of the house, with shelves to the ceiling full of the crown’s records of the Lists, three hundred years of rolls and brackets and champions’ names, and a single long table down the middle under a skylight. Oswin had been living in it for ten days. There were crumbs on the table. There was a blanket on the floor under it where he’d clearly been sleeping.

He unrolled the sheet along the table, and it kept going. Off the end of the table. Onto the floor. He knelt and unrolled the rest of it across the floorboards, and then stood up, and stepped back, and looked at Thea with his calm scholar’s face doing something that wasn’t calm at all.

“There,” said Oswin.

Thea looked.

It was a circle.

Of course it was. She’d known it would be. She’d stood in the middle of the real one, two years ago, in the hall in Solmere, and hadn’t been able to see anything at all, because she’d just been brought through it and she was screaming. Oswin had copied the real one. Over months. Last autumn, after Halvard gave him the black key to the sealed vault, he’d gone down into the summoning hall every night after the castle was asleep with a lantern and a roll of linen paper and a box of charcoal and drawn the circle on the floor, a section at a time, on his hands and knees. And then he’d taken the drawing up to the vault and checked it, line by line, against the oldest records of the circle in the archive, the ones from before the kingdom, and corrected it. And then inked it. And then done it again, because the first one had a mistake in the fourteenth ring.

It was ten feet across. It was the most beautiful thing Thea had ever seen.

“I can’t read it,” said Oswin. “I want you to know that. I’ve been looking at it for most of a year. I know every line. I could draw it from memory with my eyes shut.” He knelt at the edge of it, on the floorboards, where it ran off the table. “I don’t know what any of it does.”

“Nobody does,” said Cursiffe.

He’d been sitting in the corner of the archive room since they came in, on a stool, with his book on his knee and the little vial of dark blue ink at his throat on its chain, very calm, very slow. He got up now and came and stood beside Thea at the table.

“Halvard knows some of it,” said Oswin. “He’s the one who used it. He says he learned it from the records and from the court mages before him, the way you learn a recipe. Do this, then this, then this, and it works.” He looked at the circle. “He doesn’t know why it works. Nobody’s known why for four hundred years. Maybe longer.”

Thea didn’t say anything.

She was already looking.

It didn’t look like code at first.

It looked like a circle. Rings inside rings inside rings, fourteen of them, drawn in Oswin’s careful black ink, and between the rings, and across them, and through them, lines of this world’s script, very small, very old, in forms of the letters nobody had used in centuries. Spokes going out from the middle. Knots where the spokes met the rings. A space in the very centre, empty, the size of a man lying down.

That was where the sword had been.

She knew that. Everybody knew it now. Medium: one blade, her window had said, under everything, since the first day, in a line nobody had understood. And then, in the autumn, after a king and a fisherman cut it in two on a lake shore: Medium: one blade · parted. She checked it every morning before she got up. She’d checked it this morning.

She looked at the empty space at the centre of the circle where the sword had lain, and Parsed.

And it came apart.

It always surprised her, the moment it happened. Two years and it still surprised her. One moment she was looking at something, a candle, a ward, a spell, a circle of ink on linen paper, and it was just the thing. And then she let her sight go the way she’d learned to let it go, down, under the surface, the way you’d let your eyes unfocus on one of those pictures that turned into another picture if you stared at it. And the thing came apart into its structure.

The circle came apart.

Not on the paper. In her sight. The rings lifted off the page, very faint, like smoke, and hung in the air above the table in layers, fourteen of them, one above another, and she could see what each one was for. Not the words. The shape of the words. The way they called each other. The way the third ring reached down into the second and pulled something up out of it, and the seventh ring reached across to the eleventh and handed something over, and the whole thing turned, very slowly, like the inside of a clock.

“Oh,” said Thea.

“What,” said Oswin. “What, what, what—“

“Shh.”

She walked round the table. Slowly. With her eyes on the air above it. She didn’t need to look at the paper any more. She was looking at the thing the paper was a drawing of. The thing that had brought her here.

It was a program.

Of course it was. She’d known it would be, the way she’d known the windows would be, the year before, when she looked under her own window and found it was written, all the way down, in English, in a child’s words. Everything in this world that worked, worked because somebody had written it. Spells were written. Windows were written. And this was the biggest thing she’d ever seen written. Fourteen nested layers, calling each other, handing things back and forth, turning.

She could see what it did.

Not all of it. She could see the shape of it. Like reading a page of code in a language you only half know: you can’t follow every line, but you can see where the loops are, and what calls what, and where the thing starts and where it ends.

“Talk,” said Cursiffe. Quietly. “Tell us what you see. I’ll write it down.”

Thea looked round at him.

He had a sheet of something in his hands. Not linen paper. Thinner. Oiled, so you could see through it. He laid it carefully over the near edge of Oswin’s circle, where the outermost ring ran along the table, and smoothed it flat, and unstoppered the vial at his throat, and wet the tip of a pen in the dark blue ink.

“I’ll write it on top,” said Cursiffe. “In English. So it doesn’t touch his.” He nodded at Oswin’s black ink, under the oiled sheet. “Nobody here can read mine, anyway. It’s safe.” He looked at her. “You read. I comment.”

Thea stared at him.

“Comments,” she said. “You’re going to write comments.”

“Is that what they’re called?”

“That’s exactly what they’re called.” She laughed. She couldn’t help it. “In code. When you write something nobody’s going to understand, you write little notes beside it in plain language. So whoever reads it next knows what it does.” She looked at the oiled sheet, at Cursiffe’s pen waiting over it. “Nobody ever does. Everyone’s code is a mess. Nobody ever comments.”

“I’ll comment,” said Cursiffe, calmly. “Go on.”

So she read.

She walked round the table and the floor and the ten feet of circle with her eyes on the layers in the air, and talked, the way you talk when you’re reading code out loud to someone at two in the morning. And Cursiffe followed her round with the oiled sheets and the pen, laying them down over each part of the circle as she reached it, and wrote, in small slow English capitals in dark blue ink that no native in this world could read or counter or even look at for very long, what she said.

OUTER RING: THE CALL. REACHES OUT. A LONG WAY. FURTHER THAN THIS WORLD.

SECOND RING: WHAT IT’S LOOKING FOR. TAKES ITS SHAPE FROM WHATEVER’S IN THE MIDDLE.

CENTRE: THE ANCHOR. THE THING YOU PUT IN THE MIDDLE. IT HAS TO BE STRONG. IT HAS TO BE TANGLED IN SOMETHING.

“Tangled,” said Oswin, very quietly, from the floor. “A summoning is a net. It catches what is tangled in it. That’s in the oldest record. I quoted it to the Advisor. In the first month. He didn’t reply.”

“That’s this,” said Thea. She pointed at the air above the second ring, where a fine web of lines went out from the centre in every direction, like threads. “That’s the net. It goes out from whatever’s in the middle, along everything the middle’s tangled in. And it pulls.”

THIRD RING: THE NET. FOLLOWS THE ANCHOR’S THREADS. PULLS WHAT’S ON THE END OF THEM.

“The sword was tangled in him,” said Cursiffe slowly, writing.

“And he was tangled in us,” said Thea. “All of us. Every one. Somebody who’d brought somebody to somebody.” She looked at the empty space in the centre. “That’s why it was thirty-five. Not three. Every summoning before was three, at most. Because the anchor was just a sword, or a crown, or a bone. Tangled in a few people. A family. A knight.” She swallowed. “This one was tangled in a man who’d spent his whole life on Earth talking to people every night. In a box of light. Across the whole world.”

Nobody said anything for a while.

Cursiffe wrote it down.

THIS ONE CAUGHT 35 BECAUSE THE ANCHOR WAS TANGLED IN SOMEONE WHO WAS TANGLED IN EVERYONE.

She went round. Ring by ring. It took all morning.

The fourth ring was the bringing: the part that took what the net had caught and carried it through, from wherever it was to here. The fifth was the setting-down. The sixth was the body: it looked at what had been caught and decided what shape to set it down in, and Thea stood over the sixth ring for a long time, Parsing, because it was the part that had looked at her and decided she’d come through as herself, and looked at Hana and decided she’d come through as a cat princess, and looked at Hyvred and decided her eyes would work.

“It read our models,” said Thea. “The VTubers. It read what we were, there. Online. The shape people knew us by. And it made that.” She looked at her own hands. “And it read the rest of us as our real bodies, because that was the only shape anyone knew.”

SIXTH RING: THE BODY. USES WHATEVER SHAPE YOU WERE KNOWN BY.

The seventh and eighth were the binding. That was the part that held you here once you’d been set down: that tied you to the anchor, and to the circle, and to each other. Thea knew it the moment she saw it. It was the part that had written a line into every one of their windows on the first day, the line under everything: Medium.

“It’s still running,” said Thea.

Oswin looked up. “What?”

“The binding. It’s still on.” She pointed. “It’s not a thing that happened once. It’s a thing that’s still happening. Every day. It’s what keeps the medium line in our windows. It’s what made the line say parted when they cut the sword.” She thought about it. “It’s what the channel’s tied to. I found that out in the autumn. The night before they cut the sword, I had to rescope the whole channel live so we wouldn’t all drop out. I thought it was the windows.” She looked at the eighth ring. “It was this. The channel’s scope is the binding’s scope. Everyone the circle’s holding.”

SEVENTH AND EIGHTH: THE BINDING. STILL RUNNING. HOLDS EVERYONE IT BROUGHT. THE CHANNEL LIVES INSIDE IT.

Cursiffe wrote that down very slowly, and then sat back on his heels and looked at it for a long time.

The ninth ring through the thirteenth were the parts she couldn’t read.

Not because they were hidden. Because they were too old. The script in them was older than the script in the outer rings, much older; Oswin said so, and Thea could see it in the shape of the structure, the way you can see old code in a program that’s been patched for years, the original parts written by somebody long ago in a style nobody uses any more, and newer parts wrapped round them, calling them, never touching them.

“Somebody wrote the middle,” said Thea, “and then other people added the outside. Over hundreds of years. Wrapping it. Like—“ She stopped, looking for the word. “Like nobody dared change the original, so they just kept building round it.”

“That’s the kingdom,” said Oswin, softly. “That’s every institution in the kingdom. That’s the Council.”

Cursiffe laughed, slow and relaxed, and wrote: 9–13: LEGACY CODE. DO NOT TOUCH.

Thea laughed too. And then stopped, and stood over the thirteenth ring, and looked at it, and looked past it.

At the fourteenth.

She’d been looking at it all morning without seeing it.

That was the thing she’d think about afterwards, lying awake. It had been there the whole time. The fourteenth ring. The outermost one. The one Cursiffe had laid the very first oiled sheet over, at the near edge of the table, and written THE CALL on. She’d read it first. She’d read it and said reaches out, a long way, further than this world, and moved on.

She’d read half of it.

The fourteenth ring wasn’t one ring. It was two. Two arcs, each half a circle, meeting at the top and the bottom. The left arc was the call: full, dense, written all the way along, every line in place, reaching out, the way she’d said. She’d read the left arc and assumed the right arc was the same.

It wasn’t.

The right arc was the same shape. The same curve. The same width. The same knots where the spokes would meet it. Somebody had drawn it, very carefully, all the way round, so that it matched the left arc exactly, a mirror of it.

And then hadn’t written anything in it.

Thea stood over the right half of the fourteenth ring and Parsed it, as hard as she’d ever Parsed anything, and it didn’t come apart.

There was nothing to come apart. It was a shape. An outline. A ring drawn in ink with the spaces for the writing left blank. Every spoke had a knot waiting for it. Every knot had a space beside it where the line should go. It was all there, the whole structure, the way a form is all there before anyone’s filled it in. It just didn’t do anything.

She knelt down on the floorboards, because the right arc ran off the end of the table and down onto the floor, and looked at it close.

“Oswin,” she said. Her voice had gone strange. “Is this right? This half. Is it really blank? On the real one? In the hall?”

Oswin came round on his knees. He looked at the right arc for a long time.

“Yes,” he said. “I copied it. Four times. I thought it was worn away. I thought the writing had rubbed off, over four hundred years, people walking on it.” He frowned. “But it’s not worn. Look at the lines. The outline’s as crisp as the left side. Somebody drew it like that. On purpose. And left it.”

“What does the record say?”

Oswin was quiet. Then he got up, and went to the shelves at the far end of the archive room, and came back with a very old book, bound in something that wasn’t leather, with pages like dry leaves. He opened it on the table, very carefully, to a page near the beginning, and put his finger on a line.

“The second hand is for the one who can hold it,” he read. Slowly. Translating from a script nobody had used in centuries. “No hand living can hold it. When a hand can, it will be filled.” He looked up. “That’s all. That’s the only line about it. I always thought the second hand meant a second mage. A helper. Someone to hold the circle steady.”

Thea looked at the right arc of the fourteenth ring.

“It’s not a second mage,” she said. “It’s a second half. The second hand of the clock.” She swallowed. “The left half reaches out and pulls. That’s the call. That’s what brought us.” She put her hand flat on the floorboards, beside the blank arc. “This half would push. Back. Along the same threads. The same way we came.” She was very quiet. “It’s the way home.”

Nobody said anything in the archive room for a long time.

The skylight let the noon sun down onto the table in a long bright square that lay across the circle and Cursiffe’s oiled sheets and his small blue English capitals. Out in the city somebody was singing; the ballad girl, probably, in the square, for coppers. And she let it fall, and she let it go.

“Why is it blank?” said Cursiffe. Very slowly.

Thea looked at it. Parsed it again. Not the writing; there wasn’t any. The space. The shape of the space where the writing should be. And she saw it.

“Because nobody could write it,” she said.

She pointed. At the knots on the blank arc, the places where the spokes would meet it. At the size of them. At the spaces beside each knot where the lines would go.

“Every part of a spell has a size,” she said. “How much it needs. To run. I can see it. I’ve always been able to see it; it’s like looking at how much memory a program uses.” She touched the floorboards beside the nearest knot on the left arc, the full one. “This needs a lot. The call. It needs a court mage, Comet-tier, and a whole circle, and an anchor, and a night. Halvard can just about run it. Just.” She moved her hand across to the blank arc. To the matching knot. To the empty space beside it. “This needs more.”

“How much more?”

Thea looked at it for a long time.

“I can’t see the top of it,” she said. “It goes up past anything I can measure. Past the Advisor. Past the king, if the king were a mage.” She took her hand away. “It needs a mage nobody alive has ever been.”

“A Sun,” said Oswin. Very softly.

“A Sun,” said Thea.

Cursiffe wrote it down.

He knelt on the floorboards over the blank right arc of the fourteenth ring, with a fresh oiled sheet, and wet his pen in the dark blue ink at his throat, and wrote, slowly, in small square English capitals, the way he wrote everything:

14 (RIGHT): THE WAY BACK. SAME SHAPE AS THE CALL. PUSHES INSTEAD OF PULLS. ALONG THE SAME THREADS.

He stopped. Then went on.

NOT WRITTEN. NOT WORN AWAY. LEFT BLANK ON PURPOSE BY WHOEVER DREW IT, BECAUSE NOBODY COULD HOLD IT.

NEEDS A SUN.

He looked at it. Then he added one more line, underneath, smaller.

IT’S A FORM WITH A BLANK BOX.

Thea read it over his shoulder and made a sound she hadn’t meant to make.

“Like Alea’s,” she said.

“Like Alea’s,” agreed Cursiffe. He put the stopper back in the vial. “Left blank until someone’s won something that’s theirs.” He looked at the arc. “Whoever drew this knew exactly what they were doing. They drew the whole shape. They knew where every line would go. They just couldn’t fill it in themselves.” He was quiet. “So they left it for somebody who could.”

“Four hundred years,” said Oswin.

“Longer,” said Thea. She was looking at the ninth through thirteenth rings, the legacy code, the old middle that everyone had wrapped round for centuries and nobody had dared to touch. “The blank half’s drawn in the same style as the middle. The oldest part. Not the outside.” She looked up. “Whoever drew the blank half drew the original. Before everyone else. Before the kingdom.”

“Who?”

“I don’t know,” said Thea. “They didn’t comment.”

Cursiffe laughed. Slowly, relaxed, and then not relaxed at all.

The king was right, Thea thought.

She sat on the floorboards with her back against the leg of the table, after, with the ten-foot circle spread round her and Cursiffe’s oiled sheets on top of it, blue English capitals over black native script, and thought about the king at the top table last night with goose fat on his fingers, saying there’s a plough blade in every bar of iron in the world. Nobody’s ever seen it. You can’t see it until you’ve made it. That doesn’t mean it isn’t there.

There was a way home.

Not a song. Not a rumour. Not a line in a record about a second hand. A shape. Drawn. Real. Right there on Oswin’s copy, and on the floor of the hall in Solmere where she’d been brought through, every line of it waiting. Every knot. Every space. A function with its signature written out and its body left empty, for four hundred years, waiting for somebody who could fill it in.

It needed a Sun.

Nobody alive had ever been a Sun.

She opened her window. Out of habit.

Second tier. Two years. The first of the thirty-five to the first tier and the first to the second. The fastest mage in the kingdom, the guides said, apart from nobody, because nobody had ever climbed this fast. She’d been at ninety-nine percent for eleven days once, before the bonfire. She’d climbed a whole tier in a year since.

Eight more tiers.

She closed it.

He came in at the end of the afternoon.

She’d known he would. Not because anybody told him. Because he always knew where everybody was, and because Cursiffe had written in the channel at noon, very briefly, Thea’s found something in the circle, and somebody in the Lance had read it out at the long table, and the man at the end of the long table had read it himself and put down his cup.

He stood in the door of the archive room with the rod on his shoulder and two bees on his head and looked at the circle on the table and the floor, ten feet of it, with Cursiffe’s oiled sheets laid over it like scales.

“Thea,” he said.

“Ice.”

He came in. He didn’t say anything else. He walked round the table, slowly, the way she had, looking down at the circle. At the blue English capitals. He could read them. He was the only person born in this world who could. He read them all, ring by ring, Cursiffe’s slow square comments, THE CALL, THE NET, THE BINDING, STILL RUNNING, LEGACY CODE. DO NOT TOUCH. She saw his shoulders go at that one, very slightly.

He stopped at the right arc of the fourteenth ring.

He read Cursiffe’s comment. All of it. THE WAY BACK. SAME SHAPE AS THE CALL. PUSHES INSTEAD OF PULLS. NOT WRITTEN. LEFT BLANK ON PURPOSE BY WHOEVER DREW IT, BECAUSE NOBODY COULD HOLD IT. NEEDS A SUN. IT’S A FORM WITH A BLANK BOX.

He stood over it for a long time.

“I looked for this,” he said eventually. Very quietly. “For thirty years. In every library I could get into. I asked every mage I met. I asked Halvard, without telling him why.” He didn’t look up from the blank arc. “I wanted to know if anyone had ever been born somewhere else and gone back. If there was a door.”

“There isn’t,” said Thea. “Not for you.” She’d thought about it all afternoon. “This pushes back along the threads. The same way it pulled. You didn’t come through the circle.” She looked at him. “Nobody brought you. You just woke up.”

“I know.”

“So it’s not your door.”

“No,” he said. “It’s yours.” He looked down at the blank arc. “All thirty-five of you. Back along the threads. To wherever you were. Two years ago. In a different country each.” He was quiet. “A year or two later, there, maybe. By the time somebody gets to Sun.”

Thea didn’t say anything.

“Your families will have stopped looking,” he said, very gently. “Some of them. Not all. Yan’s mother won’t have. She’ll have been waiting the whole time.” He turned the rod on his shoulder. “And you’d get to tell them where you’d been. And they wouldn’t believe a word of it.”

“Would you want us to?”

He looked at her then. She couldn’t see his face. She’d never been able to see his face, not once, not since a market road on the first day when he’d stood there with a basket of fish and looked at every one of them in turn and said almost nothing.

“I want you to be able to choose,” he said. “Every one of you. That’s all I’ve ever wanted, since the first day. Since I saw you on the road. That you’d get to choose.” He looked at the blank arc again. “And now you can. If somebody gets there.”

“It’ll be somebody,” said Thea.

“It will.”

“It might be me.”

“It might.”

“It might be Yan. He’s got the strongest reason. Or Ina. Ina’s fire climbs the tiers literally, his names are all fire.” She was talking fast now, the way she talked when she was frightened and didn’t want to say so. “Or somebody nobody’s thinking of. Mashi. Malcolm. Anyone. It doesn’t have to be the fastest. It just has to be the one who gets there.”

“I know,” he said.

“Eight more tiers,” said Thea. “Eight. I’m two. Nobody’s ever done ten. The Advisor’s seven and he’s been a mage for forty-five years.” She stopped. “It could take decades.”

“It could.”

“Our families—“

“Time runs differently,” he said quietly. “Eleven years here for every one there. If it takes you twenty years here, it’s two there. Less.” He looked at her. “That’s the one good thing about this world, Thea. You’ve got time. More than you think.”

Thea sat on the floorboards with her back against the table and looked at him for a long time.

“You worked that out,” she said. “On purpose. Just now. To make me feel better.”

“I worked it out last year,” he said. “From a date under my window. I’ve been carrying it round ever since, waiting for somebody to need it.”

She asked Cursiffe for the ink, in the end.

He gave it to her without asking why. Unstoppered the little vial at his throat and held it out, on its chain, and she wet the tip of his pen in it, the dark blue English ink that no native could read or counter or look at for long, and knelt on the floorboards over the right arc of the fourteenth ring.

Over the blank half. On the oiled sheet, on top of Cursiffe’s comment, in the empty space at the very centre of the arc, where the first line of the way home would go if anybody ever wrote it.

She wrote one word. Very small. In English capitals, the way Chris would have, the way every programmer she’d ever known would have, at two in the morning, at the bottom of a function they couldn’t finish yet but meant to.

TODO.

Oswin, on the other side of the table, leaned over to look.

“What does it mean?” he said.

Thea didn’t answer. She was looking at it.

Cursiffe answered for her. Slowly. Calmly. With his head tipped back a little, the way he held it when he knew something nobody else did.

“It means,” said Cursiffe, “that somebody’s coming back to it.”

On the other side of the table, the man with the rod laughed. Quietly. Once. The laugh of a man who’s been told a good joke at the end of a long day, by somebody who used to sing to him at two in the morning while he played, a lifetime ago, in another world.

“Still here,” said Thea, to the blank arc. Under her breath. The two words she’d written into a bonfire once, and into a channel at three in the morning on a hill above this city, when she couldn’t sleep.

Nobody in the room asked what she meant. Two of them knew.
