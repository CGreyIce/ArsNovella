The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 2: Letters

Chapter 49

The Roof

4,370 words · 19 min read

People brought Kei their grief. He’d never understood why.

It had started in the autumn, in small ways. A soldier on the wall at night, sitting down beside him without a word and then, after a long time, talking about his sister. The old woman in Coldwater with her peas, holding Little Tam’s wooden horse in her lap, telling him things about her grandson she hadn’t told anyone. Corvin, once, very late, in the long room, about two years of being laughed at in the capital. Hyvred, about the summoning hall, and the ceiling, and how it felt to cry because you could finally see.

They’d come and sit near him and say nothing for a while. And then they’d start talking. And Kei would listen.

That was all he did. He didn’t have answers. He didn’t give advice. He just sat there, calm, with Hisui coiled on his wrist, and let them say it.

“It’s your face,” said Hisui, the first time Kei had wondered about it out loud. “You’ve got a face like a warm bath. People see it and want to get in.”

“That’s disgusting.”

“It’s a compliment. I’m a snake. We don’t do compliments. Take it.”

Kei didn’t think it was his face. He thought it was something else, and he knew exactly where he’d learned it.

A bad night, years ago, in another world. A breakup that had gone the worst way a breakup can go. Kei sitting on the floor of his room at two in the morning with his back against the bed, not crying, not doing anything, just sitting there with his phone in his hand. And messaging Ice, of all people. Not his closest friend. Not the obvious choice. Just the name that happened to be green in the sidebar at two in the morning.

you up?

And Ice had called. Straight away. No questions. And then he’d stayed on the call until five.

He hadn’t fixed anything. He hadn’t tried to. He hadn’t said she wasn’t good enough for you or you’ll find someone better or any of the things people say. He’d just stayed. Talked when Kei wanted to talk. Been quiet when Kei didn’t. Played some stupid game in the background for a while, so the silence had a sound to it. And at five, when the sky was getting light and Kei finally felt like he could sleep, Ice had said okay. go to bed. I’m here tomorrow too.

That was all. Three hours of a man staying.

Kei had never forgotten it. He’d never stopped trying to give it back.

Ossie’s father came on a hot afternoon, the day after the cold one died.

The whole of Thornwall was still talking about Yan’s message from the morning. The cold one’s gone. Something killed it. One stroke. The soldiers had read it in the summoned’s faces before Brannoch read it to them out loud, and the mood in the fort had gone strange and light and giddy, the way it does after a storm passes over without breaking. Somebody had started singing in the kitchen. Tiger had run the walls six times before breakfast, shouting.

And then, at three in the afternoon, the gate sergeant sent a boy up to the long room to say there was a man at the outer gate asking for the captain. A big man, on foot, very dusty. With a carpenter’s bag on his back.

Kei saw Tiger’s face when the boy said carpenter.

His name was Aldo Pell. He came from Harrowford, a village in the valley a week’s walk east and south, where the river came down out of the hills and the roads met. He’d been a carpenter there for thirty years, and his father before him. He was a big slow quiet man of about fifty, with enormous hands and grey stubble and a face that looked as if it had been carved out of the same oak he worked with.

He had Ossie’s eyes. That was the worst thing. Kei saw it from across the courtyard. The same eyes, set the same way, in a different face.

Brannoch met him at the inner gate himself, and took him into the gatehouse, and shut the door. They were in there a long time. Long enough for half the Western Watch to find reasons to be in the courtyard, carrying things that didn’t need carrying, not looking at the gatehouse door.

Kei waited by the well with Hisui on his wrist. Tiger was at the north wall, by the shed. He’d been there since the boy came up. He wasn’t doing anything. He was just standing in front of the three crooked walls with his hands hanging at his sides.

Drew was standing beside him. Of course he was. He’d been beside Tiger since the night of the gate, as much as he could be, without ever seeming to make a point of it. Kei had watched them all spring: the big calm one and the restless quick one, nine years of friendship, the kind where you didn’t have to explain anything. Drew didn’t say anything now. He just stood next to Tiger in the hot sun, looking at the shed, the way you’d stand next to someone at a graveside.

“He’s going to ask for Tiger,” said Hisui quietly.

“I know.”

“The soldier wrote home. Ossie. About the races. You can smell it on the father. He’s been carrying a letter for months. In his coat, next to his chest. He’s read it so many times the paper’s soft.”

Kei didn’t ask how Hisui could smell that. Hisui could smell everything.

“He’s going to ask for Tiger,” Hisui said again. “And Tiger’s going to fall apart.”

“I know,” said Kei. “That’s why I’m here.”

The gatehouse door opened. Brannoch came out first, grey-faced. Pell came after him, and stopped in the sunlight, and looked round the courtyard, slowly, at all of it. The walls. The well. The soldiers pretending not to watch.

Then he saw Tiger, by the north wall, and his face changed.

“You’ll be the lad with the ears,” he said. His voice was deep and quiet and rough, the voice of a man who didn’t talk much. “Os wrote about you. Every letter since the spring. The summoned lad with the tiger ears who could run like the wind and couldn’t build a shed to save his life.”

Tiger made a sound. Kei was already moving.

He got there just as Tiger’s knees started to go. Drew had him by one arm; Kei took the other. Not holding him up. Just there. Tiger stood between them, shaking, staring at Ossie’s father with Ossie’s eyes, and couldn’t say a word.

“I’m sorry,” said Tiger, at last. It came out cracked. “I’m sorry. I’m so sorry. I’m—“

“Hush,” said Pell. He came across the courtyard, slowly, and stopped in front of Tiger. Up close he was even bigger. He smelled of road dust and sawdust and sweat. “Hush, lad. I didn’t come to make you sorry.”

Then his face did something, and he looked at the ground.

“I came to ask,” he said, very quietly. “They’re saying, in the valley. At Harrowford. A trader came through from the west last week, and he said. He said my boy came back. To the gate. At dusk.” He lifted his head. His eyes were wet. “He said my Os walked up to the gate of Thornwall and asked to come in, and you wouldn’t open it.”

The courtyard had gone completely silent.

“Is it true?” said Pell.

Tiger couldn’t answer. Kei could feel him trying. Could feel the whole of him straining toward an answer and not finding one, the way a man strains toward a ledge just out of reach.

So Kei answered.

He didn’t decide to. He just found himself speaking, in the calm low voice that people said made them want to listen, and he heard it the way you hear yourself in a dream.

“Something came to the gate,” he said. “Three weeks after the patrol. At dusk. It had his face.”

Pell turned to look at him.

“It had his face,” said Kei. “And his voice. And his walk. It knew his name, and Tiger’s name, and the shed. It knew things only Ossie would know.” He held Pell’s eyes. He didn’t look away. That was the important part; he’d learned that from a long call at two in the morning. You didn’t look away. “And it wasn’t him.”

“How do you know?”

“Because it didn’t breathe,” said Kei. “Not unless it remembered to. Because it didn’t blink. Because every dog in Thornwall knew before we did, and howled. Because the girl with the gold eyes, Hyvred, can see the light in people, and she said his light was there, but there was a hand inside it, holding it up.” He paused. “Because my snake can smell anything that lives, and he said it smelled of nothing at all. Like a stone that’s been in a river.”

Pell’s face didn’t move. But his huge hands, at his sides, had closed.

“And because,” said Kei, more quietly, “if it had been Ossie, Tiger would have torn that gate down with his bare hands to get to him. You’d only have had to see his face. We had to hold him back. Hild had to stand in his way. Tiger would have opened that gate for your son if it had killed him. He didn’t open it, because it wasn’t your son.”

There was a long silence in the courtyard.

“Then where is he?” said Pell. “My Os. If that wasn’t him. Where is he?”

Kei could have said a lot of things. He could have said we don’t know, which was true, or he might still be alive, which might be true, or he’s at peace, which nobody had any way of knowing at all. People always wanted to hear something. People always wanted you to give them something to hold on to.

He thought about the call. Three hours. He didn’t fix anything. He didn’t try to.

“I don’t know,” said Kei. “I’m sorry. I won’t lie to you. I don’t know where he is, or if he’s alive. Nobody does. The thing at the gate walked away north, over the hills, toward the passes. That’s all we know.”

Pell looked at him for a long time.

Then the big man’s face folded up, all at once, like a house coming down, and he put his huge hands over it, and stood there in the middle of the courtyard of Thornwall with the whole Western Watch watching, and wept.

Nobody moved. Then Tiger did. He pulled his arm out of Drew’s grip, and Kei’s, and stepped forward, and put his arms round Ossie’s father as far as they’d go, which wasn’t very far. And the two of them stood there together in the hot sun by the well, the big man and the lad with the tiger ears, and held on.

Drew stood behind Tiger with his hand on his back. Kei stood beside them. Hisui, on his wrist, for once, said nothing at all.

Kei sat with Pell that night.

Brannoch had given him a bed in the officers’ quarters, but he didn’t use it. He sat in the long room at the top of the north tower, at the big table under Corvin’s maps, with a cup of something hot that Kei had made him, and didn’t sleep, and didn’t seem to want to.

Kei stayed.

He didn’t do anything. That was the thing he’d learned. You didn’t have to do anything. He just sat on the other side of the table, with Hisui coiled in his lap, and was there. Sometimes Pell talked. About Ossie as a boy: too-big feet, always falling over things, couldn’t sit still in the workshop, drove his father mad. About the day Ossie’s aura had come up, at sixteen, in the middle of the village green, and he’d run straight through a fence without noticing. About Ossie’s mother, at home in Harrowford, who couldn’t make the walk with her hip, and who’d sent Pell with a shirt she’d mended for Ossie and a cake that had gone hard on the road.

Sometimes Pell didn’t talk. Then Kei didn’t either. They just sat there, and the fire burned, and outside the window the stars went slowly round over the Greyspine.

Around midnight Kei made him another drink. He’d been working on it all spring: something for the soldiers who came back from patrol and couldn’t sleep. Valerian and lime-flower and a little of the grey moss that grew on the north wall, which Hisui said was the same thing the shepherds used for their teething babies. In the right dose, it let a body rest. In the wrong dose, it would stop a heart.

Poison and medicine are the same thing, Hisui had said, in the winter. The difference is the dose. And the intent.

Kei measured it out very carefully. Then, with the cup in his hands, before he gave it to Pell, he felt for the gap.

He’d been feeling for it since Thea’s letter. In his herbs. In his antidotes. In the little twists of dried root he carried for the patrols. He’d found it once or twice, faintly, and never known what to write into it. Heal felt too big. Help felt like begging.

Tonight, sitting across the table from a man whose son had been taken, he knew.

Rest.

He wrote it into the cup. Not a command. An offer. The way you’d say it to someone at five in the morning, on a call, when the sky was getting light. Okay. Rest now. I’m here tomorrow too.

The drink in the cup shivered, very slightly, and for a heartbeat it smelled of nothing but summer: cut grass and warm hay and lime flowers in the sun.

He gave it to Pell. Pell drank it. And a little while later the big man’s head went down on his folded arms on the table under Corvin’s maps, and his shoulders, which had been up round his ears since he walked through the gate, came down at last, and he slept.

Kei sat and watched him sleep until dawn.

“Thirty-four,” said Hisui, very quietly, sometime around four. Looking at Kei’s window, which Kei had forgotten was open. “Up three. In one cup.” A pause. “That was well done.”

Kei looked at him. “I thought snakes didn’t do compliments.”

“We don’t,” said Hisui. “Don’t get used to it.”

In the morning, Pell asked to see the shed.

Tiger took him. Kei went too, and Drew, and somehow, by the time they reached the north wall, so had Bee, and Hyvred, and half a dozen of the Watch. Nobody had said anything. They’d just all ended up there, in the bright morning, in a loose quiet ring round the three crooked walls.

Pell stood in front of it for a long time.

It wasn’t much. Three walls of salvaged board, crooked, nailed together by someone who’d clearly never built anything before, against the inside of the north wall of the fort. A gap for a door. No roof. Grass growing up inside it now, after a month and more.

Pell walked round it, slowly. He ran his hand along the top of one wall. He crouched down and looked at the joints. He looked at the nails, bent over the way you bend them when you’ve missed the head twice. And Kei watched his carpenter’s face do what a carpenter’s face does, looking at bad work: wince, very slightly, and then go soft.

Then he stepped through the gap where the door should go, and stopped.

On the inside of the back wall, in white chalk, a little faded now, there was a drawing of a roof. A proper one, with rafters and a ridge and a little chimney, the way you’d draw it for a child. And underneath it, in small square letters: OSSIE’S.

Pell stood in front of it without moving.

“Who did this?” he said.

“Me,” said Bee, from the ring. She didn’t say anything else. She didn’t need to.

Pell looked at the drawing for a long time. Then he turned round and looked at Tiger, through the gap in the wall, with his son’s eyes.

“He promised you a roof,” he said.

“Yeah,” said Tiger. His voice wobbled. “He said his dad was a carpenter. He said he’d show me how to do a proper one.”

Pell nodded slowly.

“Well,” he said. “His dad is a carpenter.”

He put his bag down on the grass. He opened it. And he started taking out tools.

They built the roof that day.

It took all of it, from the morning to the long gold evening. Pell did it properly, the way he’d been doing it for thirty years, and he did it slowly, and he made Tiger do every part of it himself. He wouldn’t let Tiger watch. You’ll not learn by watching. You’ll learn by your hands. So Tiger measured the rafters, and cut them, and cut them wrong, and cut them again. Tiger notched the joints, while Pell stood over him saying gentler, lad, it’s not a fight. Tiger hammered in the pegs.

Drew was beside him the whole time.

Kei watched it all afternoon, from the well, from the wall, from the shade of the gatehouse. Drew holding the ladder. Drew handing up nails before Tiger asked for them. Drew catching a rafter Tiger nearly dropped, without a word, and setting it back in his hands. Drew saying nothing at all, most of the time, just being there, a big calm steady shape at Tiger’s elbow, the way he’d been at Tiger’s elbow for nine years, through a dozen games and a dozen bad nights and one whole other world. You could see it in the way they moved round each other. They didn’t need to talk. They’d stopped needing to talk a long time ago.

At one point, late in the afternoon, Tiger missed a peg and hit his thumb so hard he dropped the hammer and sat down on the grass and swore, very creatively, for about a minute. Drew sat down next to him and waited until he’d finished. Then he picked up the hammer and held it out.

“You’ll build another one,” said Drew.

Tiger stared at him. Then he laughed, through his teeth, holding his thumb. “That’s what you said about the shed.”

“It was true about the shed.”

“It got kicked over by a horse.”

“And then you built another one.” Drew put the hammer in his hand. “And then you’ll put a roof on it. Go on.”

Tiger went on.

The others helped, in the end. They couldn’t not. Bee climbed up on the wall and passed down boards. A couple of the Watch carried shingles from the stores. Hyvred sat on the wall in the sun and called out encouragement and, every so often, very kindly, told Tiger which bits of wood were going to split before he nailed them. Corvin came down from the long room with his spectacles on his head and stood with his arms folded looking anxious until Hild told him to either hold something or go away.

Kei held the ladder while Pell went up to set the ridge-beam. The big man was surprisingly light on the rungs. At the top he stopped, and looked out over the north wall, at the hills. North, where the track went up toward the passes.

He stayed there a long time, looking.

Then he came down, and handed Tiger the last peg, and stood back, and let him drive it home.

When it was finished, the sun was going down. They all stood back and looked at it.

It was a shed. That was all. A small rough shed against the north wall of a fortress, with three crooked walls and a new door that Pell had made from scratch that afternoon in about an hour, and a roof. A proper roof. Rafters and ridge and shingles, pitched to throw the rain, with a little overhang at the front so the Pollen Puffs could sit out of the wet. Inside, on the back wall, under the new roof, Bee’s chalk drawing was still there, untouched. OSSIE’S.

The Pollen Puffs flew in through the door, and circled once, buzzing, and settled on the ridge-beam, and went to sleep.

Tiger stood in front of it with his hands hanging at his sides and his thumb swollen up like a plum.

“It’s standing,” he said.

“It’s standing,” said Pell.

“Things I build don’t stand,” said Tiger. His voice had gone very small. “They never stand. Not here, not back home. Something always comes and knocks them down.”

Pell looked at him. Then he put one huge hand on Tiger’s shoulder.

“This one will,” he said. “I built houses for thirty years, lad. I know what stands.” He looked at the shed. “That’ll stand till you’re my age. Longer. It’s got a good roof on it.”

There was a storm that night.

It came over the Greyspine at midnight, the first big storm of the summer, all wind and thunder and rain coming down so hard it bounced. Kei was awake for it. He went to the window of the long room and looked down into the courtyard by the lightning, at the north wall.

The shed stood.

The rain hammered on the new roof and poured off the overhang in a sheet, and the wind tore at the shingles and found nothing to tear. And under the overhang, in the flashes of lightning, two small round shapes sat on the ridge-beam, dry, asleep.

Kei stood at the window and watched it for a long time. Then he turned round.

Tiger was standing in the doorway behind him, in his shirt, barefoot. He’d come up to look too.

“It’s standing,” said Tiger.

“It’s standing,” said Kei.

Tiger came and stood at the window beside him, and they watched it together, in the storm, until it had passed.

Pell left two days later.

He went the way he’d come: on foot, east and south, down the long road toward Harrowford, with his bag on his back. Brannoch had offered him a horse. He’d said no. He’d said he’d rather walk; he had a lot to think about, and you thought better walking.

He left Ossie’s mended shirt behind. He gave it to Tiger, folded. She’d want it to be here, he said. Where he’ll come, if he comes. Tiger hung it on a nail on the back wall of the shed, under Bee’s chalk roof.

At the gate, Pell stopped and turned round. Kei and Tiger and Drew had walked him down.

“If he comes back,” said Pell. “The real one. My Os.” He looked at Tiger. “You tell him his roof’s done.”

“I will,” said Tiger.

“And you tell him,” said Pell, “that it’s a good roof. You did it right. In the end.” His mouth twitched. “Mostly.”

He shook Kei’s hand, with his enormous carpenter’s hand. Then he did something Kei didn’t expect. He put his other hand on top, over Kei’s, and held it there a moment.

“Thank you,” he said. “For sitting up.”

Kei didn’t know what to say. So he didn’t say anything. He just held on, the way Ice would have.

Then Pell let go, and turned, and walked out of the gate of Thornwall into the morning, and down the long road, until he was a small dark figure, and then nothing.

They stood at the gate for a while after he’d gone.

“The passes’ll be open in four weeks,” said Drew, eventually, quietly. “Fenwick said this morning. The snow’s going fast. Brannoch’s going to send the deep patrol. Over the Greyspine. Forty men, Hild, Fenwick.” He paused. “Hyvred’s going. She has to. She’s the only one who can see them.”

“Then I’m going,” said Kei.

Drew looked at him.

“They’ll need a medic,” said Kei. “Someone who knows what grows up there. What’s poison and what isn’t.” He looked down at Hisui, on his wrist. “And someone who can smell a thing that smells of nothing.”

Hisui was quiet for a moment.

“If I die up there,” he said, “I’m haunting you.”

“You said that last time.”

“I meant it last time. I mean it more now.”

Tiger didn’t say anything. He was looking north, at the hills, where the track went up toward the passes, the same way Pell had looked from the top of the ladder. Then he turned and looked back across the courtyard at the north wall, at the small rough shed with its new roof and its new door, standing.

“If I go,” he said, “will you come?”

He was talking to Drew. Drew didn’t hesitate.

“Yeah,” he said. “Obviously.”

Kei wrote to the channel that night. Just one line. He didn’t think it needed more.

[ Kei: The roof’s on. It stood through a storm. ]

Underneath, after a moment, Tiger wrote:

[ Tiger: it’s a good roof. ossie’s dad says i did it right. mostly ]

And then the channel filled up, the way it did now, with people from everywhere, one after another. Maigo, in capitals. Gigichi, good. Newish, a HOUSE that STANDS??? tiger??? Hako, who wrote: Adding this to the book.

Kei read them all, sitting on the wall of Thornwall in the warm dark with Hisui round his wrist. Then he closed his window, and sat there a while longer, looking down at the shed.

He thought about a call that had lasted until five in the morning, in a room in another world. About a man who’d said I’m here tomorrow too, and meant it.

I’m trying, he thought. I’m trying to give it back.

Below him, in the courtyard, the shed stood in the starlight, with two small round shapes asleep on its ridge-beam and a mended shirt hanging on its back wall, waiting.
