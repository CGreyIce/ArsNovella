The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 4: Threads

Chapter 92

Root

3,841 words · 17 min read

They used to call every night.

Thea thought about that a lot, that winter, walking down the lake road at dusk with the others. On Earth, for years, before school got busy: a call that opened at ten and closed at two or three, most nights of the week. Sometimes he played a game for her, because she’d asked, because she wanted to see the ending and couldn’t be bothered to get there herself. Sometimes he played with her. Sometimes they didn’t play anything at all; they just left the call open while they did other things, and talked when they felt like it, and didn’t when they didn’t.

And she sang. That was the part she’d never told anyone. Late, when the game was paused and he’d gone quiet, she’d start humming, and then singing, half to herself, whatever was in her head. He never asked her to. He never asked her to stop, either. He just went quiet on the other end of the call and listened, and she knew he was listening, and that had been the whole of it.

And then school had got busy. And the calls went from every night to some nights, to sometimes, to we should call soon. And she’d meant to. She’d had the button right there. And then one morning a message came at six a.m. and she’d sat on the edge of her bed with her phone in her hand and not been able to read it properly for a long time.

She’d wished, for three years, that she’d talked to him more.

Now she sat at his table every night.

She had a new skill.

Patch. It had come with Candle, at the bonfire, a year to the night after Here. Light. She’d felt it arrive the way the first one had, like a word you’d been trying to remember for a long time suddenly turning up on your tongue. Parse let her read windows. Patch let her change them. Small things. Not much. But real.

She’d spent the first week finding out what that meant.

She fixed Azac’s progress bar, first. It had been flickering since the summer, one number to the next and back again, too fast to read. Azac had written about it in the channel, half joking, my window’s having a breakdown, and nobody had been able to do anything. Thea looked at it from a long way upriver, through the channel, through the shared part underneath that every window inherited, and found the little stutter in the line that drew it. Like a dropped stitch. She picked it up. Azac wrote IT STOPPED in capitals and then thea what did you DO and she wrote nothing and felt, for the first time in a long time, very pleased with herself.

She fixed a gap in Bee’s skill page that had been there since the first week. She fixed the line in Chris’s window that showed his numbers in an order he hated. She fixed Maigo’s capitals; or rather, she found the line that made his window shout every time he got excited, and asked him if he wanted her to fix it, and Maigo wrote DON’T YOU DARE and she didn’t.

Small things. Mending. The kind of thing she’d done on Earth for fun at two in the morning, before she knew anything about this world: finding the bug, fixing the bug, going to bed.

She couldn’t touch the bottom.

She’d known she wouldn’t be able to. She’d tried, in the first week, on her own, at night in the upper room with Higu holding the lamp the way she had in the spring. She’d gone down through her own window, past the skills and the channel and the medium line, toward the place where everything ran into the first window. And stopped. Not a wall. More like reaching the bottom of the sea and finding the sand there, and knowing that under the sand there was rock, and under the rock there was more, and none of it was hers.

The first window was his.

He offered on the fourteenth evening.

She hadn’t asked. She’d wanted to ask every night since the supper and hadn’t, because it felt like asking somebody to show you inside their own head. She’d asked him a hundred other things instead. He’d answered all of them, in English, patiently, sitting across the table with his hands round a cup. About the window. About when he’d first seen it.

He’d been born with it. That was the first thing he’d told her, the first night after the supper, when she finally got up the nerve. He’d woken up in a body that couldn’t lift its own head, in a cottage that smelled of smoke and goats, with his whole life from before still in his head and no way to say any of it. And floating at the edge of everything, faint, the way a reflection floats on a window at night, a box. He couldn’t read it, at first. His eyes didn’t work properly yet. By the time they did, he’d known what it was.

“I was six before I tried to open it on purpose,” he’d said. “Before I could make my mouth say anything that wasn’t the language of the cottage. I lay in bed at night and tried, in English, under my breath. Status. And it opened.” He’d turned his cup round in his hands. “I cried. I was six and I lay in bed and cried, because it was the first word from home that had ever done anything here.”

On the fourteenth evening, when the others were getting their coats on, he said: “Stay a bit, if you like. I’ll walk you up.”

Thea looked at him.

“You keep looking at the bottom,” he said. “When I open it. You look past the skills, and your eyes go down. Every time.” He looked at her across the table. “You want to see it.”

She didn’t say anything. She didn’t need to.

“Then stay,” he said.

The others went. Gigichi looked back once at the gate, at Thea, with an expression Thea couldn’t read. Higu didn’t look back at all; she just put her hand on Gigichi’s arm and walked her on, the way Higu always knew to do. Alea went up the stairs and closed her door, very pointedly, the way you’d close a door to make it clear you weren’t listening at it.

They went down to the jetty.

It was cold, and clear, and the lake was white under the stars. He brushed the snow off the end of the jetty with his hand and they sat down on the boards, side by side, with their feet over the ice. Thea had her coat buttoned to her chin and her hands in her sleeves. He didn’t seem to feel the cold at all. He sat there in his shirtsleeves and the same old coat, open, and looked at the lake.

“I’ve never done this,” he said. “Shown anyone. Not like this. Not Halvard. I showed him how to look at his own, when I was fifteen, on a wall. I never showed him mine.” He was quiet a moment. “Diwa saw the top of it. Alea’s seen all of the top. Nobody’s seen the bottom.”

“I won’t touch anything,” said Thea.

“You can’t,” said Ars. “But you’ll want to. You’ll see something broken and your hands will itch.” Something moved at the corner of his mouth. “Tell me. I’ll fix it.”

He opened the window.

She’d seen the top before. At the supper. Status. Skills. Eight words in a row, ending in Annihilate.

He let her see the rest.

It went down. That was the first thing. Hers went down too, under the top, through layers, the way she’d described it to the others: the skills, the inventory, the channel, the medium line, the shared part underneath, all the way to the place where it ran into the first window. Like a tree, upside down, with its roots in his.

His didn’t run into anything.

It just went down. Further than hers. Layer under layer, all of it written in the same plain English, the same writing she’d been reading for a year and a half, the window’s own hand. She’d known it was English. She’d told everyone, in the spring, the night she built the channel: it’s English, all the way down. But she’d only ever seen the bits of it that had been copied into her own window, the way you’d only ever seen photocopies of a letter.

This was the letter.

“It’s simple,” she said.

She hadn’t meant to say it out loud. She was Parsing it, reading it the way she read everything, and it was coming up into her head in shapes, and the shape of it was simple. Not stupid. Simple, the way a child’s drawing of a house is simple: a square and a triangle and a door, and everything you need to know about a house is there.

“The words,” said Thea. “At the bottom. Where it starts. They’re…” She stopped. “They’re a kid’s words. Status. Inventory. Skills. Tier. Progress. It’s the words a kid would know from games. Nothing fancy. Nothing anybody would choose if they were building it on purpose.” She looked at him. “It’s the words you knew. When you were six.”

“It’s the only ones I had,” said Ars.

“And every window in the world is built out of them.” She looked back at the window. At the root of it. “Every native who can see a window. Every one of us. All of it. Built on a six-year-old in a goat cottage crying under his blanket because status worked.”

He didn’t say anything.

Thea found she had to stop reading for a moment.

She found the medium line at the very bottom.

She’d half expected it wouldn’t be there. Hers was. Everybody’s was, all thirty-five: Medium: one blade · parted · Ice, now, since the night at the door. Maren’s didn’t have one. Alea’s didn’t. Nobody born in this world had one; it was how she’d scoped the channel in the first place.

His had one.

It was right at the bottom of everything, under the last layer, where the root of the root ran out. A short line, in the same plain hand.

Medium:

And nothing after it.

Not sealed. Not hidden. Not one blade, or a name, or anything. Just the word, and a colon, and then nothing at all, as if the line had been started and never finished.

Thea looked at it for a long time.

“Nobody brought you,” she said.

He looked at the empty line beside her.

“No,” he said. “Nobody brought me. Halvard brought you. A sword brought you. Nobody brought me.” He was quiet. “I just woke up.”

They sat there for a while.

The lake was very still under its snow. A long way off, on the far shore, an owl said something to the pines and the pines didn’t answer. The cold came up through the boards of the jetty. Thea didn’t feel it much.

She thought about it. A summoning needed a medium. A sword, a ring, a comb. Something that pulled you out of wherever you were and across into here. Oswin had written to Cursiffe about it, and Cursiffe had told the channel: a medium remembers its hands. Every summoning that had ever worked had something on the end of the line.

His didn’t. His line was empty. Whatever had brought him here, if anything had, it hadn’t left its name.

“Do you know why?” she said. “Why you? Why here?”

“No.”

“Have you ever—“

“For thirty years,” said Ars. “Every way I could think of. I read every book in every library I could get into. I asked every mage I met, carefully, so they wouldn’t know what I was asking. I asked Halvard, without ever telling him why.” He looked at the empty line. “There’s nothing. Nobody’s ever been born with a window before me. Nobody’s ever been born remembering somewhere else. Not that anyone wrote down.” He was quiet a moment. “I stopped asking when Alea was born. I had other things to think about.”

She found the sealed value next.

Not in his window. In the place where his window touched everyone else’s. The shared part. She’d been looking at it from the other side for a year, from inside her own window: the sealed value beside Medium: one blade, the door with no handle, rattling every time he walked past. She could see the other side of it now, from his. Thirty-five little doors, one in every summoned window, all of them hanging off the same place at the bottom of his.

Five of them were open.

She counted twice. Five. Hers, and four others. She knew whose without having to look.

The other thirty were shut.

“They open one at a time,” said Thea slowly. “Don’t they. When you tell them.” She looked at him. “Not when they find out. Not when they work it out. We worked it out in the summer, at the table, and nothing happened. It opened at the door. When you said it.” She swallowed. “It’s me. It’s Ice. Out loud. To us.”

Ars looked at the thirty shut doors for a long time.

“I didn’t know that,” he said. “I didn’t do that on purpose. I didn’t know the window would do that.” He was quiet. “It’s my secret. I suppose it’s mine to open.”

“Thirty more,” said Thea.

“Thirty more.”

“Gabe’s is one of them.”

He didn’t say anything to that. He didn’t have to. She watched him. Not his face, which still slid, the way it did for everyone; the rest of him. And she saw something go through him that she thought was probably what his voice had done on Earth, years ago, on a call, when somebody had said a name that mattered to him. She’d never seen his face on Earth either. She’d only ever heard his voice. She thought she’d have known it anyway.

She asked him about the way home at the end.

She’d known she would. She’d been carrying the question since the summer, since the night at the table when they worked out who he was and Higu did the sum: thirty-four years. She’d thought about it every night since. A man who’d been here thirty-four years. Who’d built every window in the world without meaning to. Who’d fought a war and buried his wife and raised a daughter. Who must, at some point, at the very beginning, have wanted to go home more than anything.

“The way home,” she said. “The Sun tier. The Advisor told us, the first day. Only a Sun mage, in theory. No one we can confirm.” She looked at him. “Did you look?”

He didn’t answer for a long time.

“I gave up,” he said at last. “A long time ago.”

Thea waited.

“I looked,” he said. “At first. Every day. Then every week. Then every year.” He was looking at the lake. “Alea said it once, to somebody. I told her when she was small, and she remembered. Every day, then every week, then every year, and then one day you notice you haven’t thought about it in a long time. And that’s the worst day.” He was quiet. “I had that day when I was twenty-three. On a wall at the garrison. I realised I hadn’t thought about home in a month, and I sat there and was more frightened than I’ve ever been in my life, before or since. And then I got up and went on with it.”

He looked down at the empty line in his window. Medium: and nothing.

“Nobody’s ever reached the tenth tier,” he said. “Not a mage. Not a swordsman. Not in any book I ever found. And even if somebody did, I didn’t know if it would work for me. I wasn’t summoned. Nobody brought me. There was no line to pull back on.” He closed the window. “So I stopped looking. I had a war, and Diwa, and then Alea. This was home. I made it home.” He looked at her. “It is home.”

Thea sat on the end of the jetty with her hands in her sleeves and looked at the lake.

She thought about thirty-four years on his own. About a six-year-old crying under a blanket because one word from home had worked. About a boy on a wall at twenty-three, realising he hadn’t thought about his father in a month. About every book in every library, asked about carefully, so nobody would know why.

On his own.

“We didn’t,” she said.

He looked at her.

“Give up,” said Thea. “We didn’t. Any of us.” She turned on the boards to face him. “Milli’s been saying it since the hall. It’s a plan. It’s a real plan. The night of the sum, when we found out it was only a month at home, he said ten years here is less than one there, the way home isn’t a rumour anymore. Thirty-four people have been working on it for a year and a half. Chris logs it. Oswin’s in a vault in the capital reading four hundred years of summonings. Cursiffe’s writing it all down. Hyvred can see the turned from the top of a mountain. Niko’s trying to make two kinds of power into one.” She swallowed. “We didn’t give up. We just didn’t know you were here, so we didn’t know to tell you.”

He didn’t say anything.

“You looked alone,” said Thea. “For thirty years. With nobody. Asking carefully so nobody would know.” She held his eyes. “You’re not alone now. There’s thirty-six of us.”

He didn’t answer for a long time.

When he did, it wasn’t what she expected.

“You don’t have to want me to go,” he said.

Thea stared at him.

“Home,” he said. “If there’s ever a way. You don’t have to want me to go back. I’m not sure I would.” He looked at the lake, at the white ice going away to the pines, at the lamp in the cabin window behind them. “Alea’s here. Diwa’s here. My whole life is here. Thirty-four years of it.” He was quiet. “My father’s three years older, and he’s got someone, and he’s been sad about me for three years and he’ll have started to be less sad by now. If I turned up at his door at sixty in a stranger’s body…” He stopped. “I don’t know. I don’t know what I’d want.”

“You don’t have to know,” said Thea.

He looked at her.

“You don’t have to want to go,” said Thea. “You never have to. That’s yours.” She put her hand out of her sleeve, finally, in the cold, and put it on his arm. “But I’m not giving up on the door just because you stopped knocking on it. None of us are. When we find it, you’ll know where it is. And then you can decide.” She held on. “That’s all. You get to decide. You didn’t get to, last time.”

He looked down at her hand on his arm.

“Thank you,” he said.

On the way back up the jetty, he stopped.

“Can you do something for me?” he said.

Thea looked at him.

“With Patch,” he said. “Something small. I’ve never been able to work out how to do it. I’ve tried for years. I can write anything at the bottom; I can make the channel keep two hundred lines. But I can’t do this.” He looked faintly embarrassed, which she’d never once seen him look. “I don’t think the way you do. I build things like a kid builds a house. Square, triangle, door.”

“What is it?”

He opened his window again, just the top.

“The date,” he said. “The slow one. Chris found it in your channel, the stamp on every line. The date at home.” He looked at his own window. “Mine doesn’t have it. Mine’s never had it. I had to go digging under the channel to find it, the night of the sum, and I can’t make it stay.” He was quiet. “I’d like to see it. In the morning. When I open the window. What day it is there.”

Thea looked at him for a long moment.

Then she opened her own window. She found the stamp, the slow Earth date on the tail of every line in the channel, the one she’d found in the summer. She looked at how it was built. Then she wrote a small patch, very carefully, three lines in the window’s own language, that took the date off the tail of the last line in the channel and put it at the top of her own window, small, under her name, where she’d see it first thing.

She showed him.

“I can’t do yours,” she said. “I can’t reach. But that’s how. Copy it.”

He looked at the three lines for a long time. Then he did what she’d never once seen anybody do except herself, in sixteen months in this world: he read code, and understood it, and wrote it again, in his own hand, at the bottom of his own window.

He looked at the top of the window.

She saw it there too. Over his shoulder. Small, under his name and his tier and his long line of terrible skills, in the window’s plain hand.

A date. A day of the week, and a day of the month, and a month, in English. Six weeks after the night thirty-five people had gone to sleep in their own beds on Earth and woken up in a castle.

Ars looked at it for a long time.

“It’s a Tuesday,” he said.

His voice had gone strange.

“At home,” he said. “Right now. It’s a Tuesday. In the afternoon, probably.” He looked at the date. “Somebody’s at work. Somebody’s having lunch. My father’s…” He stopped.

Thea didn’t say anything. She didn’t take her hand off his arm.

They stood on the jetty in the snow, under the wrong stars, which didn’t look wrong anymore, and he looked at a Tuesday in another world for a long time.

“Thank you,” he said again, at last.

“It’s a patch,” said Thea. “It’s small.”

“It’s not small,” said Ars.

He walked her up the lake road. At the top, Rowan stepped aside, and then stepped back. And Thea walked up the hill to the militia hall alone, in the dark, in the snow, with her lantern, and her own window open in front of her, looking at the same small date under her own name.

A Tuesday. Six weeks.

She’d never once, in a year and a half, thought of it as something she could look at every morning.

She thought she would, now.
