The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 3: The Anniversary

Chapter 90

Supper

5,556 words · 24 min read

Hi, Ice, said Higu, and walked past him into the house.

He stood in the doorway with the cold coming in round him and the cloth still over his shoulder and watched her go. Into the gold light. Past the table with its borrowed white cloth and its seven bowls. To the fire. She stopped there, with her back to him, and held her hands out to it, the way you’d warm your hands after a long walk in the snow, and didn’t turn round.

Nobody had called him that in thirty-four years.

He’d thought it would feel like something breaking. He’d thought about it for sixteen months, on the jetty, at night: what it would be like if anyone ever said it. He’d thought it would feel like the Redmarch, or the morning after, or the night he read three years. Like a blow.

It didn’t. It felt like a door he’d forgotten was a door, swinging open on its own, onto a room he’d lived in for twenty-six years and then left, and finding that somebody had kept the fire lit.

He stood in the doorway, and the others were still on the step.

Gigichi came next.

She had two lanterns. Thea’s and her own. She came up onto the step with one in each hand, and stopped in front of him, close, and looked up at him with her jaw set and her eyes very bright, the way she’d looked at him on the market road sixteen months ago when he said you’re blocking the road and she said then walk around.

“Chi 1,” he said.

In English. The name she’d made with PenguChi in the first week, because they kept both answering to Chi. He’d read it a thousand times in the channel. He’d never once said it out loud.

Gigichi’s jaw went tighter. She didn’t say anything. She held his eyes for a long moment, furiously, the way you’d hold your breath. Then she pushed one of the lanterns into his hands, hard, so that he had to take it, and walked past him into the house without a word.

He stood there holding Thea’s lantern.

Xola didn’t walk past him.

She came up onto the step with her own lantern and Squiddles on her head gone a deep, shining gold, and her face already crumpling, and she didn’t even stop. She dropped the lantern in the snow and walked straight into him, the way you’d walk into a wall in the dark, and put her arms round his middle, and held on, and cried into his shirt.

He stood there with a lantern in one hand and Xola round his middle and didn’t know what to do with his other arm.

“Xola,” he said.

“You gifted me subs,” said Xola, into his shirt, furiously, through it. “Every stream. When I had like nine people. You gifted me subs and you were in my chat every night and you made everyone laugh and you were always there, and then you went and died, and I hadn’t talked to you in months, and I never said—“ She hit him in the chest with her fist. Not hard. “And now you’re a fisherman.”

He put his other arm round her.

“I’m a fisherman,” he agreed.

Squiddles, on her head, reached out one small purple tentacle and patted him on the face.

PenguChi came up onto the step slowly, with Waddles’ head poking out of the front of her coat. She waited until Xola had let go, and gone in, wiping her face on her sleeve and saying sorry, sorry, oh my god. And then she stood in front of him, on the step, quite still, with her golden eyes on his face.

“Chi 2,” he said.

PenguChi looked at him for a long moment. Then she smiled. Slowly. The way she’d smiled at him on the shingle, the day of the stones, when she handed him the biggest stone on the whole lake road and he said thank you. Like the sun coming out.

“Thank you for the lessons,” she said.

She said it in her calm low steady voice, and he felt it do the thing it did to rooms. Felt it settle into him like warmth. He’d read about her voice in the channel, all year. He’d never once had it pointed at him.

“They were Alea’s lessons,” he said.

“They were yours,” said PenguChi. “She told me. Every time. My dad says.” She tilted her head. “I’m on seventeen. To Tempered. I wait for it to arrive now.”

“I know,” he said. “I read it.”

PenguChi didn’t say anything. She just went on smiling, and stepped past him into the house. As she went, Waddles leaned out of the front of her coat and put his beak, very briefly, on the back of Ars’s hand.

And Thea.

She was the last. She was standing at the bottom of the step, in the snow, with nothing in her hands, because she’d given her lantern to Gigichi to knock with and then never knocked. Her hands were at her sides. Her face was white. The pink and violet in her hair caught the light from the doorway.

She’d walked into him at the market, sixteen months ago, with a basket of eggs. She’d said I’ll pay you back. And then, very carefully, looking at his face, Trust.

He’d said there’s plenty of fish, and walked away.

He looked at her now, on the step, in the gold light from his own door.

“Thea,” he said.

She didn’t move.

“You said trust,” said Thea. Very quietly. “At the market. I said trust, on purpose, to see what your face did.”

“I know.”

“And you said there’s plenty of fish.”

“I know,” said Ars. “I’m sorry.”

Thea looked at him for a very long time.

Then her face did something he’d seen it do on stream, years ago, when somebody in her chat said something so stupid that she couldn’t decide whether to laugh or ban them. It crumpled and lit up at the same time.

“There was plenty of fish,” said Thea. “That’s the worst part. You sent five a day.”

And she came up the step and put her arms round his neck, and he dropped the lantern, and held on.

He shut the door behind them.

He stood with his back to it for a moment in the warmth, with five people from another world in his house, and his daughter by the hearth with a ladle in her hand and her ears flat and her face doing something he’d never seen it do, and three hens in the snow outside, sitting by the gate like loaves, quiet for the first time in five years.

The five of them were standing in the middle of the room. Not sitting. Not touching anything. In their coats, the ones he’d sent up the hill on a cart the first winter, mended, each one exactly the right length. Looking at him. Waiting.

He’d rehearsed it to the hens. It hadn’t come.

He tried again.

“It’s me,” he said.

His voice cracked on it. He let it.

“It’s Ice.”

Nobody said anything.

They’d known. All of them. Since a night in the summer at a table in the upper room of a militia hall. Higu had said it at the door. He’d known they’d known; he’d watched them know, for six months, in the channel, saying nothing. He’d thought saying it would be nothing. A formality. A word everybody already had.

It wasn’t nothing.

He watched it land. On all five faces, one after another, the way he’d watched things land on their faces through the channel for a year, without ever being able to see them. Xola first, of course; she was already crying, and cried harder. Then Gigichi, whose jaw finally gave, and who sat down very suddenly on the bench at the table and put her face in her hands. PenguChi, who closed her eyes. Higu, at the fire, who turned round at last and looked at him with her calm half-lidded eyes wet. And Thea, nearest him, who’d let go of his neck and stepped back to look at him, and who put both hands over her mouth.

“Ice,” said Thea, through her hands.

“Here,” he said, “it’s Ars. Mostly. Nobody here can say the other one.” He looked at them. “You can call me whatever you like.”

“Ice,” said Xola, wetly, fiercely. “We’re calling you Ice.”

From the hearth, very quietly, Alea said: “He answers to Dad.”

Everyone looked at her.

She’d gone bright red to the tips of her ears. She was holding the ladle in both hands like a sword.

“Sorry,” said Alea. “Sorry. The stew’s ready. If anyone wants it. It’s terrible.”

It was terrible.

He knew it was. He watched them taste it. The five of them, round his table, on his benches, in their coats because nobody had thought to take them off, with Alea standing at the end of the table with the ladle, watching their faces like a hawk. Squiddles had a bowl. Waddles had a bowl. Alea had insisted. The table groaned, the way it always did, and Xola jumped, and then laughed.

They tasted it.

There was a silence.

“It’s supposed to be assam pedas,” said Ars. “It isn’t.”

“What’s assam pedas?” said Gigichi, faintly, with her spoon still in her mouth.

“A fish stew. From Malacca. Sour and hot and red.” He looked at his own bowl. “There’s no tamarind here. No laksa leaf. No torch ginger. I’ve been trying to make it with sour plums and a root from the far shore for thirty years.”

Xola took another spoonful. Her eyes were watering.

“It tastes,” said Xola, “like a fire in a swamp.”

“Thank you,” said Alea, from the end of the table.

And then Xola ate the whole bowl. And asked for more. And so did Gigichi, coughing, with her eyes streaming. And PenguChi, very slowly, very steadily, as if it were a lesson. And Higu, who didn’t say anything about it at all, and ate it the way she did everything, calmly, all of it. And Thea, who tasted it and put her spoon down and looked at him across the table and said, “You made this for thirty years? On your own?”

“Every winter.”

“Who ate it?”

He thought about it.

“Me,” he said. “After Alea went to bed. She says it’s—“

“A fire in a swamp,” said Alea.

Thea picked her spoon back up.

“Then we’ll eat it,” she said.

The pumpkin was very good. He’d known it would be. It was Diwa’s.

He told them that. He hadn’t meant to. Gigichi took a mouthful of it, after the stew, and closed her eyes and said oh, that’s not fair, that’s actually good, and Alea said it’s Mum’s, before he could say anything, and the table went quiet.

He looked at Alea. She was looking at the pumpkin.

“Honey and red pepper,” said Alea. “In the hearth till the skin goes black. He put pepper on pumpkin the first winter and she laughed at him and then ate half of it.” She swallowed. “And then she did it better than him every year after. He’s never got it as good as she did.”

“It’s close,” said Ars.

“It’s close,” Alea agreed.

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then Higu, beside Alea, reached out without looking and put her hand on Alea’s arm, and left it there. And PenguChi said, in her calm voice, “Thank you for letting us have it,” and Alea’s ears went flat, and then up, and she said you’re welcome very fast and went to get more bread.

And then they talked.

He didn’t know how it started. It just did, the way it had always started on calls, years ago, at two in the morning. Somebody said something, and somebody else answered, and then everybody was talking at once, and the table was too small and the room was too warm and nobody cared.

Higu started it. She put her spoon down and looked across the table at him and said, quite calmly:

“I saw you. In the spring. With a moth.”

“I know,” said Ars. “I saw the moth.”

“You did not.”

“I did. A moment too late. You’d already gone.” He looked at her. “You didn’t write it in the channel. You never wrote it anywhere. You told nobody.”

“I wrote it in my notebook,” said Higu. “Alea is his. He has a window.” She was quiet a moment. “I never showed anyone the page.”

“You banned a man,” said Ars, “with an apology poem. In my name.”

Higu stared at him.

“I what—“

“Still banned. Trust.” He’d read it in the Ice book, in the summer, and laughed so hard on the jetty that Alea came out to see if he was dying. “You wrote that, in the channel. In the summer. For Hako. I’d forgotten the poem. I’d forgotten I was the kind of person who’d write a man a poem and ban him.”

“He deserved it,” said Higu.

“He did,” said Ars. “He absolutely did.”

And Higu, calm Higu, who thought before she spoke and never laughed first, put her face in her hands and laughed until she couldn’t breathe.

PenguChi told him about Words on Stream. The first night Thea raided her, and he’d stayed, and played, and found words nobody had ever heard of, and chat said that’s not a word, and he’d said it’s Latin. trust.

“Was it Latin?” said PenguChi.

“Some of them.”

“The other ones?”

“I made them up,” said Ars. “The game was generous.”

PenguChi laughed. Slowly, the way she did everything, and then all at once.

Xola told him about the Apex tournament where they’d first played together, and how he’d died in every round and narrated it, and how she’d laughed so hard she’d fallen off her chair and missed the final circle, and they’d come second. “You owe me a first,” said Xola. “You owe me a whole tournament.”

“There’s one at midsummer,” said Ars.

Xola stared at him.

“In Valcrest,” said Ars. “Drew’s running it. The king said so. I read it.” He looked at her. “You could win that one.”

Thea didn’t say much.

He noticed. She sat across the table from him with her hands round her cup and listened to the others and laughed when they laughed, and watched him. The way she’d watched windows for a year. As if he were a structure she was trying to read all the way down.

And then, in a gap, when Xola had gone to look at the hens through the window and Gigichi was arguing with Alea about the sword, she said, very quietly, just to him:

“Was it you?”

He looked at her.

“The two hundred lines,” said Thea. “The times. In the channel. The night Dennis asked.” She held his eyes. “It was in the window’s own writing. Not mine. At the very bottom, where I couldn’t reach.” She swallowed. “Was it you?”

The table had gone quiet. He hadn’t noticed it go. Everybody was looking at him.

“Dennis had to post three times,” said Ars.

Nobody said anything.

“I’ve read it,” said Ars. “All of it. Since the night it opened. The first line. Althea: Can everyone see this?” He looked at his hands on the table. “Every night. On the jetty. Every line, before it went off the top. And then, after the two hundred, I could scroll back, and I read the ones I’d missed.” He was quiet a moment. “I should have told you. I didn’t know how. I’m sorry.”

“We know,” said Xola, from the window. Her voice was wobbling. “We worked it out. Three weeks ago.”

“I know,” said Ars. “Thea said thank you. At one in the morning.” He looked at Thea. “Whoever did the two hundred lines: thank you.”

Thea’s face did something complicated.

“You read that,” she said.

“I read that. And then I read Maigo. And Tiger. And Rachael, and Newish, and Hako, and Dennis asking about my rates.” He couldn’t help it; the corner of his mouth went up. “I sat on the end of the jetty and read half the kingdom tell nobody that they loved them, in the middle of the night. I didn’t sleep.”

Gigichi had gone white.

He saw it. He’d been waiting for it. She was sitting at the end of the bench nearest the fire with her arms round herself, and she’d gone completely still and completely white, and she was looking at the table.

He knew what she was thinking. He’d read it in the summer, in the Ice book, at the bottom of a page of other people’s stories. He sent me “you okay?” once, when nobody else noticed I’d gone quiet. I never answered. I’m answering now. I’m okay. Mostly.

He’d read it on the end of the jetty, and put his window down on his knees, and sat there for a long time.

“Chi 1,” he said.

“Don’t,” said Gigichi. Very fast. Not looking up. “Don’t you dare.”

He didn’t say anything.

The table was very quiet. Higu, beside Gigichi, didn’t move, but Ars saw her hand go to Gigichi’s back and rest there, very lightly.

He waited. The way he’d waited for fish for thirty years. The way he’d waited at the end of a lost round, once, holding an angle, for somebody to make up their mind.

“You asked me if I was okay,” said Gigichi, to the table. “Years ago. When nobody else noticed.”

“I did.”

“I never answered.”

“You did. In the summer. I read it.”

“That doesn’t count.” She looked up at him, finally, furiously, with her eyes streaming. “That doesn’t count. I wrote it to a dead person. I wrote it because I thought you’d never read it. That’s the opposite of answering.”

He looked at her.

“Then answer now,” he said.

Gigichi stared at him.

And then he did it. The thing he could see, on her face, she was most afraid of and most wanted. He looked at her, across his own table, in his own house, and asked her. Out loud. In English. Lowercase, the way he’d typed it at two in the morning years ago.

“You okay?”

Gigichi didn’t say anything for a long time.

The fire cracked. Outside, one of the hens shifted in the snow. Alea had stopped breathing at the end of the table.

“No,” said Gigichi. “I wasn’t. Then. When you asked.” She swallowed. “I’d gone quiet. I’d stopped answering people. I didn’t know how to say no, so I didn’t say anything, and then it was too late, and then you were dead.” Her voice cracked. “And this year I’ve been angry at Thea and sorry to Thea and less angry at Thea and I nearly broke a hedge and I’ve been frightened every single day of something, and today I felt sick all day because I thought you’d ask me that, and I didn’t know what to say.” She wiped her face with the back of her hand. “I’m not okay. Not all the way. I’m mostly okay. Sometimes I’m fine. Right now I’m—“ She stopped. She looked at him. “Right now I’m sitting at your table eating your terrible stew and you’re alive and you asked.”

She put her face in her hands.

“That’s the answer,” she said, through them. “It’s a mess. I’m sorry. That’s the answer.”

Ars looked at her for a long moment.

“That’s the best answer anyone’s ever given me,” he said.

Gigichi made a sound into her hands that might have been a laugh, and might have been a sob, and was probably both. Higu put her arm all the way round her. And Ars sat across the table and let himself, for once, not look away from somebody’s face while it did what it did.

He told them why.

They didn’t ask. Not one of them, all evening. He told them anyway, because they hadn’t asked, the way they hadn’t asked for sixteen months.

Kharvos, and the hunters. Halvard’s one target. Wanting them to grow on their own, without leaning on him. All of it. He told it plainly, the way he’d laid it out on the jetty the night before, one reason after another, and showed them each one, and showed them that none of them were true anymore.

“And the last one,” said Ars.

They waited.

“I was frightened,” he said.

Xola turned round from the window. “Of us?”

“Of what you’d see.” He looked at his hands. “You knew a boy who made you laugh. Who gifted you subs and narrated his own deaths and wrote poems in ban messages. I’m not him. Not all the way. I’ve been here thirty-four years. I’ve done things he’d never have done. I’ve lost things he never had.” He was quiet a moment. “I was frightened you’d open the door and look for him, and not find him, and your faces would do something I couldn’t stand to see.”

Nobody said anything.

Then Thea, across the table, said: “We didn’t look for him.”

He looked up.

“At the fire,” said Thea. “Last night. We didn’t look for him. We looked at you.” Her voice was very steady, for Thea. “We’ve been looking at you for sixteen months. At the market. On the road. At the gate. At the forge. We know what you are now. We worked it out on our own, all of it, at a table, in the summer. And we waited anyway.” She held his eyes. “We’re not here for the boy, Ice. We’re here because you asked us to supper.”

That was when they told him about the rules.

He didn’t know. That was the thing. He’d read every line in the channel for a year, and he didn’t know, because they’d never written them. Higu told him, quietly, with her arm still round Gigichi.

“Rule five,” said Higu. “We never write her name. Alea asked us, the week the channel started. In the yard. Not her name, not her dad, not the fish.” She looked at Alea, at the end of the table. “She stood in front of you. All year. Between you and all thirty-five of us. And we said yes, and we never did. Not once.”

Ars looked at his daughter.

Alea had gone very red again. She was looking at the floor.

“You never told me,” he said.

“It was mine to keep,” said Alea, to the floor. “You’d have told me not to.”

He didn’t say anything. He couldn’t.

“And rule six,” said Higu.

“There’s a rule six?”

“We never wrote it down,” said Higu. “It was said once. On a wall, in the summer, at two in the morning, the night the clock came. After we’d worked it out.” She looked at him with her calm half-lidded eyes. “We wait for him.”

He had to get up from the table.

He went to the window, where Xola had been, and stood there with his back to them all, looking out at the snow and the hens and the dark lake, for a while. Nobody said anything. Nobody followed him. They let him.

We wait for him.

Sixteen months. Five people at an upper window, every night, watching his lamp. Never once asking. Never once writing a word. Waiting, the way he’d taught Alea to wait for aura on the ice, and Alea had taught PenguChi. Waiting for the second tug.

He’d thought he was the one waiting. On the end of the jetty, every night, for a year, frightened of their faces. He’d thought he was the patient one.

They’d been better at it than him. They always were.

He stood at the window until he could trust his face. Then he went back to the table, and sat down, and nobody said anything about it, and Xola pushed the pumpkin toward him without a word.

Late, very late, when the stew was gone and the pumpkin was gone and Squiddles was asleep in his bowl and Waddles was asleep on Alea’s lap and the fire had burned down to red, Thea sang.

He didn’t ask her to. She just did, the way she used to on calls, years ago, at night, when he’d been playing a game for her, or with her, and she’d start humming, and then singing, quietly, half to herself, without stopping talking in between. Every night, for years, before school got busy. He’d forgotten. He hadn’t let himself remember.

She sang something from home. He knew it. He hadn’t heard it in thirty-four years. Quietly, at his table, in the warm, with her eyes on the fire and her hands round her cup, the way she’d sung it into a microphone half the world away, and he’d had his headset on and his game paused and his eyes shut.

He shut his eyes now.

Nobody talked over it. Not even Xola. Alea, at the end of the table with Waddles asleep in her lap, put her chin on her hand and listened, and her ears went soft.

He sat in his own house, in a body that had been born in a cottage on the other side of the world, with five people from home at his table, and listened to Thea sing. And the quiet came. And stayed.

He didn’t open his window. He didn’t need to.

It was nearly midnight when they went.

Thea knew because she checked. She always did. She stood on the step in the snow, last, the way she’d been last coming in, with her lantern relit and her coat buttoned, and the others already at the gate, past the three hens, who’d got up and were following PenguChi about the hen-yard like ducklings.

He was in the doorway. Where he’d been when it opened. With the gold light behind him and his sleeves still rolled and the cloth still, absurdly, over his shoulder; he’d forgotten it all night. Alea beside him with her arm through his.

“Come tomorrow,” he said. In English. He’d been speaking English all night; it was coming easier and easier, like a road you’d walked as a child. “Or the day after. Or whenever. The basket stays five.”

“Five,” said Thea.

“Five,” he said. “And bring the basket back. I want it.”

“He wants his basket,” said Alea.

Thea laughed. She couldn’t help it. She stood on the step and laughed, with her eyes streaming, at a man who’d killed a demon general and routed an invasion and been the Hero of the Redmarch for eight years, standing in his own doorway with a cloth on his shoulder asking for his basket back.

And then, out of habit, because she did it first thing every morning and last thing every night and had for sixteen months, she opened her window to check the time.

And stopped.

She’d scrolled past it without thinking. Down past the channel, past the time, to the very bottom, where she always looked last. The line most of the thirty-five had forgotten was there. The one she’d looked at every morning for a year and a half, like a photo on a lock screen.

And beside it, where it had always been, the sealed value. The one she couldn’t open. The door with no handle. The one that had rattled the first time a plain man walked past her on the market road, and slammed at the market, and gone still, just once, when she held a stone out to him on the shingle.

It wasn’t sealed.

Thea stood on the step in the snow and looked at it.

Three letters. In English. In the same plain writing as everything else in the window, the writing of the first window, his writing. Not rattling. Not still. Just there. Open. As if it had always been there, and had only been waiting for somebody to say it out loud first.

She didn’t understand, for a moment. And then she did.

She thought about the water butt behind the militia hall, sixteen months ago, in the first autumn. Alea and Thea, with their hands in the cold water, and Alea saying something Thea had never forgotten, about secrets. A secret belongs to everyone it would hurt if you told it.

He’d told it. Himself. At his own table. In his own voice. To the people it belonged to.

And the window had let it go.

“Thea?” said Higu, from the gate.

Thea turned the window round, with shaking hands, and held it out so that he could see, in the doorway. So that all of them could. Higu came back up the path, and Gigichi, and Xola, and PenguChi with three hens following her, and they stood round her on the step in the snow and read it, one after another.

Higu read it last. She didn’t say anything. She just looked at the three letters for a long time, and then up at him, in the doorway.

He was looking at it too. Thea watched his face. It was the first time she’d ever been able to hold on to it, she realised. All night, she’d been able to hold on to it. It didn’t slide anymore.

“It opened,” said Thea. “When you told us.”

He looked at the three letters in her window, his own name, his old one, in the writing of the first window in this world.

“Good,” he said quietly.

They walked back up the lake road in the snow.

Five lanterns, in a row, in the dark, the way they’d come down. Slower. Nobody talking much. Xola holding Higu’s hand. Gigichi holding Thea’s, which she hadn’t done since before the drifting, since before the bad year, since before any of it, and which neither of them mentioned. PenguChi on the end, with Waddles asleep in her coat.

At the top of the road, Rowan was still there.

Of course he was. The town had gone home hours ago; the square was empty, and dark, and full of snow, with the black ring of the bonfire in the middle of it. But Rowan Hale was standing at the top of the lake road in his mayor’s chain, alone, in the cold, with snow on his shoulders, the way he’d stood there for eight years. Waiting.

He looked at the five of them come up out of the dark with their lanterns. At their faces.

He didn’t say anything. He nodded. And then he stepped back into his place, at the top of the road, in front of it, and turned round to face the town, and folded his arms.

Nobody follows us down.

Except, now, five people.

Thea looked back once.

From the top of the road, by Rowan, before they turned the corner into the square. Down the long white lake road in the dark, past the fields and the long stone wall, to the end.

The lamp was in the window. Still lit. The way it had been every night for a year.

And in the doorway, small and far away and gold against the light, two figures. One with fox ears. One with a cloth on his shoulder. Standing there in the cold with the door open, letting the warmth out, watching five lanterns go up a road.

The smaller one lifted her hand and waved.

After a moment, so did the other.

Thea waved back. So did all of them. Five lanterns at the top of a road, swinging, in the snow, at midnight.

Then they turned the corner, and went up the hill.

In the upper room of the militia hall, much later, Higu sat down on her cot and picked up her notebook.

Thea watched her do it from the window. She’d left it there all evening. Closed. It was the first night in sixteen months she hadn’t carried it.

Higu opened it. Not to the lists. Not to the column of fish, or the column of lamplight, or the page in the middle on its own. To the very back. To a page where, Thea saw, something had been written in Higu’s small neat hand, a long time ago, near the top, and nothing else.

Rule six. We wait for him.

Higu looked at it for a long time.

Then she took her pencil, and drew one straight careful line through it. Not hard. Not so hard you couldn’t read it anymore. Just enough.

And under it, she wrote one word.

Thea leaned over and read it.

Done.

Higu closed the notebook. She put it on the shelf above her cot, and lay down, and pulled the blanket up, and was asleep, Thea thought, before her head was on the pillow. For the first time in sixteen months, Higu slept before the lamp at the end of the lake road went out.

It didn’t go out. Not that night.

Thea knew, because she stayed up and watched it until dawn.

End of Volume Three.
