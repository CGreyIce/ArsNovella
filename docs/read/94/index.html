The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 4: Threads

Chapter 94

Pairs

3,124 words · 14 min read

Hanarisu kept her hands busy.

She’d learned that in the first month, in the castle, after the Keeper. After the first time the charm came up in her and went through somebody before she knew what she was doing, and an old man who’d guarded the archive door for forty years stood aside for her with a look on his face like a man walking in his sleep. She’d gone back to her room afterwards and sat on the bed and shaken for an hour.

Her magic had two halves. Everybody said so. Oswin, who took notes on everything; the Advisor, who’d looked at her window in the first week and said charm, in both senses. One half went into people. The other went into things. Little things, made with your hands: a knot, a bead, a twist of ribbon. Charms, the old kind, the kind grandmothers made for babies.

The first half frightened her. So she kept her hands busy with the second.

She’d been doing it for a year and a half. Every evening, in the rooms above the archive, while the others talked, while Milli read and Jared sat by the door and Cursiffe wrote in his ledger and Aleaka lay on the rug with a cat. Knots. Beads from the bead-seller in the Lantern Quarter, who gave her a good price because she said his name. Spare ribbon from the castle stores, that Ama brought up with the hot water in the mornings without being asked. She knotted cords, and threaded beads, and wove little hearts out of ribbon, the size of her thumbnail, the way she’d made things on Earth on stream, with her hands, while she talked.

They never did anything. They were just things. She gave them away. Ama had one. Pellam had three; he kept them on the handle of his broom. Hew on the archive door had one tied to his sword-belt that he touched, sometimes, when he thought nobody was looking.

None of them did anything. She knew that. She’d have felt it if they had.

She made them anyway. It kept the other half quiet.

Winter in the capital was grey.

Not white, the way the channel said it was in the north, where Thea and the others were buried to their windowsills. Not white like the Redmarch, where Yan said the snow had stayed for weeks. Grey. Rain, mostly, and sleet, and the river brown and fast below the castle walls, and the long gallery cold enough that Pellam swept it in his coat.

Kestra had been back since the autumn. She drilled Jared and Milli in the mornings, in the courtyard, in the rain, the way she always had. Again, to Milli. Again. And Milli, who’d learned to wait in the first month, waited, and Kestra said better, and then again anyway, because she said that was what better was for.

But she was different.

Hana watched her. She always had. She’d learned to read Kestra the way Kestra read everyone: feet first. And Kestra’s feet, since she came back from the north with the king and a cold and the songs are worse than the man, kept turning. Not much. In the courtyard, between drills. At the window of the long gallery. At dinner, in her chair. Very slightly, without seeming to know they were doing it, they turned north.

Hana had never asked where she’d been. She’d decided, on the morning the king rode out with a roll of hammers, that she wouldn’t. It isn’t mine. It’s his.

She watched Kestra’s feet anyway.

Oswin had found something.

He told them at dinner, three weeks into the winter, with ink on his face and dust in his hair and the black key on its string round his neck, the way he’d looked every evening since the autumn. He’d been in the vault every night. Five weeks, then ten. He’d read four hundred years.

“There’s a door,” he said.

They looked at him.

“At the back,” said Oswin. “Behind the last shelf. I didn’t see it for weeks; the shelf was in front of it. I moved the shelf to get at a box that had fallen behind, and there it was.” He held his hands up, a little way apart. “Small. Iron. About so big. A door in the wall, like a cupboard. With a seal on it.”

“Whose seal?” said Cursiffe.

“Nobody’s,” said Oswin. “Nobody alive. It’s a king’s seal, but it’s three dynasties old. A king called Aldemar. He’s in the records; he died three hundred and twelve years ago. Nobody’s had his seal since. Nobody can.” He turned his cup round on the table. “The vault’s got two seals on the outer door, the king’s and the Advisor’s. This has a third. Inside. On its own. On a door that somebody hid behind a shelf.”

“What’s behind it?” said Aleaka.

“I don’t know,” said Oswin. “I didn’t open it.”

They all looked at him. Oswin, who’d been told to leave it three times and hadn’t. Oswin, who’d run up the archive stairs at midnight in the autumn with the Advisor’s last page in his hand.

“I went to the Advisor,” said Oswin. “This morning. I asked him.” He was quiet. “He looked at me for a long time. And then he said, That one needs the king. And the king needs a reason.” Oswin looked down at his hands. “So I’m leaving it. For now.” He looked up. “I’ve never left anything in my life. It’s very strange. I keep wanting to go back down and look at it.”

“Do you think it’s about the summonings?” said Milli. Very calmly. Too calmly. Hana heard it, and put her hand on his knee under the table.

“I think,” said Oswin, “that somebody, three hundred years ago, put something in a cupboard in the summoning archive and sealed it with a seal nobody would ever have again, and then hid the cupboard behind a shelf.” He looked at Milli. “I think that’s the kind of thing you do with the answer to a question you don’t want anybody to ask.”

Nobody said anything.

Milli’s hand, on the table, had gone very still. Hana felt it, under her own.

The way home. Nobody said it. They didn’t need to. Milli had been saying it since the hall: it’s a plan. It’s a real plan. And somewhere under the castle, behind a shelf, behind a seal three hundred years old, there might be a door with the next piece of the plan behind it.

And it was shut.

Hana went up the tier on the night of the first frost.

She didn’t mean to. She hadn’t been trying; she’d stopped trying, months ago, after Thea’s letter and the edge and one small line in its language, because she’d tried every line she could think of and none of them had done anything. She’d been at ninety-nine for three weeks. She’d decided, sometime in the second week, that she’d go up when she went up, and stopped looking.

She was making a pair.

It was for Milli. And for her. She’d been meaning to make it for a year and never had, because it was the kind of thing that felt silly once you thought about it, and she’d thought about it. A cord, knotted, with one bead in the middle, a purple one from the bead-seller’s best tray. One long cord, knotted once, all the way along, with the bead in the middle. And then cut in half, straight through the bead, with the little knife Jared had given her, so that each half had half a bead on the end. One half for her. One half for him.

They’d done things like that on Earth. Matching things. Silly things. Honey things. Milli pretended he hated it. He wore every one.

She sat on the end of the bed with the two halves of the cord in her lap, and the halves of the bead, and looked at them.

They looked lonely.

That was the thing. That was what she’d remember, afterwards. Not anything clever. She’d cut the cord in half, the way she’d meant to, and sat there looking at the two halves in her lap, and they looked lonely. The way a cord looks when you’ve cut it, and it’s two things now instead of one, and both halves still remember being one thing.

She felt it. Not with her hands. With the other half. The half she kept quiet. The half that went into people.

It went into the cord.

Not much. A thread of it. She felt it go, the way you feel warmth go out of your hand into a cup when you hold it. Into the half of the cord in her left hand. And through it, somehow, along a line that wasn’t there, into the other half, in her right hand. As if the two halves were still one cord, really, and only looked like two.

She didn’t decide to. She found the edge the way you find a step in the dark with your foot, and there it was, at the bottom of the thread, where Thea had always said it would be.

She wrote one word. It was what the cord wanted. It was what she wanted, too, about most things.

Remember.

The halves warmed.

Both of them. In her two hands. Not hot. Warm, the way a hand is warm when someone’s been holding it. And she felt them know each other. That was the only word for it. Two halves of a cord, a hand’s width apart in her lap, knowing each other, the way two people who’ve been married a year know where the other one is in a dark house.

She opened her window. Her hands were shaking.

The fifteenth. Of thirty-five.

The first of Group 2.

She sat on the end of the bed and looked at it for a long time, with the two halves of the cord warm in her hands. Keepsake. Not Charm. Not anything that went into people. The world had looked at her, sitting on a bed with two halves of a cut cord, and given her the half of her magic she wasn’t afraid of.

She started crying. She didn’t mind. Nobody was there.

Milli was in the long gallery, reading by the big window. She could have gone and found him. She didn’t. She wanted to know if it worked.

She went to the door of their room and called down the passage. “Honey?”

“Mm?” From the gallery. Far off.

“Catch.”

She threw it. Not to him; he was round two corners and down a passage. Down the passage, just, so that it landed on the floor outside the gallery door, half a cord with half a purple bead on the end. She heard Milli’s chair scrape. Heard him get up, and come to the door, and stop.

“What’s this?”

“Pick it up.”

A pause.

“Honey, what’s—“

“Pick it up. Hold it. Don’t say anything.”

Another pause. Longer. She stood in the doorway of their room with her own half of the cord in her hand and her heart going much too fast, and waited.

And felt it.

It warmed. In her hand. All at once, the way a cup warms when somebody pours tea into it. And through it, along the line that wasn’t there, faint, the shape of a hand. His hand. Holding the other half. Round two corners and down a passage, in the cold grey light of the long gallery. She could feel exactly where it was.

“Oh,” said Milli. From the far end of the passage. In a voice she’d heard him use only twice in his life. Once at their wedding. Once in the summoning hall, when he’d woken up and found her beside him.

“It’s warm,” said Milli. “Honey. It’s warm.”

“I know,” said Hana. And laughed, and cried, and went down the passage to him.

She made pairs all winter after that.

She couldn’t stop. It was the most fun she’d had in a year and a half. She sat on the end of the bed every evening with the bead-seller’s tray and Ama’s ribbon and Jared’s little knife, and made cords, and knotted them, and cut them in half, and wrote Remember into the cut, and gave them away.

One for Aleaka, who held her half and cried and then made Hana give the other half to Brisk, the furious hawk, who let Hana tie it round his leg and then bit her anyway. Aleaka said she could feel where Brisk was all the time now, when he was out over the city. She said it was the best present anyone had ever given her, and then she said it was the second best, and wouldn’t say what the first was.

One for Jared and Cursiffe. Jared wore his on his wrist. Cursiffe wrote on his, very small, in blue ink, a word he wouldn’t tell anyone. They said they could feel each other across the castle. Jared said it was mostly useful for knowing when Cursiffe had fallen asleep in the archive again.

One for Pellam, and his wife, who lived in the town below the castle and whom he hadn’t been able to see much since the king doubled the guard. He held his half on the handle of his broom and smiled at the floor all day.

One for Ama, and her grandson in the river watch.

One for Hew on the archive door, and his sister in Kingsferry, which Hana sent with a rider, and which arrived, Hew said, a fortnight later; and the morning it arrived he stood at the archive door with his hand on his sword-belt and his face doing something he couldn’t stop.

She made them for everyone. Everyone who had someone a long way off.

And then she made one more.

She didn’t tell anyone. She did it late, on her own, on the end of the bed, when Milli was asleep. The best cord she had. Purple, the colour of her hair. And instead of a bead, in the middle, she threaded on a tiny charm she’d been keeping since the first month: a little gold heart, the size of a fingernail, like the one on her choker. She’d found it in the market and bought it and never known what for.

She knotted the cord. She cut it in half. She cut the heart in half too, very carefully, with Jared’s knife, so that each half of the cord had half a gold heart on the end.

She wrote Remember into the cut.

And then she sat there and looked at it, the two halves warm in her hands, knowing each other.

She didn’t know where the other half was going. She didn’t know where he was. She’d decided she wouldn’t. It isn’t mine. It’s his. All she knew was a direction, and that only from the way somebody else’s feet turned.

But she knew who did know.

She found Kestra in the courtyard at dawn, before drills, in the rain.

Kestra was standing by the wall in her plain dark coat, with her sword on her hip and her little black book in her pocket, the way she stood every morning, waiting for Jared and Milli. She saw Hana come across the wet cobbles and didn’t move. She just watched her feet. Then her hands. Then her face.

Hana held it out. Half a purple cord with half a gold heart on the end.

Kestra looked at it.

“I’m not going to ask where,” said Hana. “I said I wouldn’t. I won’t.” She held it out a little further. “But if you ever go north again. For any reason. If you ever stand in front of him again.” She swallowed. “Give him this half. That’s all. You don’t have to say anything. You don’t have to tell me.” She looked at the half-heart in her palm. “I’ll keep the other one. And if it ever gets warm, I’ll know.”

Kestra didn’t take it.

She stood there in the rain by the courtyard wall and looked at it for a very long time. At the purple cord. At the half of a little gold heart. And then at Hana’s face, the way she’d looked at it at Vane’s banquet, more than a year ago, the night Hana let the charm rise and put it back down in a room full of people who’d never have known.

“You didn’t use it,” said Kestra. “On me. You could have. Just now. To find out where.”

“I know,” said Hana.

“You’d have found out.”

“I know.”

Kestra looked at her a moment longer. Then she reached out, and took the half-cord out of Hana’s hand, very carefully, the way you’d take something that might break. She looked at it in her own palm. Then she opened her little black book, and laid it inside, between two pages near the end, and closed the book, and put it back in her coat.

“If I’m told to,” said Kestra.

Hana looked at her.

“And if you’re not?”

Kestra was quiet for a moment. Her feet, on the wet cobbles, Hana noticed, had turned. Very slightly. North.

“Then perhaps anyway,” said Kestra.

Jared and Milli came out a moment later, and drills started, and Kestra said again to Milli in exactly the same voice as always, and nothing in her face had changed at all.

But Hana stood at the edge of the courtyard in the rain with her own half of the cord in her hand, the purple one with half a gold heart, and held it, and waited.

It didn’t get warm. Of course it didn’t. The other half was in a book, in a coat, on a woman in the same courtyard, and books didn’t hold things.

She didn’t mind. She’d known it wouldn’t. Not yet.

She tied it round her wrist, under her sleeve, where she’d feel it if it ever changed. And went in out of the rain, and up the archive stairs, past Hew at the door, who touched the cord on his sword-belt and smiled at her, and past Pellam in the long gallery, who had his on his broom.

Remember, she’d written. Into every one.

She thought it was the right word. For a winter. For a castle full of people with someone a long way off. For thirty-five people from another world, and the one more, wherever he was.

She thought it was the only word, really.
