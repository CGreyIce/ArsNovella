The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 4: Threads

Chapter 98

Waiting at Duskhollow

3,393 words · 15 min read

Xav was good at waiting.

He hadn’t always been. On Earth, at seventeen, at a polytechnic in Singapore, he’d been the opposite: the one who went first, who pushed, who got bored halfway through anything and ran at it. Declan had cured him of that. Not by telling him. By sitting beside him, a thousand late nights, so patient that everybody else forgot he was there, and then, every single time, when they finally moved, already looking at exactly the place they were moving to.

You just have to wait longer than them, Declan had said, once, on a call, when Xav asked how. That’s all it is. Everyone gets bored. You just get bored last.

And then, under his breath, at the last moment, something in Latin that he never translated.

Petra had finished the job here. Twenty minutes standing still in the yard behind Mother Annick’s. Then forty. Then two hours, in the cold, with her watching from the gate, until Xav could stand in front of anyone in the world and wait them out. Better, she’d said. And then, the morning he went up a tier, with one stroke that landed half a heartbeat before he did: That’s how he moved.

He’d been waiting on the quay at Duskhollow for nine days.

He went down every morning at dawn.

Deston’s letter had said when the river opens. The river had opened ten days ago. Xav had felt the ice go out from his bed at Mother Annick’s at three in the morning: the long groan of it, and the crack, and the roar, like the whole town breathing out. He’d lain there in the dark listening to it, and thought: two weeks from Kingsferry, maybe three, with the current against them. And got up anyway, and gone down to the quay, and stood there in the grey light, looking south down the river.

He’d stood there every morning since. An hour, two. Then gone back up the hill to drill with Petra, and to the guild hall, and to the bricked door below the first ring with John. And come back down at dusk, and stood there again.

Nobody asked him what he was doing. They knew. All four of them. And Petra, who stood at the top of the quay steps most mornings with her arms folded, watching him wait, and didn’t say anything at all.

He didn’t mind. He’d been waiting three years. A few more weeks wasn’t anything.

Garrick had come round, in the end. Sort of.

He’d said no in the autumn, in the guild hall, and meant it. I need you here. I need John’s eyes. I need Azac’s shields at the top of that stair. And then Petra had gone to talk to him. And then Petra had gone to talk to Harrow, the guild master, which nobody did if they could help it. Xav didn’t know what she’d said. He’d seen Harrow come out of her office afterwards looking like a woman who’d been told something she didn’t want to hear by someone she couldn’t argue with.

The answer, in the end, had been: go.

Garrick was staying. He’d said so in the first week of the thaw, over supper at Mother Annick’s, in his raid-leader’s voice, as if he were assigning positions. “Somebody has to keep the light company. Harrow’s got half the guild on rotation at that door, and none of them know what it looks like when it changes.” He’d looked at John. “You’ll teach Fenn what to watch for before you go. Two weeks. Every morning.”

“I will,” said John.

“And you’ll come back,” said Garrick. “All five.” He looked round the table at them. “I’ve led a lot of parties into the Hollow. I always counted them back out. I’ll be counting.”

“We’ll come back,” said Xav.

Garrick looked at him for a long time, the way he’d looked at him in the autumn. Then he nodded, once, and went back to his stew.

Petra was coming.

She’d told them the same night, after Garrick had gone up to bed. She’d been sitting at the end of the table, the way she always sat, very quiet, with her sword across her knees. And when the table had gone quiet too, she’d said, without looking up:

“I’m coming with you.”

They all looked at her.

“Upriver,” said Petra. “When the others come for you. I’ve told Harrow. I’ve told Garrick.” She turned her cup round on the table. “Somebody has to watch your backs, and Garrick can’t, and I’m better than whatever sword Harrow would send.” She was quiet a moment. “And I’d like to see who taught him.”

She didn’t look at Xav when she said it. She didn’t have to.

He’d never told her. None of them had. Not Declan, not Ice, not the Hero of the Redmarch. Not the faded thread or the lake country or anything Deston had written. Petra had stood at the gate every morning for a year and a half and watched Xav fight like a man she’d seen once, for less than a minute, from a ditch on the Redmarch, eight years ago. She’d told him he’d had two teachers. She’d asked him, in the first week: where did you learn to fight like that? And he’d said a friend, at home, and she’d said where is he?, and he’d said he died.

She’d never asked again.

She’d just watched. And waited. Petra was good at waiting too.

“Okay,” said Xav.

The barge came on the ninth morning.

He saw the sail before he understood what he was seeing. He was standing on the quay in the grey dawn with his hands in his coat pockets, looking south down the river the way he’d looked every morning for nine days, at the brown water and the low green banks and the line of the hills. And far off, at the very end of the long straight reach below Duskhollow, where the river came round a bend out of the south, there was a sail.

Coming north.

That was wrong. Xav knew that much about rivers. Barges didn’t sail north in spring. The wind was off the lakes, out of the north, every year, for a month, two; it had been in his face every morning on this quay. Barges came up the Wren in spring on poles and towropes, a mile a day if they were lucky, with men on the towpath leaning into the ropes.

This one was sailing. Sail full. Bellied forward. Coming up the long reach against the current and against the wind at a speed no barge on the Wren had any right to, with the brown water curling white at her bow.

And on the mast, on the crosstree, there was somebody sitting. Very small at this distance. Waving both arms.

Xav stood very still on the quay.

Then he took his hands out of his pockets.

The whole town came down.

It happened fast. The way things happen in a small town when something strange is coming up the river. A fisherman on the next jetty saw it and shouted. Somebody in the chandler’s came out and stared. By the time the barge was halfway up the long reach, there were fifty people on the quay, and by the time it came round the last bend below the town, there were two hundred, and most of them were pointing.

At the sail, full the wrong way. At the water round the hull, rippling north while everywhere else on the river the wind was ruffling it south. At the young man on the crosstree with his arms round the mast and his face split in half.

At the people on the deck.

Xav knew them before he could see their faces. He’d known them since he was seventeen.

Gabe, in the stern, enormous, unmistakable, with his blade across his back. Deston at the bow. Standing at the very front of the barge with one hand on the rail and the other out over the water, flat, palm down, pointing. Not north. Not anymore.

Pointing at Xav.

He didn’t remember going down the quay steps. He was just at the bottom of them, on the landing stage, at the edge of the water, with the barge coming in. John beside him. Malcolm. Azac with his shield on his back out of habit. Rui Xiang, who’d been there a moment ago and wasn’t now, and then was again, standing just behind Xav’s shoulder with a grin on his face, because Rui Xiang had started doing that since he went up a tier, and thought it was hilarious.

The barge came in. The sail came down. An old man at the tiller roared something at two boys on the poles, and they threw ropes, and somebody on the landing stage caught them.

Deston was over the rail before the barge stopped moving.

He jumped. Onto the landing stage. Nearly fell. Caught himself on a bollard. And then he was standing in front of Xav, close, a pace away, breathing hard, with his face doing something Xav had never once seen it do in all the years since they were seventeen, with Declan between them.

Deston didn’t say anything. He held out his hand.

Xav took it.

And felt it. Not with his eyes. He couldn’t see threads; he’d never been able to. But he felt something. A tug. In the middle of his chest. The same tug he’d felt, faintly, every evening for a year and a half, standing still at the end of something he couldn’t see, and the squeeze that came back down it. The same squeeze he’d felt in the spring, when Deston was on a roof in Kingsferry and Xav was standing at a vigil by an open seal, waiting for an expedition that didn’t come back.

Deston squeezed his hand.

“I felt you waiting,” said Deston. “The whole way. Nine days. Every morning.”

“I’m good at waiting,” said Xav.

“I know,” said Deston. His voice cracked. “I know you are.”

Then everybody.

Gabe came off the barge like a wall coming down and put his arms round Xav and lifted him off the landing stage, the way he’d lifted him years ago after the first match the three of them ever won together, and put him down again, and didn’t let go. Rachael, from the deck, waving, laughing, calling Xav! in a voice that made half the quay turn round. Dennis behind her, very well dressed, carrying a ledger. Calvin coming down the mast so fast he slid the last ten feet and landed on the deck in a heap, shouting DID YOU SEE IT, DID YOU SEE THE SAIL.

And Group 6, on the landing stage, all at once. John and Deston gripping each other’s arms. Malcolm, who never touched anyone, and Rachael, who didn’t either, standing in front of each other on the landing stage and nodding, very seriously, the way two people nod when they’ve understood each other perfectly, while Malcolm’s face did something complicated. Azac and Gabe, the two biggest men on the river, slapping each other on the back so hard it echoed off the warehouses. Rui Xiang appearing behind Dennis, hush, and saying boo, and Dennis screaming.

The two hundred people of Duskhollow on the quay above watched all of it in total silence.

Ten of the thirty-five. On one landing stage. For the first time since a ferry town on a river road, a year and a half ago, when Deston saw gold threads for the first time and told Xav, and Xav said write to me when you know what it is.

He’d written. He’d known.

They stayed one night.

Mother Annick fed all of them. Thirteen people round a table built for six, and Haskett and his boys at the door, and Mother Annick going round and round with the pot, saying nothing, filling bowls. Emrys sang. Not well; his voice was still cracked from the night market. But he sang, a river song, and Mother Annick stopped going round with the pot and stood at the end of the table with her ladle and listened.

Pryce came. The young Honed swordsman who’d climbed back alone out of the Hollow in the spring, the only one of twelve. He’d been living at Mother Annick’s all winter. He sat at the end of the bench and didn’t say much, and when they were all getting up to go to bed, he caught Malcolm’s sleeve.

“Your book,” said Pryce. “The IF and THEN.”

Malcolm looked at him.

“Write one for upriver,” said Pryce. “Write all the bad ones down. The way you did before Orla went down.” He swallowed. “Write them all down, so they’re on paper and not in your head. And then go anyway.”

Malcolm looked at him for a long time.

“I already have,” he said. “Eleven pages.”

“Good,” said Pryce. And let go of his sleeve.

Xav and Deston sat up on the roof of Mother Annick’s after everyone else had gone to bed.

Not a roof, really. A flat bit of leads over the kitchen, behind the chimney, where you could see the river. Like the roof above the chandler’s in Kingsferry, Deston said. They sat there in the cold with their backs against the warm chimney stack and looked north up the river, at the dark.

“Tell me,” said Xav.

Deston told him.

All of it. From the beginning. The way Deston told everything, in order, with his hands open in his lap. The faded thread in the spring, colourless and old, from his own chest, as thick as Gabe’s, running north. Past Xav. Who do you know up north? Nobody. The tremble, when they hummed the song and left the gap. The summer. The night of the sum. And the thread getting warmer, after that. And warmer. And warmer still, every day of the last nine, every mile up the river.

“I should have told you in the spring,” said Deston. “I’m sorry. I couldn’t. I’d lost him once already. In a phone call. I couldn’t let myself hope about something nobody else could see.”

“I know,” said Xav.

“You’re not angry.”

Xav thought about it. Honestly. The way Declan would have wanted him to.

“No,” he said. “I’d have done the same. I did do the same. Petra told me, in the summer, about the Black Blade saying things under his breath before he moved. Words in no language anyone knew.” He looked at the dark river. “I knew what that was. The second she said it. And I wrote it to you and Gabe, and nobody else. Not the channel. Not Group 6, for a week.” He was quiet. “We all did the same thing, Deston. We all held onto it. Because we’d lost him once.”

Deston didn’t say anything for a long time.

“It’s warm,” he said at last. “Right now. Up there. Warmer than it’s ever been.” He put his hand out over the edge of the roof, flat, palm down, pointing north. “It’s like it knows we’re coming.”

“He doesn’t know,” said Xav.

“No.”

“He’d have said something. In the channel. If he knew.” Xav looked at the thread he couldn’t see. “He’s reading it. He has to be. Hana’s let them. The two hundred lines, the night Dennis asked. Somebody’s reading every word. But nobody’s written Groups 4 and 6 are coming upriver. Not once. Not even a hint.”

“No.”

“So he doesn’t know,” said Xav. And then, very slightly, at the corner of his mouth, something that might have been a smile. “Good. Let him find out the way I always found out where he was. When he’s already looking at the wrong door.”

Deston laughed. It came out wet.

They left at dawn.

Garrick came down to the landing stage. And Harrow, the guild master, which Xav hadn’t expected, standing at the top of the quay steps with her arms folded and her face like a woman who’d been talked into something and wasn’t going to say so. And Pryce. And Mother Annick, with a bag of bread.

Petra came down the steps last, with a pack on her back and her sword on her hip, and stopped in front of Garrick.

“Bring them back,” said Garrick.

“I’ll bring them back,” said Petra.

“All five.”

“All five,” said Petra. “And the others.” She looked at the barge, at Group 4 on the deck, at the old man at the tiller, at the young man already halfway up the mast. “I’ll count them.”

Garrick looked at her for a moment. Then he held out his hand, the way raid leaders do at the start of a run, and she gripped it, forearm to forearm, and let go, and stepped onto the barge without looking back.

Xav watched her go to the bow and sit down beside Sabine, two Reaching swordswomen, both with their swords across their knees, and look at each other, once, the way two cats look at each other in a doorway. Neither said anything. They both turned and looked at the banks.

He thought that was probably the start of a friendship.

The sail went up. Calvin on the crosstree said something to the air that Xav didn’t hear. The sail filled forward, the wrong way, against the wind and the current, and the Patient Widow leaned into the brown water and went. North. Up the river. Away from Duskhollow, past the last houses, past the guild hall on its hill with the Hollow’s arch behind it, black against the dawn.

John was standing at the stern.

Xav saw him go still. The way John went still when he saw light change before something came round a corner. He was looking back at the town. At the guild hall. At the arch of the Hollow, getting smaller.

“John,” said Xav.

John didn’t answer. He was staring at the Hollow. His face had gone white.

“It flared,” he said. Very quietly. “Just now. The light. Under the bricks.” He swallowed. “White. For a heartbeat. The whole door. Like it did in the spring, at the bottom of the stair, before eleven people walked into it.” He turned his head and looked at Xav. “Like the summoning hall.”

Nobody said anything.

Malcolm already had his book out. He was writing, very fast, standing on the deck of the barge with the wind of Calvin’s tailwind pulling at the pages.

“Garrick will see it,” said Xav. “He’s watching. Fenn’s watching. Harrow’s got half the guild on that door.”

“I know,” said John.

“We can’t go back.”

“I know,” said John. He looked at the Hollow a moment longer, getting smaller behind them, black against the morning. “I’m just telling you what I saw.”

Malcolm finished writing. He looked at what he’d written for a long time. Then he turned the book round and showed it to Xav, the way he always did.

IF the circle under the Hollow flared the morning we left, THEN somebody noticed we were going.

And under it, smaller:

THEN we don’t stop, and we don’t go anywhere alone.

Xav went and stood at the bow.

Deston was there. With his hand out over the water, flat, palm down. Pointing north. Up the long brown river, between the green banks, toward the hills, and past the hills, toward a country of lakes Xav had never seen, and a man he’d buried.

Xav stood beside him and looked north too.

He’d waited three years. He’d waited at seventeen, night after night, beside a man who never moved first. He’d waited in the yard behind Mother Annick’s for two hours in the cold until Petra said better. He’d waited on this quay for nine days.

He could wait a little longer.

He found, standing at the bow of a salt barge going upriver against the wind, that he was saying something under his breath. He hadn’t known he was going to. He didn’t know where it came from. Something he’d heard on a call, years ago, at the last moment before Declan moved, in a language Declan had never once translated.

He didn’t know what it meant.

He thought he’d ask.
