Xav had been fighting a man who wasn’t there for eleven days.

Every morning. On the end of the town quay at Duskhollow, before the light, before Petra’s class, before anybody else in the town was up except the baker and the river. With a stick. A plain straight length of ash he’d cut from the hedge behind Mother Annick’s on the first morning home, about the length of a practice sword and the weight of nothing. He stood on the end of the quay with the stick held low, the way Declan held a driftwood stick on a shingle beach, and looked at the empty air in front of him where a man would be standing if a man were standing there.

And waited for him to move.

He never did. He wasn’t there. That was the point. Xav stood on the quay in the grey with the river going past brown and fast below him and the mist coming off it in long rags, and looked at nobody, and waited for nobody to decide, and moved in the breath before nobody decided.

Once.

And then, in his head, the man answered. The way the man had answered on the shingle, in the mist, on the twelfth morning, when Xav had stepped sideways in the breath before and for the first time in anybody’s memory the man had had to move twice. He saw it. Every morning. He’d seen it so many times he could have drawn it: the driftwood stick coming round, unhurried, already where Xav was going to be, and Xav already not there, and the stick coming round again.

Twice.

Once more than anyone, the man had said, afterwards, on the shingle, with the mist lifting. Ever. Nobody’s ever made me move twice. Not the general.

And then Xav had said okay, again, and the man had laughed, and they’d done it again, every morning, for the rest of the spring. And it had never once been three.

He stood on the quay with the stick, and moved, and saw the stick come round, and moved again, and saw it come round again. And then stopped. Every morning. In the same place. Because he didn’t know what the third one was.

He didn’t know what it looked like. He didn’t know where it came from. He’d thought about it for two months, on the road south and in Valcrest and on the road north and on a barge going down a fast brown river, and he’d thought about it the way he thought about everything, slowly and from every side, until he could see the whole shape of it. And he couldn’t see this one.

He knew the first move. It was his. You moved while the other man was still deciding.

He knew the second. It was the man’s. The answer.

The third had to be something neither of them had decided.

And Xav had never in his life done anything he hadn’t decided.

They’d come in on the ninety-fourth day, in the mist, off the Patient Widow.

Garrick had been on the quay. Of course he had. He’d been on the quay at Duskhollow every morning since the spring, he said, counting, because he’d said he’d stay and watch the Hollow while the others went upriver and he’d meant it. He was a big grey man of fifty-one with twenty years of the Hollow’s deep rings in his face and a raid leader’s way of standing, with his weight on both feet and his eyes on everything. He stood at the end of the landing stage with his arms folded and watched them come off the barge, one at a time, and counted them out loud. One. Two. Three. Four. Five. And then, Petra. And then he unfolded his arms.

“Well,” said Garrick. “You’re all here.”

“We’re all here,” said Xav.

Garrick looked at him for a long moment, the way he’d looked at him in the first month when they’d argued about who called the shots, before he’d worked out that Xav was usually right. Then he looked away, at the river, at the barge pulling out again with Gabe in the stern waving, and cleared his throat.

“I’ve been counting,” he said. “Every day. The Hollow. Four months.” He was quiet. “Nothing came up. Not once. Not a sound. The light under the bricked door’s gone the colour of nothing and stayed there.” He looked back at Xav. “It’s waiting, I think.”

“For what?”

“I don’t know,” said Garrick. “I was hoping you would.”

Xav thought about it. Then he reached into his pack and took out the thing he’d been carrying for twenty-six days, since a room above a tavern in Valcrest. A short knotted cord, purple and gold, with half a bead on the end and a strip of thin blue paper wound round the knot. He held it out.

“From Hanarisu,” he said. “For you. She made one for every guide. Write on it and hold it, and she’ll hold hers, and the words go.” He paused. “It works now. It didn’t, quite. Thea fixed it. Last night. On a jetty.”

Garrick took it. He looked at it in his big scarred hand for a long time.

“Words go,” he said. “From here. To the capital.”

“In a heartbeat.”

Garrick turned it over. Then he put it, very carefully, in the inside pocket of his coat, against his chest, and buttoned the pocket.

“Twenty years,” he said, “I led parties into the Hollow with no way to tell anyone on the surface we’d died.” He didn’t say anything else. He didn’t need to.

Mother Annick fed all six of them that night at the long table in her kitchen, the way she’d fed thirteen in the spring, with her sleeves rolled up and her face red from the oven and her voice going the whole time. Too thin. You’ve all gone too thin. What did they feed you in Valcrest, paper? And they ate everything she put down, and she put down more, and old Pryce at the end of the table, who’d been a Hollow-runner forty years ago and lodged at Mother Annick’s for the rest of his life, told Malcolm to write all the bad ones down, the way he always did. And then go anyway.

And Malcolm, who’d been writing all the bad ones down since the first week, said I know, and opened his book.

Xav saw the page.

He didn’t mean to. He was sitting next to Malcolm on the bench, and Malcolm had the book open on the table between his plate and the jug, and Xav’s eyes went to it the way his eyes went to everything: the hands first, then the feet, then the whole thing. And the book was open at a clean page. New. Near the back.

At the top of it, in Malcolm’s small square capitals, the way he started every page:

IF HE COMES BACK FOR THE TENTH, THEN

And under it, nothing.

Xav looked at it for a long time.

He knew what it meant. Everybody in Group 6 knew. They’d been on the horse trough in the yard of the Broken Lance in Valcrest, or near enough, the night Declan had looked at Gabe and Deston and Xav in a row and said next year. The tenth. The anniversary. I want to walk it with you, Gabe had said, once, just once. And Declan had said next year. To the three of them.

To the three of them.

Malcolm saw him looking. He didn’t shut the book. He just put his pen down on the page, across the blank space under THEN, and looked at it.

“I don’t know what goes after it,” said Malcolm.

Xav didn’t say anything.

“I’ve been trying for a month.” Malcolm turned the pen over. “Every night. IF he comes back for the tenth. And then I sit there.” He looked at the page. “He said you three. Gabe and Dest and you. On the trough. I heard him.” He was quiet. “He didn’t say us.”

“He didn’t say not you.”

“No.” Malcolm picked up the pen again and put it down. “That’s the problem. I can’t write a THEN for a thing nobody’s said.”

Across the table, John had stopped eating. And RX, beside John, who’d been talking very fast for the last quarter of an hour about Valcrest and the Lists and the king and an arm-wrestle, had stopped talking, which RX never did. And Azac, at the end, the biggest of the five, with his shield on the floor beside his chair out of habit and the old white scar down the side of his face from the Hollow, had put his fork down.

They were all looking at the page.

“I’d want to go,” said John. Quietly. “To the grave. If he’d let us.” He looked at his hands. “I was in the corner of his stream for a year. Every day. He’d say that’s John, he lives here now.” He swallowed. “I’d want to be in the corner of that, too. Just once. If he’d let me.”

“I’d go invisible,” said RX. “He wouldn’t even know I was there.”

“He’d know,” said Xav.

“He’d know,” RX agreed, slightly deflated. “He always knows. He knew in Wrenhaven. You don’t click the circle.” He looked at the page. “I’d go anyway.”

Azac didn’t say anything. He was looking at the page. At the blank under THEN. He looked at it for a long time, with his big hands flat on the table either side of his plate.

Then he reached over, slowly, and took Malcolm’s pen.

And wrote, under THEN, in his own big round untidy hand, two words.

WE ASK.

He put the pen down.

Nobody said anything for a while. Mother Annick, at the oven, had stopped clattering. Old Pryce had stopped chewing.

“That’s not a plan,” said Malcolm, eventually. Not unkindly.

“It’s the only one that doesn’t go wrong,” said Azac.

Malcolm looked at it. Then he looked at Azac. Then, after a long moment, he picked the pen back up, and underneath Azac’s big untidy capitals, in his own small square ones, he wrote: NEXT SUMMER. TOGETHER. AND IF HE SAYS NO, THEN WE STAND AT THE TOP OF THE ROAD.

He shut the book.

“Pass the bread,” said RX, very fast, a little thickly, and Mother Annick passed it.

Petra’s class was on the gravel bar.

It was below the town, where the river bent round under the quay and dropped a long low shelf of grey pebbles and sand along the inside of the bend, and the water went past it shallow and quick and very clear. In the spring, before the barge came, Petra had run her dawn class in the guild yard, on the packed earth, with wooden swords and a list. She didn’t any more.

“He taught on a beach,” she said, the first morning, when Malcolm asked. She’d stood at the top of the gravel bar in the grey light with her sword on and her arms folded and looked at the river. “Stones under your feet. Water in front. Nothing to lean on.” She was quiet. “I didn’t understand why, for a week. Then I did.”

“Why?” said John.

“Because you can’t stand still on stones,” said Petra. “Not really. They move. You have to keep deciding where your feet are.” She looked at the five of them in a row on the gravel. “And because if you fall over, it’s only the river.”

She ran it the way he’d run it. Xav saw that on the first morning, and every morning after. The same order. The same quiet. Watch the heron: there was one, on the far bank, most mornings, the way there’d been one on the lake. Watch the whole thing. And at the end, the rule: anyone could try her, once a morning, with a practice sword, and she’d answer with a driftwood stick she’d carried all the way from the shingle at Lake Ilse in the bottom of her pack. The same stick. His. She’d picked it up off the stones on his last morning on the shore and never put it down.

She was very good.

That was the thing. Xav had known she was good since the first month, when she’d killed a thing in the Hollow with Reaching aura that had split Azac’s shield like paper. But she’d been good the way a sword is good: sharp, straight, fast, waiting for you to come. Nine years she’d waited. She’d been the best waiter Xav had ever seen except one.

She didn’t wait now.

She’d learned the breath before. On the shingle, on the ninth day, from the man with the driftwood stick, who’d said Xav says you never move first and Good. I’ll teach you when to. And she’d moved first against Xav on the ninth morning, before he’d decided, and the man had nodded at her from the end of the jetty, once. And she’d never stopped.

Every morning on the gravel bar, somebody tried her. Malcolm, with a practice sword he held like a pen. RX, who hushed, and vanished, and came at her from nowhere, and met the driftwood stick in the place where nowhere ended. Azac, with his shield, who she tapped round it. John, who was a mage and shouldn’t have been trying at all and tried anyway, because he said Declan would have.

Every morning, one move. Every morning, they lost.

And then Xav.

She wanted to beat him. Xav knew that. Everybody knew it. Declan had known it, in Valcrest, on the horse trough: she’s got you. You’re the one she wants to beat.

They’d been fighting each other every dawn for two years. In the guild yard, at first, when she was teaching him to wait, and then on the shingle with the man watching, and now here, on the gravel. And for two years it had gone the same way. She waited, and he waited, and they stood there, and somebody moved, and usually it was a draw, a touch each, a stick and a sword crossing in the air between them and stopping a finger short.

Since the shingle, it had been different. She moved first now. In the breath before. And he moved in the breath before that, sideways, the way he’d moved to make the man move twice. And she answered. And it was a draw again, but a faster one, a better one, two people moving at the very front edge of each other’s deciding like two people stepping onto the same stone in a river from opposite sides.

Two moves each. Every morning. And then the stick and the sword stopping a finger apart, and Petra saying again, and Xav saying okay.

Never three.

John streamed it.

That was new. That was the thing that changed everything, in the end, though nobody knew it yet.

He’d started on the third morning. He’d been sitting on the gravel at the edge of the bar with his knees up, watching, the way the mages in the class mostly watched, and his three little motes of light had been going round his head the way they always did. And Xav, on the bar, facing Petra in the grey with the practice sword low, had seen John’s face change. Seen him sit up. Seen him lift one hand off his knee and put a finger to the air, the way they all did, and write.

Look.

And a square of gold light had opened over the river.

About the size of a door. Hanging in the air over the shallows at the end of the gravel bar, soft and steady, the way it had hung over the parapet of the Broken Lance in Valcrest on the night of the threadless. With a picture in it.

Them.

The gravel bar, from above. From the height of a man on another man’s shoulders, a little to the side, the way a bird would see it, or a mote of light. Xav and Petra, facing each other on the stones in the grey. Small, and clear, and both of them very still. And the river going past, and the heron on the far bank, and the whole of the class in a row on the edge of the bar with their faces turned up, looking at the square of light and seeing themselves in it.

“Oh,” said Malcolm.

“That’s us,” said RX. “That’s—John, that’s us. From above.”

John was grinning.

Xav had never seen a grin like it. Not on John. John had a kind tired steady face, the face of a man who’d been at ninety-nine percent for two and a half months and never complained, and Xav had known him eleven years and seen him smile perhaps a hundred times, quietly, sideways, as if he didn’t want to take up too much room. This wasn’t that. This went all the way across his face and stayed there.

“It’s a replay,” said John. “It’s—we can watch it back. Like a demo. Like a VOD.” He was laughing. “Xav. Xav, you can watch your own feet.”

Xav looked up at the square of light over the river. At himself in it, very small, on the gravel, with the practice sword low. Looking up at himself.

He looked at his own feet.

He’d never seen them. Not from outside. Not while he was doing it. Two years of standing on shingle and gravel and packed earth deciding where his feet went, and he’d never once seen where they actually were. He looked at them now, in a square of John’s light, and saw that the left one was turned in, very slightly, a finger’s width, toward Petra. Always. Every time. A tell. A thing he’d never known he did.

“Huh,” said Xav.

Petra was looking up at the square too. At herself. Her face, which was quiet and intense and hard to impress, did something Xav had seen it do perhaps twice.

“I lean,” said Petra. Very quietly. “Before I move. I lean.” She looked down at her own feet on the gravel, and then back up at the square. “Nine years. I never knew I leaned.”

“Everybody’s got something,” said John. Still grinning. “He used to say that. On stream. Every time somebody died to the same thing three times. Everybody’s got something. Go watch your demo.”

After that, John streamed every morning.

He couldn’t stop. That was the thing. Xav watched him not be able to stop. He’d sit on the edge of the gravel bar with his knees up and his three motes going out over the class, one high, one low, one at the side, and the gold square over the river showing whatever the motes saw, switching between them the way a stream switches cameras. And the class would fight, and then stop, and turn round, and watch it back, and John would hold the square still on a moment, like a paused frame, so everyone could look. There. Your shoulder. Watch your shoulder.

And he grinned the whole time. The whole time. For eleven days. Like a man who’d been trying to do one thing for two years and had finally done it, and couldn’t believe it, and was going to do it every day for the rest of his life in case it went away.

“You’re going to sprain your face,” said RX, on the sixth morning.

“I know,” said John, grinning.

RX tried to break it, of course. On the seventh morning he hushed, the way he did, and vanished off the gravel bar entirely, and came at Petra from nowhere. And Petra met him with the driftwood stick in the place where nowhere ended, the way she always did. And then everyone turned round to watch it back in John’s square of light.

And RX was in it.

Not hushed. Not invisible. There, in the gold light, from above, plain as day: RX, crouched, creeping across the gravel toward Petra with his practice sword low and his tongue between his teeth and an expression of intense concentration, looking, from directly above, exactly like a man trying to sneak up on a cat.

The class lost it.

Malcolm sat down on the gravel. Petra put her hand over her face. Azac made a sound like a bear coughing. And RX stood on the gravel bar with his mouth open, looking up at himself creeping across the stones in John’s light, very slowly, with his tongue out.

“That’s cheating,” said RX.

“That’s the stream,” said John.

“Unseen means unseen. It’s in the name.”

“Not from above, apparently,” said John, and grinned so hard that RX gave up and started laughing too, and hushed out of sheer embarrassment, and nobody could find him for twenty minutes, except John, who could.

Xav watched his own feet every morning.

He fixed the left one. It took four days. Four days of standing on the gravel bar and making his left foot point where he wanted it to point instead of a finger’s width toward whoever he was facing, and going back to the square of John’s light afterwards and checking. On the fifth day it was straight.

And he watched the rest.

He watched himself move in the breath before. From above. Sideways. He’d felt it a thousand times and never seen it. He watched Petra answer. He watched the practice sword and the driftwood stick come round and cross a finger apart in the air between them, two moves each, every morning, and stop.

And he looked at the space where the third one wasn’t.

That was what the square of light showed him, in the end, on the tenth morning, held still on a frame by a grinning man on the edge of a gravel bar. Not the third move. The place where it should have been. Him and Petra, a finger apart, frozen in John’s light. Both of them stopped. Both of them having decided to stop.

He’d decided to stop.

Every morning. He saw it now. Not had to. Decided. At the end of the second move, at the exact moment when the next thing would have had to come from somewhere he hadn’t planned, he stopped. Very neatly. Very sensibly. The way a careful man stops at the edge of a drop. And in the square of light, from above, it was so obvious that he wondered how he’d never seen it.

The third had to be something neither of them had decided.

He’d thought that meant he had to decide something cleverer. Something the other man hadn’t thought of.

It didn’t. It meant he had to stop deciding.

He’d never in his life done anything he hadn’t decided.

He did it on the eleventh morning.

He didn’t plan to. That was the point; you couldn’t plan to. He’d lain awake the night before at Mother Annick’s with Azac snoring across the room and tried to plan not to plan, and it had been the stupidest night of his life, and he’d got up at the fourth hour and gone down to the quay with the ash stick and fought nobody for an hour and not known what he was doing.

Then Petra’s class.

The gravel bar in the grey. The river high and quick from rain upstream. The heron on the far bank. The five of Group 6 along the edge of the stones: Malcolm with his book on his knees, RX not hushing for once, Azac with his shield on his back. And John at the far end, where the bar ran out into the shallows, sitting on a big flat stone at the water’s edge with his knees up and his eyes shut, because he’d found he could hold the square steadier with his eyes shut and see what the motes saw better. His three motes up over the bar. The gold square hanging over the river beside him, showing the gravel from above.

Petra stood at the top of the bar with the driftwood stick.

“Xavier,” she said.

He went down onto the stones with the practice sword.

They stood. The way they’d stood every morning for two years. Ten paces apart, on the gravel, in the grey, with the river going past. She had the stick low. He had the sword low. And they waited.

She moved first. In the breath before. The way the man had taught her.

He moved in the breath before that. Sideways. Left foot straight.

She answered. The stick came round, fast, already where he was going, the way it always did.

He moved again. Away. The second move. And he saw the stick come round again, the way it always did, and felt the end of the second move coming, the edge of the drop, the place where he stopped.

And didn’t decide.

He’d never be able to say what he did.

He tried, afterwards, to the others, and to himself on the quay for weeks, and to Gabe in the channel in the middle of the night, and to the man himself, a year later, on the end of a jetty. He never got it right. It wasn’t a thing you could say, because it wasn’t a thing he’d done. Not on purpose. He’d got to the edge of the second move and not stopped and not decided anything at all, and something underneath him had moved instead. Lower than thinking. It’s lower, PenguChi had said once, on the shingle, about the breath before. It’s under the thinking.

This was under that.

He felt his body go somewhere he hadn’t sent it. Not sideways. Not away. In. Into the space between the stick and Petra’s hand, where nobody in their right mind would go, where he’d have said, if he’d been deciding, there was no room. And there was room. There was exactly room. And he was in it, with the practice sword coming up in a line he’d never once in his life practised, before he knew he was doing it.

Petra had to move a third time.

He saw her see it. He was close enough. Her face, a hand’s width from his, and her eyes going wide, the way they’d never gone wide in two years of dawns. Because she’d decided her answer to his second move. And he’d done a third that nobody had decided, not even him. And there was nothing she’d planned for it.

So her body answered on its own.

The way his had. From under the thinking. With everything it had.

And everything Petra’s body had was years of Reaching.

He felt it go past him.

Not the stick. The stick came round and met his practice sword and stopped, a finger from his ribs, the way it always did. But something came off the stick. Past the end of it. The thing Reaching meant, the thing that made a Reaching-tier swordswoman what she was: aura going out beyond the edge of the blade, a hand’s length, an arm’s length, a hard invisible edge cutting the air a pace past the steel. Petra had held it in for two years of dawn classes, every morning, so carefully that Xav had forgotten she had it.

She didn’t hold it in now. She hadn’t decided to. Nothing about this had been decided.

It went past Xav’s shoulder. He felt it. A cold line in the air, like a draught through a door, very fast, very thin, a hand’s breadth from his ear. Out past him, across the gravel bar, low and flat and invisible, toward the water.

Toward the far end of the bar. Where the stones ran out into the shallows.

Where John was sitting on a flat stone with his knees up and his eyes shut, holding a square of gold light over the river, seeing everything from above and nothing in front of him at all.

Xav saw it in the square of light.

That was the worst of it. That was the thing he’d see at night for a long time afterwards. He was standing on the gravel with Petra’s stick a finger from his ribs, and he turned his head, and he saw it, not with his eyes, but in John’s light, from above, the way the mote saw it. The gravel bar from above. Him and Petra, frozen, in the middle. And a line going out from the end of Petra’s stick across the stones toward the water, faint, a ripple in the air like heat over a road. Fast. Straight. At the small figure on the flat stone at the end, with its knees up and its eyes shut.

Twenty paces. Fifteen.

Nobody could get there. Xav saw that too. In the light, from above, he saw everybody and where they were and how far. Petra, at his side, her face gone white, already knowing. RX on the edge of the bar, ten paces off, starting to move, too late. Malcolm with his book. Azac.

Azac, on the edge of the gravel, with his shield on his back. Twenty paces from John. Further than anyone.

He couldn’t get there either. He was the biggest of the five and the slowest, and he had a shield on his back and twenty paces of loose stones between him and his friend, and Xav saw him, in the square of light, from above, see that. See that he couldn’t get there with his body. That for the first time in his life, the spot was somewhere he couldn’t stand.

And Xav saw him lift his hand.

Don’t be a wall, the man had said to Azac. On a shingle beach. With his open hand against the shield. Walls break. Be a jetty post.

Azac didn’t run.

He put one finger to the air, the way they all did, the way Ina wrote catch and Winter had written still and John had written look. Big and slow and untidy, the way Azac wrote everything. Twenty paces from John, on the edge of a gravel bar, with no time.

One word.

Post.

And at the end of the bar, between the line in the air and the flat stone where John sat with his eyes shut, something came up out of the gravel.

Xav saw it in the light first and then with his eyes. A post. That was all it was. A pale upright of light, about as thick as a man’s leg and as tall as a man, standing straight up out of the stones at the water’s edge, a pace in front of John, the way a mooring post stands up out of a quay. Not a wall. Not a shield. Nothing round, nothing wide. A single post, driven into the ground, between John and the river and everything coming at him.

Petra’s edge hit it.

Xav heard it. A sound like a stone dropped on an anvil. And he saw, in John’s square of light from above, the line in the air meet the post and stop. Not bounce. Not break. Stop. And go down. Through the post, into the ground, the way the man’s open hand had gone through Azac’s shield on the shingle and down into the stones. The way force goes into a jetty post and through it and into the lake bed and is gone.

The gravel round the foot of the post jumped.

All of it, in a ring, a hand wide, a perfect circle of grey pebbles leaping off the ground at once and falling back, rattling, like hail on a roof.

Not an inch back.

And on the flat stone behind it, John opened his eyes.

Nobody moved for a long time.

The square of gold light was still hanging over the river. John was still holding it. He sat on his stone with his eyes open now, very wide, and looked at the post standing up out of the gravel a pace in front of him, pale, upright, about the height of a man, with the ring of pebbles round its foot still settling. And then up at the square of light, which showed him the same thing from above.

The post went out. Softly. The way a lamp goes out when somebody turns the wick down. It was there, and then it was a faint shimmer in the air, and then it was nothing, and the stones at the water’s edge were just stones again, with a ring in them a hand wide.

Azac was on his knees on the edge of the gravel bar.

Xav didn’t see him go down. He was just down. On his knees on the stones with one hand still up in the air, the finger still out, and his big scarred face gone completely white. And in front of his face, in the grey light, plain and white and steady, his window had opened.

Xav was close enough to read it. Everybody was.

[ Azac · Tier 1: Spark · Skills: Ward ]

Azac looked at it.

He looked at it for a long time. On his knees. With his hand still up.

“Oh,” said Azac. Very quietly. In his big slow voice. “Oh. There you are.”

Petra got to John first.

She’d dropped the stick. Xav didn’t see her drop it. He saw it lying on the gravel at his feet, the driftwood stick she’d carried from a lake, and Petra already gone, running, across the bar, faster than he’d ever seen her move, to the flat stone at the end. She stopped in front of John. She didn’t touch him. She stood there with her hands open at her sides and her face white and her chest going, and looked at him.

“Are you hurt,” said Petra.

“No,” said John.

“Are you hurt.”

“No,” said John. He was looking at her. Then at the ring in the stones. Then up at the square of light, still hanging there, which he hadn’t let go of; he’d forgotten it was there. “I didn’t see it. I had my eyes shut. I was looking at you from above.” He swallowed. “I didn’t see anything coming at all.”

Petra sat down on the gravel.

Not carefully. All at once. The way Halvard had sat down on the step of a tier in the Lists, Xav would hear later. She sat down on the stones in front of John with her knees up and put her face in her hands.

“I didn’t decide,” said Petra, through her hands. “I didn’t decide to. It just came. I’ve never—nine years, I’ve never once let it out when I didn’t mean to.” Her voice cracked. “I didn’t decide.”

“I know,” said Xav.

He’d come up behind her. He didn’t know when he’d walked over. He was standing on the gravel beside her with the practice sword hanging from his hand, looking down at the ring in the stones.

“I didn’t decide either,” he said.

Petra took her hands away from her face and looked up at him.

“That was three,” said Xav.

They watched it back.

Of course they did. John held it. He sat on his stone with his hands shaking and his face doing six things at once and played it back in the square of gold light over the river, from above, slowly, the whole thing. The five of them and Petra and Garrick, who’d come down the quay steps at a run at the sound like a stone on an anvil, standing on the gravel in a row and watching.

Petra moving first. Xav moving before that. Petra answering. Xav moving again. And then the third.

Xav watched himself do it.

He didn’t recognise it. That was the strange thing. He watched a man in a square of light, from above, step in, into a space between a stick and a hand where there was no room, and there was room, and he didn’t know who it was. It didn’t move like him. It didn’t move like anyone. It moved the way water moves into a gap in a wall: not deciding, just going where it could.

“Again,” said Malcolm, very quietly. “John. Again. Just that bit.”

John played it again.

The third move. Petra’s face, a hand’s width away, eyes wide. The line going out from the stick. The post coming up out of the gravel. The ring of stones.

“Again,” said RX.

John played it again. And again. And they stood on the gravel bar in the grey with the river going past and watched it eleven times, the way you watch the last round of a match you can’t believe you won, and nobody said anything, and John grinned, and grinned, with his face wet, the whole time.

Azac posted it that night.

He didn’t want to. He said so, at Mother Annick’s long table, with his window open and his big fingers hovering. It’s not mine. It’s John’s. I just put a post in the way. And Malcolm said that’s what a ward is, Azac. That’s the whole thing. And John said put it up or I will. And RX said I’ll put it up for you and I’ll spell it wrong. So Azac put it up.

[ 21:04 · Azac: Spark. Ward. ]

And then, after a long time, under it, slowly, with one finger, the way he wrote everything:

[ 21:06 · Azac: It was a post. He said be a jetty post. So I was a post. ]

Xav watched the channel go white.

Maigo first, of course, in capitals. TWENTY-SIX. Then Chris: 26 of 35. Updating. Then Gabe, from Kingsferry: AZAC!!!! Then Hyvred, from Thornwall: Twenty-six. Then Thea: A post. Of course it was a post. Then everyone.

And Niko, from the wall at Cinderhold, very late: nice one. again.

And nothing from the man. Of course not. There never was.

But Deston, from the roof in Kingsferry, an hour later, when the channel had gone quiet: His thread went bright for a second. All the way from the fort. Just for a second. Like somebody looking up from a book.

Azac read that one for a long time.

Then he closed his window, and sat at the end of Mother Annick’s long table with his big hands flat on the wood, either side of his plate, and his face doing something Xav had never seen it do in eleven years. And Mother Annick, at the oven, put down another plate in front of him without being asked, and Azac ate it, every bite.

Xav went down to the quay before dawn the next morning with the ash stick.

He stood on the end of it in the grey with the river going past brown and fast and the mist coming off it in long rags, and held the stick low, and looked at the empty air in front of him where a man would be standing if a man were standing there.

He didn’t move.

He stood there for a long time. He thought about the third move. About a space between a stick and a hand that wasn’t there, and was. About Petra’s face a hand’s width from his, with her eyes wide, and her body answering on its own with seven years of Reaching. About a line in the air going past his shoulder toward a man with his eyes shut.

He thought about the man on the shingle, saying once more than anyone. Ever. Not the general.

Nobody’s ever made me move twice.

He thought he understood that now. He hadn’t, on the shingle. He’d thought the man meant nobody had been good enough. That wasn’t it. Or it wasn’t all of it.

Nobody had ever made him move twice, Xav thought, because the last time anything had made that man move without deciding, nine years ago, on a rise, against a general, he’d moved from under the thinking with everything he had. And a plain had gone red.

The third move wasn’t a move. It was a door. And you didn’t open it on somebody who couldn’t catch what came through.

Xav stood on the quay and looked at the empty air where nobody was.

There was one person in two worlds who could catch it. He’d caught everything Xav had ever thrown at him for nine years, in two worlds, in a server at two in the morning and on a shingle beach at dawn. He’d catch this. He’d move a third time, and whatever came through the door would come through, and he’d catch it, the way a jetty post catches the lake.

Next year, Xav thought. On the jetty. For the tenth.

He lowered the stick.

Behind him, on the quay steps, he heard someone come down. Petra. With the driftwood stick under her arm and her face still a little pale from yesterday. She stopped at the top of the steps and looked at him standing on the end of the quay, not moving.

“You’re not practising,” she said.

“No.”

“What are you doing?”

Xav thought about it.

“Waiting,” he said.

Petra looked at him for a long moment. Then she came down the steps and stood beside him on the end of the quay, with the stick under her arm, and looked at the river too, and didn’t say anything.

They stood there until the sun came up. Two people who’d learned to wait from the same man, a long way apart, and had both, in the last month, learned to move first, and had both, yesterday, moved third without deciding to.

“Again?” said Petra, eventually. At the gravel bar, when the class came down. When John sat on his flat stone at the end of it, a little further back from the water than yesterday, with Azac standing beside him, not quite in front, with his shield on his back and his hand loose at his side, ready.

“Okay,” said Xav. “Again.”

Two moves. Every morning.

Not three. Not here.

He’d save the third for somebody who could catch it.
