The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 9: The Other Front

Chapter 246

Last Plank

5,596 words · 24 min read

The hen saw him first.

He knew she would. Odette had said so, on the shingle, with the bird in her apron like a loaf: I’ll put you in the window that looks down the lake road and you’ll see him come over the hill before the girl’s even said it’s him. And Alea was a hill ahead, the way she always was, and he watched her come up onto the last rise above the lake in the afternoon light and stop, with her ears forward, and look down; and he was still two hundred paces behind her on the road with the water skins on his shoulder when, a mile off and below, in the town on its slope, in the upstairs window of the mayor’s house that looked down the lake road, a small red shape stood up on its cushion.

He couldn’t see it from there. Nobody could have. He felt it the way you feel somebody look at you across a room.

He laughed. Out loud, on the road, so that Rui Xiang behind him said what, and he said nothing, and kept walking.

By the time he came over the rise himself, Alea had already turned round and was waiting for him at the top, the way she’d waited at the top of this hill last autumn. Not saying come on. Just there.

“She’s in the window,” said Alea. “The hen. I can see her from here.”

“I know.”

“She’s standing.”

“She saw me,” he said. “Before you did.”

Alea looked at him for a long time with her ears all the way up.

“That’s not fair,” she said.

“Odette said she would.”

“Odette says a lot of things.” And then, because she was her mother’s daughter and couldn’t help it: “She was right, though.”

The lake was there.

He stood at the top of the last hill and looked at it, the way he’d looked at it from this hill every time he’d come home for thirteen years, and it was the same, and it wasn’t. Grey going to gold in the late light. The far shore with its trees beginning to turn. The town on its slope with the smoke going up. And below the town, at the bottom of the shingle, a cabin, very small, with a jetty going out from it, thirty-four planks, and a lamp on the end of it that somebody had lit an hour early because somebody had seen them on the road.

And the hedge. On the shelf above the shingle. Green. With the white flowers closing along the top in the evening. And in the gap that faced the lake road, built into the briar where the gap had been, something new: a bench. A low seat of woven green and white, grown, not made, facing up the road toward the hill where he was standing.

There was somebody on it.

He tapped his fingertips together. Three times.

Well, then.

He went down.

The town came out. It always did, when he came home, though it pretended not to; the egg-woman on her step with her boys, Robin at the front, waving both arms; the chandler’s girl; Edric in his bakehouse door in his flour. Old soldiers with their hands on their chests. He didn’t look away from them. He’d stopped doing that.

Rowan was at the top of the shingle with two cups.

The same cups. The brown glazed ones from the potter in the square. He’d had them on the table in his kitchen, facing the door, for forty-eight days; Ars knew that without being told, because Rowan’s hands, holding them up, had the look of hands that had set two cups out every morning and put them away every night for seven weeks. Rowan didn’t say anything. He held them up.

“Both of us,” said Ars.

“Both of you.” Rowan lowered them. He looked past Ars, at Alea, coming down the shingle with her sword on her back and her face burnt from the road; and then at the eight behind her, the Kingsferry boys and the Duskhollow lot and the woman with the stick. He looked at them all for a long time. “You came back with the same number you went with.”

“I usually do.”

“You’d be surprised how often people don’t,” said Rowan, who’d kept a girl of eight behind his own door through the worst month of a war, and knew. “Did you tell her? The step. The cloth. The hens.”

“Alea did,” said Ars. “She told her the year. All of it. She told her the hens.”

Rowan stood there with the cups in his hands.

“Good,” he said, after a while, gruffly, and turned round, and went back up the shingle toward the town. Ars watched him go. Halfway up, Rowan stopped, and stood for a moment with his back to them, and put one of the cups in his coat pocket, carefully, and went on with the other in his hand.

The hen came down the lake road at a run.

Odette had let her out. Of course she had. She came down out of the town and along the lake road and down the shingle in the lurching waddle that was all a hen had for running, wings half open, making a sound like a door that needed oiling; and went straight past Alea, and straight past him, without looking at either of them, and out along the jetty. Thirty-four planks. To the end. And sat down on the last plank, by the lamp, in the place where he sat, facing the water.

And looked back at him over her shoulder with one eye.

“She’s saying you’re late,” said Alea.

“She’s a hen.”

“She’s saying it,” said Alea, and went up the slope to the cabin, laughing.

He didn’t go out to the end of the jetty. Not then.

He went into the cabin first. He always did. He’d always done it, coming home: put the rod by the door where it stood, and the skins on their peg, and his pack on the bed, and then stood in the middle of the one room and let it be home again before he did anything else.

The sword was on the wall.

Grey-silver, laced with frost, the length of a man, on two iron pegs over the table. Over a drawing in blue crayon of a jetty with forty-one planks. Beside the drawing, an empty nail where a list had been.

He looked at it for a long time.

It didn’t do anything. It was a sword. But he could feel it, the way he’d been able to feel it from across the room the morning he left: the patience. It had waited for him under sailcloth in a smith’s yard for fifty-eight years and in a hearth by a lake for an afternoon, and it was waiting now, on its pegs, the way the hen waited on the last plank. Not cross about it. Just there.

“Not that day,” he told it.

He’d said it on the way out. He said it on the way in. He wondered, standing there, how many times he’d say it, and to how many things, before there was a day it wasn’t true.

He took the list out of his coat. From between the drawing and Halvard’s letter, against his ribs, where it had been for forty-eight days. Higu’s neat pencil, every word on it crossed through except one. He unfolded it and hung it back on its nail beside the drawing, under the sword.

He hung it with the back showing.

He didn’t know why he did that either. The front was the old list, the done list, Twelve dinners and Gabe and East and the rest, crossed through in his own hand, and Fish at the bottom. The back was blank. Alea had turned it over on Oakes’s table at Cinderhold and put the pencil on it and said next year and I’ll wait.

She hadn’t written anything yet.

He looked at the blank side of it, hanging on the nail beside a child’s drawing under a sword, and found he didn’t mind at all.

The quay was in the morning.

Haskett had brought the Patient Widow up the Wren in the last week with the autumn flour, Dennis’s barge, the one that had taken Alea down the river in the spring; he’d come up the lake road on the second evening with his hat in his hands and said Master Dennis says I’m to take whoever wants taking, on his account, and looked at Ars with an expression of deep and lasting suspicion, because he’d brought a fisherman and a woman and a small fox girl up this river once, thirteen years ago, and never forgotten the fisherman, and never been able to say why.

So they went down to the mill quay in the morning, all of them, the way they’d gone down in the spring for Alea. Group 1 from the militia hall. Maren with her list. Bram, big and grey, with a clerk’s ruler in his belt next to his sword, which he’d stopped explaining to anybody. The town on the quay steps. And the eight who were going: Gabe and Deston, home to Kingsferry; Xav and the four from Duskhollow, and Petra, home to the gravel bar.

He carried the water down. Somebody had to. Four skins, full, from the pump in the square, for the barge.

Haskett’s boys took them off him at the plank without being asked, with the expression of boys who’d been told by their father exactly who this was and still didn’t believe it.

Gabe had his hand in his pocket.

Ars had watched him do it for a month. Across the lake country and the downs and the grey grass and the red. At the grave. At the twelfth dinner. On the road home, every day, walking second behind Alea, with his big hand going to his right-hand coat pocket every few hundred paces and closing on something there and staying closed, the way you’d hold a stone you’d picked up off a beach and couldn’t put down.

He knew what it was. He’d known since the fourth day at the lake, when he struck and the king held and Gabe tapped there with the little cross-peen, and the steel moved, and a sliver of the end that didn’t want to be part of the point came away and dropped hissing into the ash at the side of the hearth. He’d felt it go. He hadn’t looked. He’d been looking at the blade, the way you do.

And that night he’d felt somebody go back to the hearth in the dark with the tongs and rake through the cold ash for a long time, and find it, and stand there holding it.

He hadn’t said anything. He didn’t say anything now.

Gabe came along the quay to him with the sledge over his shoulder in its sling and stood in front of him, short and square and big as a door, and didn’t say anything either. His hand was in his pocket.

“Kingsferry,” said Ars.

“Harl’s,” said Gabe. “For the winter. He wrote. He says the forge is cold without me and he’s had to do his own bellows.” He paused. “I’ve got a thing I want to make.”

“Good.”

“A small thing.”

“The small ones are hardest,” said Ars.

Gabe looked at him for a long moment. And Ars saw him realise, the way you see a man realise a thing all at once, that Ars knew. Exactly what was in the pocket. Exactly where it had come from. And that he wasn’t going to ask, and wasn’t going to say, and was never going to say, because it was Gabe’s.

Gabe’s face did something complicated.

Then he took his hand out of his pocket, empty, and put it on Ars’s shoulder, the big black-nailed smith’s hand, and held it there for a count of three, the way the king had held his on the shingle.

“Sleep,” said Gabe. “Seriously.”

Ars looked at him.

It was the last thing Gabe had ever said to him on Earth. On a call, at three in the morning, with Yan and Mashi and Newish still on. Sleep, Declan. Seriously. And he’d laughed and said okay and left the call and gone to sleep and not woken up in that world, and woken up in this one as a baby in a house behind a field, and been thirty-four years getting back to the man who said it.

Gabe had never said it again. Not once. Not on the jetty, not at the lake, not in two years of saying everything else.

He’d said it now. On a quay. With his hand on Ars’s shoulder and his face doing the complicated thing, and his voice perfectly steady.

“I will,” said Ars.

“You won’t,” said Gabe. “You never did. You’ll be on the end of that jetty at four in the morning.” He took his hand away. “But I said it. So.”

He went up the plank onto the barge without looking back.

Deston didn’t say anything. He put his arms round Ars on the quay, the way he had at the top of the lake road the first spring, and held on for a long time, and hummed. Very quietly. Under his breath. A low note, the one Ars was always humming along to the lake with his mouth shut without knowing; and Ars heard it, and found he was humming it back.

Then Deston let go, and grinned, and went up the plank.

Xav shook his hand. Just that. Then didn’t let go of it for a while.

“I’ll come in the spring,” said Xav. “To the shingle. With the stick.”

“She’s got the stick.”

“Then I’ll bring her,” said Xav, and let go, and went.

The four from Duskhollow went up the plank in a clump, the way they did everything, Malcolm with his book, Azac with Malcolm’s pack as well as his own, John with a little gold light already up on the end of his finger because he couldn’t stand on a quay without filming it. Rui Xiang went last of the four, and stopped at the top of the plank, and turned round, and pointed at Ars with both hands.

“Ditch,” said Rui Xiang. “In the spring. Best of five. Bring the shepherd’s cards.”

“They’re the shepherd’s.”

“I’ll buy him new ones,” said Rui Xiang, and disappeared, entirely, at the top of the plank, so that one of Haskett’s boys dropped a rope.

Petra was last.

She came along the quay with her sword on her hip and the stick under her arm, very straight, the way she walked everywhere. She stopped in front of him.

She could see his face. He knew that. She’d been able to since the ditch, since the moment on the red when she turned round with her sword shaking and said I can see you. Nobody under the sixth tier could hold it, Halvard had told his daughter once, in the snow. Nobody knew why. She was the sixth now. She’d looked at his face every day for three weeks on the road west, quite openly, the way you’d look at a hill you’d been told about all your life and finally walked up; and she’d never once said what she saw, and nobody had asked her.

She looked at it now.

“Goodbye, girl from the ditch,” he said.

She didn’t smile. Petra almost never smiled. But something happened at the corner of her mouth.

“I’m not her any more,” said Petra.

“No?”

“No.” She shifted the stick under her arm. “I’m the one who stood in front of you.”

He looked at her for a long time.

“Yes,” he said. “You are.”

She nodded, once. As if that settled it. Then she did a thing he’d never seen her do: she put her hand out, not to shake, flat, palm toward him, the way she held it out on the gravel bar to show a class where the line went. And held it there between them on the quay. A hand’s width from his chest.

He felt it come off her. Faint. Grey. A count of one.

Just enough. A wall the size of a hand, between his chest and the river, for one heartbeat, in the morning, on a quay, in front of the whole town.

Then it was gone, and she took her hand back, and went up the plank onto the barge, and stood at the stern with Xav, and didn’t look back, and he knew she’d look back the moment the barge was round the bend and nobody could see her do it.

The Patient Widow went down the Wren on the morning current, slow and heavy and low in the water, with Haskett at the oar and his boys on the poles and eight people from three different places standing along her rail; and the town on the quay steps watched her go, and Group 1 on the bollards, and Maren with her list, and Bram with his ruler, and a girl with a black sword on her back and her ears forward; and him, at the end of the quay, with his hands at his sides.

At the bend, a little gold light went up off the stern. John. Up, and up, over the water, and hung there in the morning air over the river like a lamp nobody had to hold.

He thought, watching it: he’s filming us.

He lifted his hand. Not the rod. It was by the cabin door. His hand.

On the barge, eight hands went up.

Then the river took them round the bend, and the light went with them.

Maren’s hands were bandaged.

He noticed it in the evening, at the cabin, when Group 1 came down the lake road for supper the way they’d come down it most evenings for two years. She came in behind Higu with her list under her arm and both palms wrapped in clean linen to the wrist, and sat down at the end of the table, and didn’t say anything about it, and nobody else did either, in the very particular way that meant everybody had been told not to.

He looked at Thea.

Thea looked back at him, across the table, over a bowl of his fish with onions, and her face did a thing it had learned to do in the last year: it went very still, the way his went still, so you couldn’t read it. She’d got good at that.

She told him after. On the step, when the others had gone down to the shingle to watch Xola try to teach Waddles to stand on a float.

“She burned them,” said Thea. “Both palms. Two weeks ago. While you were on the road.”

He waited.

“I’m Lantern now,” said Thea. “Since the tenth. And she’s Lantern. She’s been Lantern since she was twenty.” She looked at her own hands. “I’ve been her student for three years. She taught us everything. What a Candle was. What a Lantern was. And now I’m the same as her. The same tier. And I can do Handshake, and she can’t, and she was fifty-eight to Torch for seven years and she’s sixty-six since the summer and she’s never once in her life been in a room where somebody she taught was the same as her.” She breathed. “So she asked me. Properly. To teach her. Torch.”

“And?”

“And I wouldn’t,” said Thea. “I said what you’d say. You’ll find it. I said say it out loud and stop when you don’t know why a word’s there, the way I did last autumn.” She was quiet. “And she went into the militia hall kitchen at night when nobody was there and tried to light the big lamp over the table with a Torch. Not a Lantern. A Torch. Real fire. Combat fire. The kind that goes where you point it.” She swallowed. “She thought Torch was more. Louder. Brighter. More of it. She pushed. Everything she had. Into a lamp.” She looked at the shingle. “The curtain went up. And she put it out with her hands.”

He didn’t say anything.

“I wanted to fix it,” said Thea. “I can see how. I can see exactly what she got wrong, the way I can see a broken line in a program. It’s not more. It’s aimed. Torch isn’t brighter than Lantern; it’s meant. It goes where you mean it to go and nowhere else. She meant the lamp and she pushed at the whole room.” She put her hands over her face. “I could tell her that in one sentence. I’ve been not telling her for two weeks. I sit at the cord with her every night and watch her read the strips with her hands in linen and I don’t tell her.”

“Good,” he said.

Thea took her hands away and looked at him.

“That’s very annoying,” she said.

“I know.”

“You leave comments. I told you on the lake road. Here’s where it gets interesting. And then you let them find it.” She looked at the dark water. “I’m doing it to Maren. And it’s horrible. It’s the worst thing I’ve ever done. I don’t know how you’ve been doing it to all of us for three years.”

“Badly,” he said. “Mostly. I nearly told Mashi about a hundred times.”

“Did you?”

“I told him to be last,” he said. “That’s all. The rest was his.”

Thea sat there on the step beside him in the dark for a long time.

“She’ll find it,” said Thea, at last. Not a question.

“She’ll find it.”

“It’s going to take ages.”

“It took her seven years to get to fifty-eight,” he said. “And ten months to get from fifty-eight to sixty-six, once she started reading slowly with you.” He looked down the shingle, where somebody, Xola, was shrieking with laughter, and a penguin was falling off a float. “She’ll find it.”

Thea told him the other thing at the end of the evening, on the step, when the others had gone up the lake road with their lanterns.

He’d known there was another thing. He’d known all evening. She’d been sitting on it the way Gabe had been sitting on a piece of star in his pocket, and he’d let her, the way he’d let Gabe.

“Hyvred saw something,” said Thea. “On the tenth. After.”

He went still.

He felt himself do it. He didn’t decide to. It was the thing that happened to him sometimes, at the bottom of him, under the deciding, where the red was; it went quiet. All of it. The way the lake goes quiet before a pike comes up.

“She kept Range open when Deston let go,” said Thea. “She didn’t mean to. And she looked west. Past the valley. Past the mountains. Past the end of Corvin’s maps.” She looked at her hands. “There’s a shape. In the dark. Bigger than the valley. Still. With parts. Not a light and not a hole. And it looked back at her. On purpose.” She swallowed. “She told me first. Through Corvin’s cord. She told the king when he came to Thornwall. She’s not looking west now. She faces south on the tower with her eyes shut. Hild taught her.”

He didn’t say anything for a long time.

The lake was very still. The lamp on the end of the jetty burned without a flicker. On the last plank, beside it, the red hen sat with her feathers puffed up against the cold and her head under her wing.

“The king knows,” he said.

“He’s there. At Thornwall. Corvin says he’s at the forge. Making shields.”

“Shields,” he said.

“For holding. Not killing.” Thea looked at him. “Corvin said that’s what he said. For holding.”

He thought about Severin at an anvil in a grey stone town at the foot of the Greyspine, with his father’s little cross-peen in his hand, making shields so that a line of soldiers could hold their own neighbours off without opening them.

He thought about a shape in the dark past the end of the maps that had turned, on purpose, toward a cow girl with a gold eye on a tower.

He thought about a sword on his wall.

“Not yet,” he said.

He said it the way he always said it. Thea heard it, and he saw her hear it, and he saw her not ask what he meant, because she’d been his friend for a long time in two worlds and she knew what he meant, which was: I heard you. I’m not going to do anything. Not yet. But I heard you.

She nodded. And got up off the step, and picked up her lantern, and went up the lake road after the others, and at the top she turned round and lifted the lantern once, the way Alea lifted her fingertips; and went.

Gigichi was on the last plank.

He hadn’t seen her go out. He’d been on the step with Thea, and then on his own, and then he’d got up and walked down the shingle the way he walked down it every night, toward the jetty, toward the lamp; and she was already there, at the end, sitting on the last plank with her legs over the water, beside the hen, in her cardigan, with the forget-me-nots in her hair gone a little brown with autumn.

She’d made a seat. In the hedge. Facing the road. She’d said she would, on the lake road, the morning he left: I’ll make a seat in the hedge. Facing the road. I’ll ask you at the jetty. On the last plank.

She’d sat on the seat in the hedge, he knew, every afternoon for forty-eight days. Facing the road. And this afternoon she’d seen him come over the hill, a hill behind his daughter, and not got up, and not waved, and not come down; and waited, the whole evening, through supper and Maren’s hands and Thea on the step; and then come out here, ahead of him, to the end of the jetty, and sat down on the last plank, and waited again.

He walked out. Thirty-four planks. He sat down on the last one beside her, with the hen between them, the way Petra had sat beside him with the hen between them on the night before they went.

She didn’t say anything for a while. Neither did he. The lake was black and very still and full of stars, and the lamp burned beside them, and somewhere on the far shore an owl called once and stopped.

“You okay?” said Gigichi.

In English. Very quietly. The way she’d said it the first time, at a cabin table, at midwinter, two years ago, when he’d only just said it’s me. The way he’d said it to her in her chat on Earth a hundred times when she went quiet, that she’d never once answered.

He thought about it. He’d always thought about it, when she asked. She deserved that. She’d asked him twice in two worlds and both times he’d said yes and the first time it hadn’t been true.

“Yes,” he said.

She waited. She knew there’d be more. There was always more, with that one.

“I stood on the red,” he said. “Where I stood. Four paces out from the ditch, the way I stood ten years ago, the second night, when I knew by midnight it was the wrong line and stayed anyway, and in the morning my wife was gone.” He hadn’t said it like that before. Not to anyone. He heard himself say it. “And the girl from the ditch climbed out of it, and walked past me, and stood in front of me. And made a wall.”

Gigichi didn’t say anything.

“I’d been standing in the wrong place for ten years,” he said. “In my head. Every night. Wrong place. Wrong place. That’s what that night was. The wrong place.” He looked at the water. “And she stood in front of me in it, and I thought: oh. It was the right place for somebody.”

The hen made a small sleepy noise between them and settled.

“And I sat down,” he said. “At a table. And ate. And my daughter told her mother the year, and the hens, and I hummed a song in a gap I haven’t sung in for sixteen years. And a man I never met in one life cooked me noodles at two in the morning that wasn’t two in the morning.” He was quiet. “And I’m not as tired. As last year.”

He stopped.

He didn’t know if that was the answer. It was all of it, anyway.

Gigichi sat on the last plank beside him with her hands in her lap and her face turned to the water. He couldn’t see what it was doing. He didn’t need to; he’d never needed to see her face. He’d known her in a chat, on a stream, for years before he knew her face, and he’d always been able to tell from the shape of how she was quiet.

She was crying. Quietly. The good kind.

“Okay,” said Gigichi, after a long time. Very small. “Okay. That’s a real yes.”

“It’s a real yes.”

“The first one wasn’t.”

“No.”

“I knew it wasn’t,” said Gigichi. “At the table. You said yes and I knew. And I asked again at the jetty, in the spring, and you gave me all of it, and that wasn’t yes either, it was no, and here’s why.” She wiped her face on the sleeve of her cardigan. “This is yes. I can hear it.”

He didn’t say anything.

“I made a seat,” said Gigichi. “In the hedge. I sat on it every afternoon. Facing the road. Forty-eight days.” She laughed, a little, into her sleeve. “Higu counted. She counts everything I do now, she says it’s practice.”

“I saw it. From the hill.”

“I know you did. You tapped your fingers.” She looked at him sideways. “I saw you do it. Three times. Like Alea does.”

“Diwa did it,” he said. “At the tent flap. Before she went in.”

Gigichi was quiet for a long time.

“Can I keep it?” she said. “The seat. In the hedge. Facing the road. For when you go again.”

“It’s your hedge.”

“It’s your road,” said Gigichi.

He thought about that.

“Keep it,” he said.

They sat there. The lake. The lamp. The hen between them, asleep, warm against both of them. The town up the slope with its lights going out one at a time. The cabin behind them, dark, with a sword on its wall over a drawing and a list hung back to front on a nail, blank side out, waiting for a girl to write on it.

After a while, because it was the thing you did, because it went both ways, because he’d asked her a hundred times in a chat on the other side of the world and she’d never once answered, he turned his head and looked at her, in the dark, on the last plank.

“You okay?” he said.

Gigichi went very still.

Then she laughed. The real one. Out loud, startled, over the black water, so that the hen woke up and glared at her, and an owl on the far shore stopped halfway through its call; the first time he’d ever heard her laugh like that, in two worlds.

“Yes,” said Gigichi. “I am. I really am.”

He believed her. He could hear it.

He opened his window on the step, much later, when she’d gone up the lake road with her lantern and the hen had been carried up to Odette’s under his arm, protesting, and the lamp on the end of the jetty was the only light on the shore.

Sixty-six. The same as at Oakes’s table. It hadn’t moved for the road, or the quay, or Gabe’s sleep, or a girl from a ditch putting a wall the size of a hand between him and the river. It hadn’t moved for yes.

That was all right. He didn’t need it to. He’d learned, a long time ago, on this step, at a percent a year, that the bar wasn’t the point. It just told you, sometimes, afterwards, which bits had been.

He closed it.

On the wall behind him, through the open door, in the dark, a long grey-silver blade lay on its pegs over a drawing and a blank page, and was patient. To the south and west, a long way off, past the hills, in a grey stone town at the foot of the mountains, a king who’d been a smith was banking a forge for the night, with twelve shield bosses cooling on the bench. And past that, past the valley and the rings and the absence at the foot of the pass, past the end of anybody’s maps, something very large sat in the dark with its parts, and was patient too.

He sat on the step and looked at the lake.

Not yet, he thought.

He didn’t know which of them he was saying it to.
