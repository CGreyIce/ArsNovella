The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 5: The Lists

Chapter 145

Not the Last

6,773 words · 29 min read

Winter watched secrets the way other people watched weather.

She hadn’t always. On Earth she’d been the opposite: the one who didn’t notice, who was off doing her own thing, who found out about everything a bit late and shrugged. She’d liked it that way. It was restful. Let other people keep track of who’d said what to whom. She’d go to Vietnam for two months with her phone mostly off, and come back, and catch up.

She’d gone to Vietnam for two months with her phone mostly off.

She’d come back. She’d turned it on. And there’d been messages, weeks old, that started hey, I don’t know if you’ve heard, and she hadn’t heard, and nobody had thought to make sure she had, because everybody assumed somebody else had told her. She’d sat on the edge of her bed in her own room with her bag still packed and read it four times. And then she’d scrolled back up through two months of messages in every chat she was in, all of them, and watched it happen, all of it, in order, without her. The first message. The disbelief. People getting quieter. People getting back to normal. Everybody else had been through it together, in real time, holding onto each other. She’d been through it in an afternoon, on her own, two months late, scrolling.

She’d been the last to know.

She’d decided, sitting on the edge of her bed that afternoon, that she was never going to be the last to know anything again.

So she’d learned. Here, especially. She’d learned to notice which way things were moving in a room. Who’d heard what. Who was carrying something they hadn’t said yet. Which of the people at the long table had just found something out, from the way their face went still, and who they’d tell first. She’d watched it all through the first year in Valcrest, the way you’d watch weather coming in over a plain. And when something started to move, she knew. Before it got to her. Every time.

She’d known about the man at the lake for a year before he walked through the north gate. Not who. Not where. Just that something was moving, under everything, in the channel and in the guides’ faces and in the way Chris’s beat went still whenever somebody said north.

We think something, she’d said to Newish, in the winter. It’s not ours to give you.

She’d been right. She was usually right. She’d stopped being surprised by it.

This one was moving now.

She’d watched it start at sunset, two nights ago, at the closing. Daughter of Diwa, a healer. And of Ars. Lucan’s voice going out over the Lists without breaking. And forty thousand people going still, and then turning round, a stand at a time, to look at a man on the fourth tier with a fishing rod in his hand. And their eyes sliding off him. And then the paper.

She’d thought, on the bottom tier, with her hands in her lap and the north stand chanting her name again behind her: now it’ll go everywhere. Now the whole city knows.

And it had. The whole city knew there was a Hero, and that he was here, and that the fox girl with the sword was his. That had gone everywhere by dawn. She’d heard it in every street. The ballad girl in the square had a new verse. The crowds outside the Broken Lance were six deep, asking for a seven-foot man with eyes like coals.

But that wasn’t the secret. That was just the news.

The secret was the other thing. The one under it. And it wasn’t going everywhere at all.

She saw it first in the baker’s.

The morning after the closing, the sixty-sixth day, the morning of the king’s supper. She’d gone down early to the bakery on the corner of the high street with Ina, because Lucan had forgotten to order bread again and the water-carrier was apparently busy. She stood in the queue in her plain coat with her long white hair down her back and her two black horns, and her face doing nothing, the way it did nothing in queues, at strangers looking.

The baker’s wife was looking at her. That was normal.

Then the baker’s wife looked past her. Out of the shop window. At the street.

Winter turned her head.

The water-carrier was walking past. On the other side of the street. With a sack over his shoulder and his rod over the other one, going down toward the market. Through the crowd, which didn’t look at him. Through the people looking for a seven-foot man, who stepped round him without turning their heads.

The baker’s wife watched him go past. All the way. Her eyes didn’t slide off him. They didn’t land on him either; they sort of hung near him, the way your eyes hang near something very bright that you can’t look at directly. And her face did something very slow, and very careful, and then went back to being the face of a baker’s wife in a queue.

She didn’t say anything. She didn’t point. She didn’t run out into the street shouting.

She took a loaf off the rack behind her, the best one, the long braided one with seeds on top that was always gone by the second hour, and wrapped it in a cloth, and put it under the counter. Out of sight. And then went back to serving the queue as if nothing had happened.

Winter watched her do it.

When she got to the front of the queue, the baker’s wife sold her twelve ordinary loaves for the Lance, and counted out her change, and then, just as Winter was turning to go, reached under the counter and put the braided loaf in its cloth on top of the pile in Winter’s arms.

“For the long table,” said the baker’s wife. Quite quietly. Not looking at her.

“I didn’t pay for this one.”

“No,” said the baker’s wife. “It’s not for you.”

“Did you see that?” said Ina, outside, on the step.

“I saw.”

“She knows.” Ina had the mask on his shoulder, because it was early and the streets weren’t full yet. His voice was very low. “She looked at him. In the street. She knew.”

“Yes.”

“How?”

Winter thought about it.

“She was at the closing,” she said. “She was sitting near him. On the fourth tier. Or near someone who was.” She looked down at the braided loaf in its cloth on top of the pile. “She saw the rod. She saw who we all turned round to look at.” She paused. “And she’s decided not to say.”

Ina looked at her for a long moment.

“The whole city’s looking for him,” he said. “And she’s not going to tell them.”

“No.”

“Why not?”

Winter didn’t answer. She was thinking about a town at the top of a lake road, in the north, that she’d never seen. That she’d only read about in the channel, in Group 1’s careful silence, and then in Calvin’s letters, and Deston’s: a town that had known for eight years who the man at the end of the jetty was, and had never once said. That had brought him pies and not looked at him and kept his name off every lip, and stood in the square at dawn when he left and said nobody follows.

“Because she saw his face,” said Winter. “When we all turned round. Not his face. Nobody can see his face. She saw what his face was doing. In the way he stood.” She shifted the bread in her arms. “She saw he didn’t want to be found.”

She watched it move after that. All day. All the next day.

Not through the city. Through some of it. A thread here, a thread there, the way Deston said a song goes through a choir: not everyone at once, just the people standing near the ones who already had it.

The marshal who’d kept a paper fishing rod in his coat instead of folding it. She saw him at the Lists the next morning, taking down the last of the rods with the others, and when the water-carrier came in by the side gate to stand at the fence of the practice ground and watch the dawn class, the marshal stopped work. Just for a moment. And put his hand on his chest.

The old soldiers. The Redmarch men from the third tier of the south stand. She saw two of them in the market, at noon, sitting on the edge of the fountain while the ballad girl sang the terrible new verse about the lake so cold and a sword of old. They weren’t listening to the ballad. They were watching the water-carrier, at the back of the crowd, with his bread, listening to it. And when he paid the girl silver and turned to go, both of them stood up. Not saluting. Just standing. The way you’d stand up when an officer walks past a bench you’re sitting on. And sat down again when he’d gone, and didn’t say a word to anyone.

A woman with a baby. Winter didn’t know her. She saw her outside the Broken Lance, at the edge of the crowd that was six deep looking for a seven-foot man, not looking for anyone. Just standing there, with the baby on her hip, until the water-carrier came out of the side door with Gabe. And then she went up to him, through the crowd, and the baby reached out one fat hand and took hold of the end of his rod, the way it apparently had at the closing, and the woman said something very quietly that Winter couldn’t hear. And then went away again.

The ballad girl, who’d been paid silver twice, and who sang the terrible new verse in the square three times a day now, and never once looked at the back of the crowd while she did it, and never once sang it badly.

Lucan, who knew. Who’d looked at the fourth tier at the closing until his eyes stopped sliding. Who was on the step of the Broken Lance every morning in his red cloak with his arms out, telling the crowd six deep that he had no idea, no idea at all, it’s a mystery, just in case, and weeping, and lying through his teeth with the conviction of a man who’d been selling stories to crowds for fifteen years.

That was the secret. The real one. Not the Hero’s here. Everybody had that.

The Hero’s the water-carrier. And we’re not going to tell.

“It’s like Wrenhaven,” said Winter.

She was on the roof of the Broken Lance, the second night, the sixty-seventh, with Ina. The flat bit above the back stair, where the landlord dried washing and where Kei and Yan had sat with two snakes. They’d taken to coming up here in the evenings, the two of them, the way they had in the first summer, before anybody knew anybody. They sat on either side of the chimney with their backs against it and looked at the city.

“What is?” said Ina.

“The city. With him.” Winter pulled her knees up. “It’s doing what Wrenhaven did. For eight years. Not looking. Bringing bread. Standing up when he walks past and not saying why.” She looked out at the lamps going down the hill. “Group 1 told me, once. In the winter. Gigichi did. She said the whole town knew, and nobody ever said, and it was the most protective thing she’d ever seen a whole town do. Like a hedge round a house.”

“And now it’s happening here.”

“A bit,” said Winter. “Not all of it. Some of it. The ones who saw his face. The ones who were close.” She was quiet. “It’s spreading the way Deston’s threads spread. Person to person. Only the ones who were near.”

Ina looked at her across the chimney.

“You’ve been watching it.”

“I’ve been watching it since the closing.”

“Of course you have,” said Ina, gently. He had the mask on his shoulder, the oni face grinning at the sky. “You’re never the last.”

Winter didn’t say anything.

She’d been the last for three years.

Not in Valcrest. Not in the channel. She’d been first, here, every time, about everything. But on the inside. Where it mattered. She’d carried it the whole time: two months, a bag still packed, a phone in her hand on the edge of a bed. The scroll. Watching everyone else go through it together, without her.

And then a north gate. A water-carrier putting down his buckets. And everyone in the square going still at once, and she’d been there. Not two months later. Not reading it in a chat. There. In the square. When it happened. When twelve faded threads went gold at once and Deston’s knees went.

You’re not the last, he’d said to her. Standing two paces off, the way you stop short of a cat you don’t know. This time.

And the frost had come out of the ground round her feet.

She’d thought, afterwards, lying awake, that that would be the end of it. That the thing she’d carried for three years would just go, the way a thing goes when you finally get to set it down.

It hadn’t. Not all of it. She’d found, these last few days, watching the secret move through the city, that she still did it. Still noticed. Still kept track. Still wanted to know, first, every time, which way everything was going.

She thought, now, on the roof, that maybe that was fine. Maybe it wasn’t a wound any more. Maybe it was just a thing she was good at.

“I like watching it,” she said, to Ina. “This one. I like watching who keeps it.”

“It’s a good one to watch.”

“It’s the best one I’ve ever seen,” said Winter.

It was Hyvred who saw them start moving.

The third night. The sixty-eighth. The last night of the week the king had lifted the order for, though the king had lifted it for good now, at his supper, and the groups were leaving in two days, on four roads. The Lance was loud. Everyone was packing and nobody was packing, the way people don’t pack on the last nights of something, and the long table was covered in everybody’s things and Lucan was weeping in the kitchen door and Calvin was trying to get somebody to take the kettle north for him, which nobody would.

Winter was on the roof again with Ina. Hyvred came up the back stairs very fast.

“Winter,” said Hyvred. “Winter. They’re moving.”

Winter was on her feet before she’d finished.

“Which ones?”

“All of them.” Hyvred’s glasses were up in her hair and her left eye had gone the way it went, sharp and gold, the little red crosshair in it locked on something a long way off. She went to the parapet and put both hands on it and looked out over the city. “Yan felt it. Downstairs. He went white and said they’re going. And I came up to see.” She was breathing hard. “Look.”

Winter looked. She couldn’t see what Hyvred saw. Nobody could. She saw the city, at night, lamps and roofs and the dark bowl of the Lists, and the gates in the wall, four of them, north and south and east and west, each with its lamps.

“I can’t see them,” said Winter.

“I’ll tell you,” said Hyvred.

Thirty-nine.

Hyvred counted them out. Standing at the parapet of the Broken Lance with her scope eye locked on the city, turning her head very slowly, a little at a time, the way she’d turned it in the Lists in the torchlight, the crosshair locking and letting go.

“Green coat,” she said. “In the Street of Coopers. Walking. North. Toward the north gate.” Lock, let go. “The woman with the shawl. By the fountain. Walking east.” Lock, let go. “The boy in the cap. On the west road. The old woman who was knitting. The two men with the nuts. By the Lists, going south.” Lock, let go, lock. “All of them. Every one. Walking. Out of the city. At the same time.”

“Toward the gates?”

“Toward every gate,” said Hyvred. “All four. They’re not together. They’re never together. They’re on their own, every one of them, in different streets, walking at the same speed.” She swallowed. “Like they all heard the same bell.”

Winter stood beside her at the parapet. She couldn’t see them. She could see the city: the streets, the lamps, people. Ordinary people, walking home late, carrying things, talking. Any of them could be one. All of them could. She looked at the street below the Lance, at a man walking up it with a lantern, and couldn’t tell.

That was the worst of it. You couldn’t tell. Unless you were Hyvred, or Yan.

“They’re leaving,” said Ina, behind her. Very quietly. “Aren’t they.”

“They’re leaving,” said Hyvred. “All at once.” She didn’t take her eyes off the city. “They counted us all week. Thirty-five, and then him. Every day. And now we’re all leaving in two days, on four roads, and they’ve—“ She stopped. “They’ve finished counting.”

Winter didn’t say anything.

She was thinking about a woman with apples on the downs, a week before the north gate, watching Rachael, who walked into the barley and the barley didn’t move. And a woman with apples on the seventh tier of the east stand, two rows behind Rachael, leaning toward her the way a hand leans toward something it’s about to pick up. And a man with a rod sitting down between them. And the woman getting up and walking out of the north gate into the dark with her basket.

“The apple-seller,” said Winter. “Is she with them?”

Hyvred turned her head. Slowly. All the way round the city. Lock, let go. Lock, let go.

“No,” she said. “She isn’t.”

“I want to see them,” said Winter.

She hadn’t meant to say it out loud. It came out of her at the parapet, flat and blunt, the way things came out of her when she’d been holding them a long time. She’d watched every secret in Valcrest move for two years. She’d been first to every one. And now the biggest thing that had moved in this city all week was walking out of it in the dark under her eyes, and she couldn’t see it. She had to be told. By somebody else. The way she’d been told, once, two months late, by a message on a phone.

“I want to see them,” she said again. “Not be told. I want to see.”

“You can’t,” said Hyvred, gently. “Nobody can. Only me and Yan.”

“And me,” said somebody on the stairs.

John came out onto the roof.

He’d come up the back stairs behind them without any of them hearing, the way John moved through most places: quietly, a little apart, with three small motes of light going round his head like gnats round a lamp. He’d had them since the first winter at Duskhollow. They saw for him. They went round corners and over walls and into the dark ahead of the party and showed him what was there, the way your own eyes would if your eyes could fly.

He’d been at ninety-nine percent since a gorge on the river in the spring.

Everybody knew. The Narrows. The turned coming down both cliffs head first like spiders, and Deston’s Listen lighting every thread at once, and John’s motes going up the cliff in the dark to find a boy in a cap so RX could reach him. He’d been one percent from the first tier ever since. Two and a half months. He’d never once complained. He’d just gone on sending his lights out, every night, wherever they were, and looking.

“I can follow them,” said John. He came to the parapet beside Hyvred. “If you tell me where. My lights can. I’ll see them.” He was quiet. “But that’s no good to you, is it. Me seeing them. You want to see them yourself.”

“Yes,” said Winter.

John looked at her for a long moment. He had a kind, tired, steady face, and the little motes of light going round his head lit it from underneath, gold, the way a fire lights a face.

“I used to be on his stream,” he said. “Ice’s. When he did the facecam. I was always on it. In the corner. Every stream, for about a year.” He smiled, a little. “He’d be playing, and I’d be in the little box in the corner, and people in his chat would say who’s that, and he’d say that’s John, like that explained everything.” He looked out at the city. “I’ve been trying to do it the other way round for two years. Not be in the box. Be the box. Show people what I see.” He breathed. “I’ve never managed it. Not once. My lights see, and I see what they see, and nobody else does.”

“Try,” said Winter.

He sent them out.

All three. Off from round his head and over the parapet and down into the dark, three small gold points of light dropping over the roofs of Valcrest like sparks going down a chimney the wrong way. Hyvred called it. Green coat. Street of Coopers. North. And one of John’s lights went that way, very fast, low over the roofs. The shawl. The fountain. East. The second. The boy in the cap. West. The third.

John shut his eyes.

Winter watched him. He was seeing it. She could tell, from his face. Three streets at once, from three lights, the way Hyvred saw everything and Yan felt everything. He was standing at the parapet with his eyes shut and his face very still, seeing three of the thirty-nine walking out of a city in the dark.

And she still couldn’t.

“Show us,” said Hyvred, beside him. Quietly. Not pushing. The way you’d say it to somebody standing at the top of a high board. “John. Show us.”

John’s hands came up.

He put one finger to the air in front of him, the way they all did, the way Ina wrote catch and Winter had written still, and wrote, slowly, in English, one word.

Look.

The air over the parapet lit.

Not a flare. Not a spark. It went the way a window goes when somebody lights a lamp in the room behind it: a soft square of gold light, hanging in the dark in front of the four of them, about the size of a door. And in it, there was a street.

The Street of Coopers. Winter knew it; she’d walked it a hundred times. From above, a little, at the height of the second-floor windows, the way a bird would see it, or a mote of light. The barrels stacked outside the cooperage. A lamp on a bracket. A dog asleep in a doorway. And a man in a green coat, walking north, at an even pace, past all of it.

“Oh,” said Hyvred.

Winter couldn’t say anything.

She was seeing it. Not being told. Seeing. A man in a green coat walking up a street in Valcrest at night, from above, in John’s light, as if she were standing on the sill of a window looking down at him. And it was the strangest thing she’d ever seen, and she couldn’t at first work out why, and then she could.

The lamp on the bracket lit the barrels, and the dog in the doorway, and the cobbles. Everything in the street had a shadow. The barrels had shadows. The dog had a shadow. The bracket had a shadow.

The man in the green coat didn’t.

He walked under the lamp and the light went round him, the way water goes round a stone, and lit the cobbles on the other side of him as if he wasn’t there, and there was no dark shape on the ground behind him at all. No shadow. A man made of nothing, wearing a coat, walking through a lit street, and the light didn’t even notice.

“That’s what you see,” said Winter. Very quietly. To Hyvred. “That’s what you’ve been seeing. All week.”

“That’s what I see,” said Hyvred.

The square of light changed. The fountain. A woman in a shawl, walking east, past the water, and her reflection not in it. Changed again. The west road. A boy in a cap, walking out past the gate sergeant’s hut, under the paper fishing rods, with no shadow at his feet.

Three windows of light, one after another, hanging over the parapet of the Broken Lance. Changing as John’s lights moved. Like the screen of a stream, Winter thought, with somebody switching cameras.

John stood at the parapet with his eyes shut and his hands up and his face lit gold from underneath, and tears running down it.

“I’m doing it,” said John. His voice cracked. “I’m doing it. You can see it. Can you see it?”

“We can see it,” said Ina, behind him. Very gently. “We can see it, John.”

Winter heard it before she saw it. A small sound, like a bell very far away, that she’d heard twenty-four times now in two years, in the channel and in person.

A window opened. In front of John’s face. Under his own square of gold light, small and plain and white.

[ John · Tier 1: Spark · Skills: Glimpse ]

John opened his eyes. He looked at it.

“Oh,” he said. “Oh. There you are.”

“Twenty-five,” said Hyvred. Very softly. As if she were telling it to the city.

Down in the yard of the Broken Lance, two people had come out of the back door and were standing in the dark looking up. Yan, with a white snake round his neck. And a man with a rod. Looking up at a square of gold light hanging in the air over the roof, about the size of a door, with a street in it.

The man with the rod didn’t move for a long time. Winter couldn’t see his face. She saw him look at the light. And she saw his shoulders go, very slightly, the way they’d gone at the end of the Lists.

That’s John, Winter thought he might be thinking. Like that explained everything.

They watched until the last one went out.

All of them. Through John’s light. It didn’t take long. Thirty-nine people walking at the same speed through a city at night, each one alone, each one on a different street, with no shadows, and a square of gold light following them like a camera. Hyvred called them out as they reached the gates. North gate, three. East gate, a woman in a shawl and a man with a pack. West gate, the boy in the cap, walking out past the gate sergeant’s hut without anyone looking up, under the paper fishing rods still hanging from the gatehouse. South gate, the rest.

And then nothing.

“That’s all of them,” said Hyvred. “Thirty-nine. All out.” She was gripping the parapet. “They went at the same time. Exactly the same time. Like the absence at the pass, when it turned. Not like people. Like one thing doing it thirty-nine times.”

Down in the yard, Yan hadn’t moved. He was standing in the dark with Suzu round his neck and his face turned up toward the city wall, very still.

And beside him, a man with a rod.

Winter knew he’d been feeling them go. The way he’d felt them in the Lists, all week. She couldn’t see his face. She could see the way he stood. Weight on the back foot. Easy. Ready to go either way and in no hurry to choose.

He stood in the yard of the Broken Lance with Yan beside him until Hyvred, on the roof, said that’s all of them. And then he turned his head and looked up at the four of them on the roof, at the parapet, as if he’d known they were there the whole time.

He lifted his hand. Not a wave. Just I know.

Winter lifted hers back.

She didn’t sleep much.

Nobody did. The Lance stayed up most of the night, the way it did now, with everybody at the long table not packing and the channel going on their windows, everyone posting what they’d felt or seen or not seen. Hyvred’s count. Yan’s they’re gone. Kei’s Hisui says the air tastes clean for the first time since we came. John’s window, posted plainly, the way John posted everything: Spark. Glimpse. Sorry it took so long. Chris, logging: Twenty-five. Tiger, in capitals, wanting to know if it was over, and then, a line later, in bigger capitals, about John.

It’s not over, Yan wrote, at about two. They finished counting. That’s all.

Nobody wrote anything for a long time after that.

Winter went up to bed at about four. She lay in the narrow room under the eaves that she’d had for two years, with the window open and the city going grey outside it, and thought about secrets, and who kept them, and which way they moved.

They finished counting.

She thought: they know how many of us there are now. And where. And which road each of us is taking. She thought: and they know who he is. They stayed on him longest. Every count. She thought: and the one who watched Rachael didn’t go with the others.

She thought: I’m going to be first to know whatever happens next.

And then she slept, for about an hour, with frost on the inside of her window that she hadn’t meant to put there.

It was Winter who found the apple.

She’d known she would. She didn’t know how. She just woke at dawn on the sixty-ninth day with the frost melting on her window and a feeling under her breastbone, the one she’d learned to trust, that something had moved in the night that nobody had noticed yet. And she got up, and put her coat on over her shift, and went down the back stairs barefoot, and along the passage on the first floor where Group 4 had their rooms, past Gabe’s door, and Deston’s, and Dennis’s, which had a ledger propped against it outside for some reason, to the room at the end.

Rachael’s.

Rachael had the end room because it had a window on the yard and she’d said she liked being able to see who was coming. The door was shut. Winter didn’t knock. She didn’t need to go in. She went out of the side door into the yard instead, barefoot on the cold cobbles, and turned round, and looked up at the window of the end room on the first floor.

It was open. Rachael always slept with it open. She’d spent twenty-six years on Earth being careful about everything, windows included, and the first thing she’d done in this world when the Advisor touched her wrist and she didn’t break was open every window she could find.

On the sill, outside, on the stone, there was an apple.

Winter stood in the yard and looked at it for a long time.

A red one. Bright and smooth and perfect, the kind the apple-sellers polished on their aprons and stacked in pyramids at the front of their baskets, the ones that looked best and tasted of nothing. Sitting on the outside sill of Rachael’s open window. Exactly in the middle. Placed. Not dropped. Not thrown. Somebody had climbed up there, or reached up there, in the night, while the whole of the Lance was awake and the thirty-nine were walking out of the gates, and set one red apple on the sill of the window where the healer slept.

And then gone.

She didn’t go with the others, Winter thought. She stayed behind to do this.

She heard the side door behind her. She didn’t turn round. She knew who it was. She’d have known that walk anywhere now, in a yard at dawn, barefoot, the way she’d know which way a secret was moving in a room. Easy. Weight on the back foot. Not hurrying.

He came and stood beside her. Two paces off. The way he always stood, with her.

He looked up at the apple.

He didn’t say anything for a long time.

“I was first,” said Winter. “This time.”

“You were.”

“I woke up and I knew. I didn’t know what. I came down and I looked.” She was very calm. Her face was doing nothing. Inside it, very little was calm at all. “Nobody else has seen it yet. Not even her.”

“No,” he said.

“It’s a message.”

“Yes.”

“What does it say?”

He looked at the apple on the sill for a long time.

“It says I saw you sit down,” he said. “And I’m still here.”

Rachael came to the window at that moment.

Winter saw her come. A small dry woman with her hair everywhere and her shift on and a face that said she’d been asleep about an hour and had views about being woken. She leaned on the sill to look out at the yard, to see who was down there, the way she always did, because she liked to be able to see who was coming.

And saw the apple. Six inches from her hand.

She went very still.

Winter watched her look at it. She watched Rachael’s face do the thing it did in the infirmary in Kingsferry, Calvin had written once, when somebody came in bleeding: go completely flat and practical, all at once, as if a door had shut on everything else. Rachael looked at the apple. Then down at the yard. At Winter, barefoot on the cobbles. At the man two paces from her, with no rod, for once, looking up.

“That’s rude,” said Rachael.

Winter blinked.

“Leaving me fruit,” said Rachael. Her voice was perfectly dry. Perfectly flat. It shook, just a hair, on the last word, and she didn’t let it shake again. “In the night. On my sill. Like I’m a horse.” She looked at the apple with an expression of deep contempt. “Not even a good one. It’s a show apple. You can tell. They polish those. They taste of cardboard.”

“Don’t eat it,” said the man in the yard.

“I wasn’t going to eat it,” said Rachael. “I’m not an idiot, Declan.” She looked down at him. “I told you that. In the Lists. I’m not an idiot.”

“I know.”

“Three hunters. A man in a hat. A woman in the barley. A woman with apples two rows behind me.” Rachael’s hand had come up and was resting on the sill, very close to the apple. Not touching it. “And now one on my sill.” She breathed. “I’m fine.”

“I know you are.”

“I’m fine,” said Rachael again. And Winter, who’d been watching which way things moved in rooms for two years, heard it. The thing under it. Dennis would have called it a cracked bell.

The man in the yard didn’t say anything for a moment.

Then he went to the wall. Under Rachael’s window. And reached up, the way you’d reach up to a shelf, though it was a good deal higher than any shelf, and took the apple off the sill. Without touching Rachael’s hand. Without hurrying. He held it in his palm and looked at it. A red show apple, polished on someone’s apron, that tasted of nothing.

Then he closed his hand.

Winter didn’t see him do anything. She didn’t see his hand tighten. He just closed it, the way you close your hand round a stone. And when he opened it again, there was nothing in it. Not juice. Not pulp. Not pieces. Nothing at all. A very fine grey dust that the morning wind took off his palm and carried away across the yard, and then not even that.

He brushed his hand on his coat.

“Gone,” he said.

Rachael looked down at him from the window for a long time.

“You didn’t have to do that,” she said.

“I know.”

“I’d have thrown it in the river.”

“I know you would.”

“Then why—“

“Because I could,” he said.

The same thing he’d said in the Lists, sitting behind her on the sixth tier. Because I could. Rachael heard it. Winter saw her hear it. Saw her face, which had been flat and practical and dry since she leaned out of the window, do something it very rarely did, which was nothing at all, for a long moment. And then something else.

“Fine,” said Rachael. Very quietly. “Fine. Thank you.” And then, because she was who she was and had always been, and was getting better at it every day in a world where she wasn’t the fragile one any more: “Next time just knock.”

“I’ll knock.”

“You won’t,” said Rachael. “You never knock. You just turn up.” And she pulled her head back in, and shut the window for the first time since she’d come to this world, and Winter heard her, through the glass, say something very short and very rude to the empty room.

Then, a moment later, the window opened again. All the way. And stayed open.

“You’ll tell the others,” said Winter, after a while. In the yard. At dawn. With her bare feet going numb on the cobbles.

“Yes. Today. Before the roads.” He looked at the open window. “Group 4 are going north with Group 1 and 6. To the river. Kingsferry. She’ll have Gabe and Deston and Sabine and the whole of Group 6 round her for three weeks.” He was quiet. “And then the infirmary at Kingsferry, with Sister Maud, who’s had her for two years and isn’t letting her go anywhere.” A pause. “They’ll keep her.”

“They will.”

“So will she,” he said. “She’s been keeping herself her whole life. She’s very good at it.”

Winter looked at him. Two paces off. A face she couldn’t see. A man she’d known for four months on Earth, playing Apex with Chris, who’d have made the joke about a girl called Winter with ice magic if he’d been there to see it, and who’d died while she was in Vietnam with her phone off, and who’d stood two paces off at a north gate a week ago and said you’re not the last.

“I’m going to keep watching,” said Winter. “Whatever happens next. Whichever way it moves. I’m going to see it first.”

“I know you are.”

“I’ll tell you.”

He was quiet for a moment.

“Tell Chris,” he said. “And Ina. And Newish. And the channel.” He turned toward the side door. “Tell everyone, Winter. Not just me. That’s the thing about seeing it first.” He paused at the door. “It’s no good unless you make sure nobody else is last.”

He went in.

Winter stood in the yard on her own with her bare feet on the cold cobbles, and looked up at Rachael’s open window for a long time, in the grey light, with the city waking up round her.

Then she went in, and up the back stairs, and woke Chris, and Ina, and Newish. And opened her window, and wrote it in the channel, first, before anyone else, so that nobody would read it two months late.
