Holder

6,276 words · 27 min read

The holder didn’t fight until the last day. That was the rule, Lucan said, and it was three hundred years old, and Alea hated it.

She hadn’t known. Nobody had told her; there’d never been a holder from Wrenhaven before, or from anywhere north of the Sallow, and the rule was the kind of thing everybody in Valcrest knew the way they knew which way the river ran, so nobody thought to say it out loud. You won the Fledgling, and your name went at the top of next year’s tree in gold, HOLDER, and then for a year it was yours. And when next year came, you didn’t climb the tree. You sat at the top of it. Sixty-four of them climbed it, round by round, six rounds over four days, and you sat on the holder’s bench at the side of the near ring and watched them come, and at the end of the fourth day, whoever was left at the top of the tree came and stood in front of you, and you got up and found out if it was still yours.

“It’s to see if you can wait,” said Lucan. On the first morning, at the holder’s bench, which was a long seat of old oak under a little awning painted with the crossed lances of the Lists, with a cushion on it that somebody had embroidered with a fish. Lucan had had the fish put on in the spring. “That’s what the old masters said. Anybody can fight. The holder has to sit still for four days and watch the thing that’s coming for her get bigger.” His eyes filled up. “I think it’s cruel. I’ve always thought it was cruel. I’d change it if I could. It’s the one rule they won’t let me touch.”

“It’s all right,” said Alea.

It wasn’t. She sat on the bench with Astra across her knees and the sun on the near ring and the noise of forty thousand people coming down off the stands like weather, and watched the first round of the Fledgling, thirty-two bouts, boys and girls under twenty from every guild hall and garrison school and fishing town in the kingdom, coming at each other in the chalk ring with everything they had; and she didn’t move.

She’d learned that on a boat. You didn’t hold the boat. You let it hold you.

She held the bench anyway. With both hands. Under Astra, where nobody could see.

She’d looked at her window once, that morning, on the bench, before the first bout.

Forty-four, past the Gullhaven bar. Sixty-one now, after eighteen days in Valcrest standing on sand that didn’t move, after six weeks on something that did. The sand had been doing something to her since the sea gate, and she didn’t know what. She closed it. She didn’t look at it again all week.

Sorrel was in the first bout of the first round.

Lucan had put her there. He’d said he couldn’t put anybody opposite anybody, and he couldn’t; but the first entry in the kingdom went in the first box, that was the rule, and he’d lettered it in gold, because he’d run out of black that day, and the first box fought the first bout. So at the second hour of the first morning Sorrel walked out onto the near ring barefoot in her fisherman’s smock with a boat-hook in each hand and her sailcloth headband on and her grin like a gap in a fence, and the whole of the near stand, which remembered her from last year, stood up and shouted HOOKS.

She looked up at the holder’s bench before the marshal said begin. Straight at Alea. And didn’t wave, or grin, or anything. She just looked, for a moment, the way you’d look at the far end of a road you were going to walk.

I’ll get there, she’d written to Lucan, in the autumn, by a coast carrier. If you can’t put me opposite her, I’ll get there.

Then the marshal said begin, and Sorrel did the new thing.

Alea had wondered what it would be. Sorrel had had weeks. You’ve got my thing, she’d said, in the stern of NEXT YEAR, past the Gullhaven bar, sitting on the thwart laughing until she cried. I’m going to have to get a new thing now. And for the three weeks they’d had left at Gullhaven she’d said nothing about it at all, which on Sorrel was the loudest thing in the world, and gone out past the bar alone at dawn twice a week in NEXT YEAR without Alea, and come back with her hands raw and her face very pleased with itself.

The new thing was that she hooked the ground.

The boy across from her was a garrison cadet from Ashby, Garth’s school, in the grey coat, with a plain soldier’s sword held exactly the way soldiers are taught, and he came at her the way Garth had come at Alea last year, on the third count, very well, very correctly. And Sorrel let him come, and at the last moment drove her left hook down into the sand at her own feet, deep, the way you’d drive a pick into ice, and pulled on it.

Not on him. On the ground.

And went sideways, fast, faster than anybody could go sideways on their own feet, hauled across the sand by her own arm round a hook stuck in the earth like a boat swinging round its anchor; and the cadet’s sword went through the place she’d been, and her right hook came round the other way and caught him behind the knee, and he sat down.

The near stand made a noise like a wave hitting a harbour wall.

Alea sat on the holder’s bench and laughed out loud. She didn’t mean to. Nobody had ever done that on the sand of the Lists in three hundred years; she could tell from the noise. Sorrel had gone out past the bar and stood in the stern of a boat and thought, if I can’t go with the boat any more because the lake girl stole it, I’ll go with the whole world.

Sorrel looked up at the holder’s bench again. She was grinning now. All the way.

That’s mine, the grin said. Get your own.

She watched Sorrel climb the tree.

Four days. Six rounds. She sat on the holder’s bench under the painted lances with the fish on the cushion and watched every one of Sorrel’s bouts, and most of everyone else’s, because there was nothing else to do and you couldn’t not watch.

She’d watched the rest of the Lists the week before, from the long bench with the Lance, before the holder had to sit anywhere: the great wall on the far side of the bowl with its forty lamps and its thirty-six boxes and the names in gold, and the summoned’s Swiss happening under it, round by round. She’d seen Dennis go down with his head opened and Rachael kneel beside him, and heard the bowl stand up. She’d seen Xav stand in a ring doing nothing, and then be somewhere else. She’d felt the sand jump, all the way across the bowl, under the bench, under her feet, and found out it had been Salty, and laughed until Maren gave her water. And on the first morning of the Fledgling, which was midsummer day, sitting on the holder’s bench for the first time, she heard the bell: high and clear and long, from the main sand, where Niko was. She sat very still with her hand flat on Astra until it stopped.

She didn’t look at the stands.

That was the thing she’d decided on the coast road, on the fourth day out of Gullhaven, with the sea beside her and Sorrel talking. She wasn’t going to look. Not for him. Last year she’d looked for him every round, every bout, up in the stands, until she found him on the fourth tier of the east stand and felt her whole back go easy, like a horse with the saddle off. She’d needed it. She’d won because of it, partly. She’d known it at the time and not minded.

She minded now. She’d gone to the sea so she wouldn’t need it. She’d stood on something that moved for six weeks so she could stand on sand that didn’t without her back going easy because somebody was watching. She’d written I’m not asking on the king’s cream paper, and meant it.

So she didn’t look.

It was very hard. She found out on the first morning how hard. Her eyes kept going to the stands on their own, the way your tongue goes to a loose tooth; to the east stand, the fourth tier, his place, and finding a woman with a baby in it, and a man with a pie, and coming back. She made them come back. She looked at the sand. At Sorrel. At the cadets and the guild girls and the fishermen’s boys climbing the tree toward her.

And she noticed something.

It took her until the second day to see it, because it was the kind of thing you don’t see, the way you don’t see a room is quiet until somebody tells you. The Lance was being kind to her.

Not unkind before. Kind differently. All of them. Thirty-three of them at the long tables at night, and Maren, and Bram, and the guides, and the kitchen: every time she came in, every face that looked up at her looked up a little too gently and looked away a little too fast. Gabe had gone red every time he saw her for four days and turned his chair round. Deston kept smiling at nothing. Xav, when she’d passed him in the yard behind the Lance, had looked at her for a long moment with his chin on his fist and said you’ll be fine in a voice like somebody saying a thing they knew and weren’t allowed to say. Rachael had looked at the window. Group 1 had sat at their table every night saying nothing, very loudly, the way they’d once said nothing, for a very long time, about a fisherman on a jetty.

Something was being kept.

She knew what it looked like. She’d grown up inside a thing being kept. A whole town keeping a man on a jetty for years; a mayor in a chain at the top of a lake road so that nobody would go down it. She knew exactly what thirty-three people being kind to you in a particular way looked like.

She didn’t let herself think about what it was.

She’d said she wasn’t asking. If she let herself think it, she’d be asking. Just in her head. And then she’d look up at the stands every round looking for the answer, and her back would go easy or it wouldn’t, and either way she’d have lost the thing she went to the sea for.

So she looked at the sand. And when Sorrel, at supper on the second night, looked at her sideways over the end of the long table with her face doing something Alea had never seen it do, which was keep something, Alea said, “Don’t,” and Sorrel said, “I wasn’t going to,” and they both looked at their plates.

Sorrel went out in the semi.

On the third day, in the afternoon, on the near ring, to a cadet from the king’s own guard school in Solmere. Alea knew him. Everybody in the Fledgling knew him. His name was Tamlin Brand, and last year he’d come through five rounds without anybody touching him, the best cadet in the Fledgling, everyone said so; and in the semi he’d stood across from a fox girl with a sword the size of a door and watched her centre the way the guard school taught you to, weight balanced, sword at the perfect angle, eyes on the opponent’s middle; and she’d moved early, and Astra had come down on his guard from somewhere he hadn’t been taught to look, and let it fall, and his sword had gone into the sand. One stroke.

She’d said something to him, afterwards. She hadn’t meant to. He’d been standing in the ring looking at his own sword in the sand with his face doing what faces do when everything you’ve been taught turns out to be not quite enough, and she’d heard herself say it, the thing her father had said to her on the fourth tier that week, turned round the other way: Look at the whole thing. Next year.

He’d looked at the whole thing.

She watched him do it to Sorrel. He came out onto the near ring this year a different shape. Not taller; the same, nineteen now, the same guard-school coat. But he stood differently. Not weight balanced, not eyes on the centre. Loose. Looking at all of her: her feet and her hooks and her hands and her shoulders and the sand round her, the whole of Sorrel at once, the way you’d look at a boat in a swell to see which way it was going to go before it went.

Sorrel did the new thing. She drove the left hook into the sand and hauled on it and went sideways, fast, the way she’d done to every one of the four before him.

And Tamlin let his sword fall.

Alea stood up off the holder’s bench. She didn’t mean to.

Not on Sorrel. He didn’t go after Sorrel at all. Sorrel went sideways, hauled round her own anchor by her own arm, and her right hook came round for his sword, and his sword wasn’t there to be caught; he’d taken it up off his shoulder and stepped the other way, toward the anchor, and let its own weight bring it down. The way Alea had let Astra fall on a lord’s son in the first round last year, and on Garth of Ashby, and a spear, and a staff, and on him. Except he let it fall on the hook. On the shaft of Sorrel’s left hook, where it went into the sand, a hand above the ground. Flat. It drove the hook another hand’s depth into the sand and pinned it there.

Then he put his boot on the blade, and stood.

Sorrel’s left hand was still on the shaft. She pulled. It didn’t come. She’d hauled herself round it like a boat round a mooring, and now somebody was standing on the mooring, and she went round it the rest of the way, round and round like a dog on a rope, and sat down hard on the sand at the full stretch of her own arm.

Tamlin took his boot off the blade and lifted it. He touched her, very lightly, on the shoulder with the flat of it.

“Touch,” said the marshal.

The near stand didn’t make a sound for a moment. Then it did, but not the sound it had made for Sorrel. A different one. The sound a crowd makes when it sees something done very well that it didn’t want done.

Sorrel sat on the sand with her hand still on the hook and looked up at Tamlin Brand for a long time.

“You let it fall,” she said.

“They were shouting it all week,” said Tamlin. He nodded up at the holder’s bench, at Alea, standing. “Last year. Let it fall. I watched her do it from the tunnel, and then she did it to me. I’ve been thinking about it for a year.” He held out his hand. “You hook the ground now. I didn’t know that. I had three bouts to watch you do it.” He was quiet. “I looked at the whole thing.”

Sorrel took his hand. He pulled her up.

She didn’t look at the holder’s bench on her way off. She walked straight across the near ring with her hooks over her shoulder and her face perfectly still, and out through the tunnel, and Alea heard her, a moment later, under the stand, kick something wooden very hard and say a word she’d learned off the Gullhaven quay that would have got Alea’s ears boxed in Wrenhaven.

She found her on the holder’s bench at dusk.

Sorrel had come back. She was sitting on it, on the cushion with the fish, with her hooks across her knees and her face red and blotched and furious, looking at the near ring, which was empty, with the marshals raking the chalk.

Alea sat down beside her.

They sat there. The lamps came on along the great wall across the bowl, all forty, one after another, violet and then yellow, the way they’d come on every night since Ina had lit them; and the noise of the city came up over the rim of the bowl, and the swifts went round.

“It stays,” said Sorrel.

“I know.”

“NEXT YEAR. On the stern. It stays.” She wiped her face with the back of her wrist. “I said if I lost it stays. I lost. It stays.” She was quiet. “Abner’ll be so pleased. He’s been wanting me to paint it out for a year so I’d say no.”

“You’ll say no again.”

“Course I will,” said Sorrel. “It’s a name. Not a date.” She looked at the hooks across her knees. “I didn’t get there.”

“You got to the semi. In the whole kingdom. With your dad’s hooks.” Alea looked at the empty ring. “You hooked the ground.”

“He stood on it.”

“He’d been watching you for three bouts. And he let it fall. On the hook.” Alea was quiet. “He did it because I did it. Last year. He said.”

Sorrel looked at her sideways.

“So it’s your fault,” said Sorrel.

“It’s my fault.”

“Good,” said Sorrel. “Then you can beat him.” She stood up. She put her hooks over her shoulder. She looked down at Alea on the holder’s bench, and her face did a thing it had been doing all week at supper, the keeping thing, and then, for the first time, decided not to. “Alea.”

“Don’t.”

“I’m not going to tell you,” said Sorrel. “I said I wouldn’t. Everyone said they wouldn’t. I’m just going to say one thing, and it’s not the thing, and you can’t be cross.” She looked at the near ring. “Tomorrow, when you’re in the ring with him. And you’re losing. Because you will be, for a bit, he’s very good, he’s better than last year, he’s better than me. When you’re losing.” She swallowed. “Don’t look at the horizon.”

Alea looked up at her.

“Look at the whole thing,” said Sorrel. And went.

The final was on the last day, in the afternoon, on the main sand.

Not the near ring. The holder’s final was always on the main sand; Lucan had told her on the first morning, under the awning, with his eyes filling. The whole bowl. Forty thousand. The summoned’s bracket had finished at noon, the eight boxes at the right-hand end of the great wall, and Gabe had won it, over Niko in the final, because nobody in the whole of the Lists could get past Gabe when he planted his feet behind a blunt armoury sword and leaned, not even a bell. She’d heard the bowl roar for it from the holder’s bench and seen Gabe standing in the middle of the main sand afterwards, enormous and redder than she’d ever seen anyone go, not knowing what to do with his hands.

Tamlin Brand had won the last round of the tree in the morning, on the near ring, without anybody touching him.

Now it was hers.

She walked out of the south tunnel onto the main sand with Astra on her back, and the bowl went up.

She’d thought she remembered what it was like. She didn’t. Last year she’d walked out for the final against Sorrel and it had been loud; this was something else. Forty thousand people on their feet, and every one of them holding something. Paper fishing rods. Lucan’s. All forty thousand of them, one for every seat, all the colours of a market, with little paper fish on the end of the lines, that had been stacked under the north stand in the dry all week in bundles and given out that morning at every gate. They were holding them up. All round the bowl. Not shaking them, not waving. Just holding them up, still, the way you’d hold up a candle at a vigil.

She stopped in the mouth of the tunnel.

Last year, at the closing, at sunset, when she’d written her mother’s name in the box and Lucan had read it out, the whole of Valcrest had held up paper fishing rods. Twenty thousand. For him. Because of him. She’d stood on the platform and watched the city understand who’d been sitting on the fourth tier all week, with the real one across his knees.

They were holding them up for her now. She knew that. She knew it was for her, the holder, the girl from the lake, Diwa’s daughter, they’d read it in the roll.

She didn’t look at the fourth tier of the east stand. She didn’t look at the north stand. She didn’t look anywhere.

She looked at Tamlin Brand.

He was waiting in the middle of the chalk ring with his sword low, loose, looking at all of her.

The marshal said begin.

He didn’t move. She didn’t either.

She’d known he wouldn’t. He’d had a year. He’d watched her let it fall on a lord’s son from the tunnel, and on Garth, and on him, and he’d watched her beat Sorrel in the final by going with the hook, and he’d stood in the ring yesterday and let his own sword fall on a hook. He wasn’t going to come at her. He was going to wait for her to come at him, sideways, from under where he was looking; and he was going to be looking at all of her, the whole thing, so there wasn’t an under.

So she waited.

She was good at waiting now. She’d learned it on a jetty from a man who could sit on the end of one for nine years. She’d learned it again on a boat. She stood in the chalk ring on the main sand of the Lists in front of forty thousand paper fishing rods and let the sand hold her, the way NEXT YEAR held her, and waited for the fish.

He waited too.

A long time. Longer than any bout she’d ever fought. The bowl went quiet, slowly, the way a bowl goes quiet when nothing happens for long enough; the rods came down a little, here and there, as arms got tired, and went back up. The marshal shifted his feet. Somewhere high up in the north stand a child said are they going to fight in a voice that carried, and somebody shushed it.

And she felt her weight go back.

Just a hair. Onto her heels. The way it had gone on the gravel bar at Duskhollow at dawn, in John’s little picture, a hand from Petra’s knee. Because the sand didn’t move. Because she’d been standing on it for a long time, waiting, and waiting on your heels felt like waiting, and her body did it before she could stop it, after six weeks on a boat, after all of it.

And Tamlin Brand, who’d been looking at the whole thing, saw it.

He came.

He came fast. Faster than last year; faster than she’d thought a cadet could. Straight. Not sideways, not tricky, just straight and very hard, the guard school’s best cut, the one they taught you on the first day and made you do ten thousand times, at her left shoulder, while her weight was on her heels and she couldn’t go anywhere but back.

She went back.

She took it on Astra. Up, in both hands, the flat of the broad black blade, clang, and the force of it went into her and she was on her heels and it pushed her back, a step, two, across the sand, toward the chalk.

He didn’t stop. He’d watched her for a year. He knew that if he stopped she’d get her feet back, and if she got her feet back she’d go sideways. He came again. And again. Not fast now; heavy. The way you’d drive a nail. Every cut at the same shoulder, every one taking her back another half-step, every one keeping her on her heels, so that she couldn’t get her weight forward to go anywhere at all.

Four steps. Five. The chalk was behind her. She could feel it, the way you feel the edge of a jetty behind you in the dark.

She was losing.

She knew it. The bowl knew it. The rods were coming down, all round, slowly, the way they’d come down last year in the second exchange of the final when Sorrel’s hook had caught the guard and pulled her across the ring with her heels on the chalk. Somebody shouted ALEA. Somebody else shouted HOLDER. In the near stand, very loudly, Sorrel’s voice: LOOK AT THE WHOLE THING.

Her heels touched the chalk.

And she felt him.

She didn’t look. She’d decided. She didn’t look anywhere. But she felt him the way she’d felt him all her life, the way you feel somebody in a room behind you in the dark without turning round, before you know you’ve felt anything. Not magic. Not the way Yan felt people, or Deston heard them. The other way. The way you know the end of a jetty is there in the dark because it’s always been there.

He was in the east stand. The fourth tier. His place.

She didn’t know how she knew. She’d been in the ring for a long time with her eyes on Tamlin Brand and she hadn’t looked up once. But he was there. Not the north stand, where she’d felt something all week at the edge of her and not let herself feel it. The east stand. The fourth tier. Where he’d sat last year. Where everybody in the bowl could see him, if their eyes would stay on him, which they wouldn’t. He’d moved. For this. He’d come down out of wherever he’d been hiding and walked round the bowl and sat down in his place, in front of everyone, because whatever he’d decided about not being seen, he’d decided about this differently.

She knew it the way you know a lamp is lit on the end of a jetty behind you when you’re rowing away from it, without turning round. A warmth on the back of your neck.

She didn’t look.

Her heels were on the chalk. Tamlin Brand’s sword was coming again, at her left shoulder, the same cut, the tenth, the nail.

Count to three, her mother said.

She hadn’t said it. Not out loud. Not ever, that Alea knew. But she’d done it. At the flap of every tent on the border, her father had told her on a slope above Valcrest last summer, the night before the Lists, with the city’s lamps below: she’d stand at the flap of the infirmary tent with her hands together and her ears flat and count to three, in her head, and then go in and be the best healer on the border for twelve hours. Then count to three.

One.

She let her weight stay on her heels. She didn’t fight it. You couldn’t fight it; it was the sand, it didn’t move, and her body wanted to sit on it. Fine. Let it sit. She’d learned on a boat that you didn’t hold the thing. You let it hold you.

Two.

The sword came. She didn’t take it on Astra this time. She didn’t move Astra at all. She stood on the chalk with her weight on her heels and her sword low and waited, the way you wait for a fish, the way she’d told PenguChi in the first week. It’s lower. Like waiting for a fish. Not tight. Not ready. Just there. With everything in her gone down, low, into the thing in her hands, the way it went down into a line when you felt the first touch on the hook and didn’t strike yet.

It went down into Astra.

All of it. She felt it go. The aura she’d been pushing into her feet for years for Spring, and through the whole of her for Bend, all of it, not into her feet, not into her body: down her arms and into the steel. Into the broad black blade her father had cut out of his own sword on an anvil with a king. Into the crooked letters by the grip, Ad astra per aspera. Into the edge.

The edge went silver.

She saw it. Out of the corner of her eye, without looking away from Tamlin Brand. The whole length of Astra’s edge, the broad black slab of it, from the guard to the point the king had forged, went silver, the way water goes silver when the sun comes out from behind a cloud; a thin line of light along the steel, like frost along a window, like the light along Petra’s driftwood stick on the gravel bar, but quieter. Not reaching past the edge. On it. Coating it. The way paint coats a boat.

Three.

The sword came down. Tamlin Brand’s, the guard school’s best cut, the tenth, at her left shoulder.

She lifted Astra into it. Edge on. Not flat. For the first time in the whole bout, edge on.

And his sword stopped.

Not because she’d stopped it. Because it had met an edge that wasn’t going to give, that was coated in something that wasn’t steel and wasn’t her and was both, and it simply couldn’t go any further, the way an axe can’t go through an anvil. It stopped dead on Astra’s silver edge with a sound like a bell struck with a stone, and the shock of it went back up Tamlin Brand’s arm, and his weight, all of it, everything he’d been driving into her for ten cuts like a nail, had nowhere to go.

It went forward.

He came forward with it. A hair. Onto his toes.

And her weight was on her heels.

She’d been sitting on them for ten cuts. She’d let it. She’d let the sand hold her on them. And now everything she’d sat down into came back up, all at once, the way a boat comes back up off the bottom of a swell, and she went forward. Not sideways. Not from under. Straight forward, off her heels, with everything she’d been sitting on behind her, the way you’d come up off the thwart of a boat when the swell lifts you, into the place where Tamlin Brand’s weight had just gone onto his toes.

The flat of Astra touched him on the chest. Very lightly.

“Touch,” said the marshal.

She didn’t move for a moment.

She stood in the chalk ring with Astra’s flat on his chest and the silver still running along its edge, and Tamlin Brand standing very still with his sword still up and his weight on his toes and his face doing something she’d seen it do before, last year, in the semi: the face of somebody who’d done everything right and found out it was still not quite enough.

But it wasn’t the same face. Not quite. There was something else in it this time.

“You sat on your heels,” said Tamlin. Very quietly. “On purpose.”

“Not on purpose,” said Alea. “I just let it.”

He looked at the silver on her edge. At her face. At the whole of her, the whole thing, the way he’d learned.

“I’ll look at the whole thing again,” said Tamlin Brand. “Next year.”

“I know you will,” said Alea. “I’ll be in the stands.” She took Astra off his chest. “You’ll win it. Next year. You’ll be the holder.” She was quiet. “You’ll have to sit on that bench for four days. It’s horrible. Nobody tells you.”

Tamlin looked at her for a long moment.

Then he laughed. A surprised short laugh, a cadet’s laugh, that the guard school had probably tried to drill out of him and hadn’t. And put his sword down in the sand at his feet, point first, the way Garth of Ashby had, and saluted her, fist to the chest. And walked off the sand with his back very straight.

She didn’t look at it. She knew what it said. She’d felt it go down into the steel.

Wait.

Of course. Of course that was the word. The first thing she’d ever said about aura to any of them, to PenguChi, in the first week. It’s lower. Like waiting for a fish.

The bowl was making a noise. All of it. Forty thousand people on their feet, and the rods going up, all of them at once this time, all round, forty thousand paper fishing rods with paper fish on the end of their lines, up over every head in the Lists in the gold light of the last afternoon of midsummer.

She looked down at Astra in her hands.

The silver was going. Slowly. Off the edge, the way frost goes off a window when the sun gets to it. But the edge was still there. Sharp. Bright. Not a mark on it from ten of the guard school’s best cuts. Not dulled, not nicked, not a hair out of true. It would never dull now, she understood, looking at it. Not while she was holding it. Not while she was Honed. Her father’s sword. Her mother’s stone’s other half. Coated in her.

She’d won.

Then she looked.

She’d decided she wouldn’t, all week, and she’d kept it all week, and she’d won without it. She’d sat on her heels with the chalk at her back and counted to three and waited and won, and she hadn’t once looked anywhere but at the boy in front of her. It was hers. This one was hers. Nobody could say her back had gone easy because somebody was watching. Her back had been on the chalk.

So now she could look.

She turned round. On the main sand of the Lists, in the middle of the chalk ring, with Astra in her hands and forty thousand paper fishing rods held up all round her, she turned round to the east stand, and lifted her face to the fourth tier.

He was standing up.

In his place. Where he’d sat last year, with a woman with a baby beside him, who’d moved over for him without being asked. A plain man in an old coat, standing, with his hands at his sides.

She couldn’t see his face. She never had. She’d never needed to.

She saw the rest of him. She’d been reading the rest of him all her life. The shoulders. The hands. The way he stood. And she saw, from the middle of the main sand, across forty paces of sand and four tiers of stand and forty thousand paper fishing rods, exactly what the rest of him was doing.

He wasn’t holding anything.

He’d left the rod behind the blue door off the Street of Coopers, she’d find out later. He’d come round the bowl and sat down in his place in front of everyone with nothing in his hands at all. And he was standing there now, with his empty hands at his sides, not lifting anything, not doing anything, the way he’d stood on the quay below the mill with his empty hand up; and she understood, looking at him, that he’d sat in the north stand all week, at the back, where she couldn’t see him, so that she’d stand on the sand without him.

And then, for the final, he’d moved.

Not so she’d look. He’d known she wouldn’t. He’d moved so that if she did, at the end, after it was hers, he’d be where she’d know to find him.

She stood in the middle of the main sand with her eyes stinging and the whole of her back, she felt it, going easy. All at once. Like a horse with the saddle off.

It was all right. It was after. It was allowed to go easy after.

Up on the fourth tier, her father lifted his hand.

Just his hand. Empty. Open. Up beside his face, the way he’d lifted it on the quay below the mill when the Patient Widow went round the bend.

She lifted hers back.

And all round the bowl of the Lists, forty thousand people who’d kept a thing for a week, very kindly, looked from the girl in the middle of the sand to the man on the fourth tier and back, and understood that they didn’t have to keep it any more; and the noise they made then was not a noise Alea had ever heard a crowd make, before or after, and she didn’t have a word for it, and didn’t need one.
