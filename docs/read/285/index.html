The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 10: The Sword's Country

Chapter 285

What He Carries

5,723 words · 25 min read

The Advisor had picked something up.

Aleaka felt it at breakfast. She wasn’t looking for it; she never looked, these days, unless she meant to, because it was rude, and because if you went through a castle feeling what everybody carried you’d be in bed by noon with a headache like a nail through your eye. But some things you felt whether you meant to or not. Big feelings carried, she’d told Chris once, for his page; they carried the way Brisk’s temper carried across the whole mews. And the thin grey man at the far end of the hall, at the high table, alone, with a cup of something he wasn’t drinking, had picked something up in the night that was so heavy she felt it from the door.

She stopped in the doorway. Brisk, on her shoulder, stopped too, and put his feathers up.

“What,” said Milli, behind her.

“Nothing.” She went in and sat down. “Eat your eggs.”

She didn’t look again. She didn’t need to. She’d known the shape of the Advisor’s weight for three and a half years, ever since the hall, the first morning, when she’d felt it come off him across forty paces of stone floor like cold off a river, and believed every word he said because of it. People who are lying don’t look that sorry. It had always been the same. A great deal of it, more than anybody in the castle, more than anybody she’d met except one man at a gate in Valcrest; and most of it, nearly all of it, one thing. One heavy covered thing, that he’d carried so long it had worn a groove in him.

That was still there.

But there was something on top of it now. New. Not as big. Sharper, the way a new stone in your boot is sharper than an old one you’ve stopped feeling. And it hadn’t been there yesterday.

Across the table, Cursiffe was eating his eggs very slowly and not looking at the high table either. He had his hand flat on his jacket, over the inside pocket. He’d had it there for a week.

She’d always been able to tell how heavy. That was the trouble.

It was what Accord was made of. She’d found that out in the first year, with a hawk nobody else could go near and a grey stable horse that bit, and six castle cats who followed her into a lord’s banquet like an honour guard. You felt what the animal was carrying, its anger or its fear or its plain hunger, and you didn’t take it away from it, because you couldn’t; you just carried it with it for a moment. Picked up the other end. And the animal felt the weight go lighter, and looked at you, and something between the two of you agreed.

With animals it worked every time. She’d stopped counting by the second winter. Brisk. The vixen, who’d come out of the castle woods in the autumn with a hurt foot and never gone back. The grey horse. The cats. A dog in the Lantern Quarter that had gone for Jared’s leg and then sat down and put its head on her knee. A heron, once, on the river, which had been the most surprising afternoon of her life.

With people, it didn’t.

She could feel what they carried. She’d always been able to. How much. Whether it was heavy or light, new or old, grief or fear or the dull flat weight of a thing somebody was ashamed of. But not what it was. With people it was like lifting a basket somebody had put a cloth over: she could tell what it weighed, and nothing else. And when she tried to pick up the other end, the way she did for Brisk, it slid. Or it came all at once, everything, a jumble, and she was on the floor with blood coming out of her nose and Hana holding her head, which had happened twice in the first year and once more in the autumn, with Milli.

Three times in ten, maybe. With people. If she was lucky, and they were someone she loved, and she didn’t try too hard.

The window called that Spark. It had for two years.

Candle, Oswin’s books said. It works every time.

She’d had a theory since the autumn. She’d written it in the back of the little book where she kept the animals’ names, under the heron.

Animals say yes.

That was the difference. She’d thought about it for a long time and that was all she could find. When she reached for Brisk, Brisk knew. He felt her coming the way he felt weather, and he decided, and when he decided yes, the other end came up into her hands like a thing on a string. People didn’t know. They didn’t know she was reaching; they didn’t feel anything; and so the thing they were carrying didn’t know whose hands were coming for it, and slid, or came all at once.

So: ask. Out loud.

She’d read it in the channel, in the second year, about Rachael. She asks first. Every time. Even if you’re bleeding. She says “can I” and waits. Calvin had written it, laughing, after Rachael had stood over him on the quay with her hands out while he bled into his collar and said can I three times until he said yes, God, yes, Rachael. Aleaka had read it and thought nothing of it. Then she’d thought about it for a year.

She’d tried it on Milli, in the autumn.

Can I? He’d said yes. And she’d reached, and he’d let her, and it had still come all at once, everything he was carrying in a great heap, Hana and the way home and a cat demihuman’s ears in a human kingdom and the Lists and a hundred small things, and she’d ended up on the floor of Hana’s room with her nose bleeding and Milli on his knees saying sorry, sorry, I didn’t do anything, I just said yes.

He hadn’t done anything wrong. She’d lain on the floor and thought about it, with Hana’s hand on her head, and understood that it wasn’t the asking. The asking was right. It was what she’d done after. He’d said yes, and she’d gone and tried to pick up all of it. The whole basket. Every bit of what Milli carried, at once, the way you’d try to pick up a table on your own.

You didn’t pick up a table on your own. You took one end.

She’d written that under Animals say yes, in the autumn. One end. Not the table. And under it, after Jared on the stair, in the last week, with Cursiffe’s ink still drying on Jared’s arm: Only where it lands.

She hadn’t tried again since. She’d been afraid to. Ninety-six. She’d been ninety-six since midwinter.

The Advisor came down to the east wing that evening.

He didn’t, as a rule. In three and a half years she could count the times on one hand: the night the king opened Aldemar’s door, and once when Hana had been ill. He was a man who sent for people. He sat at the top of the west tower with his map and his drawer and his letters to a man at a lake asking about hens, and people went up to him.

He came down. He knocked on the door of the copying room, where Oswin and Cursiffe had been all day with their heads together over the register, and stood in it, thin and grey, with his hands folded in front of him the way he folded them at the Council table.

“Oswin,” he said. “Thank you for your report.”

Oswin stood up very fast and knocked his ink over.

“And Cursiffe,” said the Advisor. He looked at Cursiffe at the end of the table, who hadn’t stood up, who sat with his hand flat on his jacket over the inside pocket and looked back. “Thank you also. Oswin tells me the purse book was yours. The asking.”

“It was a question,” said Cursiffe. “I just asked it.”

“Yes,” said the Advisor. “That’s the part I never did.”

He didn’t say anything else. He stood in the door a moment longer, looking at the two of them, and at the copies of the register spread on the table, the fifty-six frames, the little dense marks in the middle of each one. Then he nodded, and turned, and went away down the passage toward the stair.

Aleaka was in the passage. She’d come along it with Brisk and a dish of scraps for the vixen and stopped when she saw him in the door, and stood against the wall to let him past. He saw her. He inclined his head, the way he did to all of them, very correctly.

And as he went past her, a pace off, she felt it.

Not the old thing. The old thing was always there. The new thing, on top of it. Close up, it wasn’t a stone in a boot at all. It had a shape. She could feel the edges of the shape through the cloth over the basket, for the first time with a person, without reaching; it was so near and so sharp. Long, and narrow, and heavy at one end, and cold.

And under it, holding it up, a feeling she knew from her own body. From the night in the autumn, on the floor of Hana’s room, with her nose bleeding. The feeling of somebody trying to carry a whole table on their own.

“Advisor,” she said.

He stopped. At the top of the stair. He turned round.

She didn’t know what she was going to say. She stood in the passage with the dish of scraps and Brisk on her shoulder glaring at him, and looked at the thin grey man at the top of the stair, and thought about Jared on the Council stair a week ago with the iron shoe of a staff against his chest, not moving.

“Can I?” she said.

He knew what she meant. That was the strange thing. She hadn’t said what. But he looked at her for a long moment from the top of the stair, with his hands folded, and she saw him understand: the way he’d understood in the hall, the first morning, when she’d looked at him across forty paces and he’d looked back and known that she could see it.

“You saw it,” he said. “In the hall.”

“Yes.”

“I wondered. You were the only one who looked at me as if you were sorry for me.” He was quiet. “I’ve never known what to do with that.”

“I’m not sorry for you,” said Aleaka. “Not exactly. I just know how heavy it is.”

He didn’t say anything for a while.

“Most of it isn’t mine to show you,” he said. “Most of what I carry. It belongs to somebody else. I’ve carried it for him for a long time because he couldn’t, and he’s never asked me to put it down, and I won’t, until he does.”

“I don’t want that bit,” said Aleaka. She meant it. She’d felt the edges of the old thing for three and a half years, the big covered weight with a groove worn in it, and she’d never once wanted to know what was under the cloth. Some things you didn’t lift. “Not the old one. The new one. The one on top.” She swallowed. “You’re trying to carry it on your own. All of it. And it’s not that heavy, it’s not as heavy as the other one, but it’s sharp, and you’ve got it on top, and you’re holding it like a table. I can feel it from here.”

The Advisor stood at the top of the stair.

“It came in Oswin’s report,” he said, at last.

“I know.”

“About a sword.”

“I know,” said Aleaka. “Cursiffe’s had his hand on his pocket for a week.”

Something happened at the corner of the Advisor’s mouth. She’d never seen it before. She didn’t know if he’d ever smiled in his life; if he had, it had been somewhere else.

“Come up,” he said.

His study was at the top of the west tower, up a stair that went round and round inside the wall so long that Brisk got bored and went to sleep on her shoulder. It was a small room with two windows and a fire and a long table covered in letters, and between the windows, on the plaster, a map.

She’d heard about the map. Everybody had. The kingdom of Aurelan from the sea to the Greyspine, six feet by four, on oiled linen, nailed to the wall. He’d made it smaller every year for twenty years, people said. Not the map; the kingdom on it. He’d shaded out what was lost. At the bottom, in the south, below the downs, there was an inch of blank linen with nothing on it at all, where the elves had come from. And on the right, the east, past the Redmarch, the map went black. Not shaded. Black, the way old mapmakers did for nothing here. All the way to the edge of the linen.

He stood in front of the fire with his back to it.

“How does it go?” he said.

“I don’t know, with people. Not really.” She put the dish of scraps down on the end of the table, among the letters, and Brisk woke up and looked at it, and decided against it. “With animals I just pick up the other end. They say yes, and I take one end of whatever they’ve got, and for a moment we’re both carrying it, and I can see what it is.” She looked at him. “With people it slides. Or it all comes at once and I fall over. I think it’s because I try to take all of it.” She took a breath. “So I’m only going to take one end. Of the new thing. Not the old one. Not the whole table.”

“And then?”

“And then I’ll know what it is. And you’ll know somebody knows. And it’ll be a bit lighter.” She stopped. “That’s all. That’s all it’s ever done. It doesn’t take anything away. It just means you’re not on your own with it for a minute.”

The Advisor looked at her across the small room for a long time.

“Yes,” he said.

She didn’t reach.

That was the first thing. She’d always reached, with people; leaned toward them, the way you lean to pick something up off a high shelf. She didn’t. She stood where she was, in front of the long table, with her hands open at her sides, and waited for him to put it down where she could get at it. The way Brisk did. The way the vixen had, in the castle wood in the autumn, lying on her side with her hurt foot out, watching her come, and deciding.

And she felt him decide.

It wasn’t anything he did. He stood with his back to the fire and his hands folded and his face showing nothing; but the new thing, the long narrow cold thing on top of the old one, shifted. The way a man carrying a table on his own shifts it when somebody comes up on the other side, and he lets one end tilt toward them. Just one end. Not the whole of it.

She took it.

Only that end. Only where it was tilting toward her. She didn’t go round the side for more. She didn’t lift the cloth off the old one underneath, though she felt it there, huge, with its groove worn in it, a breath away. She took the one end he’d let tilt toward her, and held it, and let it be heavy.

And she saw.

A gate.

She was on a gate. On the top of it, the walk along the top of a stone gate in a fort, at dawn, at the end of a summer, with the air already cold and the light coming up grey and long behind her. A fort on the end of the world. She knew it the way you know a place in a dream: the walls going away on either side, every colour of grey; the yard behind, with its well and its wagons; and in front, east, past the gate, a road going out across a plain that was red. Red grass, going away to the edge of the world. And past the edge, black.

She was young. That was the strange part. She was a young man, thin, in a court mage’s grey coat that was too good for the Redmarch and too thin for it; and her hand, his hand, was on the stone of the gate, on a ward he’d laid there himself, the first winter he’d come, a careful ward, a good one, a young man’s ward who’d done everything properly. And the ward was singing.

Like a glass with a wet finger going round the rim. Higher and higher. Something was coming up the road.

She looked. He looked.

A boy.

Walking up the road out of the red, out of the east, at a walk, slowly, in the dawn. A garrison boy in a soldier’s grey coat gone black with dirt, nineteen, she knew it, he knew it, he’d watched the boy come into this fort at fifteen with a borrowed sword and a bad temper and he’d known him four years. Thin. Thinner than in the spring. With his hands wrapped in rags gone brown and red. And over his shoulder, a strap. And on the end of the strap, dragging behind him in the dust of the road, a sword.

As long as he was. Longer. Black. The way the bottom of a well is black.

It had cut a line in the road. All the way back. She could see it, from the gate, in the long grey light: a groove in the dirt of the road, straight, coming out of the red, out of the east, as far as she could see, behind the boy and the sword, like the line a plough leaves. Out of the black. All the way.

The ward sang so high it hurt her teeth.

And the boy stopped under the gate, and looked up.

At her. At the young court mage on the walk, with his hand on his singing ward. She couldn’t hold his face. Nobody could, then; it slid off the young court mage’s eyes the way it had slid off hers at a gate in Valcrest, like rain off a window. But she knew him by the rest. The shoulders. The way he stood, with his weight on both feet, waiting. Nineteen, with the dirt on him, and very tired.

“Open it, Halvard,” said the boy.

She felt him not open it.

That was the thing he’d carried. She knew it the moment she felt it, the way you know a stone in your boot the moment you put your foot down. Not the boy. Not the sword. That.

He stood on the gate with his hand on his ward and didn’t open it. Because the ward was singing. Because the thing on the end of the strap was making the stone sing under his hand like nothing he’d ever felt in his life, and the dogs in the yard behind him had gone under the wagons, all of them, at once, whining, and the horse-lines were screaming. Because he was young, and he’d done everything properly, and nothing in all his proper careful reading had ever told him what to do when a garrison boy came out of the black after a whole summer with a thing like that on a strap and said open it.

He was afraid of it.

He stood there. A count of ten. Twenty. She felt every one of them, the whole long count, his hand on the stone and the boy under the gate looking up at him with his hands bleeding through the rags, not saying anything else. Not asking again. Just waiting.

Thirty.

And then he opened it.

He took his hand off the ward and went down the gate-tower stair and drew the bar himself, because there was nobody else; the gate guard had gone under the wagons with the dogs. And the boy walked in. Past him. Dragging the sword. The line it cut went on in through the gate and across the yard, through the dust, past the well. And the young court mage stood in the gate passage and watched him go, and didn’t say anything. Didn’t ask where. Didn’t ask what. Didn’t ask anything at all.

Across the yard a door opened. The infirmary. A young woman came out of it with a baby on her hip. Ears. A fox girl. She stood in the door and saw him, the boy, and the sword, and she put the baby down on the step and came across the yard and hit him in the chest with both her fists, hard, and then sat down in the dust and cried.

The young court mage turned away. He didn’t watch the rest. He went back up on to the gate, and put his hand on his ward, which had stopped singing, and stood there in the dawn with his back to the yard.

That night he wrote it in the fort’s log, in his careful hand. Trooper Ars returned from the east this morning, alone, with a weapon of unknown make. His hands are hurt. The infirmary has him.

And the next week, when the boy came up on to the gate to stand his watch beside him, with his hands stitched, and stood there a long time looking east, and said, without looking round, don’t ask me, the young court mage didn’t. And didn’t the week after. Or the year after. Or the eighteen after that.

He’d never asked. Not once. He’d kept a gate shut for a count of thirty on a boy with bleeding hands because he was afraid of what was on the end of the strap, and then he’d spent eighteen years not asking what it was, because the boy had said don’t, and because he was afraid of the answer.

And now Oswin had told him.

A forge in the north yard of this castle, three hundred years ago. Two people from another world, who’d asked a king every day for nine years to send them home, and stopped asking, and made a thing to hold the mouth, and taken it east, and never come back.

And Halvard had stood in a hall three and a half years ago and pulled thirty-five more of them out of that world. Out of their lives. Without asking. And he’d never once, in eighteen years, asked the one who was already here what he’d carried out of the black.

That’s the part I never did, he’d said, in the copying-room door.

She put it down.

Gently. The one end. The way you put down your end of a table when the other person’s ready, so it doesn’t drop on their feet. She felt him take the weight of it back. And she felt, as he took it, that it was lighter. Not much. Not gone. It was his; it would always be his. But it had been carried by two people for a moment, and somebody knew, and it sat on top of the old thing a little easier, the way a stone sits easier when somebody’s seen it in your boot.

She was standing in the Advisor’s study, at the top of the west tower, in front of the long table. Her nose wasn’t bleeding. Her head didn’t hurt. She was standing up.

Brisk was on her shoulder with his feathers flat, for once, looking at the Advisor.

The Advisor was standing with his back to the fire. His face hadn’t changed; it never did. But his hands had come apart. They’d been folded in front of him the whole time she’d known him, three and a half years, at the Council and in the hall and at the door of the copying room. They weren’t now. They were at his sides.

“Thirty,” said Aleaka. Very quietly.

He looked at her.

“You counted to thirty,” she said. “On the gate. Before you opened it.”

“Yes,” said the Advisor.

“He waited.”

“He always waited.” The Advisor looked at the fire. “Even then. He’d wait for you. He’d stand under a gate with his hands bleeding and wait for you to be brave enough. And he’d never say a word about it afterwards.” He was quiet. “He never has. Eighteen years. He’s never once said you kept me waiting. He just said don’t ask me. And I didn’t.”

“Because you were afraid.”

“Because I was afraid,” said the Advisor. “Of the answer. And then because he’d asked me not to. And then because it had been so long that asking would have been a kind of accusation, and I’d sooner have carried it.” He turned his head and looked at the map on the wall. At the black, on the right, past the Redmarch, going to the edge of the linen. “And now I know anyway. Without asking. Because a boy with ink on his hands asked a different question, about a dead king’s purse.”

Aleaka didn’t say anything.

“It was holding something,” said the Advisor. “Wasn’t it. Out there. To hold the mouth. It was holding something, and he pulled it out, and dragged it home, and I opened the gate.” He was very still. “And then I filled a hall with his friends.”

“You didn’t know.”

“No,” said the Advisor. “I didn’t ask.”

She went down the stair a long time later. Round and round, inside the wall, with Brisk awake on her shoulder and the dish of scraps forgotten on the Advisor’s table among his letters.

At the bottom of the stair she stopped and leaned against the cold stone of the wall and opened her window.

She knew what it would say. She’d known on the gate, on the walk at the top, in the dawn, with a young man’s hand under hers on a singing ward. She’d known it the way she knew Brisk was on her shoulder. Not because the bar would have moved. Because she’d stood in a room with a person and asked, and waited, and been given one end of a thing, and taken only that, and held it steady, and seen it whole. And she knew that if she went back up that stair tomorrow and asked again, it would work again. And with anyone. If they said yes.

Heft.

She laughed. Out loud, on her own, at the bottom of the west tower stair, with her hand over her mouth. It was such an ordinary word. It was what you did in a market with a bag of potatoes: pick it up, feel the weight of it, put it down. Heft. Not carry. Not take. Just: pick up the other end, and feel what it weighs, and know.

She tested it the way Oswin’s books said you should. Ten times. That night, and the next morning, all through the east wing, asking every time, out loud, can I?, and waiting, and taking one end.

Brisk: his temper, which was mostly about the vixen. The vixen: a dream about her foot. One of the castle cats: hunger, and a mouse behind the bread-oven. Milli, who said yes and then wait, what are you going to find, and she found the way home, the whole of it, how much he wanted to be told it was real; and she put it down gently and he looked at her and said oh, and then thank you. Jared, who was carrying almost nothing at all now and laughed when she told him. Hana, who carried the cords; all of them; every guide in the kingdom; she’d known that, but not that it felt like holding seven warm cups at once and being afraid of spilling one. Cursiffe.

She stopped at Cursiffe.

He’d said yes. He was sitting at the copying-room table with his hand flat on his jacket, and he’d looked up at her and said yes, the way he said everything, slowly and calmly. And she’d stood there with her hands open and waited for him to tilt an end toward her. And she’d felt it. A folded thing. In his pocket, under his hand. Folded in four, very carefully, beside an old letter.

She felt the shape of it. That it wasn’t his.

“No,” she said. “I don’t want that one.”

Cursiffe looked at her.

“It’s somebody else’s,” said Aleaka. “You’re just holding it for them. I can feel that it’s not yours.” She put her hands down. “Give me something of yours.”

He thought about it. Then he gave her a woman in a cold forge three hundred years ago, with her hand on a star, writing one word on it with a little loop at the end like a girl finishing her name; and how much he’d wanted, in the archive, to have been able to tell her that somebody had read it.

Ten.

Ten out of ten.

It cost her. She found that out in the morning.

Not the way it had, the three times she’d gone on the floor; not blood, not a nail through the eye. Something smaller and stranger. For three days afterwards she kept hearing a glass singing. Very faint, at the edge of things, high, the way the ward had sung on the gate; in the mews, in the copying room, once in the middle of the night, so that she sat up in bed with her heart going. And twice, crossing the castle yard, she flinched for no reason she could see, and only afterwards understood that she’d been expecting the dogs to go under the wagons.

It faded. Rachael would have known what to call it: an echo. The sting where the cut was. You carried a thing with somebody for a moment and a little of it stayed on your hands.

She didn’t mind. She’d have minded once. She’d have thought it meant she’d done it wrong.

She went up the west tower stair on the fourth day, with the dish of scraps, because she’d left it there and the vixen was sulking. The Advisor was at his table with a letter. He didn’t look up when she came in; he said the dish is by the window, and she went and got it. And on the way back, by the door, she stopped, because she couldn’t not.

The old thing was there. It would always be there; it was huge, and covered, and it wasn’t his, and it had a groove worn in him the shape of eighteen years.

The new thing was there too. On top. Long and narrow and cold.

But he wasn’t holding it like a table any more. He was holding it like you’d hold a letter you hadn’t decided how to answer: in one hand, flat, waiting.

“Advisor,” she said.

He looked up.

“He’s going to write to you,” said Aleaka. She didn’t know why she said it. She didn’t know it; she didn’t have anything of the Hero’s, she’d never asked him for anything, she’d met him once at a gate in Valcrest and felt a mountain on a yoke. But she’d felt the end of the Advisor’s thing, the one he’d let tilt, and there’d been something at the very bottom of it, under the gate and the count of thirty and the boy saying don’t ask me. Something very small. A man in a tower who’d written letters to a lake for twenty years asking about hens, and been answered, every time, and never once been written to first.

“I don’t know that,” she said. “I’m sorry. I just think he will.”

The Advisor looked at her across the small room for a long time.

“I’ve been waiting eighteen years,” he said, at last. Very quietly. “I can wait a bit longer.”

He went back to his letter. She took the dish down the long stair.
