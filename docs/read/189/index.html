The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 7: Myths

Chapter 189

Walking Roots

4,627 words · 20 min read

Maigo told the tree things on Thursdays.

It had been a Thursday the first time, or what his window called Thursday; the kingdom had its own names for the days, and the window turned them into the ones he knew, the way it turned everything. The morning he’d carried her out across the red to the grave and planted her, the first spring, with the thaw still in the ground. And every Thursday since, Maigo had gone out to the knoll.

In the summer it was easy. An hour’s walk across the red, with his root foot in the grey grass and his antlers full of leaves and the sky so big over the plain it made his chest hurt. He’d sit on the green at the foot of the cairn with his back against the ash and tell it the week. What Salty had burned. What Rhea had shouted. What Yan had said to Suzu and Suzu had said back, now that Yan passed her on properly, rude bits and all. What the horse had nearly told him its name was, and then hadn’t.

In the winter it was harder. The snow on the red came up to his knees, which on Maigo was most of his legs, and the wind came off the mountains with knives in it, and the hour was two. But he went. Every Thursday. He’d promised. You didn’t stop telling a tree things just because it was asleep.

“It’s asleep,” Wendy had said, the first winter, when he came back blue. “It can’t hear you.”

“It can,” Maigo had said. “It just can’t answer. That’s not the same.”

Wendy had looked at him for a long time and then put another blanket on him and not said anything else, which Maigo had decided meant she agreed.

This Thursday it was snowing.

Not hard. The small dry kind that came down out of a low white sky without any wind behind it, so that the whole red was soft and grey and silent and the markers stood up out of the white in their rows like people waiting for a bell. Maigo went out of the east gate at first light with his hood up and his bells on and the leaves Nell had sewn on his collar crunching with frost, past Teague on the gatehouse, who lifted his good hand.

“Thursday?” said Teague. He’d learned that, the second winter.

“Thursday,” said Maigo.

“Tell it the recruits are useless.”

“They’re not useless,” said Maigo. “Garth’s very good. He stands right.”

“Tell it anyway,” said Teague. “It’s a soldier’s tree. It’ll want to know.”

Maigo thought that was probably true, and said he would.

The red didn’t flinch any more.

That was the thing he’d tell anyone, if anyone asked, and mostly nobody did, because mostly nobody else could feel it. The first winter, the red had flinched under his root foot every step. Like walking across somebody’s bruise. Every step out from the walls, the ground had gone tight and small under him, the way a dog goes small when you raise your hand, and he’d felt all of it: every grave, every marker, every place where somebody had fallen and stayed. He’d cried the whole way out the first time. He hadn’t told anyone. Then he’d told everyone.

It didn’t flinch now. Not since the summer before last, when a man had walked across it with a rod on his shoulder and the grey grass had gone still round his boots. Not since the autumn, when a fox girl had walked beside him and it had gone still round hers too. It was as if the whole plain had been holding its breath for nine years and had finally, carefully, let some of it out.

It was still sad. Maigo could feel that. You didn’t stop being sad because somebody walked across you nicely. But it wasn’t frightened.

He walked across it in the soft grey snow, with the bells on his antlers ringing very quietly, for two hours, and the ground let him.

The ash was asleep.

He’d known it would be. It had gone to sleep in the autumn, at the first hard frost, after the fox girl and her father went west. Every leaf down. Not scattered; laid. In a ring, on the black stone and round its foot, the way you’d lay flowers. Maigo had found them like that the first Thursday after and written it in the channel in capitals and then, underneath, very small, it’s fine. it’s just asleep. I asked.

He stood on the green at the foot of the cairn now, with the snow on the grass, and looked at it. A young tree, taller than Rhea, bare, grey, with its branches going up into the white sky like the fingers of a hand held open. Leaning, very slightly, even asleep, toward the black stone. The way a person leans toward somebody they’re sitting with.

He went and put his hand on it.

Then he sat down in the snow with his back against it, and his root foot flat on the frozen grass, and told it the week.

He told it about the archive. That a man called Cursiffe had walked into a fire in Solmere and written KEEP on a box in English and the fire had gone round it, and then he’d written it on a man, and Maigo had read that in the channel and laughed so hard he fell off his bunk. He told it that a girl in Wrenhaven called Xola had gone under the ice of a lake after a little boy, and found out she could breathe water, and asked the whole lake to go round, and it had. That Group 1 were all five now. That the count was thirty-two.

He told it that the recruits weren’t useless, and that Teague said to tell it they were.

He told it that Salty had been practising the twelfth dinner for three months and the fort smelled of wrong peppers and the cook had threatened to leave twice and not left.

He told it that Yan was sleeping more.

He hadn’t told anybody that. Wendy knew; Wendy kept a list in her head of everything. Maigo didn’t keep lists. But he’d noticed. Yan, who was always the last one up and the first one down. Since the night on the fallen tree, when he went green. An hour more every night, maybe. Maigo had noticed because he got up early on Thursdays, and on Thursdays now, sometimes, Yan’s door was still shut.

“I don’t know what it means,” Maigo told the tree. “Suzu knows. She told Kei’s snake she knows. She won’t tell Yan.” He pulled his knees up. “I’m worried about him. Don’t tell him.”

The tree didn’t say anything. It was asleep.

Maigo leaned his head back against the cold grey bark, with the snow coming down on his face, and closed his eyes, and let his root foot go down.

He did that sometimes. At the end. When he’d told it everything.

It wasn’t anything. Not magic, or not the kind with a window. It was just what his foot did. The root-toes, the wooden ones, that had come through the floor of the hall like that because that was how he’d been drawn, and that had turned out, the first winter, to be the only part of him that could feel the ground. He’d put it down flat and let it go, the way you’d let your hand go slack in warm water. And feel.

The ground under the knoll. Cold, and frozen at the top, and under the frost not frozen. The ash’s roots, going down. He knew them now the way you know the inside of your own pocket. Down through the grass, and the dark red earth under the grass, and round the long black spike of the stone where it went into the ground like a key. And under the stone, round the cold thing.

He didn’t go near the cold thing. He’d felt it once, in the autumn, when the swords rang. A small dense coal of wrong, a long way down, held. The ash’s roots had gone round it that night like fingers round a stone, and held it, and they were still holding. Two hands, he’d told Alea. Three, with the spike. He felt the roots holding it now, in the winter, asleep. Steady. Patient.

He didn’t go near it. He went the other way.

Out. Along the roots. Away from the cairn, under the green, under the grey grass beyond the green, out under the red. The ash’s roots went a long way for a tree so young. Further than he’d have thought. They’d gone out under the plain in the two years since he planted it, the way roots do when nobody’s told them where to stop, and they’d found the old dead roots of whatever had grown on the red before the war, and some of those had found older roots still, under them, older than the war, older than the fort.

And those went south.

He’d never followed them before. He didn’t know why he did now. He was cold, and it was snowing, and he’d told the tree everything, and his foot was down and loose and the roots were there, and he went along them the way you’d follow a path through a wood you didn’t know because it was there. South. Under the red. To the edge of the red, where the grey grass stopped and the downs started. And under the downs.

The downs were full of roots.

Maigo had walked across the downs three times. Twice in a cart and once on foot. He’d talked to every sheep on them. He’d never once put his foot down on them like this and felt what was under the grass, because on the downs he’d been busy, and talking, and looking at sheep.

He felt it now. A long way off, through the ash’s roots and the old roots under the red and the older roots under those. The downs. Mile after mile of grass, and under the grass, chalk, and under the chalk and through it, roots. Old ones. Thorn and hazel and the deep slow roots of oaks that had stood at the edges of the downs for longer than anybody had been counting. All of them, touching. All of them, all the way south. Like a crowd of people in the dark, holding hands.

And something was coming up through them.

He felt it as a pressure first. The way you feel somebody walk into a room behind you before you hear them. A long way south. Further than the downs; past the end of them, where Maigo had never been, where Aldous’s map stopped and somebody had drawn a few trees and written nothing. Something there, under the roots, or on top of them, moving.

Moving north.

He held very still against the ash with his eyes shut and his foot down and felt it come.

Not fast. Steady. The way a person walks who’s been walking for a very long time and isn’t going to stop. Step, and step, and step. And with every step, the roots under the step knew it. Felt it go over. And told the next roots. And those told the next. All the way up the downs, root to root, oak to thorn to hazel to the old dead roots under the red to the ash. The way a message goes down a line of people in the dark: passed hand to hand, whispered, and nobody in the line knowing what it means, only that it’s important and it has to get to the end.

Walking, said the roots.

Maigo opened his eyes.

That was all it was. Not a word. Roots didn’t have words. But he’d been listening to them for two years and a half, and he knew what they meant the way Yan knew what Suzu meant, now, out loud. Walking. Something walking. On the roots. North. A long way off. And the roots didn’t flinch for it.

That was the thing. That was the thing that made him put his hand flat on the cold bark behind him and hold on.

They didn’t flinch. When a man walked across the downs, the roots felt it and didn’t care. When a horse went over, a cart, a flock of sheep, the roots felt it and didn’t care. When the cold things came over the red at night, the turned, the scouts, the roots flinched: tight and small and frightened, the way the red had flinched under him the first winter.

This wasn’t either. The roots didn’t ignore it, and they didn’t flinch.

They leaned.

Toward it. All the way up the downs. The way the ash leaned toward the black stone. The way, Yan said, souls leaned toward what they loved. Every root it walked over turning, very slightly, in the cold earth, toward the feet going over it, and then passing it on. Walking. Walking. Here. Here.

Like they knew it.

Like they’d been waiting for it a very long time, and had nearly forgotten, and hadn’t.

He didn’t remember getting up. He was on his feet in the snow at the foot of the cairn with his hand still on the ash, and the bells on his antlers ringing all at once because he’d stood up too fast, and his heart going.

“Did you feel that?” said Maigo. To the tree. Out loud. “Did you—”

The tree was asleep.

But it had moved. He saw it. He’d swear to it afterwards, to anyone, to Wendy, to Aldous, to the king if he ever met the king again. The ash was asleep, bare and grey in the falling snow, and it had moved. Not much. Not a branch. The whole of it. Very slightly. The way a person asleep turns over, without waking, toward a sound they half know.

It had been leaning toward the black stone.

It was leaning south.

He ran back.

He didn’t run well. Nobody built like Maigo ran well; his legs were short and one of his feet was a root and the snow was most of the way to his knees. He ran anyway. Two hours across the red, in one and a half, with the bells going and his breath coming out of him in clouds and his hood blown back and the leaves on his collar coming loose one by one behind him in the snow. He fell over three times. He got up three times. Teague saw him coming from the gatehouse a mile out and had the postern open before he got there.

“What,” said Teague.

“Something’s walking,” said Maigo.

Teague looked at him.

“Something’s walking,” said Maigo again, louder, because Teague hadn’t moved. “In the south. Past the downs. On the roots. Coming north. And the roots aren’t frightened of it, Teague, they’re leaning, they’re all leaning toward it, every one, the whole way up the downs, like it’s—like it’s—“ He ran out of air. He stood in the postern with his hands on his knees.

Teague looked south. At the downs. Grey and white and silent under the snow, going away in their long low folds to the edge of the world.

“I don’t see anything,” said Teague.

“You wouldn’t,” said Maigo. “It’s three weeks away. Maybe four. It’s walking.” He straightened up. “I have to tell Yan.”

He went past Teague into the fort.

Behind him, he heard Teague, very quietly, not to anyone: “Tell it the recruits are useless. Gods.”

They were at supper. The long hall. The east fire. The table where Group 3 had sat since the first winter. Aldous at the end with his book. Salty, smelling faintly of the wrong peppers. Wendy with her silent headphones round her neck. Niko with his hand out flat beside his bowl, counting heartbeats. Rhea across from Salty, looking at his arm.

And Yan. With Suzu round his neck. Eating slowly.

Maigo came in out of the snow with his hood down and his bells ringing and half his collar leaves gone and stood at the end of the table, dripping.

“Something’s walking,” he said.

Everyone looked at him.

He told them. All of it. Too fast, the way he told everything, out of order, with his hands. The ash. The roots. The downs, full of roots, holding hands in the dark. The thing in the south, past the end of Aldous’s map. The steps. The roots passing it on. Walking. Here. Not flinching. Leaning. The tree turning over in its sleep toward the south.

Nobody said anything when he’d finished.

Aldous had put his spoon down. Salty had stopped chewing. Rhea, for once in her life, didn’t say anything loud.

And Wendy.

Wendy was looking at Yan. Not at Maigo. At Yan, and at the white snake round his neck. And Maigo saw what she was looking at, and went cold all over in a way that had nothing to do with the snow.

Suzu had lifted her head.

She’d been looking south since the song. Everybody in the fort knew. Ten weeks, nearly. Through the wall, through the snow, toward the downs, every day, with her head a finger’s width off Yan’s shoulder. Maigo had stopped noticing it, the way you stop noticing a clock.

She’d turned her head. Toward him. Toward Maigo, dripping at the end of the table with half his leaves gone. And she was looking at him with her two green eyes, very still, the way she’d looked at the man with the rod on the red, once, before she laid her head on his boot.

“Yan,” said Wendy. Very quietly. “What’s she saying?”

Yan didn’t answer at once. He had his hand on Suzu. He was listening; you could see him listen, now, now that everyone knew what it was. The way Kei listened to Hisui. His face didn’t do anything. Yan’s face never did.

“She says soon,” said Yan.

The table was very quiet.

“She says Maigo heard them,” said Yan. “She says she’s been able to feel them since the song, but she didn’t know where they were. She says the roots know where they are.” He was quiet a moment. “She says good tree.”

Maigo felt his face go hot under the bark.

“She means the ash,” he said.

Yan listened.

“She says she means both of you,” he said.

Wendy said it, after.

At the end of the table, when the others had gone up, and it was just the four of them who always stayed: Wendy and Salty and Maigo and Yan, by the east fire, with the hall emptying round them and the cook banging pots in the kitchen. Wendy sat with her hands round her cup and her silent headphones round her neck and looked at the fire for a long time.

“Twice,” she said.

Maigo didn’t know what she meant. He looked at Salty. Salty was looking at Wendy, very steadily, the way he looked at her when she said something that mattered.

“In the snow,” said Wendy. “The night of the song. She looked south, and I went up on the wall and stood there for an hour and there was nothing, and I put it in the column. Once.” She turned her cup round. “I said I’d be watching for twice.”

“Is this twice?” said Salty.

Wendy looked at Maigo. At his root foot, still wet from the snow, and his collar with half its leaves gone, and his bells.

“The roots are leaning toward it,” she said. “The whole way up the downs. Like they know it.” She breathed out. “That’s twice.”

Nobody said anything for a while.

“I’m a skeptic,” said Wendy, to the fire. “I’ve always been a skeptic. It’s what I’m for.” She put her cup down. “I don’t think I’m going to be allowed to be one much longer.”

Salty put his arm round her.

Maigo went out to the postern every night after that.

Not to the grave; it was too far, in the dark, in the snow. Just to the postern in the west wall, where the road came in from the downs. He’d sit on the step inside it, with his hood up and his bells muffled in a scarf Nell had given him for the purpose, and put his root foot out through the gap under the gate onto the frozen ground outside. Flat. Loose. And listen.

It was harder from here. The fort was stone, and stone didn’t have roots. But the road had verges, and the verges had grass, and the grass had roots, and the roots went out under the downs to the old ones, and the old ones were still passing it along, every night, a little closer. Walking. Walking. Here.

He told the channel on the third night. Not in capitals, which surprised everyone, including him.

On the fifth night he was sure there were three.

He could feel the steps, now, close enough to count. Three people. Walking north. Not together, exactly; one in front, and two behind, a little apart, the way people walk who’ve walked a long way together and don’t need to talk. The one in front walked very steadily. The second walked lightly. The third walked so lightly the roots barely knew, and then leaned toward her harder than toward either of the others, as if they knew her best.

On the eighth night they reached the downs.

He felt it the moment they did. The way you feel a boat leave the shore and come onto open water. The old roots under the chalk, the oaks at the edge of the downs, the thorn and the hazel, all of them, all at once, waking. Not really. Not in the winter. But turning over in their sleep, the way the ash had, all the way up the long grey folds of the downs, toward the three sets of feet coming up the road from the south.

And something under the snow, where they’d walked, going green.

He couldn’t see it. He could feel it. Under the snow on the verges of the road over the downs, where three people had walked that day, the grass, the frozen winter grass, going green. A line of it. Under the white. All the way up the road from the south toward Cinderhold, like a thread somebody had pulled through cloth.

Maigo sat on the step of the postern with his foot out under the gate and his hand over his mouth.

On the twentieth night, he went and found Rhea.

She was on the west gate. She was always on the west gate on the late watch now; she’d taken it after Teague’s arm, she said, because somebody had to and she was bored. She was standing in the gatehouse arch with her sword on her shoulder and her breath going up, looking down the road toward the downs in the dark, and she looked round when she heard his bells.

“Thursday?” said Rhea.

“It’s not Thursday,” said Maigo. “It’s tomorrow.”

“What’s tomorrow?”

Maigo looked down the road. White, between its verges, going away south into the dark over the first long hill of the downs. And under the white, all the way from the top of that hill to the edge of the world, a line of green that only he could feel.

“Visitors,” said Maigo.

Rhea looked at him.

She was loud, Rhea. She was the loudest person he knew, in two worlds, louder than Tiger, louder than Maigo, who was loud. She turned everything into a contest. She’d arm-wrestled Salty every night for two years and the man with the rod every night for five weeks and she’d shouted CANDLE at the top of her voice at the foot of a grave and then put both hands over her mouth.

She didn’t shout now.

“What kind of visitors?” she said.

“I don’t know,” said Maigo. “Three. They walk soft. The roots love them.” He swallowed. “And Suzu’s been looking at the road all day. Not south. At the road. At the hill. Where it comes over.” He looked at the dark top of the first hill. “They’ll come over that at dusk tomorrow. I think. I can feel them stopping. They stop for the night and then they come.”

Rhea looked down the road for a long time.

“Leave the gate open,” said Maigo. “Please. At dusk. Don’t shut it till they’re in.”

“I can’t leave the gate open, it’s the Redmarch, there’s a—“

“Please,” said Maigo.

Rhea looked at him. A small tree person in a hood, with bells on his antlers muffled in a scarf and half his collar leaves gone, standing in the gatehouse arch at midnight asking her to leave the west gate of the most important fort in the kingdom open for three people nobody had seen.

“I’ll ask Aldous,” said Rhea.

“Aldous will say yes.”

“Then I’ll leave it open,” said Rhea. “And I’ll stand in it. With this.” She shifted the sword on her shoulder. “Because it’s my gate. And whoever comes over that hill can ask me to come in.” She looked down at him. “That’s fair, isn’t it.”

Maigo thought about it.

“That’s fair,” he said.

He didn’t sleep.

He sat on the step of the postern all night with his foot out under the gate, and listened to three people sleep on a hillside in the snow a few miles south, and the roots under them, holding still, very carefully, so as not to wake them. The way you’d hold still if somebody you’d been waiting for a very long time had fallen asleep against your shoulder.

At dawn they got up. He felt them. One, and then two, and then the third, last, lightest, the one the roots loved best. Standing. And starting to walk.

North. Up the road. Toward the fort.

Maigo took his foot back in from under the gate, and stood up, and shook the snow off his hood, and went to wake Yan.

Yan was already awake. He was sitting on the edge of his bed in the grey light, dressed, with his scythe across his knees and Suzu round his neck. Looking at the wall. At the west wall of the barracks. Through it.

“She says they’re up,” said Yan, before Maigo could speak.

“They’re up,” said Maigo.

Yan nodded slowly.

“She says to tell you,” he said, “that the tree’s awake.”

Maigo stood in the doorway.

“It’s winter,” he said. “It’s asleep. It’s been asleep since the—“

“She says it’s awake,” said Yan. “She says it woke up an hour ago. She says it’s standing on the knoll with every branch turned south, and there’s a leaf on it.” He looked at Maigo. “One. In the snow. She says it’s green.”
