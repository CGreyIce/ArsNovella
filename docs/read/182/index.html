The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 7: Myths

Chapter 182

IF He Says Yes

6,355 words · 28 min read

Malcolm had thirty-one pages about the answer, and not one of them was any good.

He’d started them the night Azac wrote WE ASK. In the third book, the green one from the guild store, after IF HE COMES BACK FOR THE TENTH, THEN and Azac’s two big untidy words and his own small square line underneath. NEXT SUMMER. TOGETHER. AND IF HE SAYS NO, THEN WE STAND AT THE TOP OF THE ROAD.

And then he’d turned the page and kept going. Because that was what he did. You wrote down a plan, and then you wrote down every way it could go wrong, and what you’d do about each one. IF on the left. THEN on the right. That was the whole method. It had kept five people alive in the Hollow for two years. It had got eleven pages of upriver through a gorge with things coming down the cliffs head first. It worked. It was the only thing he’d ever found that did.

So he’d written them all.

IF he says no. THEN we stand at the top of the road, like it says.

IF he says no and means it. THEN we don’t stand at the top of the road. We go home.

IF he doesn’t answer. That one had taken a page and a half. IF he doesn’t answer because he hasn’t read it. IF he doesn’t answer because he has. IF he answers Xav and not us. IF he answers in a way we don’t understand. IF Gabe asks him for us and he says yes to Gabe. IF he says yes to Gabe and it’s because Gabe asked.

IF we go anyway, and he’s angry.

IF we go anyway, and he isn’t, and it’s worse.

Thirty-one pages. All on the left. IF after IF after IF. And all of them, every single one, ended in the same place: a man by a lake, saying no, or not saying anything, and five people at a long table in Duskhollow looking at each other.

His window had said ninety-seven for two months. It had moved, all through the Hollow years, a point at a time, every time he wrote down a contingency that kept somebody alive. It hadn’t moved once for these.

He hadn’t written the other one.

He’d noticed that on the twentieth page. He’d been sitting at the end of Mother Annick’s long table late at night, with the lamp low and everybody else gone up, and he’d turned back through the green book to see how many he’d done. And seen it. Thirty-odd pages of what to do if it went wrong. And no page, anywhere, for what to do if it didn’t.

IF he says yes.

He’d sat there with the pen over the paper for an hour. And not written it. And gone up to bed.

He didn’t know why. He’d thought about it a great deal since. He thought about everything a great deal; it was the only way he knew how to think. And the best he’d been able to come up with was this: that you wrote down the bad ones so that when they came you’d already be standing in the right place. And a good one didn’t need you to stand anywhere. A good one just happened to you.

And if you wrote it down, it was there. On the page. In ink. Something you’d wanted badly enough to plan for.

And then you could lose it.

The barge came on the first hard morning of the winter.

Malcolm wasn’t on the quay. Azac was. Azac was always on the quay, these days, at first light, before Petra’s class, standing on the end of the town landing with his hands in his coat and his breath going up white, watching the river come round the bend from the north. He’d started doing it in the autumn, after the gravel bar, after the post of light. He said be a jetty post, Azac had written in the channel, the night it happened, and nobody had understood it except the five of them, and Petra, and one other person a long way upriver who’d once stood on the end of a jetty in Wrenhaven and said it to a boy with a shield.

So Azac stood on the end of the quay most mornings. Like a post. Watching the river.

And that morning, out of the grey, round the bend, low in the water and very black against the snow on both banks, came Haskett’s barge.

Malcolm heard about it after. Everyone did. Azac told it at breakfast, the way Azac told everything, while it was still happening in his head: the Patient Widow on the last of the open water, her poles going in and out of the mist, Haskett at the steering oar with his cap on. The barge not stopping. It never stopped at Duskhollow going down in the winter; the wool went on to Kingsferry. But it slowing, off the end of the quay, in the current. And Haskett leaning out over the rail with something in his arms.

He threw it, said Azac, at breakfast, still not quite able to believe it. He just leaned out and said “catch, lad” and threw it. Across about four feet of river. A basket. I caught it. He’d held out his big hands, as if it were still in them. And he said “it’s from the lake. For the long table. There’s a paper.” And then he took his cap off. And then the current took them round the bend and they were gone.

Azac had stood on the end of the quay with a willow basket in his arms, in the mist, for some time.

Then he’d brought it up the hill to Mother Annick’s.

It was on the table when Malcolm came down.

He saw it from the stairs. In the middle of the long table in Mother Annick’s kitchen, between the bread and the jug, where she put the things that mattered. A plain willow basket with a lid, the string untied, the lid off and set to one side. And everybody round it. Not eating. Mother Annick at the oven with a cloth in her hands, not moving. Old Pryce at the end, with his pipe unlit. John with his three little motes of light hanging very still over his head, the way they hung when he forgot about them. Rui Xiang, who was never still, very still. Xav, standing, at the window, with his back to the room. Petra at the far end with her arms folded.

And Azac, in his chair, with his hands flat on the table either side of the basket, looking into it.

Malcolm came down the last three stairs and looked into it too.

Snow. Packed in round the inside of the willow, grey and half-melted at the edges where it had come up the hill in Azac’s arms. And on the snow, laid side by side, very neatly, the way you’d lay out cutlery, fish. Perch. Striped, cold, heavy, perfect. Their eyes still clear.

And on top of them, on a square of oilcloth, a strip of paper.

Malcolm knew the hand before he’d read the words. Everybody in the kingdom who’d been summoned knew that hand by now; they’d read it in the channel a hundred times, in Cursiffe’s copies, in Oswin’s books. Plain and narrow and very even, the hand of somebody who’d learned his letters on a slate and never once in his life written anything fancy. In English. The only English in the world before they’d come.

Yes. All of you.

Malcolm read it.

Then he read it again.

Then, because he couldn’t help it, because it was what his hands did when he didn’t know what else to do with them, he counted the fish.

Six.

He counted them again.

Six. Perch. Side by side, on the snow. He counted them a third time, touching each one with the tip of his finger, the way he’d counted Orla Fane’s eleven at the top of the stair below the Hollow, before they went down. One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six.

“Six,” said Malcolm.

“Six,” Azac agreed. He hadn’t looked up from the basket. “I counted. On the quay. And on the hill. And on the step. Six every time.”

Malcolm looked round the table.

He counted that too. He couldn’t help it. Azac. John. Rui Xiang. Xav, at the window. Himself.

Five.

He looked at the fish. He looked at the five of them. He looked at the fish again.

“He can count,” said Rui Xiang, very fast. “He’s the best counter of all of us, he counted every grenade in every round we ever played, he used to call out how many we had left before anyone asked, he knows there’s five of us. He knows.”

“I know he knows,” said Malcolm.

“So why’s it six?”

Nobody said anything.

Malcolm looked down the table.

To the far end. Where a woman of thirty-two sat with her sword on the back of her chair and her arms folded, the way she always sat, with her face doing nothing at all, the way it always did. Petra. Their guard. Who’d taught Xav to wait in the guild yard, standing still for twenty minutes, then forty, then two hours. Who ran a dawn class on a gravel bar because a man had once taught on a beach. Who’d been a recruit on the Redmarch nine years ago, in a ditch on the south line, with her arm broken, when the line was overrun.

And the Black Blade had cut his way through to them.

She’d told them that. The first month, when she’d watched Xav spar and gone pale. I saw him fight for less than a minute. I’ve spent nine years trying to fight like that. She’d told them he came through the ditch like weather and held the line till dawn and was gone in the morning, and she’d never seen his face, and never thanked him, and never would.

Malcolm looked at her.

She was looking at the basket.

Her face wasn’t doing anything. Petra’s face never did. But her arms had come unfolded, at some point while they were counting, and her hands were on the edge of the table, flat, the way Azac’s were. And she was looking at the sixth fish as if it were something she’d dropped a long time ago and had just found on a table in a kitchen, exactly where she’d left it.

“Petra,” said Malcolm.

She didn’t look up.

“I didn’t ask,” she said. Very quietly. “I didn’t ask him anything.”

“I know,” said Malcolm.

“I don’t know why it’s six.” Her voice was perfectly level. Petra’s voice was always perfectly level. “He doesn’t know me. He saw me once. In a ditch. In the dark. For an hour, nine years ago, and I was nobody, I was a recruit with a broken arm and a borrowed sword.” She was quiet. “He doesn’t know me.”

At the window, Xav hadn’t moved.

Malcolm noticed that. He noticed it the way he noticed everything, and wrote it down in his head, on the left side of a page he didn’t have. Xav hasn’t turned round. Xav, who’d sat on the end of a jetty at midnight last spring with Gabe and Deston and been told all of it, and come home very quiet. Xav, who turned round for everything. Who looked at the hands first, and then the feet, and then the whole thing.

Xav was standing at the window with his back to the room, looking at the river. And not turning round.

IF Xav knows something about the ditch, Malcolm thought.

And stopped. Right there. Before the THEN. Because he could see from the shape of Xav’s back, from the way Xav was standing, from the very stillness of him, that whatever it was wasn’t Malcolm’s. And Xav knew it wasn’t his either. And that was why he was looking at the river.

“He knows you,” said Xav, to the window. Quietly. “Petra. He knows you.”

Petra looked up.

Xav didn’t turn round. He didn’t say anything else. And after a moment Petra looked back down at the sixth fish, and her hands on the edge of the table went very slowly into fists, and then very slowly open again.

Mother Annick put down her cloth.

“Well,” she said, to the kitchen, loudly, in the voice she used for weather and lodgers and anybody too thin. “I’m cooking those. Tonight. All six. With butter, and the last of the leeks, and nobody’s looking at them all day like a lot of owls, or I’ll cook them round you.” She came and picked up the basket. “Six. In snow. Down the river.” She looked at it in her arms. “Daft,” she said, with enormous tenderness, to the basket. “Daft man.”

And took it away to the cold larder, and that was the end of breakfast.

Old Pryce caught him on the stairs that night.

Malcolm had known he would. Pryce had been a Hollow-runner forty years ago, before his legs went, and he’d lodged at Mother Annick’s ever since, at the end of the long table, eating very slowly and saying very little and watching all of them. He’d been the one, the first winter, who’d told Malcolm to write the bad ones down. Write them all down. Every way it can go wrong. And then go anyway. Malcolm had thought it was the wisest thing anybody had ever said to him. He’d built two years on it.

The fish had been very good. Mother Annick had cooked them with butter and leeks, and they’d eaten them at the long table, all of them, Petra too, Petra last and slowest, very carefully, as if it were something she’d been given and wasn’t sure she was allowed. Nobody had said much. Rui Xiang had talked, at the start, and then stopped. John’s motes had gone round and round over the table the whole meal, slowly, like moths round a lamp. Azac had eaten his and then sat with his big hands round his cup and his eyes very bright and not said anything at all, which for Azac was like a wall falling down.

Afterwards Malcolm had sat at the end of the table with the green book open, the way he did every night, at the page.

IF HE SAYS YES.

He’d written it, finally. The IF. On a clean page, at the top, in small square capitals. It had taken him a quarter of an hour.

And then the THEN, on the right, under it. And nothing. He’d sat there with the pen over the right-hand side of the page for an hour, again. With six fish inside him, and a strip of paper in his pocket that said yes, in a hand everybody in the kingdom knew.

And not been able to write a word.

He’d shut the book and gone up. And Pryce had been on the landing, in his nightshirt, with a candle, very slowly coming down for water.

“You’ve not written it,” said Pryce.

It wasn’t a question. Pryce never asked questions. He told you what you were doing, and then waited to see if you’d deny it.

“No.”

“The good one.”

“No.”

Pryce looked at him over the candle. Old, and thin, with his legs gone and his eyes not.

“I told you to write the bad ones down,” said Pryce. “The first winter. And then go anyway.” He breathed out through his nose. “I never said anything about the good ones. You noticed that?”

“I noticed.”

“I didn’t write the good ones either,” said Pryce. “Forty years in the Hollow. A book like yours, near enough. Every way a run could go wrong.” He was quiet. “Never once wrote down what I’d do if it went right. I thought I was being careful.” He looked at the candle. “I wasn’t careful. I was frightened. There’s a difference, and I didn’t know it till my legs went and there wasn’t anything left to be frightened for.”

Malcolm didn’t say anything.

“You know what a backup is, lad?” said Pryce.

“Something you do in case the first thing fails.”

“It’s something you do because you don’t believe the first thing,” said Pryce. “That’s all it is. Every one you’ve ever written. Hold, and then Backup underneath it, in case.” He shifted the candle. “Two years you’ve been telling that big lad’s shield you don’t believe it.”

Malcolm stood on the stairs.

He’d never once thought of it like that. Not once. In two years. He’d thought of the backup as care. As the second rope on a climb, the second strap on a pack. The thing a sensible person did. And the old man on the landing in his nightshirt was looking at him as if it were the most obvious thing in the world, and it was.

“He didn’t send a backup,” said Pryce. “Did he. Six fish and a bit of paper. Down forty miles of river on the last barge. If the barge had sunk, that was that.” He turned toward the stairs. “Didn’t write two. Didn’t send a letter as well, in case. Just the fish.”

He went down the stairs, very slowly, a step at a time, with the candle.

“Go to bed,” said Pryce, from the bottom. “And tomorrow, write the good one.”

He didn’t write it.

He tried. He sat up in bed with the green book on his knees and the candle and the pen, and the page open at IF HE SAYS YES, and the right-hand side empty. And he tried to write what came next, and found that he couldn’t, because he didn’t know. He’d never in his life thought about what came next when something went right. He’d only ever thought about what came next when it went wrong.

THEN we go, he thought. That was obvious. That was easy.

He didn’t write it. Because the moment he thought it, the other column started. IF the road’s closed. IF someone’s hurt. IF he changes his mind. IF it’s not the same when we get there. IF we get there and it’s just a grave and a stone and a field of grey grass and he doesn’t want us there after all and is too kind to say so.

He put the pen down.

He lay in the dark for a long time with the green book on his chest and the snow coming down past the window. He could hear the river. Mother Annick’s house was on the slope above the quay, and in the winter, when the snow muffled the town, you could hear the river all night, going past below, black and fast, toward Kingsferry and the sea. Carrying things. That was what rivers did.

Six fish, he thought. Down forty miles of it. On the last barge. With no backup.

He fell asleep, eventually. He dreamed about a classroom in Singapore. The back row, by the window, with the air-conditioning broken and the fan going. A group project, the first year. A boy beside him, talking to everyone, making the whole class laugh, who’d leaned over Malcolm’s shoulder at his list of everything that could go wrong with their presentation, all forty-one items, and read it, and laughed. Not at him. The other way.

Mal, the boy had said. Mal. You’ve got forty-one ways it goes wrong and no ways it goes right.

It’s called planning.

It’s called being scared, the boy had said, cheerfully, and taken the pen out of Malcolm’s hand, and written at the bottom of the list, in his own terrible handwriting, 42. it goes fine and we get pizza.

And it had. They’d got pizza.

Malcolm woke up in the dark in Duskhollow with his face wet and the green book on the floor where it had slid off his chest, and lay there.

He’d forgotten that. He’d forgotten the whole thing. Years of it. Declan, at seventeen, in the back row, writing 42 in terrible handwriting at the bottom of a list.

He didn’t go back to sleep.

Petra’s class was on the gravel bar at first light, the way it always was.

It was the coldest morning yet. The river was high and black and fast, running between snowbanks, with a skin of ice along the edges of the bar where the water was slack and a mist coming off the middle in long grey rags. The gravel was frozen. It crunched under their boots and shifted, the way Petra liked. You have to keep deciding where your feet are. The heron wasn’t on the far bank. It had gone south, John said, three days ago. His motes had followed it as far as the bend.

Petra stood at the top of the bar with her driftwood stick and her sword on her hip and her arms folded. She looked the same as she always did. Malcolm looked at her very carefully, and thought she looked the same as she always did except for one thing, which was that she wasn’t looking at any of them. She was looking upriver. North. Where the barge had come from.

Then she looked at them.

“Azac,” she said. “Ward.”

Azac went down onto the bar.

They’d done this every morning since the autumn. Since the post. Petra would call Azac down and tell him ward, and Azac would plant his feet on the frozen gravel and put his hand out, flat, the way he’d put it out the first time, twenty paces off with his eyes shut, when Petra’s edge had got away from her toward John. And write Post in the air. And a post of pale light would come up out of the stones in front of him. Taller than a man. Steady. The colour of a lamp seen through a window.

And Petra would hit it.

Not with her full edge. Never that. Petra was Reaching; there were maybe a few hundred Reaching swordsmen in the kingdom, and her edge went a hand’s width past her steel and could take the corner off a stone wall. She hit it with a little of that. A tenth. A fifth, on a good day, when Azac was steady. To see how much it would take.

And Malcolm would put Hold on it.

That was his part. He’d sit at the edge of the bar with his book on his knee and his pen, and when Azac’s post came up out of the gravel, Malcolm would put out his own hand and write Hold, the line he’d written on Azac’s shield in the Hollow the spring before last, the first thing he’d ever enchanted. Into the post. And feel it go in, warm, like a hand on a shoulder. And then, below it, a little lower, because he couldn’t help it, because he’d never once in two years not done it:

Backup.

A second Hold under the first. In case. And the post would take Petra’s fifth, and ring, and stand.

It had never not stood. Not once, since the autumn. Malcolm had never once found out whether it would have stood without the second line.

He’d never let himself find out.

Azac’s post came up out of the gravel.

Pale, steady, a little taller than yesterday. Azac’s hand out flat in front of him. His eyes open, these days. His big scarred face very calm, the way it went calm before a hit.

Malcolm put out his hand.

He wrote Hold. Into the post. And felt it go in, warm, the way it always did. The post brightened, a little, round the edges, the way a lamp brightens when you turn up the wick.

And then his hand moved down, the way it always moved down. To the place a little lower. For the second line.

And stopped.

He didn’t decide to stop it. He found it had stopped. In the air, in front of him, over the gravel, with the pen in his other hand and the green book on his knee open at a page that said IF HE SAYS YES and nothing after. His hand, in the place where the backup went. Not writing.

It’s something you do because you don’t believe the first thing.

Mal. You’ve got forty-one ways it goes wrong.

Yes. All of you.

Petra was looking at him. Not at the post. At him. At his hand, stopped in the air.

“Malcolm,” she said.

“I know,” said Malcolm.

She looked at him a moment longer. Petra, who watched hands and feet and shoulders the way Xav did, the way a man on a beach had taught her without ever knowing he’d taught her anything. She saw his hand. She saw where it had stopped. She saw, he thought, exactly what it meant.

“A fifth?” she said. Quietly.

Malcolm looked at the post.

He thought about the left side of the page. All of it. Every IF he’d ever written. The way it could go wrong. Her edge going through the post. Through the Hold. Into Azac, who’d stand there, because Azac always stood there; it was the only thing Azac had ever wanted to do. The way it could go wrong was very clear, and very bright, and he could see it as well as he’d ever seen anything.

He thought about the right side of the page. The empty one.

He took his hand out of the place where the backup went.

He didn’t put it down. He moved it up. To the top of the post, where the first line was. Where Hold was. Warm, and steady, and alone. And beside it, small, in the small square capitals he’d written at the top of every page in three green books, he wrote the only word he’d never once in two years written on the right-hand side of anything without an IF in front of it.

Then.

Not Hold. Not Backup. Not in case. Just the second half of a sentence, with the first half missing, because the first half had already happened. A man by a lake had already said it. It had come down forty miles of river in a basket of snow.

Then.

“Full fifth,” said Malcolm.

Petra hit it.

He saw her move. He never usually could. Petra moved the way Xav moved now, in the breath before you’d decided, so that by the time you saw it it was already done. But this time he saw it. The driftwood stick down. The sword off her back and out in one movement, in the grey, with the mist going sideways through it. And the edge. Her edge. A hand’s width past the steel, pale, the colour of frost on a window, going out in front of the blade like a breath.

A fifth of it. All of a fifth. Not a hair less.

It hit the post.

The sound went down the river.

Malcolm would remember it. All his life, in two worlds, whichever one he ended up in. Not a crash. Not a ring, even, the way it usually rang. A note. Low and long and clear, like somebody had drawn a bow across the whole frozen gravel bar at once, so that every stone on it hummed. It went out over the black river and came back off the snowbanks on the far side. It went up the slope into the town. It went, Mother Annick said afterwards, through the kitchen window and set every cup on the dresser ringing, and she’d stood there with a cloth in her hands and known exactly what it was without being told.

The post stood.

Petra’s edge lay against it, pale, a hand’s width deep. Pressing. And the post took it. The way a jetty post takes a barge coming in too fast: all of it, the whole weight, without moving. Azac’s light and Malcolm’s Hold, and above them, very small, a word in English that no native in the kingdom could read, burning like a coal.

And didn’t give.

Petra held it there. One breath. Two. Malcolm saw her shoulders. Saw her lean, the way she’d learned she leaned, the way she’d never known she leaned for nine years until John showed her from above. She leaned into it. Not a fifth now. More. He saw it go more. A quarter. More. Saw her feel the post not give and push, the way you’d push on a door to see if it was really locked.

It was really locked.

Petra took her blade back.

The note stopped. Not all at once. It went on in the stones for a while after, under their boots, very faintly, the way a bell goes on after you’ve stopped hearing it. The mist came back across the bar where her edge had cut it. Azac’s post stood in the grey, pale and steady, with nothing on it, not a mark.

Azac was staring at Malcolm.

Everybody was staring at Malcolm.

He looked down. At his own hand. Which was shaking, a little, now. And at the post. And at the word on the top of the post, small, alone, with nothing under it.

And then at the air in front of his face, where his window had opened.

He looked at it.

He looked at it for a long time. On the frozen gravel bar below the town of Duskhollow, in the grey, with the river going past black and fast and four people and a guard staring at him.

Then.

He laughed.

It came out of him the way it came out of all of them, when it happened. He’d heard it in the channel twenty-nine times, he’d read about it twenty-nine times, he’d thought, every time, that’ll never be me, that’s not how I’m built. Short and cracked and astonished. Sitting on the frozen gravel with a book on his knee open at a page with nothing on the right-hand side.

“Then,” said Rui Xiang. He’d come across the bar at a run, the way Rui Xiang went everywhere, and was standing over him, reading the window upside down. “Your skill’s called Then. Not Hold. Not Backup. Then.” He was talking very fast. “Mal. Mal. You’ve got a skill that’s half a sentence.”

“I know.”

“That’s the most Malcolm thing I’ve ever—it’s half an IF, it’s the answer bit, you’ve been writing THEN at the top of the right side of every page for two years and now it’s—“

“I know, Rui Xiang.”

“—it’s your skill.”

“Thirty,” said John.

He was sitting on the gravel at the edge of the bar with his knees up, the way he always sat, and his three motes of light had come in from wherever they’d been and were going round Malcolm’s head, very close, slowly, like moths that had found a better lamp. “That’s thirty. Of thirty-five.” He swallowed. “You’re thirty, Mal.”

Malcolm looked at him.

Thirty. He’d known the count. Everyone knew the count; Chris put it in the channel every time it moved, count stands at, like a man at the end of a long table keeping the score. Twenty-nine. Six left at the bottom. Xola, Cursiffe, Malcolm, Tiger, Drew, Niko. He’d been one of the six for more than two years, and he’d been quite sure, the way he was sure of most things, that he’d be one of the last two, and that the last one would be Niko, and that it would be fine.

He’d never once written down what he’d do if he wasn’t.

Azac hadn’t said anything.

He was still standing behind his post, with his hand out, flat, holding it up. Pale and steady on the gravel. He hadn’t let it go. Malcolm didn’t think he’d noticed he was still holding it.

He was crying. Quite openly, with his big scarred face, the way Azac did everything. Not loudly.

“You didn’t put a backup on it,” said Azac.

“No.”

“You’ve put a backup on everything. Since the first winter. Since I was in bed with the ceiling.” Azac’s voice was very thick. “Every time. Hold, and then Backup. In case.” He looked at the word on the top of his post. “You didn’t put one on.”

“No,” said Malcolm.

Azac looked at him for a long moment, over the post, with his eyes streaming.

“Okay,” he said. Just that. The way Xav said it. And let the post go, finally, so that it went back down into the gravel like a breath let out, and came across the bar, and put both his enormous arms round Malcolm where he sat, book and all, and held on.

Petra hadn’t moved.

She was standing where she’d stood, at the edge of the bar, with her sword still in her hand, point down on the frozen stones. Looking at the place where the post had been.

Malcolm saw her, over Azac’s shoulder.

She looked at the gravel for a long time. Then she looked upriver. North. At the bend where the barge had come round yesterday morning in the mist with a basket on the rail.

Then she put her sword away.

She came down onto the bar and stood in front of him. Petra, who didn’t stand in front of people. Who stood at the edge of things, with her arms folded, and watched.

“That held,” she said.

“It held.”

“I leaned on it.” Her voice was perfectly level. “I didn’t mean to. I gave it a fifth, like you said. And it didn’t move, and I leaned.” She looked at her own hands. “A third. Nearly. It didn’t move.”

“I didn’t put a backup on.”

“I know. I watched your hand.” She was quiet. “You stopped in the place where it goes. And then you went up instead.”

Malcolm looked at her.

He’d thought, for two years, that Petra didn’t like him much. He’d thought she thought he was the soft one, the one who held a practice sword like a pen and sat at the edge of the bar writing in a book. He’d been wrong about that, he thought. He’d been wrong about a great many things, very carefully, for two years.

“Petra,” he said.

“Mm.”

“The sixth fish.”

Petra didn’t say anything.

“Xav knows something,” said Malcolm. “About the ditch. I don’t know what. It isn’t mine.” He looked up at her. “But the sixth fish was yours. He counted. He’s the best counter of any of us. He knew there were five of us and he sent six.” He paused. “He knows you, Petra. Xav said so. You were in the ditch, and he came through to you, and you never thanked him, and he still knows you.”

Petra stood on the gravel bar in the cold with her hands at her sides.

“I’d like to go,” she said. Very quietly. “Next year. East. With you.” She looked north. “I’d like to walk the red with him. Once. In the daylight. And see his face, and not be able to.” Her mouth did something very small. “And say it. The thing I never said.”

“Then come,” said Malcolm.

He heard himself say it. Then. He’d never said it like that in his life. Without an IF. Just the answer, on its own.

Petra looked at him.

“That’s not a plan,” she said. Not unkindly. The way he’d said it to Azac, a long time ago, at the long table.

“It’s the only one that doesn’t go wrong,” said Malcolm.

He wrote the page that night.

At the end of the long table, with the lamp low, and Mother Annick at the oven pretending not to watch, and old Pryce at the other end with his pipe, very definitely watching. Rui Xiang had already put it in the channel. Of course he had. In capitals, at nine in the morning, while Malcolm was still sitting on the gravel bar with Azac’s arms round him. MALCOLM. THIRTY. HIS SKILL IS CALLED THEN. IT’S HALF A SENTENCE. And the channel had gone the way the channel went when somebody broke through: Tiger in capitals, and Maigo in larger capitals, and Kei, careful, well done, and Chris, count stands at 30, and Gigichi, it’s about time, and Hana, Good.

And at the very bottom of all of it, much later, alone, from a guild town on the Wren where it was the middle of the night: Xav.

Okay.

Malcolm opened the green book at the page.

IF HE SAYS YES.

And under it, on the right-hand side, where the THEN went. Empty. For a week.

He picked up the pen.

THEN we go. All of us. Petra too.

He looked at it.

Then, underneath, because he couldn’t help it, because it had been the first thing he did and the last thing he did for two years, his hand went down to the place below, the place where the backup went.

And stopped there.

He looked at the empty line under it for a long time. He could feel the other column starting, the way it always started. IF the road’s closed. IF someone’s hurt. IF he changes his mind. He could feel all of it, lined up, waiting, very bright.

He didn’t write any of it.

He wrote, instead, in small square capitals, in the place where the backup went, the only line he’d ever copied out of somebody else’s handwriting into his own:

42. it goes fine and we get pizza.

He shut the book.

At the other end of the table, old Pryce lit his pipe, finally, and drew on it, and let the smoke out very slowly toward the ceiling, and didn’t say anything at all.

Mother Annick, at the oven, blew her nose.
