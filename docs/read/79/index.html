The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 3: The Anniversary

Chapter 79

Not in a Hurry

3,790 words · 16 min read

Drew had run eleven tournaments on Earth, and he’d learned one thing from every single one of them.

The job wasn’t the bracket. Anybody could draw a bracket. The job wasn’t the rules, or the prizes, or the casters, or the stream. The job was being the person still standing there smiling when it all went wrong. When the server crashed in the final. When two teams turned up claiming the same slot. When a player’s internet died in the deciding round and his whole team looked at you as if you could fix it with your hands. You stood there, and you smiled, and you said okay, in a voice that made people believe it was okay, and then you made it okay, somehow, while they were still believing.

He’d done it at the Apex final, the year Yan and Ice won. Ice had died in every single round, loudly, narrating his own deaths like a sports commentator, and the casters had been laughing so hard they couldn’t cast, and Drew had muted them, smiling, and taken over the commentary himself, and nobody watching had ever known.

He’d been doing it at Thornwall for a year.

It was harder here. The servers didn’t crash. Things came down out of the mountains wearing people’s faces. But the job was the same. Somebody had to be standing there, smiling, saying okay.

Tiger had been his friend for twelve years.

Longer than anyone in this world. Longer than most people in the other one. They’d met twelve years ago, the way people did, and they’d been in each other’s lives ever since, the way some friends are: not every day, not always close, but there. Under everything. The kind of friend you don’t think about, the way you don’t think about the floor.

The way Ice and Xav had been, everyone said. Drew had seen it, the few months he’d known Ice. The two of them on a call, saying nothing for an hour, and then one of them saying something and the other knowing exactly what he meant. That was what he and Tiger were. You didn’t have to explain things to someone you’d known twelve years. You just had to stand next to them while they happened.

He’d been standing next to Tiger a lot, this year.

Autumn came early to the Greyspines.

It always did, the soldiers said. The first frost on the pass a month before it came down to the valley. The high meadows going brown in a week. The sheep brought down off the tops, and the shepherds’ huts shuttered, and the wind changing, coming down off the mountains from the north-west with a bite in it that went through your coat and found your bones.

Hisui didn’t like the wind.

“He says it tastes of the bottom of a well,” said Kei, at breakfast, every few days now. Kei’s snake lay coiled round his neck with his green head up and his tongue going, tasting. “He said it in the summer, at the top of the pass. Now it’s coming down.” He scratched the snake under the chin. “Every time the wind’s from the north-west. He doesn’t like it at all.”

“I don’t like it either,” said Bee. “And I can’t even taste it.”

Bee’s dogs barked at night.

That was the other thing. She’d been drawing them all summer, in chalk, on every wall in Thornwall: the gatehouse, the barracks, the granary, the shed with its roof. A dog on every wall, each one with Tell him written underneath in her small round hand, because the first one had barked once at the thing with Ossie’s face, and she’d decided Thornwall needed more. They didn’t move, mostly. They were chalk. They faded in the rain, and she drew them again.

But since the first frost, they barked at night.

Not all of them. Not every night. One, or two, or five, in the small hours, on the walls, with nobody near them: a thin chalky sound, like a dog barking a long way off in a dream. Every real dog in Thornwall would wake up and start barking back. And then they’d stop.

“They’re barking at something,” said Bee. She’d gone very quiet about it, the way Bee went quiet about things that frightened her. “I didn’t draw them to bark at nothing. They’re barking at something.”

“At what?” said Tiger.

Bee looked out of the window, north-west, toward the pass.

Hyvred went up every week.

With the patrols. Forty men, sometimes more, since the king’s order in the summer had doubled every posting and the Western Watch had grown from a company to something closer to a small army: two hundred soldiers, a Bonfire mage from the capital, siege bows on the walls. Every seventh day, the patrol climbed the old sheep path to the top of the pass, with Hild in front and Fenwick behind and Hyvred in the middle, and at the top Hyvred stood on the old paved road that ran down the far side, the road built for armies, and looked.

She’d come back that morning. Drew had watched her come in through the gate in the grey light with frost on her coat and her face set, and he’d known before she said a word.

They sat in Corvin’s map room, the five of them, and Hild, and Corvin, and Fenwick with his notebook, and Hyvred told them.

“It’s moved,” she said.

Nobody spoke.

“The absence.” She had her glasses off and was rubbing her eyes, the way she did after a long time Scoping. “In the summer it was at the far end of the valley. Beyond the town. Beyond the circle. A hill of nothing, at the far end.” She put her glasses back on. “It’s at the near end now. On this side of the town. At the foot of the pass. Where the road starts to climb.”

Corvin made a small sound and reached for a pin.

“The rings,” said Hyvred. “The turned. Standing round the circle in the valley floor. There were hundreds in the summer. Rings and rings of them.” She swallowed. “There are twice as many now. At least. I stopped counting at the eleventh ring. They go out almost to the edge of the town.”

“Standing,” said Fenwick. “Still?”

“Still. Facing in. Waiting.” Hyvred looked at the table. “The way John said the people in the cave under the Hollow were standing. The same.”

She was quiet for a moment.

“Ossie’s there,” she said. “In the third ring. Near the road. I saw him through the Scope. I’d know his light anywhere now.” She didn’t look at Tiger. “His face is turned up toward the pass. Toward us.”

Tiger left the room.

He didn’t slam the door. He didn’t say anything. He just stood up and walked out, and Drew heard his feet on the stairs, fast, and then the yard door, and then nothing.

Drew got up.

“Drew,” said Hild. She hadn’t moved. She was sitting with her eyes closed and her face turned toward where Tiger had been. “He’ll go to the shed.”

“I know.”

“Or the gate.”

“I know,” said Drew. “That’s why I’m going.”

He found him at the gate.

Not the main gate. The postern, in the north-west wall, that let out onto the sheep path. The one the patrols used. The one Dunmore’s ten had gone out through in the spring, and not come back, and the thing with Ossie’s face had come in through, a month later, smiling.

Tiger was standing in front of it with his short blades in his hands. Not drawn. Just held, still in their sheaths, the way you’d hold a pair of keys.

“Not here,” said Drew.

Tiger didn’t turn round.

“You said that at the top,” said Tiger. “In the summer. With your hands on my chest. Not here. We’re going home.” His voice was perfectly flat. “So I came home. And it’s moved. It’s at the bottom of the pass now, Drew. It’s coming. And Os is standing there with his face turned up, waiting, and I’m standing here, and every week Hyvred goes up and looks at him and comes back down and says he’s still there and I just…”

He stopped.

“I just stand here,” he said. “Every week. I’m the fastest of all thirty-five of us. I can be three paces away before anyone sees me move. And I just stand here.”

Drew came and stood beside him. Not in front. Beside.

They looked at the postern together for a while. The heavy oak, the iron bands, the bar across it. Frost on the iron.

“It isn’t him,” said Drew.

“I know it isn’t him.”

“Kei told his dad. In the summer. It had his face and it wasn’t him. You were there. You built a roof with his dad the next day. His dad left a shirt on a nail.” Drew’s voice was very gentle, the voice he used when a server crashed in a final. “Os is somewhere, Tiger. I don’t know where. Nobody does. But he isn’t standing in the third ring at the foot of the pass with his face turned up. That’s something wearing him. And it wants you to come down the road to it. That’s all it wants. It said so. It’s warm down here.”

Tiger’s hands had gone tight on the sheaths.

“Not here,” said Drew. “Not on your own.” He waited. “Not ever. You don’t go through that gate on your own. Not this winter. Not next. Not ever.” He turned his head and looked at him. “If you go, I go. And I’m slow, Tiger. I’m really slow. You’d have to wait for me the whole way down.”

Tiger didn’t say anything for a long time.

Then he laughed. It came out cracked.

“You’re so slow,” he said.

“I’m so slow.”

“You’re the slowest person I’ve ever known.”

“Twelve years,” said Drew, “and I’ve never once been on time for anything.”

Tiger put the blades back on his belt. Slowly. One, then the other. Then he turned round and put his forehead on Drew’s shoulder, the way he had at the top of the pass in the summer, and stood there, in front of the postern gate, in the frost, shaking.

Drew put his hand on the back of his neck and didn’t say anything. He didn’t need to. Twelve years.

That night the dogs barked.

All of them.

Drew woke to it. Every chalk dog in Thornwall, on every wall, at once: the gatehouse, the barracks, the granary, the shed. A thin, high, far-off barking, like dogs in a dream, from every direction, and under it, a moment later, every real dog in the fortress going mad. Soldiers shouting. Boots on the walls. Somebody ringing the alarm bell on the gatehouse, once, twice, and then stopping, because there was nothing to ring it at. Nothing on the walls. Nothing at the gate.

Drew was on the north-west wall in his shirt with his knuckles on before he was properly awake. Tiger was beside him. Of course Tiger was beside him. Kei came up the steps with Hisui round his neck, and Bee behind him with a lump of chalk in her hand, white-faced. And Hyvred, last, in her nightdress with a soldier’s cloak over it and her glasses on crooked, already looking.

North-west. At the pass. A black notch in the black mountains against the stars.

“Hyvred,” said Drew.

She didn’t answer. She was Scoping. He’d seen her do it enough times now to know what it looked like: her whole face going still, the way a hawk’s goes still before it drops, her eyes fixed on something a very long way off that nobody else could see.

Then she made a sound.

“It’s looking at us,” said Hyvred.

Very quietly. Barely a breath.

“It turned. Just now. The absence. At the foot of the pass. It’s always faced the valley, the circle, the rings. It turned round.” Her hands were gripping the parapet. “It’s facing the pass. It’s facing up the pass. It’s facing Thornwall.”

Nobody said anything. The chalk dogs were still barking, all of them, thin and far away. Hisui had flattened himself against Kei’s neck and his tongue had stopped moving.

“It doesn’t have eyes,” said Hyvred. “It doesn’t have anything. It’s a hole. But it’s looking. I can feel it looking, through the Scope, all the way up the pass and over the top and down this side.” Her voice cracked. “It’s looking at the walls. It’s looking at the gate. It’s looking at the shed.”

“Hyvred,” said Drew. “Close it.”

“I can’t, I have to see what it—“

“Hyvred.” Drew put his hand on her arm. Not hard. “If you can feel it looking at you through the Scope, it can feel you looking back. Close it.”

She closed it.

She sagged against the parapet, and Bee caught her, and for a moment nobody moved.

And then, one by one, the chalk dogs stopped barking.

The gatehouse first. Then the barracks. Then the granary. The real dogs went on a moment longer, and then they stopped too, uneasily, one at a time, as if somebody had said it’s all right in a voice they believed.

The last one to stop was the one on the shed. Ossie’s shed, with its roof. Bee had drawn that dog first, in the spring, inside the shed, by the chalk letters that said OSSIE’S. It barked on and on, alone, for a long time after all the others had stopped, thin and high and very far away.

Then it stopped too.

“It turned away again,” said Hyvred, a long time later. They were in Corvin’s map room, all of them, with a fire built up and tea nobody was drinking. Fenwick was writing. Hild had her hand on Hyvred’s shoulder. “I didn’t look. I didn’t Scope. I just… felt it go. Back toward the valley. Back to the circle.” She looked at her tea. “It looked at us for about as long as it takes to count to fifty. And then it turned away, as if it had seen what it wanted to see, and could come back for the rest later.”

“Like the scouts at Cinderhold,” said Kei quietly. “Yan wrote about them. Counting the towers.”

“It’s not counting,” said Hyvred.

“Then what’s it doing?”

Hyvred didn’t answer for a long time.

“It’s not in a hurry,” she said at last. “That’s what I keep coming back to. In the summer, when it turned toward us at the top of the pass, Hisui said it was tasting the air. And it was. And then it let us go. It let forty-six people walk away down the far side of the pass, when it could have…” She stopped. “It’s had a whole valley of people standing in rings for it all summer. It could have come over the pass any time it wanted. It hasn’t. It just… moves a little closer. And doubles the rings. And waits.”

She put her tea down.

“It’s not in a hurry,” she said. “That’s what frightens me. Things that are in a hurry make mistakes. This doesn’t make mistakes. This just waits until it’s ready.”

Nobody said anything.

Drew sat by the fire with his hands loose between his knees and looked at the map on Corvin’s table. All the pins. Coldwater. The shepherd’s hut. The burned circle. The sheep path. The pass. The valley on the far side, and the town with no smoke, and a big black pin at the foot of the road where the absence was now, and round it, in Corvin’s tiny careful hand, rings and rings of little marks.

And then he thought about the other map. The one Aleaka had made with Oswin, a year ago, on the back of a kingdom map, at night: all thirty-five names, and lines between them, who brought you to who. A river, she’d called it. A hole in the middle where all the lines pointed and nobody was standing.

Thirty-five names. Seven postings. Scattered across the whole kingdom.

Never once in one place.

He wrote to Lucan the next morning.

Or rather, Corvin wrote, because Drew still couldn’t make the native script do anything but slide away from him on the page. He dictated. Corvin wrote it down in his tiny careful hand, the same hand that had written eleven pages a month to the Advisor for two years when nobody believed him, and didn’t change a word.

Lucan.

You’ll have Hyvred’s report from Fenwick by now, or you’ll have it soon. You know what’s at the foot of the pass. You know it turned and looked at us last night, and then turned away again, like something that’s decided it’ll come back when it’s ready.

I want to talk about the tournament.

I know it’s postponed. I know the Advisor says one target. I know why. I agreed with him, in the summer.

I don’t agree with him anymore.

We’ve never once been in one place. Thirty-five of us. Not since the hall. We’ve got a channel now, and we talk every night, and we know each other’s numbers better than we know our own. Thea’s built us something nobody in this world has ever had. But we’ve never stood next to each other. We’ve never fought next to each other. Most of us have never even seen each other’s faces outside the hall, the first day, when everyone was crying.

When that thing comes over the pass, and it will, I don’t want thirty-five people who’ve talked every night. I want thirty-five people who’ve stood next to each other. Who know how each other moves. Who’ve fought beside each other in a ring in front of ten thousand people, and lost, and won, and learned.

That’s what the tournament’s for. That’s what it was always for. Not the crowd. Not the prizes. Us.

You said I could run it. I’m saying now: let me run it. Soon. Before that thing’s ready.

I’ll smile the whole way through. That’s the job.

Drew.

Corvin finished writing and looked at it for a long moment.

“He’ll cry,” said Corvin.

“Lucan cries at everything,” said Drew.

“He’ll cry at this more.” Corvin sanded the ink and folded the letter and sealed it, and wrote Lucan, Valcrest on the outside. Then he put his hand flat on it and didn’t let go for a moment. “I wrote eleven pages a month for two years,” he said quietly, “and nobody read them. You wrote one, and I think everyone will.”

That night, Drew and Tiger sat on the shed roof.

Ossie’s shed. The one Tiger had built three times, once in the first week when a cavalry horse kicked it flat, once with Bee in the spring, three walls and no roof, and once in the summer, with Ossie’s father. A carpenter called Aldo Pell, from Harrowford, who’d come all that way to find out what had happened to his son, and stayed to put a roof on a shed for a boy he’d never met because the boy had been his son’s friend.

It had a roof now. It had stood through the summer storms. Bee’s chalk dog was on the wall inside, and the letters OSSIE’S, and on a nail by the door, a shirt, mended at the elbow, that Aldo Pell had left there. Tell him his roof’s done.

They sat on the ridge of the roof with their knees up and looked north-west, at the black notch of the pass against the stars, and didn’t say anything for a long time.

“I’m not going to go,” said Tiger.

“I know.”

“Not on my own.”

“I know.”

“If I go, you’re coming.”

“Obviously,” said Drew.

Tiger laughed. Quietly this time. Not cracked.

“You always say that,” he said. “Obviously.”

“It’s always obvious,” said Drew.

They sat there. The wind came down off the mountains from the north-west, cold, and Drew thought he could almost taste what Hisui tasted in it. The bottom of a well. Something very old and very patient, a long way down.

“Do you think he knew?” said Tiger.

Drew looked at him.

“Ice,” said Tiger. “Wherever he is. With his sword and his fishing rod. Do you think he knows about this? About what’s at the bottom of the pass?” He was quiet. “Do you think he’d come? If it came over?”

Drew thought about it. Honestly. The way he’d thought about bracket seeds, once, when it mattered.

He thought about a man who’d walked across the Redmarch alone with a fishing rod and carried five of his friends. Who’d killed the cold one a week’s walk from Cinderhold in one stroke, Yan said, without Yan even being able to feel him do it. Who’d sent his sword home and taken it back and said let them. Who’d died in his sleep in Malacca, and narrated his own deaths in Apex like a sports commentator, and said I was the reason we won. Trust.

“Yeah,” said Drew. “I think he’d come.”

“Why?”

“Because he always did,” said Drew. “In every game. He’d run into fights he couldn’t win and die in them and Yan would have to go and get his banner.” He smiled at the pass. “But he always ran in. Every single time. He never once stood at the back and watched his friends fight without him.”

Tiger didn’t say anything.

“He’s been standing at the back for a year,” said Drew. “I think he hates it. I think it’s killing him. I think he’s waiting for a reason good enough to stop.”

“And when he stops?”

Drew looked at the black notch of the pass, and the stars over it, and the thing at the foot of the far side, waiting, not in a hurry.

“Then that thing’s going to find out what it feels like,” he said, “to meet somebody who isn’t in a hurry either.”

Down below them, inside the shed, on the wall, very faintly, very far away, the chalk dog barked once.

Just once. Not at the pass.

Drew didn’t know what at. He looked round, and saw nothing. Just Thornwall, asleep, and the walls, and the frost, and the stars.

Then he thought: north-east. It had barked north-east. Away from the pass. Toward the rest of the kingdom, and the river, and the lake country, a long way off.

Tell him, Bee had written under every dog.

He didn’t know if anyone was listening.

He thought, maybe, someone was.
