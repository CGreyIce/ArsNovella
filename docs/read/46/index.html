The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 2: Letters

Chapter 46

What Was He Like

3,650 words · 16 min read

Hako had known Ice for nine months.

Eight and a bit, if you were being honest, and Hako tried to be. From a Tuesday in the spring when Yan had dragged him into a voice channel and said this is the guy I told you about, to a morning in the winter when Yan had called him at the wrong time of day, in the wrong voice. Eight months and some days. Less than a year. Less than most of the people he’d met since he came through the floor.

It wasn’t enough. That was the thing he’d never said out loud to anyone. Everyone else here had a story. Gabe and Deston had thirteen years and a choir. Yan had a tournament. Xav had a classroom and a thousand games of CS. Even Newish, who’d known Ice for three months, had been in the call the last night. Hako had been asleep that night. He hadn’t even known there was a call.

What he had was this: a man with a funny voice who’d been very patient with him.

Hako had played Marvel Rivals, mostly, before. Heroes with powers and big bright ultimates. He’d never played anything where you died in one shot. He’d been terrible at CS, the first week; he’d walked round corners into people and died and walked round the next corner into the next person and died again. Most people would have muted him. Ice hadn’t. Ice had spent three nights in a row in an empty server with him at one in the morning, walking him through angles, one at a time, the same ones over and over, until Hako stopped dying to them.

You’re not bad, Ice had said, on the third night. You’re just new. New’s different from bad. New gets better.

That was the thing Hako had. One sentence, and three nights in an empty server.

He’d always wanted more.

Valcrest in early summer was a city that had decided to forgive itself.

The Crown had acted, in the end, three weeks after the junior lists. Not against Lord Vane, who was untouchable, but against the thing Lord Vane loved most after himself, which was being seen. The heralds read it out in the square in front of the Lists: by order of the king, the house of Vane and all its sworn men were barred from the Lists of Valcrest, as competitors and as spectators, for a period of ten years.

The crowd had cheered so loudly the heralds had to stop twice.

Aldric was gone. Sent to the family’s estates in the country, people said, for his health. Nobody believed it, and nobody cared. The paper horns had come back out, more of them than ever. There was a new song in the taverns about Newish’s shout, Down, Ina, down, which was catchy and stupid and which Newish pretended to hate and hummed constantly. Ina had started wearing his mask in the street on purpose, sometimes, just to watch children run up and touch it for luck.

And Hako had kept Ice’s spot warm.

That was what Chris had called it, in the winter, on the empty sand: the far angle, the long one, the one nobody wanted. That was his. Ice always took it. Hako had stepped into it in his first bout and never stepped out. He’d made it his. Every bout, he took the long angle, alone, with his spear, and held it.

He’d got good at it. He knew that now, without being arrogant about it. Ysolde had told him so, in her flat way, sitting on the fence at dawn: you hold a line better than anyone your age I’ve seen. Lucan told everyone who’d listen that Hako was the quiet heart of the team. His window said thirty-one. Five since the spring.

He’d thought it would feel like being closer to Ice. Standing where Ice had stood.

It didn’t. It felt like standing in someone’s coat. Warm, and the right shape, and nothing to do with him at all.

He asked on a warm night in the second week of the channel.

It was late. The Broken Lance had emptied out downstairs. The others had gone up, except Chris, who was at the table by the window with his three notebooks, cross-referencing Chris’s list of lines with everybody’s numbers by candlelight, muttering. Winter had gone up an hour ago. Newish was asleep in the big chair by the fire with her mouth open. Ina was reading, which he did every night, in the corner, with his mask hung on the back of his chair.

The channel was quiet. Gigichi had told everyone to go to sleep twice already that week, and it seemed to be working. The last lines were from Cinderhold, an hour old: Maigo saying goodnight to everyone, then Yan saying goodnight to Maigo, then Maigo saying goodnight to Yan again.

Hako sat on the stairs with his window open, looking at it.

He’d been thinking about it all week. Since the first night, when all thirty-four of them had poured in at once, and he’d seen the names. Names he’d only known as voices, or not at all. People who’d known Ice for years. People who’d known him in rooms, in cities, in schools. All of them, right there. One line away.

He’d never have asked in a letter. A letter took a month. You couldn’t write to thirty people and say please tell me about the friend we lost. It would have been too much, too slow, too heavy, like dragging a sack across the whole kingdom.

But this wasn’t a letter. This was just a line.

He wrote it before he could talk himself out of it.

[ Hako: Can I ask something?
Hako: I only knew him nine months. Ice. Less than nearly everyone here.
Hako: He taught me CS. That’s mostly what I’ve got.
Hako: What was he like? ]

Then he closed his window, very fast, as if it were hot, and sat on the stairs with his heart going, feeling stupid.

At the table, Chris had stopped writing.

“Hako,” he said quietly. He’d seen it. Of course he had; it was in his window too. “Hako. Open it.”

Hako opened it.

It had already started.

[ Suzuyun: He carried me in CS. I carried him in Apex. We won a tournament together that we had no business winning.
Suzuyun: In the final circle he called every one of his own deaths out loud like a sports commentator. While dying.
Suzuyun: “And Ice is down. Tragic. The crowd is devastated.”
Drew: I was hosting that tournament. I had to mute the casters because they couldn’t stop laughing.
Drew: Afterwards he told me “I was the reason we won. Trust.” He had four kills. Yan had eleven.
Maigo: HE CALLED ME MAIGOTRON
Maigo: because i got a four-kill round on a pistol once and he screamed THE MACHINE so loud yan took off his headphones
Maigo: nobody else ever called me that
Maigo: i miss it ]

[ Nikorasu: I knew him from a CS community server when I was seventeen. Years of games with him, and against him.
Nikorasu: He was the only one who ever called me Mashi. Nobody else ever did.
Wendy: Salty and I were going to fly out and visit him. We had dates. We were looking at flights.
Wendy: Salty says to tell you he was the best AWPer he ever played with and the worst loser. ]

Hako stared at it.

The lines were coming fast. Faster than he could read them. The forty-line window was filling up from the bottom, and at the top the oldest lines were already starting to slide away, gone, while he was still reading the middle. Yan’s tournament scrolled up and off and disappeared while Maigo was still typing.

“No,” said Hako. “No, no, wait—“

He looked round wildly. Chris was already moving. He’d torn a fresh page out of the back of one of his notebooks and shoved it, with a pencil, into Hako’s hands.

“Write them down,” Chris said. “Write them all down. As fast as you can. Before they go.”

He wrote all night.

Sitting on the stairs, and then at the table, with the candle burning down and then another candle, in the cheap notebook Chris gave him, with his window open in front of his face and the lines coming in and in and in. He wrote in English, in a fast scrawl that got worse as the night went on. He didn’t try to make it neat. He just tried to get every line down before it slid off the top and was gone forever.

[ Gabe: We were in the same school choir. Me, Deston, Declan. Back row.
Gabe: He was always a little behind the beat. On purpose. So he could make a face at us right before the quiet part.
Deston: We got told off for laughing during the quiet part of a song in front of the whole school. He never got caught. Not once. Gabe and I got caught every time.
Gabe: thirteen years and he never once got caught ]

[ Xavier: I sat next to him in poly. He talked me into playing CS in the first week. We played every game together after that.
Xavier: He never got angry when we lost. He got quiet and then he’d say “okay. again.” And we’d queue again.
Malcolm: He and I used to talk to everyone. Every single person in our class. We made it the most bonded class in the school. That was mostly him.
Malcolm: I kept saying I’d visit next year. ]

[ John: I was on his facecam streams. Every single one. He’d make me hold things up to the camera. A banana. A cat. Once, my own shoe.
RX: osu. He was the best of all of us at osu. I chased his scores for six years and never caught him. He played Hidden on everything like it was nothing.
RX: I was going to go up for his birthday.
Azac: He was always our tank. Every MMO. First in, last out. When he was gone there was a hole at the front of the party and none of us knew how to stand in it. ]

[ Calvin: I knew him a year. He showed me his kettle like it was a trophy.
Rachael: It was a terrible kettle.
Dennis: It was the worst kettle I’ve ever seen.
Rachael: He knew it was terrible. That’s why he loved it.
Rachael: I asked him for advice once, about something so embarrassing I’m not telling any of you, and he answered completely seriously and didn’t laugh once. Then he laughed for ten minutes.
Dennis: He was the only person who never lied to me. I’d have known. ]

Hako wrote it all. The kettle. The choir. The banana and the cat and John’s own shoe. His hand ached. His candle guttered. Chris lit him another one without a word and went back to watching his own window, very still, with his hand over his mouth.

Around midnight, the girls from Wrenhaven came in.

[ Higu: He modded my chat. He was always there. Every hour of the night.
Higu: He banned a man once for being cruel to me, and in the ban reason he wrote the man an apology poem. Four lines. Rhyming. Very sincere. It said he hoped tomorrow would be better and he was still banned.
Higu: And then “trust.”
Xola: he was in my chat every single stream. gifting subs. every stream.
Xola: we were in an apex tournament together, the first time we met. he was so loud. i liked him immediately
Gigichi: He sent me “you okay?” once, when nobody else noticed I’d gone quiet.
Gigichi: I never answered. I’m answering now. I’m okay. Mostly. ]

[ Althea: We called every night for years. He’d play games for me, or with me, and I’d sing for him.
Althea: I wish I’d talked to him more at the end. School got busy. I thought there’d be time.
PenguChi: i only knew him as the guy in chat who was suspiciously good at Words on Stream ☺
PenguChi: he beat me every time. every time. i still don’t know how ]

Hako wrote suspiciously good at Words on Stream and stopped, and looked at it, and laughed out loud on his own on the stairs at one in the morning. It was so exactly like the man in the empty server.

He’d never seen Words on Stream. He didn’t know what it was. He wrote it down anyway.

The capital came in after that, long and careful, six lines at a time.

[ Hanarisu: He watched my streams from when I was nineteen. He’d do the most ridiculous things in chat just to make me laugh on stream.
Hanarisu: He never once asked for anything back.
Jared: He made a picture of me eating at a fast food place into a vintage holiday postcard and sent it to Alice instead of me. “Your friend is on holiday. He says he doesn’t miss you.”
Jared: Eleven postcards, by the end.
Milli: I knew him as the Minecraft bingo guy. That’s all. I’m sorry. I wish it was more.
Cursiffe: Good Octane player. Fast jokes. Faster than anyone I’ve met. Here or there. ]

And then Aleaka, who’d been quiet all night. Hako had wondered whether she’d write anything. He knew her name. He knew, from Chris’s notebook, that she was the one who’d asked everyone who brought you to who, all through the winter, and been answered, by every single one of them, with the same name.

[ Aleaka: We had a game. I’d talk down to him. Bossy. Like a strict teacher. He liked it; he’d go all meek and say “yes ma’am” and pretend to be terrified.
Aleaka: And then just when I’d got him properly cowed, he’d turn it round and say “you’re cute when you’re bossy.”
Aleaka: Every single time. And I’d lose. Every single time.
Aleaka: I knew him less than a year. He was one of my closest friends. I don’t know how that happens. It just did. ]

Hako wrote that one down very slowly, and very carefully, because it seemed important.

Then, one after another, Group 5.

[ Hyvred: I posted a clip of what my tunnel vision looks like. He called it osu’s Flashlight mod. He didn’t know I was actually blind.
Hyvred: Then he found out and apologised SO much. Like forty messages. I laughed so hard I cried.
Tiger: I built a house in Minecraft for three weeks. A modded wither blew up the entire spawn. He laughed until he cried.
Tiger: And then he helped me build it again. And it got blown up again. And he laughed until he cried again.
Kei: I had a bad night once. A really bad one, about my ex. He stayed on a call with me until five in the morning.
Kei: He didn’t fix anything. He didn’t try to. He just stayed. ]

And Bee, last of the five, after a long gap.

[ Bee: I didn’t know him. He sent me a heart once, under a drawing.
Bee: That’s all I’ve got. But I’m keeping it. ]

Hako read that, and had to put his pencil down for a minute.

Group 7 went last. Hako didn’t know if that was on purpose. He suspected Chris had held them back, somehow, with a look; Chris had that kind of authority without ever seeming to try.

Newish had woken up, in the big chair, at some point, and found everyone’s windows open, and read back as far as she could, and then sat there in silence with her knees pulled up. Ina had put down his book and was sitting very straight in the corner, reading. Winter had come down the stairs in her blanket, white hair everywhere, and was sitting on the step above Hako, reading over his shoulder.

[ Chris: Apex trios. Me, him and Winter. Or me, him and Newish. Always him.
Chris: I wanted to meet him in person one day. I had a whole plan. I was going to surprise him. I never told him.
Winter: I was in Vietnam when he died. Offline. Two months. I came back and he’d been gone for two months and nobody could reach me to tell me.
Winter: I only knew him four months. I still think about the two I missed.
Ina: I always thought he was cool. I was surprised to learn, later, that he thought the same about me.
Ina: When I came back to the group, after a difficult time, he was the first to talk to me as if I’d never left. ]

And then Newish, after a long time, very short.

[ Newish: last thing he said to me was “love you guys. except newish.”
Newish: then “joking. mostly. trust.”
Newish: i said rude. that was the last thing i said
Newish: i’d say it again honestly. it was the right answer ]

Behind him on the stairs, Winter laughed, a small surprised wet sound, and put her hand on Hako’s shoulder and left it there.

It was nearly three when the channel went quiet.

Not because people stopped. Because they’d said it. All of it. Thirty-four people, one after another, and then a long silence, and then a few more lines straggling in from people who’d thought of one more thing. He used to call everything a “W.” He always said he’d be on early and never was. He hated mushrooms. He loved mushrooms, he lied about hating them. And then, slowly, nothing.

Hako looked at the notebook.

He’d filled twenty-three pages. Both sides. His handwriting had gone from bad to unreadable to something you’d need a cipher for, and back again, and the pages were smudged with candle grease and one of them had a ring on it where Chris had put down a mug of tea. Thirty-four people. Hundreds of lines. Every one of them gone from the channel now, scrolled off the top into nothing. Every one of them still here.

He turned back to the first page. Across the top, at some point in the night without really noticing, he’d written a title.

What was he like.

No question mark. He didn’t know why. It hadn’t felt like a question by the end.

“Hako,” said Chris softly, from the table.

“Yeah.”

“Read us some.”

So he did. Sitting on the stairs of the Broken Lance at three in the morning with the candle guttering, with Winter on the step above him and Newish in the big chair and Ina in the corner and Chris at the table, he read them some. The choir. The tournament. The ban poem. The kettle. The postcards. You’re cute when you’re bossy. He stayed on the call until five.

He didn’t read them his own. He hadn’t written it down. He’d been too busy writing everyone else’s.

When he finished, nobody said anything for a long time.

“He was the same,” said Ina at last, from the corner. His voice was quiet, the beautiful British voice everyone wanted to listen to, very gentle. “Did you notice? Thirty-four people. Thirty-four different stories. And he was exactly the same in every one of them.” He looked at Hako. “Patient. Ridiculous. Kind in a way he made sure nobody could thank him for. Always the one who stayed.”

Hako looked down at the notebook.

Three nights in an empty server. You’re not bad. You’re just new.

“Yeah,” he said. His voice cracked. “Yeah. He was.”

He wrote his own last of all. After the others had gone up, alone, at the table by the window, in the grey before dawn.

He taught me CS when everyone else would have muted me. Three nights in an empty server, the same angles, over and over. He never got bored. He never got annoyed. On the third night he said “you’re not bad, you’re just new. New’s different from bad. New gets better.”

I got better.

I’m holding his angle now. In an arena, in another world. I think he’d laugh. I think he’d say I’m holding it wrong. I think he’d come and stand next to me and show me.

He looked at it for a while. Then he wrote one more line, underneath, small.

I knew him nine months. I know him better now.

He opened his window before he went up. He didn’t know why; he just wanted to see the channel one last time, empty and quiet at the end of the night. It was. Forty lines of nothing but goodnights.

His own window, above it, said thirty-six.

Five, in a night. He hadn’t touched his spear. He’d sat on a staircase and written down what other people said about a dead man, and five percent had come up out of the bottom of him like water coming up in a well.

He didn’t understand it. He didn’t need to. He closed the window, and picked up the notebook, the Ice book, and held it against his chest, and went up to bed as the sky went pale over the Lists.

Somewhere else, a very long way to the north, at the end of a wooden jetty on a dark lake, someone else had been reading too.

All night. Every line. From the first question to the last goodnight.

He’d stopped once, about an hour in, to close his window. Then he’d stood up. Then, after a little while, he’d sat down again and opened it, and kept reading, as if nothing had happened.

But that was another story.
