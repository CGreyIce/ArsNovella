The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 3: The Anniversary

Chapter 78

Unseen

4,044 words · 18 min read

RX was fast.

He’d always been fast. He talked fast; his mother used to say he talked like a man who’d been told the world was ending at noon. He thought fast. He’d played osu! for six years at a level that made other people stop and watch over his shoulder: circles coming up on the screen to the beat of a song, hundreds of them, a thousand, and you had to click each one exactly when the ring closed round it, and RX could do it at speeds that made people in poly put their headsets down on the desk and say that’s not human. That’s a medical condition.

And Declan had been better.

That was the thing nobody outside the two of them ever quite believed. Declan, who died loudly in Apex and narrated it, who whiffed AWP shots and blamed the server. At osu! he’d been the best of all of them. Not a little better. Better in a way RX could see and couldn’t touch. He played Hidden on everything: the mod that takes the circles away before you click them, so that you’re hitting empty air where something used to be, on rhythm and memory and nothing else. He’d play a map RX couldn’t pass on normal, with Hidden on, half asleep, at two in the morning, talking the whole time, and full combo it.

RX had chased his scores for six years. Every week, on a call at midnight, the two of them. He’d never once caught him.

He’d written it in the channel, in the summer, for Hako’s book: He was the best of all of us at osu. I chased his scores for six years and never caught him. He’d read it back and had to go and stand in the yard for a while. Because it was the only game in the world where RX’s hands had ever felt as fast as his head, and the one person who’d been faster had spent six years on a call at midnight, every week, showing him how.

They’d drifted, at the end. That was the other thing. Declan had moved to Malacca, and the midnight calls had gone from every week to every month to sometimes, the way things do, and nobody had decided it. RX had meant to fix it. He’d meant to fly up for Declan’s birthday. He’d had the date in his phone. He’d had it in his phone for two years.

He’d been fast at everything in his life except that.

And here, of all things, he was an assassin.

The window had decided it in the first week, or the aura had, or RX had; he’d never been sure which. Aura, in most people, went out: into a blade, into a shield, into the air round you. Petra’s reached a hand’s width past her steel. Xav’s landed half a heartbeat before he did. Declan’s, the Black Blade’s, had filled a battlefield, Petra said; she’d felt it from a ditch three hundred paces away.

RX’s went in.

He didn’t know how else to describe it. When he let the aura come up, it didn’t want to go anywhere. It wanted to fold. Inward, into him, smaller and smaller, the way you’d fold a letter, until he could feel himself getting quieter. Not quieter to his own ears. Quieter to everybody else’s. Garrick had noticed it in the second month: I keep losing track of you in the yard. I don’t lose track of people.

And at the bottom of his skill page, ever since the winter, there’d been a slot. Grey, at first. Unseen · locked · requires Tier 1. Silver, since the spring, when he’d reached twenty-one percent and it had started, very faintly, to shine.

It was the first thing in this world that had felt like it was his. Not Xav’s, who’d always been the best of them. Not John’s, with his light, or Malcolm’s, with his book. His. A thing he was fast at, that nobody else could do.

He’d been at forty-eight percent for a month. The slot had been silver for six. It hadn’t opened.

Duskhollow was quiet in the autumn.

Too quiet, the old adventurers said, in the guild hall, over their beer. The Hollow had always been busy at the end of the year: the shallow levels full of apprentice parties scraping together enough coin for the winter, the first ring loud with fights and shouting and the occasional scream. Not this year. Not since the spring, when Orla Fane’s expedition had gone down through the oak-and-iron door below the first ring and eleven of them hadn’t come back up.

Harrow had bricked the door up. Three courses of brick and a layer of mortar and a guild seal stamped into the middle of it. Nobody had been through since.

John went down every morning anyway.

Not past the first ring. Just to the bricked door. He’d stand there with his hand flat against the new bricks for an hour, in the dark, with one small mote of light floating beside his head, and come back up with his face very still. RX had gone with him a few times. He’d never felt anything. John said you didn’t feel it; you saw it, if you were him. The light, the pale breathing light from far below, coming up through the gaps in the brick in thin lines like light round a door at night.

“Still breathing,” John said, every morning, at breakfast.

“Faster?” said Malcolm, every morning, with his pencil ready.

“No,” said John. “The same. Slow.” He’d look at his porridge. “It’s not in a hurry.”

Hyvred had said exactly the same about the thing beyond the pass, in the channel. It’s not in a hurry. RX had noticed. So had Malcolm; it was in the book.

The channel changed on a Tuesday.

RX found it first thing, the way everybody did. Two hundred lines instead of forty, and times on every one. He scrolled up through Dennis’s post from Kingsferry, three times, and Rachael’s I’m fine, and Xav’s where’s dennis’s post?, and then the whole kingdom waking up to it, one line after another, WHAT and I CAN SCROLL UP and Thea, at the bottom of it all: It wasn’t me.

“Somebody patched the server,” said RX, at breakfast.

They all looked at him.

“Somebody patched the server,” he said again. He was eating very fast, the way he did when he was thinking. “Overnight. Thea said the forty was written too deep for her to touch. And this morning it’s two hundred, with timestamps, the night after Dennis asked.” He waved his spoon. “That’s not a bug fixing itself. Somebody shipped a feature.”

“Who?” said John.

RX opened his mouth. Shut it again.

“No idea,” he said. “Someone with admin, I suppose. If there’s such a thing as admin here.”

Malcolm had his book open. He wrote IF somebody changed the channel, and then sat looking at it with his pencil hovering, and didn’t write a THEN. It was the first time RX had ever seen him not have one.

Xav, at the end of the table, hadn’t said anything at all. He was looking at the times on the lines, the way he looked at a scoreboard.

“Doesn’t matter who,” he said at last. “It works. Eat your porridge.”

That was all anyone said about it. Nobody knew. Not in Duskhollow, anyway.

Deston’s letter came two days later.

A rider from downriver, at noon, with a satchel. Numbered, sealed with one of Cursiffe’s blue ink strips, the way all the real letters were now, since the summer. Xavier, Duskhollow. Not to all of them. Just Xav.

Xav took it into the corner of the common room at Mother Annick’s and opened it with his back to them. RX watched his shoulders while he read. They were very still.

It wasn’t a long letter. Xav read it once. Then again. Then a third time, slowly, the way you’d read the patch notes for a game you’d been waiting on for a year.

Then he folded it up very small, and put it in the inside pocket of his coat, against his chest, and turned round.

“Upriver,” said Xav. “In the spring.”

Nobody said anything.

“Groups 4 and 6,” said Xav. “Together. When the river opens. Up the Wren. Past here, and on.” He looked at the four of them. “Deston’s thread.”

“What thread?” said Azac.

And Xav told them.

He told it the way Xav told everything, the way he’d done post-game debriefs in the first winter, when Azac was in bed with a broken shield: in order, without wasting a word, and without looking away.

The faded thread. Colourless and old, like a rope that’s hung in a window for thirty summers. From Deston’s chest, as thick as Gabe’s, running north. Past them. Up the river, toward the lake country. Seen in the spring, and kept quiet, all summer. Trembling once, when Deston and Gabe hummed the choir song and left the gap.

Warmer, since the night of the sum.

“It’s warm now,” said Xav, quoting. “It wasn’t, before.” He stopped. “That’s what he wrote.”

Azac had gone white under his scar.

John had his hands over his face.

Malcolm wasn’t writing. He’d put the pencil down on the table, very carefully, and was looking at it.

RX found he’d stood up. He didn’t remember standing up. He was standing in the middle of Mother Annick’s common room with his fists clenched, and his heart going faster than he’d ever felt it go, faster than the hardest song he’d ever played, and he couldn’t think of a single thing to say. Him. RX. Who talked like the world was ending at noon.

“Declan,” said John, through his hands.

“Declan,” said Xav.

“Not the channel,” said Xav, later.

They were still at the table. Mother Annick had come in and seen their faces and gone out again without a word, and come back with tea, and gone out again. The light was going.

“Not the channel,” said Xav again. “Deston says not yet. Gabe says not yet. Group 3 asked him straight out, on the Redmarch, and he didn’t answer. He walked away.” He turned his cup round on the table. “He’s got his reasons. He always had his reasons. He just didn’t always tell you what they were until after.”

“Like the AWP,” said John. “On Inferno. In the final. When he held banana for a minute and a half and wouldn’t tell anyone why.”

“Like the AWP on Inferno,” said Xav.

“And then three of them came through at once.”

“And he got all three,” said Xav. “And then he said trust on the call, and Malcolm called him a menace for a week.”

“He was a menace,” said Malcolm, faintly.

Nobody laughed. Then all of them did, at once, helplessly, the five of them round the table at Mother Annick’s in the dusk, laughing until Azac had to put his head down on his arms, and John’s face was wet, and RX was holding the edge of the table with both hands to stay upright.

“Not yet,” said Xav, when they’d stopped. “He doesn’t want thirty-five people turning up on his doorstep. He doesn’t want hunters following them.” He looked at his folded hands. “And maybe he wants to tell us himself. To our faces. The way he’d want to.” He was quiet for a moment. “So we go to him. In the spring. And let him.”

“Let them,” said RX, before he could stop himself.

Xav looked at him.

“That’s what Hana said he said,” said RX. “To the Advisor. About us. Let them.” He was talking fast again; he couldn’t help it. “So we let him. We don’t put it in the channel. We don’t put it anywhere. We go upriver in the spring, and we let him tell us himself, to our faces, the way he’d want to.”

“And then Gabe hits him,” said Azac.

“And then Gabe hits him,” said Xav. “Deston says he can’t. I’m the tiebreaker.” Something that might have been a smile. “Agreed? Not the channel. Not anyone.”

“Agreed,” said John.

“Agreed,” said Azac.

Malcolm picked up his pencil and wrote it down. IF we go upriver, THEN we tell nobody first. He underlined it. “Agreed.”

“Agreed,” said RX.

Garrick said no.

He said it the next morning, in the guild hall, when Xav told him. Not unkindly. Garrick was never unkind. He’d led parties in the Hollow for twenty years and he talked to Group 6 the way he’d talked to every party he’d ever led: as a raid leader talks to a raid, when he knows exactly what the raid wants to do and exactly why it shouldn’t.

“The Hollow,” he said. “You’ve seen it. The door’s bricked and the light’s still coming up through it. Harrow’s got half the guild on watch rotations. In the spring the shallow levels open again and every apprentice in the kingdom comes back, and I need people down there who know what that light looks like when it changes.” He looked at Xav. “I need you. I need John’s eyes. I need Azac’s shields at the top of that stair. I can’t spare five for a trip up the river.”

“We’ll come back,” said Xav.

“Will you?” Garrick looked at him for a long time. “I’ve led a lot of parties, Xavier. I know what it looks like when somebody’s found a reason to go somewhere and not come back.”

Xav didn’t answer. He didn’t lie, either. RX watched him not lie.

“We’ll come back,” said Xav again. “But we’re going.”

Garrick looked at the five of them for a long moment. Then he sighed, and rubbed his face with both hands.

“I’ll talk to Harrow,” he said. “I’m not promising anything.”

Petra said yes.

She didn’t say it in the guild hall. She said it that afternoon, in the yard behind Mother Annick’s, where she drilled them. Where she’d taught Xav to stand still for twenty minutes, and then forty, and then two hours, until he could wait out anyone. Where RX had spent the last six months trying to disappear, and failing, because Petra always found him.

It was the game they played. Petra’s game. Hide. She’d stand in the middle of the yard with her eyes shut, and RX would go wherever he wanted, behind the woodpile, up on the wall, flat in the shadow of the water butt, and fold his aura in, as far as it would go, and she’d count to fifty. And then she’d turn round, with her eyes still shut, and point at him.

Every time. For six months.

“You’re loud,” she’d said, the first week. “Not your feet. You’ve got quiet feet. Your aura’s loud. You fold it in, and it’s still all there, folded up in a box, humming. I can hear the box.” She’d shrugged. “Everyone can hear the box. You’ve got to get rid of the box.”

He’d never known what that meant.

He played it that afternoon because Petra told him to. He didn’t want to. He’d been up all night, the way all of them had, thinking about a thread going north and a server being patched and a birthday he’d had in his phone for two years.

He went to the corner behind the water butt. The one place in the yard with real shadow. And he crouched down, and let the aura come up, and folded it. In. Smaller. The way he always did.

“Fifty,” said Petra, in the middle of the yard, with her eyes shut.

She turned round. She pointed. At the water butt.

“Box,” she said.

He stood up. He didn’t say anything. He went back to the corner.

“Again,” said Petra.

He crouched down. He let the aura come up. And he thought, for some reason, about osu!.

About the last map he’d ever played with Declan.

He didn’t know why he thought about it. It just came up, the way things came up when you were very tired. A midnight call, a year before. Before they’d drifted the rest of the way. A hard map, with Hidden on, the kind RX had been failing for a month: the circles fading out of the screen before he could get to them, so he was clicking at nothing, late, guessing, losing the rhythm and the combo and his temper. And Declan, on the call, playing the same map at the same time and not missing anything, saying quite seriously, you know what the secret is? You don’t click the circle. You wait till the circle isn’t there anymore. And then it’s already clicked.

It had sounded like the stupidest thing anyone had ever said about osu!. RX had laughed so hard he’d failed the map.

Then he’d played it again, and stopped looking for the circles, and let them go, and hit the empty air where they’d been, on the beat, without seeing anything at all. And passed it. For the first time. Declan had said told you on the call, and trust, and RX had never forgotten it.

He thought about it now, crouched behind the water butt in another world, with his aura folded up in a box, humming.

You wait till the circle isn’t there anymore.

He didn’t fold it.

He let it go.

Not out. Not in. Away. The way you let go of a held breath, all the way, until there’s nothing left in you to hold. He let the aura come up and then he didn’t do anything with it at all. He didn’t fold it. He didn’t hide it. He didn’t put it in a box. He just let it be so quiet that it wasn’t anywhere, and then he let himself be that quiet too, crouched in the shadow in the corner of the yard, until he couldn’t have told you where he ended and the shadow started.

He found the edge. He didn’t look for it. It was just there, at the bottom of the quiet, where Thea had said it would be.

He wrote one word. It was barely a word. It was the sound you make to a room before it goes still.

Hush.

“Fifty,” said Petra.

She turned round. Her eyes were shut. She lifted her hand to point.

And stopped.

RX watched her from behind the water butt. He watched her stand there in the middle of the empty yard with her hand half raised and her eyes shut and her head turning, slowly, left, and right, and left again. Listening. The way she’d listened for six months, and found him every time.

She lowered her hand.

She opened her eyes.

She looked round the yard. At the woodpile. At the wall. At the gate. At the water butt. Her eyes went past it, and came back, and went past it again.

“RX,” she said.

He didn’t answer. He couldn’t. He didn’t want to break it.

“RX,” said Petra again. And then, very quietly, in a voice he’d never heard her use: “I can’t find you.”

He stood up.

It was the hardest thing he’d ever done. Harder than letting go. He stood up from behind the water butt and the quiet came off him like water off a dog, all at once, and he was just there again, a skinny man in the corner of a yard, shaking. Petra’s head snapped round. Her hand went to her sword before she could stop it.

Then she saw who it was. And she just stood there, in the middle of the yard, and looked at him.

He opened his window. His hands were shaking so much it took two tries.

[ RX · Tier 1: Drawn · Skills: Unseen ]

The slot wasn’t silver anymore. It wasn’t grey. It was just a word, like any other word on the page. Unseen. His.

The eleventh. Of thirty-five.

He started talking. He couldn’t help it. “I didn’t fold it. That’s the thing. You said get rid of the box and I never knew what that meant, I thought it meant fold it smaller, and it doesn’t, it means don’t, you just don’t, you let it go and you don’t put it anywhere, and he said, Declan said, years ago, about osu!, about Hidden, he said you don’t click the circle you wait till it isn’t there, and it sounded so stupid, and it was right, it was right the whole time—“

“RX,” said Petra.

He stopped.

“Breathe,” said Petra.

He breathed.

“Better,” said Petra. And then, after a moment, the corner of her mouth moved. “Much better.”

She found him on the wall that night.

He’d gone up there because he couldn’t sleep, and because the wall at Mother Annick’s looked north, up the river, toward the lake country, and he’d found himself wanting to look at it. He was sitting on the parapet with his knees up when he heard her on the steps. He didn’t fold. He didn’t hush. He just let her find him.

She sat down a little way along the parapet. She didn’t say anything for a while. Petra never did.

“Xavier told me,” she said at last. “Where you’re going. In the spring.”

RX looked at her.

“Not why,” said Petra. “He didn’t tell me why. He doesn’t have to.” She looked north, up the river, the way RX had been looking. “I was a recruit on the Redmarch, eight years ago. You know that story. Forty of us in a ditch, and the line gone, and things coming over the lip at us in the dark. And one man came for us. On his own. Across three hundred paces of them. And he only ever moved once.” She was quiet. “I watched him for less than a minute. I’ve spent eight years trying to fight like that.”

“I know,” said RX.

“Xavier fights like that.” Petra didn’t look at him. “I told him so, in the first week. I told him, in the summer, that he’d had two teachers. Me, and someone before me.” She paused. “I’ve been wondering all year who the someone before me was. Where a boy from another world learned to wait like the Black Blade.”

RX didn’t say anything. He didn’t lie, either. He found he couldn’t. Not to her.

“Go,” said Petra. “In the spring. Garrick will grumble. Harrow will say no. I’ll talk to them both.” She stood up. “And if you find the man who taught you, whoever he is, wherever he is…”

She stopped. She looked north, up the black river, for a long moment.

“Tell him a girl from a ditch on the Redmarch says thank you,” she said. “Tell him she’s alive. Tell him she’s teaching now.” The corner of her mouth moved again. “Tell him one of his is better at hiding than she is.”

She went back down the steps without waiting for an answer.

RX sat on the wall for a long time after that.

He thought about a date in his phone. Two years. A birthday he’d meant to fly up for, and kept meaning to, and kept saying next month, the way you do, because there was always going to be a next month.

He thought about a man who’d been faster than him at the one thing he was fastest at, and had spent six years on a call at midnight, every week, teaching him to catch up.

“Spring,” he said, to the river. Out loud. Fast, the way he said everything. “I’m coming in the spring. Not next month. Not sometime. Spring. You’d better be there.” He stopped. “And you’d better not have got rusty at osu!, because I’m going to beat you this time. With Hidden on.”

The river ran north in the dark, toward the lakes.

Then, because he could now, because it was his, RX let the quiet come up, and let it go, and sat there on the wall above Duskhollow, unseen, and let himself cry where nobody in the whole world could find him.
