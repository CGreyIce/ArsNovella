The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 7: Myths

Chapter 206

Scouts

9,289 words · 40 min read

Wendy counted things.

She’d always done it. On Earth it had been followers and subs and how many days since she’d last left the flat, and how many hours Salty had slept, and how many times he’d said it’s fine in a week, which was usually somewhere in the forties. She’d kept spreadsheets. Not for anything. Just because a number was a thing you could look at, and a thing you could look at was a thing you didn’t have to believe. You could just know it.

She’d kept it up out here. She didn’t have the spreadsheets any more. She had her head, which turned out to be nearly as good, and a lot of time on a wall.

She knew there were four hundred and eighty-one markers in Aldous’s rows between the east gate and the knoll, counting the ones with no names. She knew there were thirty-nine steps up the east stair and one of them was a different height, the twenty-third, and that everybody who’d been here less than a month tripped on it. She knew Niko’s dawns: every one, for two years, written down in her head in a long column, eleven and nineteen and twenty-two and twenty-two and twenty-two, and now, twice, twenty-nine. She knew Yan slept an hour and ten minutes more a night than he had before the grave. She knew Salty had made the twelfth dinner a hundred and four times since the autumn, and that the hundred and fourth had been the first one that tasted right, and that he’d been up at four to pick the peppers.

She knew she’d made two hundred and twelve arrows over the winter, and that a hundred and eighty of them were in the armoury in bundles of twenty, and thirty-two were in her quiver, and that she could hold one in the air for six heartbeats if she didn’t breathe.

She counted the riders out.

Two. Collum, on the grey, and an older man called Orrin, on the bay. Out of the east gate at ten on Thursday morning, at a canter, along Aldous’s burial track between the markers, and then off the track and down into the nearest of the old riverbeds, and away east along the green, getting smaller. She watched them until she couldn’t.

She counted the hours.

They came back on Friday at dusk. Thirty-three and a half hours.

She was on the wall when they came. She’d been on the wall most of the day; she’d brought her feathers up. Salty had brought her lunch and sat with her and not said anything, and gone back down to the forge, where she could hear him, all afternoon, hammering something that wasn’t a horseshoe.

Two specks on the red. Then two horses. Coming back up the riverbed, not at a canter. At a walk. Both horses with their heads down.

Collum was white.

Wendy had seen people come in off the red white before. She’d been white herself, two summers ago, walking home behind a man with a sword on his shoulder. She knew what it looked like when it had gone all the way through somebody and come out the other side. Collum came in under the gate on the grey with his face like paper and his hands very still on the reins, and slid off at the mounting block, and his knees nearly went.

Rhea caught him by the arm.

“Report,” said Rhea. Not unkindly. The way you’d say breathe.

“The foot of the hills,” said Collum. “Where the old rivers come down. We got there at first light.” He swallowed. “The green’s gone.”

“Gone?”

“Where they walked.” He looked at Orrin, who’d come in behind him on the bay and was getting down more slowly, an old soldier, with a face that had seen most things and didn’t want to see any of them again. “It’s grey. On every riverbed coming down off the hills. A stripe of grey down the middle of the green, where they walked on it. Like something was dragged.” He swallowed again. “And the prints. In the grey.”

“How many?”

“We couldn’t count,” said Orrin, behind him. Flatly. “Too many on top of each other. They walk in each other’s steps.”

“Single file,” said Collum. “All of them. On the green. Coming this way.” He looked at Rhea. “They don’t spread out, ma’am. Not one of them. Every print on the green, and the grass dead under it.”

Rhea didn’t say anything for a moment.

“They always spread out,” she said.

“Not this time,” said Orrin.

They didn’t see anything that night. Wendy hadn’t thought they would.

Scouts didn’t come in the day. The first winter she’d learned that, on this wall, with her hands numb on the stone: they came at dusk, or in the dark, and they stood still in daylight, out on the red somewhere, in the gullies and the dry rivers, so still you could look straight at one and think it was a dead tree. Then the light went and they moved. Step-step. Stop.

Maigo sat on the step of the east postern all night again with his foot under the door. Wendy knew, because she went down twice and he was there both times, and both times she put a blanket on him and he didn’t notice. In the morning he came up to the long hall for breakfast with his face grey under the bark and red dust to his knee, and sat down next to her, and said, “Closer.”

“How close?”

“I don’t know. I can’t tell distance. I can just tell more.” He stared at his porridge. “They stop in the day. They’re stopped now. They’re lying on her roots.”

Wendy put a spoon in his hand. He held it.

“Eat that,” said Wendy.

He ate it.

Rhea drilled them all Saturday.

Not in the yard. Rhea took them out of the east gate and a hundred paces onto the red, to a place where the ground went up in a low hump between two old gullies, and drew a ring on the top of it with the toe of her boot, thirty paces across. And then she put forty soldiers round the ring and drilled them in it until the sun went over. Shields out. Spears in the second rank, over the shoulders of the first. Archers in the middle. Ring. Ring. Ring. Over and over.

Wendy stood in the middle of it with her bow and watched the shape of it.

A low hump between two gullies. A ring on the top, thirty paces.

“That’s the knoll,” she said to Salty.

Salty, beside her, with his greatsword planted point-down in the red in front of him like a fence post, didn’t say anything.

“She’s drilling the knoll,” said Wendy. “She’s already decided.”

“Yeah,” said Salty.

“We don’t know they’re going for it.”

Salty looked at her.

He had a way of looking at her, Salty. He’d had it on Earth, on a camera, in a voice call, across a room. Patient. Not arguing. Just waiting for her to finish saying the thing she was saying so she could hear it herself.

“We don’t know,” said Wendy.

“Yan knows,” said Salty.

“Yan feels.”

“Yeah,” said Salty. And waited.

Wendy looked at the ring. At the forty soldiers going round it, round it, round it, with Rhea shouting at them. At the place in the middle where, on a knoll an hour east of here, there’d be a cairn, and a black stone, and a tree.

“Fine,” she said. “Fine. He knows.”

That was the thing about Wendy, she’d always thought. She didn’t believe in things.

She’d been like that on Earth, for as long as she could remember. You didn’t believe the weather. You didn’t believe the gacha rates or the man in the shop who said it would last a lifetime. You believed what you could see, and you kept a little bit back, always, so that when it all went wrong you’d have something left.

It had been the only argument she and Salty had ever had that mattered. Not about this world. Before. About Malaysia. About flying out to meet a man they’d talked to every night for three years, and Salty saying I’m going, the way he said everything, like it was already done, and Wendy saying you can’t just go, and all the reasons. The money. The flights. The fact that you didn’t know people, not really, from a voice on a call, you only thought you did, and what if he was different, what if it was weird, what if—

And Salty had listened to all of it, patient, the way he did, and at the end he’d said, yeah. And then, I’m going.

And she’d gone and looked up flights to Malaysia that same night, at two in the morning, with her jaw set, because if he was going she was going.

It wasn’t belief. She’d known that, doing it. It was the opposite. It was what you did when you didn’t believe in the thing at all, but you believed in the person going to it.

They’d never booked the flights.

He’d said it to her once, out here, in so many words. In the frost, the second winter, at this grave, with the snow starting to come down on the black stone they’d just carried out to it. If you go, I go. And she’d said, That one I believe.

She did. That was the trouble.

She’d never told him she saw. She’d never told anyone. On the red, with fifty of them round the ring and Aldous’s fire going out, and Salty standing in the middle of all of it with the black sword in his arms that he couldn’t swing, going come on, then, and a big one coming round behind Maigo at her, low, gathering. Her last arrow. Ten paces. Eight.

And she’d seen him.

Fifteen paces away. Letting go. His hands opening on the grip of the sword, his weight already going forward out from under it, to run to her, with the sword falling and fifty of them watching and the thing in the air and him too far, much too far, knowing he was too far, and going anyway.

She’d seen his face.

She’d never said anything about it. Not that night, when he lay in the infirmary with his ribs strapped and she sat beside him until morning. Not since. It sat in her like a stone in a shoe that you’ve decided to keep walking on. She didn’t know if she was angry about it. She didn’t know if it was the best thing anyone had ever done for her, or the stupidest. Probably both. With Salty it was usually both.

She counted it, though. It was in her head, in the long column. One time he’d let go of something that mattered more than him, for her.

She was very much afraid of the second.

They came at dusk on Sunday.

She saw them first. She always saw things first; it was what she was for. She was on the east wall at the twelfth merlon, beside Yan, who was sitting with his back against it with his eyes shut and Suzu awake on his collarbone, and the sun was going down behind the fort and throwing the shadow of the walls out long across the red, toward the mountains, and the mountains were going pink.

And something moved on the green.

A long way out. Past the knoll. Past the small green flame of the ash, out where the two old riverbeds came down across the plain from the east and met below it. On the thin green thread down the middle of the nearer one.

Grey on green. A burst. And stop.

“Yan,” said Wendy.

“I know,” said Yan, without opening his eyes.

The commander had a glass.

A real one. Brass, in sections, that slid out to the length of your arm. He’d brought it from Solmere and it was the best thing in the fort, and he kept it in a leather case on his desk and polished it, and he’d never once, Wendy was fairly sure, let anyone else look through it. He came up the east stair with it now at a run, tripping on the twenty-third step, and held it out to her without a word.

She put it to her eye.

The red jumped at her. The knoll. The ash, close enough suddenly to see the leaves, every one of them flat to the east. The cairn, grey. The black stone. And past it, down the slope, the two green threads of the riverbeds coming in from the east and meeting.

And on the threads, grey.

They were exactly as she remembered. She’d thought she might have remembered them wrong. Made them worse in her head, the way you do. She hadn’t. Tall. Thin. Bent forward from the waist, as if they were always about to fall and never did. Grey, the colour of the grass, the colour of nothing. No faces. Long arms. Long fingers. Moving the way nothing that was alive moved: a burst, quick and smooth, three steps, four, too fast, and then stopping dead, all at once, perfectly still, as if somebody had cut the string.

One behind another. Down the middle of the green.

And where they’d stepped, behind them, the green was grey.

She counted.

It was the only thing to do. She didn’t decide to. Her mouth just started, under her breath, the way it did, and her eye went along the thread from front to back.

“One. Two. Three. Four.”

They stopped. She stopped. They moved. She went on.

“Eleven. Twelve.”

They didn’t come in a line, quite. That was the hard part. They came in bursts, a few at a time, and stopped, and the next few came up behind, so you had to count the stopped ones and then the moving ones and then the stopped ones again and not count anything twice. It was like counting sheep going through a gate at night. She’d never counted sheep. She’d counted followers on a stream going up and down in real time, which was the same thing, really, only nobody had ever been going to die of it.

“Thirty. Thirty-one.”

The nearer thread. Then the further one, where it came down from the north-east to meet it. More on that one.

“Sixty-two.”

Behind her on the wall walk she could hear people coming up. Rhea’s boots, quick. Aldous, slow. Salty; she knew his step on any stair. Maigo, with his bells. Niko, who you didn’t hear. And others. Soldiers. Somebody saying what’s she doing and Rhea saying shut up.

“Ninety.”

“A hundred and four.”

The light was going. She had maybe a quarter of an hour of it left. Her eye ached. She didn’t take it off the glass.

“A hundred and forty.”

The threads were thinning out at the back. She could see the end. She could see where the grey stopped on the green, a long way out, where the green was still green.

“A hundred and fifty-eight. Fifty-nine. Sixty.” A last few, at the very back, coming up in a burst. “A hundred and sixty-one.”

She stopped.

“A hundred and sixty-one,” she said. Out loud.

Nobody on the wall said anything.

“Yan,” she said, without taking her eye off the glass.

“A hundred and sixty-one,” said Yan, beside her, with his eyes shut. “Cold candles. I counted while you did. Same number.”

Wendy let her breath out. She hadn’t known she’d been holding it.

“And four,” said Yan.

They were at the back.

She’d been so busy with the small ones she’d nearly missed them. They weren’t on the green. They were walking beside it, on the red, at the very end of the line, where the grey stopped. Bigger. Much bigger. Heavy in the shoulders, with arms too long for them and horns that curved back from where a face should have been, and they didn’t move in bursts. They trotted. Steadily. The way a big dog trots behind a cart, patient, as if it could go all day, and might.

“Four,” said Wendy.

The summer before last there’d been three.

She knew what three had done. She’d seen it. Rhea thrown twenty feet. Teague’s shield split. Salty’s sword stuck in one up to the hilt and two ribs gone. Suzu torn. Aldous’s fire walked through like a curtain.

And then a line out of nowhere and a man walking across the red, and that had been that.

Four.

She kept the glass on them.

And then she saw the thing that mattered, and it took her a moment to understand it was the thing that mattered, because it was a thing that wasn’t happening.

They weren’t looking at the fort.

The first winter, she’d stood on this wall with her hands numb on the stone and watched eleven of them come out of the dark and stop past the third row of markers, and one of them had lifted its long arm and pointed. At the north tower. At the south tower. At the gate. At the patched place in the wall. At the well. At the chapel. One after another, slowly, as if it were counting. It had been the most frightening thing she’d ever seen in her life, and she’d seen a lot since: that thing with no face, pointing, very carefully, at everything she’d have to defend.

The second winter they’d come back and done it again.

The summer after, they’d stood at the edge of the red at dusk for an hour and faced the chapel.

They always looked at the fort. That was what a probe was. You came, and you counted the walls, and you went home and told whoever had sent you.

A hundred and sixty-one of them on the green in the last of the light, and four big ones behind, and not one of them had so much as turned toward Cinderhold. Not a head. Not an arm. They were coming down the riverbeds toward the place where the riverbeds met, under the knoll, in their bursts, single file, all of them, and the fort might as well not have been there.

“They’re not counting us,” said Wendy.

She took the glass down.

Everyone on the wall was looking at her.

“They’re not counting the fort,” she said. “They didn’t even look.” She held the glass out, to anybody, and the commander took it. “They’re going for the grave.”

It went very quiet on the wall.

Then Rhea said, “Ring on the stone,” to nobody in particular, the way you’d say well, then. And turned to go down the stair.

“Wait,” said the commander.

Rhea stopped.

He had the glass up. He was looking through it, at the red, for a long time. His hands were very steady on the brass. Wendy watched him and thought, not for the first time, that whatever else the young captain from Solmere was, he wasn’t stupid.

“It’s a feint,” he said.

He took the glass down.

“It’s a feint,” he said again, to Rhea, quietly, so the soldiers on the wall wouldn’t hear. They heard. “You know it is. A hundred and sixty of them, and four big ones, the most anyone’s seen on the red in nine years, and they come down in plain sight at dusk and don’t look at the fort?” He shook his head. “You don’t look at the thing you want. That’s what a feint is. You make a lot of noise somewhere else, and you wait for the garrison to go out and look at it, and then you come over the wall behind them.”

“They didn’t make a noise,” said Rhea.

“They made a hundred and sixty-one.” He looked at Wendy. “She counted.”

Wendy didn’t say anything. He wasn’t wrong. That was the worst of it. She’d been doing the sums in her own head since the word four, and she’d got there too.

“I’ve read the reports,” said the commander. “From the night. All of them. Every page in the archive, the first month I was here.” He didn’t look at Aldous. Everybody else did. “The movement on the east was cover. The real attack was on the south line. And while the garrison was looking the wrong way, they came over the infirmary wall, and—“ He stopped.

“Yes,” said Aldous.

He said it very gently. He was standing a little way along the wall walk with his book under his arm, in his old grey cassock, with the sunset on him. Fifty-four. A chaplain. A gravedigger. The man who’d buried them all.

“You’ve read it right,” said Aldous. “It was a feint. The general judged it, and he judged it right, and he sent the one man in the world who could hold the south line to the south line, and he barred the east gate for an hour so that nobody would go out of it.” He looked out at the red. “And we stood on this wall. All of us who are left. And we watched the fires on the rise.”

The commander didn’t say anything.

“It was the right order,” said Aldous. “I’d have given it. He’d have given it; he’s said so. I’m not telling you it’s a feint or it isn’t. I don’t know.” He looked at the commander then, over the top of his spectacles, kindly. “I’m telling you that nine years ago there was a general on this wall, and a king, and a court mage, and they barred that gate for an hour. And tonight there’s you. And it isn’t an hour. And it’s your gate.”

The commander looked at him for a long time.

“That’s not fair,” he said at last, quite quietly.

“No,” said Aldous. “I’m sorry. It isn’t.”

They were in the courtyard when Wendy came down.

She didn’t know who’d told them. Nobody had told them. Nobody had needed to. They’d been on the wall, some of them, and the ones who hadn’t been on the wall had heard it from the ones who had, the way news goes round a fort, faster than a horse.

The old ones.

Twenty-five of them. The ones who’d been here nine years ago. The ones who’d put their hands on their chests on the east wall, two summers ago, when a man walked out across the red with a sword on his shoulder; and again in the autumn, when he came back, and that time he’d looked up. Grey men. Bald men. Men with a hand gone, three fingers gone, an eye. Jory Platt. The old corporal from the stables. The man who kept the chapel bell. They were standing in the courtyard in the dusk in a loose rough block, the way old soldiers stand when nobody’s told them to stand anywhere, and every one of them had a spear.

Nobody had issued them spears. They’d been to the armoury and taken them.

The commander came down the stair behind Wendy and stopped on the bottom step, and looked at them.

“What’s this?” he said.

Nobody answered.

Then Teague came out of the block. With his shield on his bad arm, strapped there, because his bad arm could still do that much, and a short spear in his good hand.

“Burial detail, sir,” said Teague.

The commander stared at him.

“We’ve walked that track a thousand times,” said Teague. “Every one of us. Out with the cart and back with it empty. We know every marker on it.” He stood very straight. “Nine years ago we stood on that wall, sir, because we were told to, and it was right. We’d do it again.” He looked at the commander. “But nobody’s told us to tonight. So we thought we’d ask.”

“This is—“ said the commander. He stopped. Wendy watched him look for the word. Desertion. Mutiny. Regulations. She watched him find all of them and look at each one and put it down.

“Sergeant,” he said, instead. “That’s twenty-five men.”

“Yes, sir.”

“I’ve a hundred and ninety on this wall. If it’s a feint—“

“Then you’ll want a hundred and ninety on the wall, sir,” said Teague. “And you’ll have them. And if it isn’t, there’ll be twenty-five of us where it is.” He paused. “Twenty-five’s not a garrison, sir. It’s a burial detail.”

The commander stood on the bottom step for a long time.

Then he said, “Forty.”

Teague didn’t move.

“Forty,” said the commander. “Your twenty-five and fifteen more. Volunteers. Archers, if any of the archers want to go. No recruits.” He turned to Rhea. “Commander Rhea.” He’d never called her that. She wasn’t; she was the king’s guard, and outranked him in some ways and didn’t in others, and they’d spent three years carefully not working out which. “They’re yours. Not mine. I can’t order the king’s summoned anywhere, and I can’t stop them, and I imagine if I tried you’d break my arm.”

“Yes,” said Rhea.

“The rest of us hold the wall. If it’s a feint, I want it to find us home.” He looked out through the open arch of the gatehouse at the red, where the light was going. “And if it isn’t—“

“It isn’t,” said Rhea.

“Then bring them back,” said the commander. “Sergeant. All of them.”

“All of them, sir,” said Teague.

The fifteen came out of the barracks in about the time it took Wendy to restring her bow.

Archers, mostly; five of them. Collum, who was still white and had been told to stay and hadn’t. The young soldier called Dace, who’d gone down beside Teague two summers ago, on the red, with his leg opened, and had walked with a stick for a year, and didn’t now. And at the end, last, with his helmet on very straight and a shield on his arm that he’d polished, Garth.

“No recruits,” said the commander.

“Sir,” said Garth. He stood at attention. He had, Wendy noticed, the last of a noodle stain on the front of his tunic, very faint, where Cook had scrubbed at it and given up.

“No recruits.”

“Sir,” said Garth, and didn’t move.

Teague looked at him for a moment. Then at the commander.

“He stands right, sir,” said Teague.

The commander looked at the ceiling of the gatehouse arch, for some time, as if there were something written on it.

“One recruit,” he said.

Hesper came too. Nobody had asked him either.

He came across the courtyard from the infirmary with two big canvas bags over his shoulders, clinking, the kind the orderlies carried onto the red in the old days after a fight, and stopped by the gate and stood there. He was big and old and slow, with soft hands, and he’d been on the infirmary floor the night it happened, and they’d counted him among the dead for a day; and he still made the infirmary tea by her rules, because, he said, somebody had to.

“Hesper,” said Aldous. “You’re sixty-eight.”

“She always came back,” said Hesper. “From the red. Every time they went out. She always came back with them.” He hitched the bags higher. “Somebody should bring the bandages.”

Aldous looked at him for a moment. Then he nodded, and didn’t say anything else.

The elves were at the gate.

Wendy hadn’t seen them come. Nobody ever did. Three tall shapes in lichen-coloured coats, standing in the arch of the gatehouse in the last light, very still. Tavrin in front, with her stave. Oen just behind, and something in the set of his long shoulders that Wendy had never seen there before, a tightness, like a dog on a short leash. Liraun at the back, with her pale hair tied up for the first time, and her hands clasped in front of her very hard.

“We will come,” said Tavrin.

The commander, who’d been about to go back up onto the wall, stopped dead.

“You’ll fight?” he said.

“No,” said Tavrin.

Everybody looked at her.

“We will come,” said Tavrin, in the kingdom’s tongue, slowly, so that there would be no mistake. “We will stand at the edge. We will see it.” She looked at Rhea. “We will not fight.”

Behind her Oen said something in the singing tongue. Fast and low and angry. Wendy heard it in English, the way they all did, and it was Tavrin, they are going out against four of them, four, and we are going to stand there and—

Tavrin said one word back. Wendy didn’t hear what it was in English. It didn’t come through as a word. It came through as a hand on the back of a neck.

Oen stopped. His ears went flat.

“Then stay here,” said Rhea. Not angry. Just practical. “If you’re going to stand at the edge, stand at the edge of this wall. It’s safer and it’s the same view.”

“No,” said Tavrin.

“Why not?”

Tavrin looked at her for a long time with her winter-ice eyes.

“Because it is not ours,” she said. “And because somebody should see it, who will remember.”

That was all she’d say.

Liraun, at the back, looked at Maigo, and Maigo looked at her, and Liraun said, very quietly, in the singing tongue, I will be with the tree. And Maigo nodded, hard, so that his bells rang.

“Great,” said Wendy, to Salty, under her breath. “Spectators.”

Salty didn’t say anything.

She posted it at the gate.

Everybody was getting ready. Rhea was going along the forty, shouting, checking straps. Aldous was standing very still with his book in both hands and his eyes shut, the way he did before a long rite. Yan was by the gatehouse wall with the scythe on his shoulder, and Suzu was on the ground beside him, white, already longer than she’d been on his neck, waking up. Maigo was sitting on the mounting block with his foot on the earth, not saying anything, which on Maigo was the loudest thing in the world. Niko had his short sword out and was looking at the edge of it, very calmly, as if it were a phone screen.

Salty was putting on his greatsword.

Wendy stood by the gate with her window open and looked at the channel.

She’d been going to not post. She’d thought about it all the way down the stair. Two summers ago, after the man on the red, she’d said it out loud in the chapel: she wouldn’t post anything that would send Gabe running onto the Redmarch. She’d meant it. You didn’t put a thing like this in front of thirty people a month away who couldn’t do anything about it, and make them sit up all night.

And one of them on the end of a jetty.

She knew he’d read it. They all knew now. Every night, on the end of his jetty, with a lamp and three hens, all of it, from the top. He’d read whatever she wrote tonight, and he’d sit there with it, a month away, and he wouldn’t be able to do a single thing. He’d know that. He’d know it the whole time he was reading.

She nearly didn’t.

And then she thought: you count for the people who can’t see.

That was what she was for. On a call. In a squad. She was the one who saw it first and said the number, so the others didn’t have to look. And the ones who couldn’t see tonight were thirty people and a fisherman, all over a kingdom, a month away, who’d want to know. Who’d rather know. She’d rather know. If it was Salty out there and she was a month away, she’d rather have the number.

She wrote it. She wrote it the way she’d have called it on Earth. Flat. A number.

She closed it.

She didn’t write anything else. There wasn’t anything for him. Nobody ever wrote anything for him; that was the rule nobody had made. He’d read the number. That was what she’d give him. That was all you could give somebody a month away: the true number, and not make it softer.

“Hey,” said Salty.

He’d come up behind her. Greatsword on his back, the big one, longer than he was tall nearly, the one that had made Rhea laugh at him the first week. Armour Oakes had made him over the winter, plain and heavy and a bit too big round the middle because Oakes said he was still growing, sideways. He looked very solid. He always did. Like a wall you could lean on.

“Hey,” said Wendy.

They stood there a moment.

“If one comes for me,” said Wendy.

“I’ll come,” said Salty. At once. Like it was already done.

“Don’t.”

He looked at her.

“Last time,” said Wendy. She hadn’t meant to say it. Two years, she’d had it in her shoe. It came out now, in the gatehouse, in the dusk, with forty soldiers going past. “Last time. On the red. You had the sword. His sword. And one came round for me, and I had one arrow, and you—“ She stopped. “You let go of it.”

Salty didn’t say anything.

“I saw,” said Wendy. “I saw your face. You knew you were too far. You knew. And you let go of it anyway. In front of fifty of them. You were going to drop it on the ground and run.”

“Yeah,” said Salty.

“Don’t,” said Wendy. “Whatever you’re holding tonight. Whatever it is. If one comes for me, don’t you dare let go of it for me.” She looked at him. “I’ll be fine.”

“You can’t know that,” said Salty.

He said it simply. Not arguing. Just saying how it was, the way he said how much a thing weighed. He was right. That was the stupid thing. She couldn’t know it. She was the one who counted, and she’d counted it, a hundred and sixty-one and four, against forty and seven and a chaplain, and it didn’t come out. She’d done the sum six times on the wall and it didn’t come out.

“No,” said Wendy. “I can’t.”

She took a breath.

“I’m going to believe it anyway,” she said.

Salty looked at her for a long time.

Then he put his big hand on the side of her face, very lightly, the way he did about once a year, and took it away again.

“Okay,” said Salty.

They went out of the east gate as the light went.

Wendy thought about that afterwards: that it was the east gate. That it had been barred, nine years ago, for an hour, and twenty-five men had stood on the wall above it and watched. It wasn’t barred now. It stood open, both leaves back against the walls, and the twenty-five went out through it first. Not marching. Walking. The way men walk on a track they know. And every one of them, as he went under the arch, put his free hand flat on his chest, and kept it there, and didn’t look up.

On the wall above them, the commander stood with his glass in his hand and a hundred and ninety men along the walk on either side of him. He didn’t put his hand on his chest. He didn’t know to. He watched them go out under him with his face very still, the way you watch something you’re going to have to write a report about, and something in his face that wasn’t for any report.

On the north tower, Old Ansel, eighty, who did the night watch because nobody had the heart to tell him not to, had his hand on his chest. Wendy could see it from the gate, a small grey shape up against the last of the sky.

Pim was at the gate with the horses. He wasn’t allowed. He was sixteen. He held the horses anyway, all of them, the forty from the spring relief, in long strings on either side of the arch, so that nobody coming in or out later would have to stop and catch one; and as Wendy went past him he said, very fast and very low, I counted you out, forty-eight, I’ll count you in, and she looked at him, and nodded.

Cook was in the arch with a basket. She put a heel of the good bread into every hand that went past. She didn’t say anything. She just did it, forty-eight times, with her jaw set, and when she got to Salty she put in two.

They ran the track.

Not fast. Rhea set the pace, a long steady jog, the kind you could keep up for an hour with a spear and a shield, and the old ones kept it, all of them, even Jory Platt with his three fingers, even Hesper with his bags clinking. Along Aldous’s burial track, in the dusk, between the rows. Four hundred and eighty-one markers. Wendy counted them going past. Names, at first, burned into the wood, the ones Aldous had known. Then just posts. Then just posts.

Aldous ran with his book under his arm. Fifty-four, in a cassock. He didn’t say a word the whole way.

Maigo ran at the front, beside Rhea. He knew the way better than anyone. Every Thursday for two and a half years. His bells were muffled in a scarf and his hood was down and his root foot hit the track every other step with a soft slap, and every so often, without stopping, he’d put it down flat and wrong-footed for one step, the way you’d put your hand on a wall in the dark, and say still there, to nobody, and run on.

The elves didn’t run. They walked. And kept up. And left no tracks.

Wendy ran beside Salty and watched the knoll come up out of the red.

They got there first.

She hadn’t been sure they would. The scouts had been past the knoll when she’d counted them, out where the riverbeds met, and the knoll was an hour from the gate, and scouts moved in bursts faster than anything that size ought to. But scouts stopped, too. They stopped and listened. And they’d been coming single file down a thread of green, one behind another, and you can only go as fast as the one in front.

The knoll came up dark against a sky going from orange to blue. The cairn on top. The black stone. And the ash.

Every leaf on it turned east. Stiff. Pointing. Even now, with forty soldiers coming up the west slope behind it at a jog and Maigo calling to it, every leaf on it was pointed east, down the slope, at the riverbeds; as hard as a tree can point. There. There. There.

Wendy looked where it was pointing. Down the far slope. Into the dark at the meeting of the riverbeds.

She couldn’t see anything.

She knew they were there.

Rhea set the ring on the green.

She didn’t have to think. She’d drilled it all Saturday a hundred paces from the gate on a low hump between two gullies, and the low hump had been this, exactly; she’d paced it. Thirty paces across. The edge of the ring on the edge of the green, where the soft short grass with the white and blue flowers in it gave way to the red and the grey tufts. Shields at the front. Spears in the second rank, the old ones mostly, over the shoulders of the first. The five archers in the middle, by the cairn, with Wendy.

“You’re with me,” said Rhea. To Teague. “South side. That’s where they’ll come up, the big ones. It’s the easy slope.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

“Niko. East. Where it goes down to the riverbeds. There’s a dip in the edge of the green by that rock. Hold it.”

“Holding,” said Niko.

He went and stood in it. Wendy watched him go. A gap in a ring, on the east side, between two of the old ones with spears, with his short sword in his hand and his weight on his back foot and his eyes not on the dark, but on the gaps in the dark. He looked exactly the way he’d looked two summers ago in the gap on the red, and exactly the way he’d looked on Earth, at three in the morning, hunched forward at a desk at three in the morning, waiting the way he’d waited a thousand times.

“Maigo. The tree.”

Maigo was already there. He’d gone straight to it. He was sitting with his back against the ash, the way he did on Thursdays, with his root foot flat on the green and his face screwed up behind his pixel sunglasses, and he didn’t answer. He’d gone down.

“Yan.”

“Middle,” said Yan.

He stood beside the cairn with his eyes half shut. Suzu was on the green at his feet, and growing, the way she did, the white of her going on and on in the dusk until she was as long as a man was tall and as thick as Salty’s arm, and her head came up off the grass to the height of Yan’s waist, and her green eyes were open, both of them, and she was looking east.

“Salty,” said Rhea.

Salty took the greatsword off his back.

“Wherever it’s worst,” said Rhea.

“Yeah,” said Salty.

And the elves stood at the edge.

Not inside the ring. Outside it. On the north side of the knoll, on the grey grass, just past where the green stopped, where they always stood. Three tall shapes in the dusk, a few paces apart, very still. Tavrin in the middle, with her stave planted. Oen on her left, with his arms folded so hard that Wendy could see the cords in them. And Liraun on her right, kneeling, with her long hands flat on the grey grass, as close to the edge of the green as she could get without touching it, with her face turned toward the tree.

Outside the ring. In the open. On the side the scouts would come round.

“They’ll be killed,” said Wendy, to nobody.

“No,” said Yan, beside her, with his eyes shut. “Watch.”

Wendy took her place by the cairn.

She hadn’t been this close to it since the autumn, since the day after the full moon, when they’d all come out at midday and eaten bread and cheese and small green apples round it because Ars had said she’d have fed everyone. A low grey cairn, waist-high. A ring of small stones round its foot, children’s stones from a lake town a month west. A white one at the head, river-smooth, the size of a fist, that she’d put there herself in the snow without knowing whose it was; it had been his, from under the end of his own jetty. A scorched hearth stone beside it. A small white stone on the very top that had been somebody’s mother’s. And standing up out of the head of the cairn, facing west, toward the lake, the black stone.

It wasn’t a stone. She knew that; she’d helped carry it. It was iron. Half of a black sword the length of a man, that a king had cut in half on an anvil by a lake, and hammered into this. A tall flat slab of black iron, very plain, standing up out of the grave like a door. And going down from the bottom of it into the ground, she knew, because she’d seen it go in, a long black spike, into the old hole where the sword had stood seven years, like a key into a lock.

There were letters on it. English. The only English on the whole red, in the whole east of the kingdom, maybe. Cut into the black iron in plain capitals by a man who’d learned his letters in another world and hadn’t written them in thirty-four years, so that they were a little uneven, the way your handwriting goes when you haven’t used it.

TO DIWA, MY LOVE.

The old ones couldn’t read it. None of them could. Only the five of them, out here. And Aldous knew what it said because they’d told him.

Wendy looked at it in the last of the light.

She’d never met Diwa. She’d never known anything about her until two summers ago, and then only what a man would say, which was almost nothing. She was a healer. And what Aldous’s book said. And what Alea said, in the autumn, with her hand on the stone, line by line, answering.

She put her own hand on it now. For a moment. She didn’t know why.

It was cold. Iron always was.

“We’re here,” said Wendy, to the black iron, very quietly, under her breath, so that nobody would hear. “It’s just us. Sorry. He couldn’t come.” She took her hand away. “We’ll do our best.”

Then it was dark.

It went fast, out here. It always did. No trees, no hills, nothing between you and the sky, so the sun went down behind the fort a long way west and the dark came across the red from the east like water filling a bowl. One moment she could see the old ones’ faces in the ring. The next she could see their shapes. The next, nothing. There was no moon. There wouldn’t be one till nearly dawn, Aldous had said, and only a thin one then.

Stars. Thousands of them, very bright, the way they only were out here, with nothing between you and them.

Wendy took an arrow from her quiver and nocked it and drew it halfway and let her aura go out into it, the way she’d learned in the winter of the second year. Not much. Just enough.

The arrow began to glow.

It was a soft light. Pale, a little blue, like moonlight on snow, all along the shaft and the head. Aloft, her window called it. She’d never been able to see it herself in the day. At night it was the only light on the knoll.

She held it up. Not to shoot. Just to see.

In the soft blue glow of a single arrow, the knoll came back. The ring of soldiers on the edge of the green, their faces pale. The tree, leaves still pointing. Maigo against it with his eyes shut. Yan, very still, beside her. Suzu, risen up to his chest now, swaying slightly, looking east. And at the north edge, just inside the glow, the three tall shapes on the grey grass, not moving.

She looked east, down the slope. Past Niko in his gap. Into the dark at the bottom, where the riverbeds met.

The glow didn’t reach that far.

“They’re here,” said Yan.

He didn’t open his eyes. He said it the way he’d said things on a call at three in the morning, when it had all gone quiet and everybody was holding their breath. Slow and deep and perfectly calm.

“Bottom of the slope,” said Yan. “East. Where the rivers meet. All of them.” He was quiet a moment. “They’ve stopped.”

Wendy held the arrow up higher.

The blue light went out down the slope, a little further. Over the edge of the green, where Niko stood, with the soft grass and the flowers closing for the night. Over the red beyond it, and the grey tufts. Over the first few paces of the slope going down.

And at the edge of the light, at the bottom of the slope, where the green threads of the riverbeds came in from the east and met, something stood up.

Tall. Thin. Bent forward, as if it were about to fall. Perfectly still.

And beside it, another. And another.

Wendy didn’t count them. She didn’t need to. She’d counted them already. A hundred and sixty-one, stopped, in a long dark line on the green threads at the bottom of the knoll, all of them, perfectly still, at the very edge of the light of one arrow, like a crowd at the back of a hall when the lights come up.

Listening.

“Aldous,” said Rhea, very quietly.

Aldous opened his book.

He didn’t look at it. He never did. He held it open in one hand at the edge of the ring, beside Teague, and lifted the other, and began to chant.

Wendy had heard it once before. Two summers ago, on the red, with fifty of them round. Old words, in the church tongue nobody spoke any more, slow and heavy, rising and falling, like a hymn. She knew now, since the winter, since the elves, that it had been a song once. That the tune had worn off it, over a great many years, and left only the words, and Aldous had been saying them all his life without knowing they’d been meant to be sung.

He sang them now.

Not well. He was fifty-four and had never sung in front of anyone but Tavrin and the dead. His voice was thin and cracked and went flat on the high notes. But it was a tune. The tune Tavrin had given back to him in the chapel in the winter. And as he sang, something came out from under his feet onto the green.

A line of light. Gold, then orange, then fire. A thin line of fire running out along the very edge of the green from the toes of his boots, round the ring of soldiers, round all of them, round the cairn and the stone and the tree and the five of them and the forty and Hesper with his bags, round in a great curve, thirty paces across, until it met itself on the other side.

And went up. Waist-high. A wall of fire on the edge of the green, hissing and roaring, burning on nothing at all.

It was exactly the same size as the green.

Wendy saw that, in the sudden light. The fire ran along the line where the soft short grass met the red, all the way round, as if the green had been measured for it. Or it for the green. As if the man who’d fed those flowers for nine years and the man who’d sung over the dead for nine years had been drawing the same circle all that time from opposite ends, and had never known.

On the grey grass on the north side, just outside the fire, the three elves stood in the light of it and didn’t move. Liraun had lifted her face. Her lips were moving. Wendy couldn’t hear what she was singing, under Aldous, under the roar. She thought it might be the low part.

At the bottom of the slope, the hundred and sixty-one stood at the edge of the firelight.

They didn’t come up.

They stood there. All of them. Perfectly still, grey, faceless, bent forward, in their long dark line on the green threads, with the fire on their grey hides. Facing the knoll. Facing up the slope, past Niko in his gap, past the fire. At the cairn.

At the stone.

Wendy could feel it. She didn’t need Yan. She could feel all of them leaning, the way you feel a crowd lean when the band comes on. Toward the black iron. Toward what was under it.

“Steady,” said Teague, somewhere on the south side. “Steady. Steady.”

And behind the line, at the back, in the dark beyond the edge of the light, something moved that wasn’t in bursts.

Heavy. Steady. Trotting.

It came up out of the dark at the back of the line and stopped. Big. Much bigger than the scouts. Heavy in the shoulders, with arms too long for it that hung down past its knees, and horns curving back from where a face should have been, and the firelight on them. Then another beside it. And a third.

And a fourth.

The scouts parted for them. Without a sound. The long grey line opening in four places, like a curtain, and closing again behind.

“Four,” said Wendy.

She said it out loud. Flat. The number. The way she’d have called it on a call on Earth, at three in the morning, so that the others didn’t have to look.

She drew the arrow to her ear. It glowed.

A hundred and sixty-one, and four, against forty, and seven, and a chaplain singing flat, and a tree. She’d done the sum six times on the wall. It didn’t come out. She did it again now, in the firelight, with her arrow drawn, and it still didn’t come out, and it was never going to come out, because it wasn’t that kind of sum.

She looked across the ring at Salty.

He was standing on the south side, by Rhea, with his greatsword up in both hands, and the forge coming up in him; she could see it, the way the air went over a hot road. He wasn’t looking at the slope. He was looking at her. Just for a moment. Across the fire.

If you go, I go.

Wendy looked back at him.

And found, standing on a knoll on the edge of the world with a glowing arrow drawn to her ear and four horned things at the bottom of a slope in the dark, that she believed it. Not the sum. The sum was never going to come out. The other thing. That they’d hold it. She didn’t know when she’d started. She didn’t know what she was believing in. Him. Them. The tree. A chaplain singing flat. A gap in a ring with a small man in it who’d been last for two and a half years.

It didn’t feel like anything moving. It never had, the few times before, out here. It just felt like standing still and not being afraid of that.

She believed it anyway.

At the bottom of the slope, the biggest of the four lifted its horned head.

And then, all four together, the way they had two summers ago, as if the fire were a curtain and nothing more, they started up the slope toward it.
