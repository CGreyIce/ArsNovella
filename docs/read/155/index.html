Chapter 155

Eleven Dinners

7,714 words · 34 min read

The list had come at four in the morning, Malacca time.

Salty remembered that. He remembered it better than he remembered most things from Earth now, which was strange, because it hadn’t been important. It had been a message. One of a thousand. He’d been asleep, and his phone had buzzed on the floor beside the bed, and he’d looked at it in the morning with one eye open while Wendy made coffee, and laughed, and shown her, and she’d laughed too, and put it in a spreadsheet, because Wendy put everything in a spreadsheet.

ok. the list. for when you come. you have to eat all of these. all eleven in one day if you’re serious about it. i’m serious about it.

And then the list.

He could see it now, at the second anvil in the forge at Cinderhold, with a hammer in his hand and a hinge on the anvil for the north gate. He could see the shape of it on the screen. Eleven lines, all lower case, because Ice never used capitals unless he was angry or it was a name, with a note on every line, and the notes were the best part. He’d forgotten most of the notes. He remembered the first one, and the last, and the shape of the thing in between.

It had started at three. That was the other part. The first version of the list, months before, had been three lines long, after the third clutch. And every time Salty clutched a round with Ice dead in the corner watching, Ice had said that’s a dinner, and the next day the list would be one line longer, and he’d send it again. updated. Four. Six. Nine. Eleven.

Eleven, a month before.

And then nothing. Because there wasn’t a twelfth clutch. Because there wasn’t a next month.

He hit the hinge. It was a good hinge. Oakes would say it was a hinge, which from Oakes was a medal.

The door of the forge darkened.

The man with the rod came in with a sack on his shoulder.

It was a big sack. Canvas, old, tied at the neck. Salty had seen it in the back of the cart all the way from Valcrest, under Wendy’s second-best blanket, and not asked, because you didn’t, with things in the back of carts; he’d had a scythe in a blanket for twenty days and called it horseshoes. It was heavier than it looked. Salty could tell from the way the man’s shoulder sat under it. Forty pounds, maybe. Odd shapes in it. Hard things and soft things.

The man swung it down onto Oakes’s bench, the long one under the window where Oakes kept his tongs and his files and his mug, and Oakes, at the main anvil, said oi without looking round.

“Sorry,” said the man.

He undid the neck of the sack.

Salty put the hammer down.

Things came out. One after another, onto Oakes’s bench, in a row, the way the man did everything: carefully, in order, as if he’d been planning the order for a long time. A cloth bag of rice. Not barley, not wheat, rice: small and white and hard, a whole sack of it inside the sack. A bunch of something long and pale green and woody, wrapped in damp cloth, that smelled, when he unwrapped it, so strongly of lemon and something else that Salty’s head came up like a dog’s. Roots. Knobbly yellow-brown ones. Ginger. Salty would have known ginger with his eyes shut. A string of garlic. A string of small red shallots. A twist of paper full of something dried and dark red and crumpled that made Oakes, ten feet away at his anvil, sneeze. Peppers. Dried hot peppers. A pot of something brown and thick with a cloth tied over the top. A cake of something dark, wrapped in leaves, hard as a brick.

And two brown things, round, hairy, the size of a man’s fist, that the man set down on the bench last, very carefully, side by side, and looked at.

Salty stared at them.

“Is that—“

“I don’t know what they’re called here,” said the man. “There’s a street in Valcrest. Behind the fish market. Where the ships from the southern islands sell what they bring up the coast.” He was still looking at the two brown things. “I went down there on the last morning. Looking for ginger. And a woman had a basket of these on the end of her stall.”

He picked one up. He shook it, gently, next to his ear.

It sloshed.

“Thirty-four years,” said the man, very quietly. “And I never once looked.”

Salty didn’t say anything. He looked at the brown hairy thing in the man’s hand, which was a coconut, which was a coconut in a world with magic and demons and a red plain and a fort at the edge of it, and found that his throat had done something.

“He sat down,” said a voice from the door. “On the step of the stall. For about ten minutes.”

Alea had come in behind her father. She had a basket on her arm, with eggs in it, and a sack of something else, and she was looking at the coconut in his hand with an expression that Salty had come to recognise over twenty days of road as I am being patient with my father.

“The woman thought he was ill,” said Alea. “She gave him water.”

“It was very good water,” said the man.

“He bought her whole basket. There are nine more in the cart.”

Oakes had stopped hammering. He was standing at the main anvil with his tongs in one fist, looking at the row of things on his bench, and the sack, and the man, with an expression Salty had never seen on him before. Like a man watching a horse walk into a church.

“What,” said Oakes, “is all this.”

“Dinner,” said the man.

“The skillet’s there,” said Oakes, eventually. He pointed with the tongs. A great black iron pan, as wide as a cartwheel’s hub, hanging from a hook by the hearth, that Salty had seen every day for two years and assumed was for some smith’s purpose he didn’t understand. “And the pot. And you’ll have the hearth when I’m done, not before. Sundown. And you’ll clean it after. Properly. And you’ll not put anything on my anvils that isn’t iron.”

“Yes.”

“And I’ll have whatever’s left,” said Oakes. “For the use of the forge.”

“There’ll be plenty.”

“There’d better be.” Oakes looked at the coconut. Then at Salty. “Short lad. You knew about this?”

“No,” said Salty.

“Hm.” Oakes turned back to his anvil. “Eleven dinners,” he said, to the anvil, in deep disgust. “In my forge.” He hit the iron, once, very hard. “What’s it for?”

The man looked at Salty.

Salty thought about it. He was bad at secrets and bad at lies and not very good at explaining things, as a rule. He usually just said what things were and let other people worry about the rest.

“He owes me,” said Salty.

Oakes hit the iron again.

“Well,” he said. “Long as it’s a debt.”

Wendy said the list that night.

She hadn’t meant to. They were in the forge at sundown, the four of them, with the doors open on the courtyard and Oakes gone up to his supper and the hearth banked down to a red glow. The man had the skillet on it. Alea was cutting shallots on a board on the end of Oakes’s bench with the good knife, very fast, without looking, the way her father must have taught her. Salty was sitting on the upturned bucket by the second anvil, where he always sat. Wendy was on the bench beside him, with her headphones round her neck.

“What’s first?” said Wendy.

The man stopped, with a ginger root in one hand.

“I don’t remember the order,” he said. “I remember the eleven. Most of them.” He turned the ginger over. “I don’t remember the order, or—“ He stopped. “There were notes. I wrote notes. I don’t remember the notes.”

Wendy was quiet for a moment.

Then she said, quite flatly, looking at the hearth: “One. Chicken rice balls. Not the famous place. The one behind it. Trust.”

The man went very still.

“Two,” said Wendy. “Asam pedas. It’s sour and it’s spicy and you will cry. Wendy will not cry. Salty will cry.”

Salty looked at her.

She wasn’t looking at anyone. She was looking at the glow of the hearth, with her hands folded in her lap, and saying it the way you’d read something off a screen. Because she was. Salty knew she was. She was reading it off a screen in her head. Off a spreadsheet, in a flat, in another world, on a laptop on a desk, with a column for flights and a column for dates and a column called ELEVEN, which she’d made at four in the morning, Malacca time, while he was still asleep.

“Three. Satay celup. You cook it yourself in a pot of peanut sauce. Salty, don’t be scared of the pot.”

“I wasn’t scared of the pot,” said Salty.

“You were going to be.” Wendy didn’t look at him. “Four. Nyonya laksa. No note. He didn’t put a note. He just put it. Five. Popiah. Eat it fast or it falls apart. It will fall apart anyway. Six. Ayam pongteh. Tastes like somebody’s grandma made it. Somebody’s grandma did. Seven. Char kway teow. Ask me about wok hei. Actually don’t, I’ll talk for an hour.”

The man had put the ginger down on the bench. He was standing by the hearth with his hands at his sides.

“Eight. Onde-onde. They explode. Not a metaphor. Wear a dark shirt. Nine. Nasi lemak. For breakfast. Yes, breakfast. Trust. Ten. Cendol. It’s hot here. You’ll need it.”

She stopped.

The forge was very quiet. The hearth ticked. Out in the courtyard, somebody was crossing to the well with a bucket. Alea had stopped cutting shallots.

“Eleven,” said Wendy.

She looked at the man then. For the first time. Straight at where his face was, though her eyes slid off it, the way everybody’s did; Salty saw them slide and come back and settle on his shoulders instead.

“Eleven,” said Wendy. “Dinner at mine. I’ll cook.” She paused. “I can’t cook.” She paused again. “Trust.”

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Salty sat on his bucket by the second anvil and looked at the floor. He’d forgotten that one. He’d forgotten the notes, mostly, but he’d forgotten that one entirely. The eleventh. The one Ice had added a month before, after the last clutch, a one-versus-three on a map Salty hated. Updated. Eleven lines. And the last one wasn’t food at all. It was come to my house and I’ll make you something terrible.

He’d been going to do it. Ice had. He’d been going to cook for them in his flat in Malacca, badly, with the cardboard still on the pizza, and they’d been going to eat it anyway.

The man by the hearth hadn’t moved.

Salty couldn’t see his face. Nobody could. But he could see the rest of him. And the rest of him was standing by a forge hearth in a fort on the edge of a red plain with a ginger root on the bench beside him, very straight, very still, the way a man stands when somebody’s handed him back something he put down thirty-four years ago and forgot he’d ever had.

“I wrote that at four in the morning,” he said eventually. In English. Very quietly.

“Four-oh-seven,” said Wendy.

“I was trying to make it funny.”

“It was funny.” Wendy’s voice wasn’t entirely steady. “It was very funny. Salty laughed for ten minutes. He added three restaurants of his own at the bottom in capitals.”

“I didn’t know that.”

“You didn’t know about the spreadsheet.” She wiped her eyes, once, fast, with the back of her wrist, and looked at the hearth again. “Nobody knew about the spreadsheet. It was mine.”

The man looked at her for a long moment.

Then he picked up the ginger again. He turned to the board on the end of the bench, where Alea was standing with the good knife, very still, with her ears up. He held out his hand for the knife, and she gave it to him, and he began to cut.

“One,” he said. “Chicken rice balls.”

It was wrong.

Salty knew it was wrong the moment he tasted it. That was the thing about food: you couldn’t lie to it any more than you could lie to a weight. The rice was the wrong rice, a little too long and a little too hard, and the chicken was a fort hen that had lived a hard life on the edge of the red and had opinions about it, and the sauce had the dried peppers in it instead of fresh ones, and there wasn’t any soy, so the man had done something with the brown paste in the pot that was nearly right and not quite.

And the rice was in balls. Little round balls, pressed in the hands, the size of a hen’s egg, cooked in the broth with the ginger and the garlic and the green woody stuff, so they shone. Six of them on each plate. Exactly the way they did it in Malacca, at the place behind the famous place, that Salty had never been to and never would.

He ate one. Then another.

“It’s wrong,” he said.

“I know,” said the man.

“It’s good.”

“I know,” said the man.

Salty ate all six. Wendy ate five and gave him her sixth without being asked. Alea, at the end of the bench, ate two from the pot with a spoon and said oh to herself in a small surprised voice, as if she’d never had it before, which she hadn’t; he’d never made it for her. He’d never made any of it for her. Salty realised that halfway through the fourth ball. Thirty-four years of cooking, and he’d never once made any of the eleven. Not even for his daughter.

He’d been saving them.

Salty put his spoon down for a moment and looked at the man, who was at the hearth with his back to them, cleaning the skillet, properly, the way Oakes had told him to.

He didn’t say anything about it. You didn’t. He picked up his spoon again and finished the sixth.

Rhea arm-wrestled him the next evening, before the second dinner, in the middle of the courtyard, in front of the whole of Cinderhold.

Salty didn’t know how the whole of Cinderhold found out. He suspected Rhea. Rhea had told the whole of Cinderhold everything she’d ever done, generally while she was doing it. But at sundown on the second day there was a table in the middle of the courtyard, the long one out of the mess, and a barrel at each end of it, and the courtyard was full. Soldiers. Grooms. Cooks with their sleeves rolled up. The young captain from Solmere, at the back, trying to look as if he’d just happened to be passing. Teague on the gatehouse stair with his cap on the back of his head. Old Wat, carried down in a chair by two boys, because he’d said he’d waited nine years to see somebody beat Rhea at something and he wasn’t missing it for his legs.

Rhea put her elbow on the table. She glared.

The man put his elbow on the table.

“Count it,” said somebody in the crowd. “Somebody count it.”

“I’ll count it,” said Teague, from the stair, and did. “One—“

Rhea’s hand hit the table.

There was a short silence.

“Half,” said Teague, judiciously. “Call it half.”

The courtyard went up. Salty had never heard Cinderhold make a noise like it. Two years on the edge of the red and he’d heard it go silent, mostly, in long rows on the wall, and tired, and frightened, and quiet in the chapel. He’d never heard it laugh. The whole fort, every one of them, laughing at once, and Rhea staring at her own hand flat on the table, and then throwing her head back and laughing loudest of all.

“AGAIN,” said Rhea.

“Tomorrow,” said the man.

“AGAIN.”

“Before dinner,” said the man. “Every night. Eleven nights.” He took his elbow off the table. “If you’ve still got arms.”

“I’ll always have arms,” said Rhea.

And she was there the next night, and the next. Every night, at sundown, in the middle of the courtyard, with the whole fort round her and Teague on the stair counting. Half. Half. Not even half, sergeant, that was nothing. Half. Somebody started a book on it by the third night, and by the fifth the book had more money in it than the garrison’s pay chest, Salty heard, and the young captain from Solmere had put a silver crown on half and was trying very hard to pretend he hadn’t.

The second dinner was fish.

Salty had known it would be. He’d known it since the list. Asam pedas. It’s sour and it’s spicy and you will cry. It was a fish thing, the man said, a sour fish stew, red with peppers and sharp with something that should have been tamarind and was, it turned out, a sour brown pod from the same street in Valcrest, which he’d bought because it smelled right.

And he caught the fish himself.

He went out at dawn. Out of the west gate, with the rod over his shoulder, to the stream half a mile off where they’d camped the night before they came in, where it ran clear and cold off the green side of the land before it gave up and went into the red. Salty went with him. He didn’t know why. He just found he was awake, and the man was going out of the gate with a rod, and he went.

They didn’t talk. Salty sat on the bank. The man stood on a flat stone at the edge of the water with the rod and did the thing Salty had heard about for two years in the channel and in stories and in a ballad sung very badly in a square in Valcrest, and never seen. He fished.

It was very boring. That was the first thing. He stood there. He cast, once, a long easy line out over the water, and the float sat on it. And he stood. For a long time. The sun came up. Salty sat on the bank with his arms round his knees and his crooked ribs aching a little in the cold and watched a man stand on a stone.

And then, after a long time, the man moved once, and there was a fish on the bank.

Salty hadn’t seen the float go. He’d been watching it. He hadn’t seen it go.

“How,” said Salty.

“Patience,” said the man. He took the hook out and put the fish in the basket and cast again. “And knowing where they’ll be.”

“That’s how you played.”

“That’s how I played.”

Salty thought about that. He thought about a hundred rounds in a hundred games where he’d gone in with the rifle and died, and come back to the call to find Ice still alive in the corner, not moving, holding an angle nobody had looked at, and then at the very end of the round, after everyone else was dead, moving once.

“You were boring to watch,” said Salty. “In games.”

“I know.”

“I used to complain. On the call.”

“I know,” said the man. “You were very rude about it.”

Salty sat on the bank and watched the float, and then didn’t, because he’d learned that watching it didn’t help. And the man caught four more, one at a time, very slowly, with nothing happening at all between them. And at the end of the morning they walked back to the fort with five fish in the basket and the sun on their backs and didn’t say anything all the way, and it was, Salty thought, one of the best mornings of his life.

He cried at the asam pedas.

He hadn’t meant to. It was the peppers. It was very spicy, and very sour, and the fish was perfect, and the sour went up the back of his nose and the peppers went everywhere else, and his eyes ran, and he couldn’t stop them.

Wendy didn’t cry. She ate hers calmly and asked for more.

“Wendy will not cry,” said the man, at the hearth, to nobody, very satisfied. “Salty will cry.”

“It’s the peppers,” said Salty.

“I know,” said the man. “That’s why I wrote it.”

After that the dinners went the way the days went at Cinderhold, which was slowly and then all at once.

The third night was the pot. A great round iron pot from the mess, on a ring of stones in the middle of the forge floor over a bed of coals, with a sauce in it made of crushed nuts and peppers and garlic and the brown paste and something sweet from the dark brick in the leaves, bubbling, thick, the colour of an old saddle. And skewers. Dozens of them. Little bits of everything, threaded on sharpened sticks: hen, and mutton, and fish, and eggs, and mushrooms, and things from the fort’s garden that Salty couldn’t name. You picked up a skewer and put it in the pot and held it there and cooked it yourself, and took it out, and ate it, burning your mouth, and did it again.

“Salty, don’t be scared of the pot,” said Wendy.

“I’m not scared of the pot.”

He wasn’t. He was, a little. It spat.

Rhea came in halfway through, smelling it, having lost half in the courtyard as usual, and stood in the forge door with her arms folded and her face doing something hungry and wistful, and the man looked at her and looked at Salty, and Salty sighed, and moved up on the bench. And then Maigo came, because Maigo had been sitting on the step outside the forge door since the first night smelling things and narrating them to Niko, and then Niko, and then Yan, with Suzu on the scythe, very politely, apologising.

“The dinner’s for two,” said the man.

“We know,” said Maigo. “We’re not here for the dinner. We’re here for the pot.”

The man looked at the pot. There was a great deal left in the pot. Salty suspected, though he could never have proved it, that the man had made three times what two people could eat, and had known exactly who’d be on the step.

“The pot’s for everyone,” said the man.

After that, the step outside the forge was the most popular seat in Cinderhold. By the fifth night there was a line. Soldiers with bowls. Grooms. Pim the stable lad, at the front, every night, because he’d worked out that if you got there before Rhea lost you got the best of it. Oakes came down from his supper on the fourth night, pretending he’d forgotten his mug, and stood at the end of his own bench with a plate, and ate standing up, and said nothing at all, which from Oakes was a sonnet.

But the bench inside stayed for two. Every night. Salty and Wendy, at the end, by the second anvil, on a cloth Alea put down on the boards. With a plate each. With the dinner on it. While the forge filled up behind them and the step outside overflowed and the man stood at the hearth with his back to all of it, cooking.

The fourth night was laksa. A soup, red and gold and rich, with noodles the man made himself on Oakes’s bench with flour and egg, cutting them with the good knife into long thin strands, and in the soup the milk of the brown hairy nuts, which he cracked on the main anvil with a single tap of a hammer while Oakes watched in silent outrage. No note, Wendy said, when he put it down in front of them. He didn’t put a note. And the man said I know. I couldn’t think of one. It didn’t need one. And it didn’t.

The fifth night was popiah, and it fell apart. It fell apart in everyone’s hands, all of them, the thin flat wrappers the man had made on the skillet tearing and the filling going everywhere, down Salty’s front and onto the bench and the floor, and Alea laughed so hard she had to sit down, and the man stood at the hearth and said it will fall apart anyway in the voice of somebody who had been right about something thirty-four years ago and was still pleased.

The sixth night was a stew. Hen and potatoes, slow, dark, sweet and salty, in the pot for the whole afternoon on the edge of the hearth while Oakes worked round it and complained. Tastes like somebody’s grandma made it, said Wendy. And Salty ate it and thought it did. Not his grandma. Not anyone’s he knew. But somebody’s, somewhere, who’d stood at a stove in a kitchen in a city by the sea for fifty years making this exact thing for people who came in hungry.

He looked at the man’s back at the hearth.

Somebody’s grandma did, the list had said.

He didn’t ask. He thought he probably knew. A man who’d moved to a city on his own at twenty-four, after his mother died and his father found somebody new. Who’d sat down in the same kitchen over and over until the old woman who ran it started putting his plate down without asking what he wanted. He’d never talked about it. Not on the call, not ever. He’d just put it on a list, with a note, for his friends, so they’d go and sit in the same chairs.

Salty ate the stew very slowly. All of it.

The seventh night, the man asked Oakes for the forge.

Not the hearth. The forge. The fire itself, the real one, the one Oakes heated iron in, at full heat, with the bellows going.

“No,” said Oakes.

“For a quarter of an hour.”

“No.”

“Ask me about wok hei,” said Wendy, to the ceiling.

Oakes looked at her. “Ask you about what?”

“Don’t,” said Salty. “He’ll talk for an hour.”

He did. Not the man. Oakes. Because Oakes, it turned out, when the man explained, standing in the forge with the skillet in his hand, quite seriously, what wok hei was, the breath of the pan, the thing that happened to food when you cooked it fast enough and hot enough in iron that it caught the edge of the fire and took the smoke into itself, the thing you could only get with a fire hotter than any kitchen ever had, went very still. And put his tongs down. And looked at the skillet. And at his forge.

“Iron,” said Oakes, slowly, “at a white heat.”

“Not white. Just under.”

“And you want to put noodles in it.”

“And eggs. And shallots. And the fish from this morning, and the brown paste, and peppers, and some little things I dried on the wall yesterday that should have been cockles.” The man paused. “Very fast. Before they burn.”

Oakes looked at him for a long, long time.

“Short lad,” he said. “The bellows.”

Salty got up.

Salty would remember the char kway teow for the rest of his life.

Not the taste. He’d remember the taste, but that wasn’t what he’d remember. He’d remember the forge going up to full heat on a warm evening in the late summer, with the doors open and the whole courtyard come to the step to see what the noise was. Oakes himself at the fire, feeding it, with his face lit orange. Salty on the great bellows, pumping, the way he’d pumped them all winter for Garrow when they were making the scythe, with the heat coming off the fire in a wall and the sweat running into his eyes.

And the man.

Standing at the mouth of the forge fire with the cartwheel skillet held out into it in one hand, bare-handed, by the iron handle, which should have taken the skin off his palm and didn’t, because he was the Black Blade and he’d decided it wouldn’t. The skillet going from black to dark red to a colour Salty had only ever seen on iron that was about to be struck. And then, all at once, everything into it. The noodles. The egg. The shallots and the garlic and the paste and the fish and the little dried things off the wall. And a sound like a waterfall. And a cloud of smoke that went up into the forge chimney and out of the top of it in a column, so that the sentries on the wall shouted fire and the young captain came running.

And the man moving the skillet. Fast. Faster than Salty had ever seen him move anything. Tossing it, so the whole lot went up into the air above the fire, into the smoke, and caught the edge of the flame, and came down again into the iron, and went up again. Over and over. Twelve heartbeats, Salty counted, with his arms on the bellows. Twelve. The way you’d count Niko on the tailboard.

And then off the fire, and onto the bench, and onto the plates.

Salty let go of the bellows. He came round the end of the hearth with his arms aching and the sweat running off him and his ribs doing something sharp, and sat down on his bucket, and picked up a fork, and ate.

It tasted of smoke. Of iron and fire and the edge of a flame. Of the breath of a forge.

He didn’t say anything. He ate the whole plate in silence while the forge cooled behind him and Oakes stood at the fire with his tongs hanging from his hand, looking at the empty skillet like a man who had just seen his life’s work used for something better.

“Well,” said Oakes, eventually. To the forge. To nobody. “Well.”

He went and got a plate.

The eighth night, the onde-onde exploded.

Little green balls of sticky rice dough, rolled in grated nut, with a piece of the dark brick in the middle of each that melted when you boiled them. You put one in your mouth whole and bit it and it burst, hot and sweet and dark, all over the inside of your mouth and down your chin. They explode. Not a metaphor. Wear a dark shirt. Wendy wore a dark shirt. Salty didn’t. He’d forgotten. He went back to the barracks that night with a front like a battlefield and Wendy laughing at him all the way across the courtyard.

The ninth was breakfast.

The man made them come at dawn for it. Nasi lemak. For breakfast. Yes, breakfast. Trust. Rice cooked in the milk of the brown nuts and the green woody stuff, so it came out rich and fragrant and faintly sweet. A red paste of peppers and shallots that he’d been cooking slowly on the edge of the hearth since midnight, black-red and so hot that Salty’s eyes ran again. Little dried fish, crisp, that he’d caught small at the stream and dried on the wall. An egg each. Nuts, fried. Cucumber from the fort’s garden, cold. All on a leaf, folded.

They ate it on the east wall. That was the man’s idea. On the wall, at dawn, sitting on the walk with their backs against the parapet, with the sun coming up over Kharvos and the red going gold. Salty and Wendy, side by side. Alea, at the next merlon, eating hers with her ears up, looking east at the tree.

Salty ate his nasi lemak on the wall at Cinderhold with the sun coming up and thought he had never in his life eaten anything for breakfast that was this good, or this hot, and that he’d been right about breakfast and Ice had been wrong, and that Ice had been right.

“Trust,” said Wendy, beside him, with her mouth full, to the sunrise.

The tenth was cendol, and it needed ice.

It was the end of summer on the edge of the red, and there wasn’t any. Salty said so. The man said there was. He went and found Teague, and Teague looked at him, and then took him round the back of the stables to a low stone hut half sunk into the hill with a heavy door and steps going down, and opened it.

Cold came out. Real cold. Winter cold, in the late summer evening.

“Ice-house,” said Teague. “Every fort’s got one. We pack it with snow off the red, in the winter. Keeps the meat. Keeps the wounded cool.” He looked down the steps into the dark. “Last winter’s still in there. The white winter. When you lot put the stone up.”

The man looked down the steps. Salty, behind him, did too. And thought about that winter. Snow all the way from the walls to the mountains, white and flat and silent, with the markers standing up out of it like masts. A black stone on a dray. Children’s stones. A star. Wendy’s arrow, going up and not coming down.

The snow from that winter. Still there. In the dark.

“We’ll need a bucket,” said the man.

So the cendol was made with snow off the red. Shaved off the packed blocks in the ice-house with Oakes’s best file, into bowls, a little hill of it in each one, and over it the milk of the last of the brown nuts, and a syrup made of the dark brick melted down, nearly black, smelling of smoke and sugar, and little green worms of jelly that the man had made from rice flour and the juice of some leaves, which was the only part he said was wrong, and which Salty didn’t care about at all.

Salty ate it slowly. It was cold, and sweet, and rich, and it tasted of the dark sugar, which tasted of the brick, which tasted, the man said, of a tree that grew on the southern islands and had a name in their language that meant almost exactly the same thing as the name of the city he’d moved to on his own at twenty-four.

It was hot in the forge. It was the end of summer. It’s hot here. You’ll need it.

He needed it.

Rhea lasted a full heartbeat on the eleventh night.

Salty saw it. Everybody saw it. The whole of Cinderhold was in the courtyard, as it had been every night, with the book on the barrel and the young captain at the back pretending not to have a crown on it. Teague on the stair. Old Wat in his chair. Rhea put her elbow on the table and glared, the way she had for ten nights, and the man put his elbow on the table, and Teague said one—

And Rhea’s hand didn’t hit the table.

It went over. It went most of the way over. But it stopped, an inch from the wood, with her whole arm shaking and her face gone red to the roots of her hair and her teeth bared, and held. For the length of a word.

“—two,” said Teague.

Rhea’s hand hit the table.

The courtyard went silent for a moment.

Then it went up like the forge going up under the bellows. Salty had never heard anything like it. Not on the red, not at the Lists. The whole of Cinderhold, screaming, for a woman who’d lost an arm-wrestle. Soldiers pounding the table. Grooms on the stable roof. Pim jumping up and down at the front. Old Wat in his chair with both his shaking hands in the air. The young captain from Solmere, at the back, shouting ONE, at the top of his voice, with his careful moustache and his careful reports, because he’d had a crown on one since the fourth night and had told nobody.

Rhea sat at the table with her hand flat on the wood and stared at it.

Then at the man.

“One,” said Rhea.

“One,” the man agreed.

“I held you.”

“You held me,” said the man. “For one.” He took his elbow off the table. “You’re getting stronger.”

“AGAIN,” said Rhea. She was crying. She didn’t seem to have noticed. “Again. Tomorrow. Every night until you go.”

“Every night until I go,” said the man.

Teague, on the gatehouse stair, took a stub of pencil out of his coat and a little book, and wrote something in it, very carefully, with his scarred arm. Salty asked him, later, what it was. It was the fort’s duty log. Nine years of entries in a sergeant’s cramped hand: weather, patrols, scouts sighted, horses lamed, men lost.

Day the eleventh. Rhea held the Black Blade one count. Fort in good order.

The eleventh dinner was fish with onions.

Salty had known it would be. He’d known since the first night, since Wendy said it out loud by the hearth. Dinner at mine. I’ll cook. Not something off a list. Not something from Malacca. Whatever the man cooked, now, at home, after thirty-four years. I can’t cook. The thing he made.

He didn’t go fishing for it. He used the last of the morning’s catch from the stream, and onions from the fort’s garden, and butter that Alea had begged off the kitchen, and salt, and the last scrape of the brown paste, and nothing else. In the skillet, on the hearth, the way he’d have done it in a cabin by a lake on any ordinary night.

There was no step outside that night. Salty didn’t know who’d told them. Somebody had. The step was empty, and the courtyard was quiet, and Oakes had gone up to his supper early and taken his mug, which he never did. Just the four of them in the forge, with the doors open on the dusk. Salty on his bucket, Wendy beside him. Alea at the end of the bench with her chin on her hand.

The man put the plates down.

It wasn’t anything. That was the first thing Salty thought. After ten nights of everything, of coconut and peppers and smoke and fire and snow off the red, it wasn’t anything at all. A piece of fish. Some onions, cooked down soft and gold and sweet in the butter. A bit of bread.

He ate it.

It was the best thing he’d ever eaten.

He didn’t know why. He sat on the bucket and ate it slowly and tried to work out why, the way he’d work out why a piece of iron was wrong, and couldn’t. It was just fish. It was just onions. It was perfectly cooked, and simple, and it tasted of butter and salt and a little of the brown paste, and of the hearth, and of nothing else. And it was the best thing he’d ever eaten.

“That’s what he makes,” said Alea, quietly, from the end of the bench. “For supper. When I come home from training.” She was looking at her father’s back. “Every week since I was four. It’s the first thing I ever remember eating.”

Salty looked at his plate.

Dinner at mine.

He was at his. This was it. This was the house. Not a cabin by a lake, not a flat in Malacca. Whatever the man stood at a hearth in, cooking for the people he cared about, that was his. The forge at Cinderhold, tonight. With Oakes’s skillet. I’ll cook.

He’d cooked.

Salty ate every bite. Wendy ate hers and didn’t give him any, and he didn’t ask. And when the plates were empty and wiped with the bread, the man came and sat down on the end of the bench beside his daughter, for the first time in eleven nights, with nothing in his hands.

Nobody said anything for a while.

“Eleven,” said Salty.

The man looked at him.

“That’s eleven.” Salty put his plate down on the bench. “Eleven dinners. Paid.” His voice had gone thick. He didn’t care. “You don’t owe me anything.”

“No.”

“In full.”

“In full,” the man agreed.

Salty sat on his bucket by the second anvil with his hands on his knees and looked at the forge, and at the empty skillet hanging back on its hook, and at Wendy beside him with her headphones round her neck and her eyes wet, and at the girl, and at the man. And thought about a list that had started at three and gone to eleven and stopped. And a phone on a floor, buzzing at four in the morning. I’m serious about it.

Then he reached down to his belt.

He’d carried it since the spring. Since the fort gate, with Garrow’s dray loaded and the oxen waiting, when Garrow had put it into his hand with his eyes wet and said a good hammer ought to be working, not riding about in somebody’s coat. Finish it. Bring it back to Wrenhaven when you’re done. The king’s father’s hammer, from a forge in Brakewater, on the river, in the south. A small old cross-peen with the handle worn dark and smooth by three men’s hands, a burn on the haft near the head, and a nick in the face the shape of a fingernail. Salty had carried it every day since, to the Lists and back, and used it every day at the second anvil, and it was the best hammer he’d ever held.

He held it out.

The man looked at it.

“It’s Garrow’s,” said Salty. “He said bring it back to Wrenhaven when I’d finished.” He held it out a little further. “I’ve finished. The scythe’s done. You’re going home. Past his forge.”

“You could give it to him yourself. Next year.”

“I could.” Salty didn’t move his hand. “But it’d sit in my belt a year doing nothing. On the red.” He looked at it. “A good hammer ought to be working. Not riding about in somebody’s coat.” He paused. “Garrow said that. He got it off the king. The king got it off his dad.”

The man didn’t take it at once. He sat on the end of the bench and looked at the hammer in Salty’s hand for a long time.

Then he took it.

He held it the way a smith holds a hammer, Salty saw. Not the way a swordsman would. Loose, at the end of the handle, with the weight hanging, the way Garrow held it and the king held it. As if he’d watched this one at work before, on a shingle, in a king’s hand, with a black sword on the anvil between them.

“I’ll give it to him,” said the man.

“Tell him it’s a good hammer.”

“He knows.”

“Tell him anyway,” said Salty.

The man put the hammer in his belt. Beside the good knife, in its sheath, and the drawing in his coat, over his heart.

They sat in the forge until the hearth went out.

Nobody said much. Wendy put her head on Salty’s shoulder, the way she did, and he let her. Alea sat with her chin on her hand and her eyes half closed. Outside, in the courtyard, the lamps came on along the walls one at a time, and somewhere up on the north tower Old Wat was going on duty, slowly, with his spear, and from the step of the forge, where nobody was sitting, there was the faint good smell of eleven nights of cooking that would probably never quite go out of Oakes’s chimney.

Salty thought about Malacca.

He’d never been. He never would be, now, probably. He’d never eat chicken rice balls at the place behind the famous place, or sit in the chair in the old woman’s kitchen. The list was a list in a spreadsheet in a flat in another world, and the man who’d written it was a stranger with a fishing rod and a face you couldn’t see, and the city was thirty-four years and two worlds away.

He didn’t mind.

He’d been. He’d been tonight, and every night for eleven nights. Not to the city. To the thing the list had been for. Come and see me. I’ll show you what I love. I’ll feed you.

It wasn’t the trip they’d planned.

It was the trip.

“Next year,” said the man, eventually, out of the dark. Very quietly. “At the lake. I’ll do the eleven again. In order. With fresh peppers.” He paused. “There are more coconuts in the cart.”

Salty didn’t say anything for a moment.

“Twelve,” he said.

The man turned his head.

“I clutched,” said Salty. “On the red. Last summer. With the sword. One against fifty.” He looked at the dark hearth. “You were watching. From the rise. Niko saw you.” He swallowed. “That’s a clutch. That’s twelve.”

There was a long silence in the forge.

And then the man laughed. Not loudly. Not the way he’d laughed on Rowan’s terrace with a boot held up in front of him. Quietly, in the dark, the way he must have laughed in the call at two in the morning, with Salty alive in the corner of a round against three and everyone else dead. Helpless. Delighted.

“Twelve,” said the man. “That’s fair. That’s a dinner.”

“I know,” said Salty. “I’m keeping count.”

“Trust,” said Wendy, into his shoulder, and Alea laughed, and in the dark of the forge at Cinderhold, for the first time in thirty-four years for one of them and three for the others, somebody updated the list.
