Thirty-Six Boxes

5,862 words · 25 min read

Session 630, Chris wrote, at the top of a clean page, in the grey before dawn on the roof of the Broken Lance. Day 1 of arrivals. Bracket: done. Paint: dry. Boxes: 36. Names in boxes: 36. Names that will be under their boxes: 34. Known issues: two (Thornwall). Not a bug.

He looked at it. He crossed out Not a bug and wrote Feature. Then he crossed that out too and wrote nothing, because it wasn’t either, it was Hyvred on a tower holding a valley in her eye, and there wasn’t a word for that in the notebook.

He’d been keeping the notebook for two years and a half. Session one had been the first morning in Valcrest, on the sand behind the Lists, with Ysolde putting a wooden sword in his hand and saying feel for it, and him not feeling anything, and writing it down in English pseudo-code because he couldn’t write the kingdom’s script and wasn’t going to pretend he could: IF grip == correct AND stance == correct THEN aura SHOULD flow. aura NOT flowing. investigate. He’d investigated for six hundred and twenty-nine sessions. He’d found a memory leak on his left side where a man used to stand, and Winter had stepped into it, and he’d gone Drawn on the fourth day of the Lists last summer with a skill called Release and the whole north stand chanting WIN-TER.

Ysolde kept one now, too. She’d started it in the second winter without saying anything: a clerk’s ledger from the Lists’ office, in the kingdom’s square script that Chris couldn’t read, a date and a measurement on every line. Once a week she stood in front of the oak post at the end of the sand with a chalk line down it, and swung so that her steel stopped short of the wood, and measured how far into the wood the cut went anyway. A hand, the first week, the way it had been for years. A hand and a finger by midsummer. A hand and two fingers by the thaw. I thought it was a number, she’d said, when he finally asked. It’s a distance. You can measure a distance. He’d written that down. It had felt very strange, a teacher who’d once put a wooden sword in his hand copying his notebook; and then less strange; and then he’d just been glad.

He’d kept the count, too. On the back pages. Since before he’d had a skill, or a trio, or a Swiss drawn on a wall. Twentieth of thirty-five. Twenty-four. Thirty-five of thirty-five. And now a new column, opened the morning the bell rang: Tier 2 or above. Two. Three. Four.

And at the bottom of every page, every night, the same line.

Line 36: still blank. still there.

He’d written it on the back page every night since the Lists. He’d written it in the channel, too, every night, at the bottom of the count. He knew who it was for. Everybody knew. The man it was for had never once answered.

The sun came up over the east stand of the Lists and lit the west wall.

He’d drawn it in the winter. Forty feet of new whitewash on the west wall of the Lists, under the stand, across from last summer’s bracket on the east wall, which Lucan had put a little tarred roof over with a rope across the front so nobody would lean on it, ever, the way you’d rope off a painting. Chris had stood in front of the forty feet of white in the cold with a bucket of lampblack and a long brush and a chalk line and Ysolde holding the other end of it, and drawn a Swiss.

Like a Major. Everybody at nought and nought. Nobody seeded, because the count wasn’t a ranking, whatever Lucan wanted it to be, and Chris had said so to his face in the master’s room with the goose dressing gown on the peg behind the door. Three wins and you went through to Drew’s bracket, at the right-hand end. Three losses and you went home. Thirty-five didn’t make a Swiss; it made a mess, an odd number, a player left over every round. So he’d drawn thirty-six boxes.

The thirty-sixth one was the bye. It moved. Every round, a different box sat in it, and nobody sat in it twice, and if you followed it down the wall round by round, by the sums, it walked all the way to the end of things.

Lucan’s painters had lettered the names. In gold. Chris had let them have the letters and kept the lines for himself, and Lucan had looked at him for a long moment with his eyes filling up, which meant yes. Thirty-five names, in Lucan’s painters’ gold, in thirty-five boxes, in the order Chris had drawn them out of a hat in the master’s room with Wil holding the hat, the same boy who’d drawn the lots last year and blown the horns this spring when Hana’s strip came down Lucan’s cord, so loudly that half of Valcrest had come out into the street thinking it was a war.

And the thirty-sixth. Painted the same as the others. Same size. Same black line round it. No gold in it. No name. No fishing rod.

Good, Ysolde had said, when she saw it.

He stood under it now, at the right-hand end of the first column, in the low gold sun, and looked up.

The bye.

Hyvred’s box was two along from it. HYVRED, in gold. And below it, three boxes down, TIGER. Lucan had wanted to paint them out when the strip came from Corvin’s cord in the spring: Hyvred and Tiger stay. Somebody has to look. He’d come down the Lance’s back stair at midnight in the goose dressing gown with his eyes red and said we’ll give their boxes to the bye, we’ll paint them over, it’s kinder, and Chris had said no.

They’re in the count, Chris had said. They’re not here. That’s not the same thing.

So the boxes stayed. Every round he’d draw the line past them and not through them. He’d decided that before he’d drawn the wall. He’d decided it the morning Hyvred’s line came up in the channel from a tower at Thornwall: still turned. button out.

He took the stub of lampblack chalk out of his pocket and, very small, in the corner of Hyvred’s box, in English, wrote: looking.

And in the corner of Tiger’s: making her come down.

They came all week.

Group 3 first, on the third day, in from the east road in the afternoon with Aldous on the box of the cart and the whole of Valcrest’s east gate leaning on the walls to look, because last summer Group 3 had come in from the east road with a scythe in a blanket and a story about horseshoes, and the city remembered. Yan in front, tall and silver-haired, with the scythe on his shoulder and Suzu wound three times round the top of the shaft, white, with her head on the curve of the iron. Salty with his greatsword across his back and a crock in both arms that he wouldn’t let anybody else carry, which turned out to be pepper paste. Wendy with her bow and her lavender headphones round her neck. Maigo on top of the baggage with his antlers so full of leaves he looked like a hedge somebody had put a hood on. And Niko, on the tailboard, with his hands in his pockets and a short sword at his hip, very chill.

Chris watched them come in from the top of the east gate. Niko had been told to pick one by everybody he’d ever told, on Earth; he’d written so in the channel, the morning after the knoll, with blood still on his nose. Chris watched him get down off the tailboard, and stand in the gate, and look up at the wall of the Lists going up behind the roofs, and Chris saw his face do the thing it did when he was about to do something he wasn’t sure about.

He went down.

“Thirty-five of thirty-five,” said Chris. “I logged it. I’ve been wanting to say it to your face.”

“I read it,” said Niko. “In the channel. On the knoll. With blood on my nose.” He looked at Chris. “Is there a box?”

“There’s a box.”

“0–0?”

“0–0 like everyone.”

Niko’s face didn’t do anything. Niko’s face never did. But he took his hands out of his pockets, which Chris had seen him do perhaps four times in two years.

“Nice one,” said Niko. “Again.”

Group 4 on the fourth day, in by the river gate behind two carts Dennis had hired and argued about the whole way from Kingsferry, so that the carters were not speaking to him. Deston with his head tipped, listening to the whole city’s threads at once and smiling the way he did, like a man at a concert. Gabe enormous and red and nervous, with Sto on his back and a roll of hammers he’d brought for no reason he could explain. Rachael with her arms folded. Dennis haggling with the porter. Calvin with the kettle. Emrys humming. Sabine, sharp and tired, with one sword.

Group 6 on the fifth, in from the north road behind a guild cart, with Garrick and his lantern and Petra with her sword on her hip and a length of grey driftwood under her arm like a riding crop. Azac with his shield. Rui Xiang talking. Malcolm with his book. John with a light on the end of his finger that went up over the gate as they came in and hung there like a lamp nobody had to hold, and half the gate-guard stopped to look at it. And Xav in the middle, dark and still, who looked up at the west wall as he passed under it, at his own box, and then, Chris saw, at the thirty-sixth one, and then didn’t look at anything for a while.

Group 2 on the sixth, from Solmere, with no Kestra, because Kestra was at Thornwall with the king, and two castle guards instead who were very frightened of Aleaka. Hana with seven halves on her wrist and a face that had gone Candle a month ago and still looked surprised about it. Milli with his tail up. Jared silent, carrying everything. Cursiffe with a pot of ink in his pocket that he kept touching. Aleaka with no hawk, because the hawk was in the west tower in Solmere on the Advisor’s arm and had declined, Aleaka said, very firmly, to come.

And Group 5, the same day, from the north road. Drew and Bee and Kei, and a cart, and a rider from Ashby, and nobody else.

Drew went straight to the west wall.

Chris saw him from the roof of the Lance; you could see the whole of the west wall from the roof, over the backs of the houses, if you sat with your back against the chimney stack. Drew walked through the Lists’ south gate with his pack still on and his 88EXP pendant swinging and went down the length of the wall, past all the boxes, past his own, without looking at it, to the right-hand end, and stopped under two boxes with gold names in them and a word written very small in the corner of each in lampblack.

He stood there a long time.

Then he took off his pack, and sat down on it, on the sand, under the boxes, with his back to the wall and his bladed knuckles in his lap, wrapped in their cloth, and didn’t move until it got dark.

The fox girl came in by the sea gate on the seventh day.

Chris didn’t see it. He heard it. Everybody in Valcrest heard it; it went across the city from the sea gate to the Lists in about the time it takes a rumour to go across a city, which in Valcrest, in the Lists week, was not very long. The holder’s in. The Wrenhaven girl. The one with the sword the size of a door. She’s walked in by the sea gate with the boat-hook girl from Gullhaven and they both smell of fish.

Lucan heard it in the master’s room and came out into the street in his shirtsleeves without his coat and ran, which Chris had never seen Lucan do, down the length of the Street of Coopers to the Lists’ south gate, and stood at the bottom of the wall where the Fledgling tree was, the sixty-four boxes he’d painted himself, with ALEA, OF WRENHAVEN · HOLDER at the top in gold, and SORREL, OF GULLHAVEN in the first box of the entries, also in gold, because Lucan had run out of black that day and said gold was close enough.

And the two of them came round the corner of the stand, a tall fox girl with a black slab of a sword on her back and a salt-burned girl with a boat-hook in each hand, and stopped, and looked up at it.

Lucan cried, obviously.

Chris heard about that too. Lucan had cried, and Sorrel had said is he all right and Alea had said he does this, and Lucan had said, through it, the holder’s place is held until sunset on midsummer eve, it’s the rule, three hundred years, I’ll never paint over it, never, and Alea had said I know, and then, Chris heard, she’d looked along the whole length of the sixty-four boxes, the way you’d look along a road you were going to have to walk, and her ears had gone flat, and then up.

He saw her that evening. At the long table in the Lance, the end one by the window, with Sorrel, eating everything the kitchen sent out, the way Group 3 had eaten everything the kitchen sent out last summer after twenty days on the road. She’d got taller. Or she stood differently. He couldn’t tell which. She looked up when he came in, and nodded, the way her father nodded, once, and went back to her plate.

He didn’t say anything to her. He’d decided not to.

He’d decided not to the week before, when Maren’s strip came down Lucan’s cord in the master’s room in the middle of the afternoon and Lucan read it out to him in the voice he used for things he was going to cry about later. Short. In Hana’s English, through the cord, the way the cords carried things: the meaning, in the reader’s own hand.

Group 1 leaves Wrenhaven on the first. Five of us, Bram, me. Six. He is carrying the water. She does not know. Do not put it in the channel. Rule five. M.

Lucan had put the strip down on his desk and looked at it for a very long time.

“Rule five,” said Lucan. “What is rule five?”

“It’s a thing Group 1 has,” said Chris. “It means keep it.”

“Keep it,” said Lucan. He’d looked out of the window, at the west wall, at the Fledgling tree with HOLDER in gold at the top. “From her.”

“From her.”

“She’ll be on that sand,” said Lucan, “for five days. In front of forty thousand people. Looking at every one of them.”

“Yes.”

Lucan had put his hand over his face.

“I’ll need a house,” he’d said, through his fingers. “Somewhere off the Street of Coopers. With a back door.” And then, because he was Lucan, and couldn’t help it, and had been master of the Lists longer than Chris had been alive and never once been asked to keep a secret from a holder: “Oh, he’s coming. Oh, Chris. He’s coming and he’s not telling her.”

Chris kept it.

It was the easiest thing he’d ever logged. He didn’t log it. He wrote it on the back page of the notebook, in pseudo-code, where nobody who could read English would think to look and nobody who couldn’t read English could read anyway: IF holder.knows == false THEN keep. ELSE keep anyway.

And in the channel, every night, at the bottom of the count, he wrote what he always wrote.

It wasn’t a lie. He’d checked. The tier column on line thirty-six had never had anything in it and never would; it was blank. And still there was true. It had always been true. It was the truest line in the notebook.

He just didn’t say where.

He kept another page too, the one he’d started after the first Lists, when Drew had asked for the numbers. AT THE EDGE. It had a second column now, for the second tier.

Ina 99. Chris 99 (still not funny). Wendy 98. Rachael 98. Deston 98. Xavier 98. Salty 97. Higu 97. PenguChi 96. Hako 96. Milli 95. Jared 95. Gabe 94.

Thirteen. Drew had read it over his shoulder on the wall, the second evening, and said what he’d said last year, word for word, as if he’d been saving it. It’ll find out who was going to.

Last year Chris had guessed four or five, and been right, and been wrong about himself. This year he guessed four. He didn’t put himself on the list of guesses at all.

They came in by the north road on the fifteenth day, at dusk, and Chris was on the wall over the north gate when they did.

He’d gone up because Winter had. Winter had gone up because Newish had said, at supper, out of nowhere, with her spoon in the air, there’s a beat on the north road. Newish heard beats the way other people heard footsteps. She’d said once, in the first winter, that everyone had one, and that if you knew someone’s well enough you could hear it coming from a mile off, a little rhythm under the noise, the way you’d know a friend’s knock. Chris had stopped arguing with it in the first winter, because she was always right, and because it was easier to believe her than to explain how she kept knowing.

“Whose?” Winter had said.

Newish had put her spoon down. “Mostly rests,” she’d said. And Winter had got up from the table and gone out, and Chris had gone after her, and Hako after him with his spear, and Ina last, at the pace of a man taking the air, with his mask on his shoulder.

So they were on the north wall when the cart came over the last rise of the north road in the last of the light.

The miller’s cart from Wrenhaven, with the miller’s grey and the miller’s son. Maren on the box with her list. Bram at the tail. Five people walking beside it: a tall one with pink-and-violet hair, a small one with a creature on her head that had gone a hopeful sort of pink, a gruff small one with her hands in her pockets, a calm one with moths round her head, a quiet one carrying a penguin.

And at the back, behind the cart, a man with two buckets on a yoke.

Chris watched him come down the last slope of the north road with the water. A plain man in a farmer’s coat, not very tall, with a fishing rod lashed along the top of the cart where he could reach it, walking at the back the way he’d walked at the back of a twenty-two-person procession last summer, carrying the water, at the pace of the slowest. You couldn’t see his face. Nobody could. Chris had stopped trying in the first week of the Lists last summer. You read the rest of him instead, and the rest of him was a man carrying water down a road toward a city, not hurrying, the way you’d carry it toward a house where somebody would need it.

He’d thought, the first time, last summer, that’s Declan. Then that’s Ice. Then, because it was the only name the notebook had ever had for him: that’s line thirty-six.

He didn’t think any of those now. He just watched him come.

“Mostly rests,” said Newish, beside him, very quietly.

At the gate, the guard said state your business, the way gate-guards in Valcrest said it to everybody in the Lists week, and Maren said the Lists, from the box, and the guard looked at the cart, and the five girls, and the penguin, and then at the man at the back with the yoke, and something happened to his face. He was old enough. He’d have been on a wall somewhere, once. He looked at the man with the buckets for a long moment, and then he stepped back out of the gate, all the way back, to the wall, and put his hand flat on his chest.

The man with the buckets didn’t stop. He nodded, once, the way he did, and came through.

And Lucan was standing in the road inside the gate.

Chris hadn’t known he’d be there. Nobody had. He’d come down from the master’s house in his good coat this time, not the dressing gown, and he was standing in the middle of the north road inside the gate in the dusk with his hands at his sides, a man who’d been master of the Lists longer than Chris had been alive and wept at everything, and he wasn’t weeping. He was standing very straight.

The cart stopped for him. It had to; he was in the way.

Lucan looked at the man with the buckets. A long look. Up, and down, and at the yoke, and at the rod on the cart, and back.

He didn’t say anything.

That was what Chris would remember. He’d log it that night and log it again in the morning and still, years later, be able to see it if he closed his eyes: Lucan, who had never in his life not said anything, who narrated his own breakfast, who’d stood on the sand at sunset at the end of the Lists last summer in front of forty thousand people and read a girl’s name into the roll in a voice that broke on every word, standing in the north road at dusk in front of a man with two buckets and saying nothing at all.

Then he stepped aside. Out of the road. And held out his hand, flat, palm up, with a key on it.

The man with the buckets set them down. He took the key. He looked at it in his palm, and then at Lucan, and Chris saw his shoulders do something, and then not.

“Thank you,” said the man. Quietly. In the kingdom’s tongue.

“Off the Street of Coopers,” said Lucan. “Blue door. There’s a back way into the Lists from the yard. Nobody uses it but the painters.” He was very steady. “I’ll send your supper. You’ll not want to come to the Lance.”

“No.”

“No,” said Lucan. “She’s at the end table. By the window. She’s eaten three suppers. The kitchen loves her.” And then, because he was Lucan, and he’d held it as long as anyone could have asked: his face went, all at once, the way a dam goes, and he put his hand over it and turned round and walked away up the north road toward the master’s house very fast with his shoulders shaking.

The man with the buckets watched him go. Then he picked up the yoke again, and the buckets, and walked on behind the cart, into the city, toward the Street of Coopers, at the back. With the water.

Nobody said anything.

That was the thing. That was the thing Chris would log at midnight on the roof with his back against the chimney stack and his hands not quite steady on the pencil. Nobody in Valcrest said anything. Not the gate-guard, who’d put his hand on his chest. Not the women at the well at the corner of the Street of Coopers, who’d looked up at a man with buckets going past in the dusk and gone very quiet and then gone on with their washing, not looking, the way you don’t look at a thing you’ve decided to keep. Not the boy with the paper fishing rod on the step of the chandler’s, who’d started to lift it, and been stopped by his mother’s hand on his arm, and lowered it, and then, when the man had gone past, held it up anyway, behind his back, where only the man’s back could see it.

The city had kept it last summer. The whole of Valcrest. For a week. It had sat in the stands and watched a man with a rod on the fourth tier and nobody had said that’s him out loud, not once, and when his daughter wrote her mother’s name in the box at sunset on the last day, forty thousand people had held up paper fishing rods and not one of them had said his name.

It was keeping it again. Chris could feel it. Going out from the north gate through the streets in the dusk faster than a rumour, quieter than one, the way a thing goes through a crowd when everybody in it decides the same thing at once without being told. Don’t. Not to her. Let her look up and find him.

The Broken Lance didn’t say anything either.

He came down off the roof at the end of the evening and went through the long room on his way to bed, and there they all were. Thirty-three of the thirty-five, round the long tables, with the lamps lit and the kitchen sending out the last of the pies; and at the end table by the window, a fox girl with her ears up, eating a fourth supper, with a boat-hook girl beside her telling a story about a conger that got longer every time. And every single person in that room knew. Group 1 had come in an hour ago with Maren and Bram and sat down at their table and said nothing, very loudly, and the room had looked at them, and at their faces, and known. Chris watched it go round the tables. Group 3, Yan’s head coming up an inch, the way it did when he felt something a long way off; he’d have felt it at the gate. Group 4, Deston, with his head tipped, smiling at nothing, listening to a thread that had come into the city at dusk and was sitting now behind a blue door off the Street of Coopers, a quarter of a mile away, like a nail. Group 6. Xav, very still, with his chin on his fist. Petra beside him with her driftwood stick across her knees, looking at the fox girl at the end table and then away. Gabe, red to the ears, trying so hard not to look toward the Street of Coopers that he’d turned his whole chair round. Rachael, eating an apple, looking at the window.

Thirty-three people from another world, and a guide, and a guard, and a kitchen, and a city. Keeping it.

At the end table, Alea looked up. Round the room. Ears forward. The way she did. Checking, her father would have said, what was wrong.

Thirty-three faces looked back at her and said nothing, kindly.

She frowned. Just slightly. Then she shrugged, and went back to Sorrel’s conger, which was now longer than the Patient Widow.

Chris went up to bed. On the stair he met Winter coming down, with her silver-white hair loose and her mismatched eyes, one blue, one pink, very bright in the dark.

“Logged?” said Winter.

“Not logged,” said Chris. “Kept.”

Winter looked at him for a long moment. Then she did the thing she’d learned to do, on a sand last summer, the thing Chris had never seen her do in the first year at all. She smiled.

Ina lit the wall the next night.

Nobody asked him to. That was how Ina did everything. He’d been a mage for two years and a half, a fire mage, the one of all thirty-five whose tiers were literally named for what he did: Spark, Candle, Lantern, Torch. He’d made his first real flame at a lantern festival in the Lists’ own bowl in the first year, with his mask on and violet fire coming off his fingers that burned hotter with the mask than without it, and he’d been Onibi ever since, the ghost-fire, and Spark, and he’d been very good-humoured about staying Spark while Thea went Candle and Hyvred and Hana went Candle in a month, and Chris had known for at least a year that he wasn’t.

On the night before the Swiss, at dusk, Lucan’s painters went down the west wall with tapers to light the lamps.

There were forty of them. Iron lamps on brackets all along the forty feet of whitewash, one over every box and four at the ends, that Lucan had had put up in the winter so the bracket could be read at night, because, he said, the best things at the Lists always happened after dark. The painters went down the wall with long poles with tapers on the end, the way they did every night, slowly, one lamp at a time.

Ina was on the sand at the end of the wall with his mask on his shoulder, watching them.

Chris was on the roof. He saw it from there, across the backs of the houses, the whole length of the wall in the blue dusk, and the painters at the near end with their poles, and the small figure of Ina at the far end in his indigo coat.

Ina took the mask off his shoulder. He put it on.

He’d done that perhaps a dozen times since the first spring. He didn’t like to. The mask made the fire hotter, and it made him look exactly like what everyone in this kingdom was afraid of, a horned thing with a white face out of a story about Kharvos; and he’d stood on the sand of the Lists that night with it on and violet fire on his hands and watched ten thousand people decide he was a monster, and then watched a small boy of seven in a paper crown of horns decide he wasn’t. He wore it for the boy. He took it off for everyone else.

He put it on. He lifted his hand.

He didn’t throw anything. He didn’t chant. He just lifted his hand, at the far end of the wall, the way you’d lift your hand to wave to somebody at the other end of a street, and flicked his fingers, once.

The lamp over the thirty-sixth box lit.

Violet. Just for a breath. Then it settled, and went the ordinary yellow of a lamp, and burned.

Then the next one. Hyvred’s. And Tiger’s. And the next, and the next, down the wall, one after another, not all at once but in a line, each one catching off the one before like a rumour going down a street. Ten. Fifteen. Twenty. The painters at the near end stood with their poles in their hands and their tapers lit, staring, as the line of lamps came toward them along the whitewash.

At the twenty-sixth it stopped.

Chris saw it stop. The twenty-sixth lamp caught, violet, and went yellow; and the twenty-seventh flickered, once, a little violet ghost of a flame on the wick, and went out. On the sand at the far end, the small figure in the indigo coat went down on one knee, hard, as if somebody had kicked his legs out from under him, with one hand flat on the sand.

The painters looked at each other. Then they lifted their poles, very respectfully, and lit the last fourteen themselves.

Ina took the mask off, slowly, still on one knee, and put it back on his shoulder; and Chris, on the roof a quarter of a mile away, heard him laugh. A big surprised breathless laugh that went up out of the bowl of the Lists into the blue, the way the laugh had gone out across the lake on the morning of the bell, so that people on the walls turned round.

It came up in the channel a moment later, in Ina’s own hand, because Ina had never once in his life been able to resist an audience.

He wrote it. He looked at it. Twenty-six.

Then he looked across the backs of the houses, past the west wall with its forty lamps burning, twenty-six of them his, toward the Street of Coopers, a quarter of a mile off in the dusk, where there was a blue door with a back way into the Lists through the painters’ yard that nobody used.

He couldn’t see it from here. You couldn’t see the Street of Coopers from the roof of the Lance; the roofs were in the way. He looked anyway.

Kagaribi. Watch-fires along a wall. So the people coming home can see the way in.

He opened the notebook on his knee, to the back page, and at the bottom, under the count, under every line 36: still blank. still there he’d ever written, in pencil, small, where nobody who could read English would look, he wrote one more line, and didn’t put it in the channel, and didn’t log it, and kept it.

Line 36: here.
