The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 5: The Lists

Chapter 139

Okay. Again.

5,547 words · 24 min read

Xav had been waiting his whole life, and he was good at it.

He’d been good at it on Earth. In a polytechnic classroom in Singapore at seventeen, waiting for the lecture to end so he could go home and play. In the last round of every Counter-Strike match he’d ever played, the one where everyone else on his team was dead and it was him against three and the bomb ticking, waiting for the one sound that told him where they’d come from. He was famous for it, in the calls. Xav’s still alive, somebody would say, and everyone would go quiet, because Xav still being alive at the end of a round meant something was going to happen, eventually, and nobody would see it coming.

Declan had talked him into Counter-Strike. In the first year at the poly. Xav had been playing something else. Declan had leaned over his shoulder in the lab one afternoon and said you’d be good at CS. You wait. Nobody waits. And Xav had tried it, and been good at it, and it had been the thing the two of them did for years, until Declan moved to Malacca at twenty-four and they drifted, the way you drift, and then there’d been a phone call.

He’d been waiting for the next round ever since. He hadn’t known it.

I just wait until I do, he’d said once, in the channel, in the first year, when somebody asked him how he knew when to move. He’d meant it. He didn’t decide. He just waited until he did.

He was standing on the side sand at dawn on the sixty-fifth day, the last morning of the Lists, with a practice sword in his hand, waiting for a fisherman to move.

“Okay,” said Xav. “Again.”

The man in front of him on the wet sand didn’t say anything. He was holding a stick. Driftwood, grey and smooth, a little longer than his arm, that he’d picked up on the bank of the river outside the west gate on the second day because the shingle at the lake was too far away and he needed a stick. He held it loosely. The rod was leaning on the fence.

The rule was the same as at the lake. Anyone could try him once a morning, with a practice sword, against his stick. Everyone lost on one move. Petra had tried him every morning for two months and lost every morning on one move. Tiger had tried him yesterday and lost so fast he’d sat down on the sand and laughed. Hako had tried him with his new peek, all the way, nothing held back, and found himself on his back looking at the sky again.

Xav had made him move twice.

Once. On a misty morning on the shingle at Lake Ilse, nearly two months ago, on the twelfth day. Xav had stepped sideways in the breath before, the way Petra taught him, and for the first time anyone had ever seen, the man with the stick had to move twice. Once more than anyone. Ever, he’d said, afterwards, very quietly. Not the general. And Xav had said okay. Again, and he’d been saying it every morning since.

He’d never made him move three times. Nobody had.

He tried now. He waited. He stood on the wet sand with the mist round his ankles and watched the whole thing, the way he’d been told to, the man and the stick and the breath and the sand, and waited for the moment before. He found it. He went sideways in it. He felt the man move.

Once.

And the stick touched his wrist, very lightly, and the practice sword went down into the sand.

Xav looked at it.

“Okay,” said Xav. “Again.”

“Tomorrow,” said the man. He leaned the stick on the fence beside the rod. “You’ve got a final.”

He did have a final.

The Singles. Drew’s tree on the forty-foot wall, under the Trios, the one Lucan had made the painter add because somebody has to stand alone in them. Sixteen of the thirty-five had entered. It had gone on all week, in the gaps between everything else, on the side sand, one bout at a time. Hako and Milli on the third day. Salty lifting Milli off the sand last night in the torchlight like a cat.

And this morning, at the second hour, on the great sand, in front of whoever was awake: Xav and Salty.

“One touch,” Lucan had said, at the long table, last night, weeping, with his arm round Drew. “For the final. The old way. Not three. One. That’s how it was done for three hundred years. One touch, and it’s over, and you’ve won or you haven’t.”

“It’s sudden death,” Drew had said.

“It’s tradition,” Lucan had said, and wept harder, and Drew had said okay.

Xav had gone to bed and not slept and thought about one touch.

He’d fought Salty before. Twice, in the channel, in words, in the first year, arguing about whether a heavy blade or a light one was better, and once, two nights ago, in the yard behind the Broken Lance, sparring with sticks, which Salty had won by hitting him so hard he’d sat down. Salty didn’t wait. That was what everyone said. Salty was short and heavy and dense with muscle, and he went in, and he hit things, and they fell over. He’d carried a black stone across the Redmarch with his ribs broken. He’d forged a scythe in six months. He’d lifted Milli off the sand last night on the end of a blunted greatsword and set him down outside the line, and apologised.

Salty fights like Ars looks, Malcolm had written, in his notebook, last night, at the end of the long table. And under it: Xav fights like Ars thinks. And under that, in Malcolm’s careful hand: IF they’re both him, THEN who wins?

Xav had read it over his shoulder. Malcolm had looked up.

“I don’t know,” Malcolm had said. “I don’t have a contingency for this one.”

The great sand was half full at the second hour.

The big crowd was coming at noon, for the Fledgling final. But the city had heard about the Singles final, and about one touch, the old way, and about two of the summoned who were both, somehow, the Black Blade, and the stands were filling up anyway in the long gold light: people with breakfast in their hands, children on shoulders, a few old soldiers who’d come in from the plain and sat together on the third tier of the south stand with their arms folded, very still, the way old soldiers sit.

The king was in the royal box. Halvard beside him. The thirty-five on the bottom tiers, in pairs, never alone, the way Yan had written in the channel last night. Don’t go anywhere alone. Thirty-nine. Xav had felt Hyvred go still, coming in, as they passed the north gate; had seen her crosshair lock on a woman with a basket and a man in a green coat and a boy in a cap, and let go, and lock on someone else.

They were still here. Counting.

He put it away. You put things away, in the last round. You didn’t think about the other team’s economy. You thought about the sound.

And on the fourth tier of the east stand, at the end of a bench, a man with a rod across his knees and two bees on his head.

He’d come up from the side sand. He’d sat down. He was sitting very still.

Xav knew what he was looking at. He’d known since Malcolm’s notebook. Two people walking out onto the sand from opposite ends. One short and broad and heavy, with a blade nearly as long as he was tall, who went in and hit things. The other one quiet, who waited, and moved once.

The way the Black Blade had looked, at twenty-two, on a parade ground, with a sword nobody could lift. And the way he’d thought.

Both halves, thought Xav. He’s watching both halves of himself.

Salty was already in the chalk ring.

He was standing in the middle of it with a blunted greatsword over his shoulder, the practice one from the Lists’ armoury, the heaviest they had. Short and broad and dense, with his crooked ribs and his blunt square face, and the king’s father’s hammer still in his belt, because he’d said he wasn’t taking it out until he’d given it back to Garrow, and he wasn’t going north till after the Lists.

He looked at Xav.

“Morning,” said Salty.

“Morning.”

“One touch.”

“One touch.”

Salty nodded. He looked down at the greatsword on his shoulder, and then up at the fourth tier, and then back at Xav.

“I’m going to hit you,” said Salty. Bluntly. The way he said everything. “Really hard. As soon as I can.”

“I know,” said Xav.

“You’re going to wait.”

“Probably.”

“I hate waiting,” said Salty. “I’m really bad at it. Garrow spent all winter telling me to wait. The heat stops being angry if you wait for it.” He shifted the sword on his shoulder. “I got better. I’m still bad.” He looked at Xav for a long moment. “You’re the best at it of anyone. Except him.”

Xav didn’t say anything.

“So I’m going to hit you as soon as I can,” said Salty, “and you’re going to wait for it, and one of us is going to be right.” He shrugged. “Seems fair.”

“Seems fair,” said Xav.

“Begin,” said the marshal.

Salty didn’t go.

Xav had been certain he would. Everyone had. Salty was the one who went in. He’d said so himself, a breath ago. As soon as I can.

He stood in the middle of the chalk ring with the greatsword on his shoulder and didn’t move.

Xav stood at the edge of the ring and didn’t move either.

The stands went quiet. Not all at once. The way a room goes quiet when everyone in it has realised at different moments that something is about to happen and nobody knows what. Xav heard it go round the bowl. Children being hushed. Somebody’s breakfast put down. The old soldiers on the third tier of the south stand leaning forward very slightly, all together.

Salty was waiting.

Xav could see what it cost him. He’d watched Salty for two years in the channel, and a week in person. Salty didn’t hold still. His hands moved on the grip. His weight shifted from one foot to the other. His jaw was tight. Every bit of him wanted to go, the way every bit of Milli had wanted to go on the side sand, the way every bit of a dog wants to go when you throw a stick and say stay.

He stayed. The heat stops being angry if you wait for it.

Okay, thought Xav. He’s learned it.

That changed things.

He’d thought it would be simple. He’d lain awake last night thinking about it, and he’d thought: Salty goes in. Salty always goes in. So you wait for him to go, and in the breath before he goes, while he’s still deciding to, you move. Sideways. The way Petra taught you on the shingle. And he goes through where you were. And you touch him. One move.

That was the plan. It was a good plan. It had worked on everyone Xav had ever fought, here and on Earth. Everyone went in, eventually. Everyone peeked.

Salty wasn’t going in.

Salty was standing in the middle of the ring doing the thing Xav did. Waiting. Badly, a bit. Visibly hating it. But waiting.

And you can’t move in the breath before somebody moves, if they aren’t going to.

Xav stood at the edge of the ring and felt the plan go, the way you feel a round go when the other team does something you didn’t expect. It went cleanly. He didn’t mind. Plans went. That was what the last round was for.

He did what he always did.

He looked at the whole thing.

Not at Salty. At all of it.

The chalk ring, on the gold sand. The greatsword on Salty’s shoulder, the weight of it, the way it pulled on him, slightly, to the right. Salty’s feet. Salty’s jaw. Salty’s hands on the grip, moving, a little, the knuckles going white and then not. The breath. In. Out. Too fast. A man trying very hard to be patient and finding it was the hardest thing he’d ever done.

The stands. The quiet. The old soldiers on the third tier.

The fourth tier of the east stand.

The man at the end of the bench was sitting very still with the rod across his knees, and his hands weren’t moving at all.

Stop looking at me, he said, every morning. Look at the whole thing.

Xav looked at the whole thing. And at the edge of it, in the middle of it, at the bottom of everything, he found what he was looking for. Not the breath before Salty moved. Salty wasn’t going to move.

The breath before Salty broke.

Because he would. That was the thing. Salty had learned to wait, all winter, at an anvil, with Garrow beside him. He’d got better at it. But he was still bad at it. He’d said so. And a man who’s bad at waiting doesn’t wait forever. He waits, and waits, and it builds in him, the way heat builds in iron that’s been in the fire too long, until there’s a moment when it can’t hold any more.

You didn’t wait for that moment to come. You saw it coming. You saw it in the hands, and the jaw, and the too-fast breath. You saw the iron going from orange to straw.

And just before it went white, you moved.

Xav said something under his breath.

He always did. He didn’t know when he’d started. Some time in the first year, on the sand at Duskhollow, before Petra or the lake or anything. Before every last move. Three words, under his breath, in Latin, that Declan used to say in voice at the start of every clutch, every single time, in the last round, with the bomb ticking and everyone else dead.

Xav had never once asked him what they meant.

He’d found out, in the spring, on the shingle, from Petra. Before he moved, Petra had said, every time, the Black Blade said three or four words under his breath. In no language anyone knew. I thought it was a prayer. She’d stopped. Now I think it might have been a joke.

It was a joke. Xav was fairly sure. It was the kind of joke Declan made: something grand and Latin and absurd, said with complete seriousness at the worst possible moment, so that you laughed in the middle of the round and lost your nerves. He’d been saying it before every clutch for nine years so that his friends would laugh. He’d been saying it on the Redmarch before he killed things.

Xav said it now. Under his breath. On the great sand of the Lists, in the half-full stands, with one touch to win.

And on the fourth tier of the east stand, very far away, a man’s shoulders went.

Salty broke.

Xav saw it come. He’d been watching it come for a hundred breaths. The hands on the grip, white, then not, then white and staying white. The jaw. The weight going forward onto the front foot, a hair, and back, and forward again, and staying. The breath going in, all the way, and not coming out.

The iron going straw.

Salty went.

He went the way Salty went everywhere: all at once, all of him, nothing held back. The heaviest practice greatsword in the Lists’ armoury coming off his shoulder in a huge flat arc, the way a short heavy man swings a blade nearly as long as he is tall, the way the Black Blade had swung a slab of black iron across the Redmarch, a cut that would have taken a door off its hinges.

It was a magnificent cut.

Xav had moved a breath before it.

Not away. Sideways. In. The way he’d moved on a misty morning on the shingle at Lake Ilse, in the breath before, and made a man with a stick move twice. He went sideways into the place where Salty was about to be, while Salty was still deciding to be there, and the greatsword went through the air where he’d been standing with a sound like wind going through a door, and Xav was inside it.

He touched Salty very lightly on the chest with the flat of his practice sword.

One move.

“Touch,” said the marshal. “The final to Xavier, of Duskhollow.”

There was a silence over the half-full Lists.

Salty stood in the middle of the chalk ring with the greatsword at the end of its swing, out to his right, still in both hands, and the flat of Xav’s blade resting on his chest. He looked down at it.

Then he looked at Xav.

And then he laughed.

He threw his head back and laughed, out loud, in the middle of the great sand of the Lists, so hard that the greatsword went down into the sand at his feet and he had to put one hand on his crooked ribs. It went out across the bowl, Salty’s laugh, big and blunt and surprised, and the half-full stands caught it, the way stands do, and went up.

“I waited,” said Salty, through it. “I waited so long. I did it right. Garrow would’ve been proud.”

“He would,” said Xav.

“And you still—“ Salty couldn’t finish. He was laughing too hard. “How?”

Xav thought about it. He wanted to give him a true answer.

“You were bad at it,” he said.

Salty stopped laughing for a moment. Then started again, harder.

“I SAID I WAS BAD AT IT,” he said. “I TOLD YOU. BEFORE.”

“I know,” said Xav. “So I waited for you to be bad at it.”

The old soldiers on the third tier of the south stand had stood up.

Xav saw them, as the stands went up round them. Seven or eight of them, grey-haired, weathered, in the plain coats men wore when they’d come in from farms on the plain. Redmarch men, he thought. He didn’t know how he knew. They were standing up very straight on the third tier, all together, not cheering. Just standing. Looking at the chalk ring.

One of them put his hand on his chest. Over his heart.

Xav didn’t know what it meant. He’d ask Petra, after.

He looked up at the fourth tier.

The man with the rod was sitting very still at the end of his bench. His shoulders had stopped going. He wasn’t standing up. He was sitting with his hands flat on his knees and his face, which Xav couldn’t see, turned toward the sand. Toward the two people in the chalk ring. The short heavy one laughing with his hand on his ribs, and the quiet one with his practice sword down.

Xav knew him well enough, after nine years, to know what he was doing.

He was looking at both of them at once. The way he looked at everything. The whole thing.

They arm-wrestled at the fence afterwards.

Salty’s idea. He came off the sand still laughing, with the greatsword over his shoulder and the hammer in his belt, and walked straight to the rail of the bottom tier where the summoned were, and put his elbow on the top rail, and held out his hand, and said “Again,” to Xav, in a voice that made it very clear this was not a request.

Xav looked at the hand.

“I’ve never arm-wrestled anyone,” he said.

“Good,” said Salty.

Xav put his elbow on the rail.

It took about as long as it took to blink. Xav’s arm went down onto the rail, flat, and stayed there, and Salty held it there, very gently, the way you’d hold down a kitten, and grinned at him across the rail like a man who has finally, after a very long morning, won something.

“Okay,” said Xav. “Again.”

“No,” said Salty, with tremendous satisfaction, and let go, and went off along the rail to find Wendy, who’d been watching all of it with her headphones round her neck and her hand over her face.

Xav stood at the rail and looked at his hand.

Behind him, somewhere, Rhea was shouting that she wanted to arm-wrestle everybody, and Petra was telling her no.

Petra was already at the near ring.

She’d gone straight there from the rail when the marshal called the Singles, without waiting to see the arm-wrestling. The Fledgling semifinals were next. Two of them, on the near ring and the far, at the third hour. And Alea was on the near ring.

Xav went.

He didn’t stop. He didn’t go up to the master’s platform when Drew called his name, or wave at the stands that were still cheering him, or find Malcolm, who’d be writing something in the notebook. He went along the bottom of the east stand with his practice sword still in his hand and his arm still aching from the rail, and through the gap in the fence to the near ring, and stood beside Petra at the edge of it.

Alea’s corner.

He’d stood there all week. Since round one, when she’d stood still while a lord’s son came at her and let her sword fall. He’d stood there for Garth of Ashby and the third count, and the spear girl who breathed, and the mage who chanted, and the big quiet boy who waited like her father, when she’d been so frightened her ears went flat and she’d counted to three and gone in.

He hadn’t said much. He never did. He’d said good once.

“You won,” said Petra, without looking round.

“I won.”

“Salty?”

“He waited,” said Xav. “Really well. For a long time. And then he couldn’t.” He looked at the near ring. “I waited for him to not be able to.”

Petra was quiet for a moment.

“That’s how he did it,” she said. “On the Redmarch. In the ditch.” She didn’t say who. She didn’t need to. “Everyone thought he went in. He didn’t. He waited for them to not be able to wait any more.” She looked at Xav sideways. “You had three teachers. Him and me and Salty.”

Xav didn’t answer that.

He kept his face very still. The way he’d kept it on a jetty in the spring, at midnight, when a man had told three people the whole story of a night on the Redmarch, and the ditch, and why. Don’t tell her, the man had said. It isn’t hers. I’d hold that ditch again. And Xav had said okay. And he’d kept it every day since, standing next to her at dawn on wet sand, in the corner of a ring, and never once let his face do anything.

“Okay,” he said.

Alea came out of the tunnel.

She came out with Astra on her back and her hair tied up and her ears forward, and she walked across the sand to the near ring the way she’d walked to it every day this week, very straight, and stopped at the edge, and looked at Xav and Petra.

“You won,” she said to Xav.

“I won.”

“I saw. From the tunnel. Salty laughed.”

“He did.”

“Dad’s shoulders went,” said Alea. “When you said the thing. Under your breath.” She looked at Xav with her mother’s look, deciding. “What do you say? Before you move?”

Xav considered.

“Something he used to say,” he said. “On Earth. Before every clutch. In Latin.”

“What does it mean?”

“I don’t know,” said Xav. “I never asked.”

Alea looked at him for a long moment. Then her ears did something. Flattened, and then went straight up, and quivered.

“I know what it means,” she said.

Xav waited.

“He told me,” said Alea. “When I was small. It’s—“ She stopped. She looked up at the fourth tier, at the end of a bench, at a man nobody looked at. And then back at Xav, and her face did the very good nothing that she’d done at the north gate for Aleaka, while her ears gave her away completely. “No. I’m not telling you.”

“Okay.”

“You’d stop saying it.”

“Probably,” said Xav.

“Then you’d lose,” said Alea, and turned round, and went into the ring.

Her semifinal was a cadet.

His name was Tamlin Brand, and he was from the king’s own guard school in Solmere, and he was eighteen and very good and very well taught, and he had come through five rounds without anybody touching him. He was the best cadet in the Fledgling Lists. Everyone said so. Lucan had said so, reading out the roll.

He was standing very still.

Not like Corran Tolley, the big quiet boy from round five, who’d waited like her father. Tamlin Brand waited like a cadet. Correctly. Exactly. The way a guard school teaches you to wait: weight balanced, sword at the perfect angle, eyes on the opponent’s centre. He’d been taught to wait by somebody who’d been taught by somebody who’d been taught to wait.

Alea stood in the near ring with Astra’s point on the sand and looked at him.

Xav watched her look at the whole thing.

He’d seen her do it a hundred times now. On the shingle, at dawn, at the lake, for six weeks: Alea and Petra and PenguChi and Xav in a loose ring round a man with a stick, all of them learning the same thing at different speeds. Stop looking at me. Look at the whole thing. Alea had been learning it for four years. Since she was thirteen. She’d been learning it from him longer than anyone alive.

And this week she’d started doing it without him.

Earlier. Not faster. Move while it’s still deciding to.

Xav saw her find it. He didn’t know what it was. Something in the cadet’s feet, or his breath, or the way his eyes stayed on her centre a fraction too long, the way a guard-school boy’s eyes always did, because he’d been taught to watch the centre and had never been taught that the centre was the last thing that moved.

She moved.

Not fast. Early. The black sword came up off the sand in its long easy line, like a rod lifting, like a cast going out over still water. And the cadet’s correct, exact, beautifully taught guard was exactly where he’d been taught to put it, and Astra came down on it from somewhere he hadn’t been taught to look, and let it fall.

The cadet’s sword went down into the sand.

One stroke.

“The bout to Alea, of Wrenhaven,” said the marshal.

The near stand went up. It had been going up for her all week; it had learned her. The fox girl. The lake girl with the door. Let it fall. People were shouting it now, from the stands. Let it fall! LET IT FALL! A man on the fourth tier of the near stand had made a banner overnight out of a bedsheet that said, in this world’s script, LET IT FALL, and was waving it.

Alea stood in the near ring and looked at the cadet’s sword in the sand. Then at the cadet, who was looking at it too, with a correct, exact, very bewildered face.

“I was watching your centre,” said Tamlin Brand.

“I know,” said Alea. “Everyone watches the centre.”

“We’re taught to.”

“I know,” said Alea. “That’s why I wasn’t there.” She looked at him, not unkindly. “Look at the whole thing. Next year.”

The cadet looked at her. Then he picked up his sword, and saluted her, fist to the chest, the way Garth of Ashby had, and walked off the ring with his back very straight.

Alea came over to the edge.

She stood in front of Xav and Petra with Astra on her shoulder and her breath coming hard and her ears up, and looked at Xav.

“That was the wait,” said Xav.

“I know.”

“His wait.” He nodded at the fourth tier. “The one I learned off him. On the shingle. That’s the one you just did.”

“I know,” said Alea. “I learned it off him first.” She grinned, suddenly, a flash of her mother’s face, the only one she had. “I was doing it before you’d ever heard of him.”

Xav looked at her. A seventeen-year-old fox girl with a door on her shoulder, Tempered, who’d learned to wait from a man on a jetty for four years before Xav ever came upriver, and who’d just done it, perfectly, on her own, in a semifinal, in front of the whole near stand.

He’d been the one in the corner. All week. He’d thought he was there to help her.

He’d been there to watch.

“Okay,” said Xav.

The far ring went a little after.

They heard it from the near ring. A roar from the west end of the Lists, and then laughter, and more roaring. When Xav looked, a noble boy in a very expensive coat was sitting in the sand outside the far ring holding his own ankle, looking astonished, and a coast girl with two boat-hooks over her shoulders was standing in the middle of the ring grinning at the sky like a gap in a fence.

“She hooked his ankle,” said Petra. “From the side. While he was looking at her other hook.”

“Of course she did,” said Alea.

The far stand was chanting something. Xav couldn’t make it out. Then he could. HOOKS. HOOKS. HOOKS.

Alea turned round and looked across the whole width of the Lists at the far ring.

Sorrel of Gullhaven looked back.

Two girls. One at each end of the great sand of the Lists, in the late morning sun, with the whole bowl roaring round them. One with a sword the size of a door on her shoulder. One with two boat-hooks on hers, like oars. Both of them fishermen’s daughters, from a lake and from a coast, both Tempered, both sixteen and seventeen, both of them people who let something else do the work: one who let her sword fall, and one who let you fall into her.

Sorrel lifted one hook. Just a little. Like somebody on a quay lifting a hand to a boat.

Alea lifted Astra back, an inch, off her shoulder. The way you’d lift a rod.

“The final of the Fledgling Lists,” said Lucan, from the master’s platform, in a voice that had given up entirely on being anything at all. “At noon. Alea, of Wrenhaven. Sorrel, of Gullhaven.”

The whole Lists went up.

Alea looked at Xav.

She didn’t say anything. She just looked at him, with her ears flat now, all the way flat, the way they’d gone flat before round five when she’d been more frightened than she’d ever been. The way her mother’s ears had gone flat, she’d told him, at the flap of an infirmary tent.

“I’m frightened,” said Alea.

Xav thought about what to say.

He could have said good. That was what her father said. He could have said count to three. That was what her mother did. He could have said a hundred things. He’d been in a hundred last rounds.

He said what he always said.

“Okay,” said Xav. “Again.”

Alea stared at him.

Then she laughed. Short and surprised, the laugh she’d laughed on the fourth tier when he’d said it’s nearly as annoying as Drew. And some of the flatness went out of her ears, not all, and she put Astra back on her shoulder, and went off toward the tunnel to wait for noon with Petra beside her.

Xav stood at the edge of the near ring on his own with his practice sword in his hand.

He looked up at the fourth tier.

The man at the end of the bench was looking down at him. Not at Alea. At him. Xav couldn’t see his face. He’d stopped minding, about two months ago, on a jetty, when a stranger with a different face had sung the third part of a song too loud and said Gabe. Deston. and then walked back along the boards and said Xav.

The man lifted his hand. Very slightly. The way you’d lift a hand in a call at the end of a round, to say nice.

Xav lifted his back.

Then he went to find Malcolm, who would be writing something in the notebook, and who would want to know, for his contingencies, what happens when two people who are both the same man meet in a final.

He’d tell him the truth.

The one who’s worse at waiting loses. And then he wins the arm-wrestling.
