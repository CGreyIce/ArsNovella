The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 9

Chapter 243

Offcut

5,709 words · 25 min read

Gabe had a piece of a star in his pocket, and he hadn’t told anybody.

It wasn’t much. It was about the size of the last joint of his thumb, a little wedge of grey-silver, dense as a sinker, with one face flat where the chisel had gone through it and the other faces rough. It had come off the prow on the fourth day at the lake, when the king held the blade in Gabe’s tongs and Declan struck and Gabe tapped the place with the king’s father’s little cross-peen and said there, and the steel had moved, and a sliver of the end that didn’t want to be part of the point had come away and dropped, hissing, into the ash at the side of the hearth.

Nobody had seen it go. They’d all been looking at the blade.

Gabe had seen it. He’d seen it because he’d been looking at the place he’d tapped, which was what you did. And that night, when everybody was asleep and the hearth was down to coals, he’d gone back with the tongs and raked through the ash at the side until he found it, and picked it up, and it had been cold. In a bed of hot ash. Cold as a well-bottom, the way the whole star had been when it came up the lake road on the dray: cold in his palm, and heavier than it should have been, and when he turned it to the coals he’d seen the frost in it. Very fine. A little branch of it running through the grey, the same as the frost all down the blade on the wall.

He’d put it in his pocket. He hadn’t decided to keep it. He just hadn’t been able to put it down.

It had been in his pocket for a month. Across the lake country and the downs and the grey grass and the red. At the grave. At Oakes’s table, at the twelfth dinner, while Salty stood at the end and watched Declan eat. It sat against his thigh, cold, all the time, through the cloth. He’d got used to it the way you get used to a stone in your boot that isn’t quite big enough to stop for.

He didn’t know what it wanted to be.

That was the thing. He’d always known. Every bit of iron he’d ever picked up at Harl’s forge in Kingsferry had told him, sooner or later, if he held it long enough, what it wanted to be: a hinge, a nail, a knife, a hook. Sto had told him on the first day. Rachael’s knife had told him before he’d even got it in the fire. The star had told the king’s father it didn’t want to be anything, and then lain under sailcloth for fifty-eight years, and then told the king it wanted to be his, and gone up a lake road on eight oxen to find out who his was.

The offcut didn’t say anything. It just sat in his pocket, cold, with frost in it, and waited.

They went out by the west gate on the fifth morning at Cinderhold.

Gabe walked second, behind Alea, where he’d walked all the way east, with his big sledge from Harl’s over his shoulder in its sling of sailcloth. He was looking at the road. He was always looking at the road; it was a habit from the forge, where you looked at the work and not at the people watching, because the people watching couldn’t burn you and the work could. So he didn’t see the arrow until the rise.

Nobody did. Alea stopped at the top of the rise and turned round, and Gabe stopped behind her, and she looked up, and he looked up with her.

There was an arrow over his head.

Hanging there. A pace above him. Blue at the tip, glowing very faintly, turning, slowly, the way a leaf turns on a still day. Going with him. He took a step sideways and it took a step sideways.

He looked at Alea. There was one over her too. And over Deston, behind him, and Xav, and all the way back down the road, over every one of them, a long line of small blue lights in the grey dawn, all the way back to the man with the water at the end. Ten.

“Wendy,” said Deston, very quietly. And then, louder, with his voice cracking in the middle the way it hadn’t since he was fourteen: “That’s Wendy. That’s counting. I can hear it. She’s—“ He turned round and looked back at the fort, at the west wall, where you could just see, if you knew where to look, a small dark shape among the twenty-five. “She’s counting us out.”

Alea put her fingertips together, and tapped them three times, and turned, and went over the rise.

Gabe went over it under his arrow. He didn’t look back again until he’d gone a little way down the far side, where the wall couldn’t see; and when he looked up, the arrow was going out. Gently. Like a candle somebody had decided to pinch. And ahead of him over Alea, and behind him over Deston and Xav, one after another, in order, all the way back, the others went too.

He stood on the road and felt something go out of the air over his head that he hadn’t known was there.

“Ten,” said Malcolm, behind him, looking at his book. “She held ten. For—“ He stopped. He looked at the page. He didn’t write anything on it. “I don’t have a number for how long.”

“Seven hundred heartbeats,” said Declan, from the back. “Give or take.”

They all looked at him.

“She counted,” said Declan. “I just listened to her count.” And settled the water skins on his shoulder, and came on down the road, and the girl at the front set the pace.

He thought about the offcut on the grey grass.

It was a good place to think, the grey grass. Nobody talked much. You couldn’t, quite; it whispered all round you, all day, in every direction, a long dry hush that Deston said was a choir of frightened people singing out of tune and that Gabe just heard as a sound like the sea a long way off. Except round Declan, at the back, where it went still. Alea let the pace down so that all of them walked in the ring of it, and inside the ring it was quiet, and you could think.

He thought about what the star had been. Not the sword; he’d been there for the sword. What it had been before. A long dark melted-looking stone the length of a man, with a plum bloom on it, that had come down out of the sky into a river at Brakewater when the king was eight and sat in a smith’s yard under sailcloth for fifty-eight years because it was asleep. That had woken up when a man with a fishing rod put his bare hand on the cold end of it in a hearth by a lake. It was waiting for him, Gabe had said, on the first day, without knowing he was going to say it. It didn’t know who for.

It knew now. It was on the wall of his cabin, on two iron pegs, over a drawing, waiting for the day he left the lake.

And a bit of it was in Gabe’s pocket. A bit that hadn’t wanted to be part of the point. That had come off on the fourth day and dropped into the ash, and been cold in the hot ash, and had frost in it.

He took it out on the grey grass and held it in his palm, walking, and looked at it.

“What’s that?” said Xav.

Xav was beside him. Xav was always beside somebody, on a road, without seeming to have moved there. He was looking at Gabe’s palm.

“Offcut,” said Gabe.

“From his?”

“From the prow. Fourth day. It came off when I said there.” Gabe turned it over. “Nobody saw. I picked it up out of the ash. It’s been in my pocket since.”

Xav looked at it for a long time.

“Does he know?”

“No.”

“Are you going to tell him?”

“I don’t know what it’s for yet,” said Gabe. “I don’t want to tell him I’ve got a bit of his sword in my pocket and I don’t know what it’s for.” He closed his hand on it. It was cold through his palm, all the way to the back of his hand. “It’ll tell me. They always tell me.”

“It’s not his sword,” said Xav.

Gabe looked at him.

“It came off,” said Xav. “You said. It didn’t want to be the point. It came off and fell in the ash.” He was looking at the road. “So it’s not his sword. It’s the bit of the star that didn’t want to be a sword.” He was quiet. “That’s a different thing.”

Gabe walked on with it in his hand for a long time.

The hail came on the downs.

They’d been on the downs a day. Green again, after the grey; long rolling green hills under a big sky, with sheep on them, and stone barns, and nobody. Petra said the hail was coming an hour before it came. She stopped on the road and looked west at a line of cloud on the horizon that was the colour of a bruise, and said “Hail,” and then nothing else, and walked on a little faster. Declan, at the back, looked at it too, and didn’t say anything, which Gabe had learned meant he agreed.

There wasn’t anywhere to go. That was the thing about the downs. No trees. The last barn was two miles back. The next was over a hill somewhere and Alea didn’t know how far.

It came in like a wall.

Gabe had seen hail on Earth. Little stuff, once or twice, that bounced off the car roofs and made the kids shriek. This wasn’t that. It came over the top of the down in front of them as a white line, roaring, the grass going flat under it as it came, and the sound of it was like gravel being poured out of the sky onto a tin roof the size of the world; and the first stones hit the road a hundred paces ahead and bounced as high as Gabe’s knee, and they were the size of his thumb.

“Down,” said Malcolm. “Everybody down, cover your heads, IF it’s bigger than—”

It was bigger.

Gabe dropped the sledge and turned round. He didn’t think about it. He was the biggest; he’d always been the biggest, at school, at the forge, in a field in Kingsferry with Calvin’s wind and Dennis’s illusions and Rachael’s hands. When something came at a group of people the biggest one turned round and put his back to it, and that was him. He turned, and put his arms out, and Alea was in front of him and Deston was beside him and he got his arms round both of them and bent over them with his back to the hail and felt the aura come up in him the way it did now, Drawn, the thing he called Wall because that was what it was: a hardness in the skin, a sort of weight, so that things that hit him hit stone.

The first stones hit his back. He felt them through the coat, hard, a dozen of them at once, like being struck all over with the round end of a hammer. They didn’t hurt, much. He was Drawn. They bounced off.

Then they stopped hitting him.

All at once. The roar didn’t stop. He could hear it, all round, louder than ever; he could hear it hitting the road beside his boots, and the grass, and the barn two miles back, probably. But not his back. He stood there bent over Alea and Deston with his arms round them and his eyes shut and waited for the next one, and it didn’t come.

He opened his eyes.

Something was over him.

Grey. Very thin. The colour of rain seen through a window. A long slanting sheet of it, going up from the road at the edge of the group, on the west side, the hail side, up over all ten of their heads, slanting, like the roof of a lean-to; and the hail was hitting it. Thousands of stones, the size of his thumb, coming down out of the sky in a white roar and hitting the grey a pace over his head with a sound like gravel on a drum, and bouncing off it, and running down it, and pouring off the low end into the grass on the east side in a long white heap.

Not one came through.

He looked along it. It went from one end of the group to the other. Over Alea and Deston and him, over Xav, over the four from Duskhollow crouched in the road with their hands over their heads, over Malcolm with his book clutched to his chest; over Declan at the back, who was standing up straight with the water skins on his shoulder and his face turned up toward the grey, looking at it.

And at the high end, on the west side, at the edge of the road, with her back to them and her face to the hail, Petra.

Standing. Feet under her shoulders. Weight even. Her sword out in both hands, held up, high, at an angle, the flat of it toward the sky; and the grey coming off the edge of it and going up and over all of them like a sail on a mast.

It lasted a long time.

Malcolm counted, afterwards. He’d counted under it, with his book against his chest and his lips moving, because Malcolm counted things when he was frightened the way Wendy counted things all the time. He said a hundred and forty heartbeats. The hail roared and roared and then thinned and then stopped, all at once, the way it had come, and the sun came out over the down behind it so bright it hurt, and the whole hillside round them was white, white as snow, a hand deep, steaming; and in the middle of it, a long slanting shadow on the road where nothing had fallen. Ten people in a strip of dry road under a grey roof.

Petra lowered her sword.

The grey went. From the high end down, the way frost goes off a window when the sun gets onto it. And the last few stones that had been lying on the top of it slid off and dropped onto the road, tick, tick, tick, and that was all.

She sat down.

On the road, in the hail, quite suddenly. The way she’d sat down on the edge of the ditch. With her sword across her knees and her hands on it, and her head down, breathing.

Nobody said anything.

Then Rui Xiang, who’d been crouched in the road under the low end of it with his arms over his head, stood up very slowly, and looked at the long white heap of hail beside him where it had poured off, knee deep, and at the strip of dry road, and at the woman sitting on it with her sword on her knees, and said, in a very small voice for him, “That was a roof.”

“It was a wall,” said Petra. Into her knees. “I tipped it.”

“You tipped a wall.”

“It’s a wall,” said Petra. “It’s in front of you. The hail was coming from above. So I put it in front of you, above.” She lifted her head. “It’s the same thing.”

Rui Xiang looked at her for a long moment. Then he sat down in the hail too, and put his face in his hands, and started laughing, and couldn’t stop.

Gabe let go of Alea and Deston.

He hadn’t noticed he was still holding them. He’d been bent over them for a hundred and forty heartbeats with his arms round them and his back to a hailstorm that had never reached him, and his arms had locked. Alea came out from under him with her ears flat and her hair wild and looked at him, and then at Petra, and then back at him.

“You did that for us,” said Alea. “You turned round.”

“Everybody turns round,” said Gabe.

“No,” said Alea. “They don’t. They get down. Malcolm said get down.” She looked at him with her ears coming up. “You turned round and put your back to it. With your arms out.”

He didn’t know what to say to that. He went over to Petra instead.

She was still sitting on the road with her sword across her knees. She’d stopped breathing hard. She looked up at him when his shadow fell on her, and her face was the way it always was, a soldier’s face, that you couldn’t read; but her eyes were very tired.

“How long was that?” said Petra.

“A hundred and forty. Malcolm says.”

“The ditch was a hundred and twelve.”

“I know.”

“That was bigger,” said Petra. “Than the ditch. And I made it on purpose. I saw the hail coming over the down and I thought, I’ll put a wall in front of them, and I did.” She looked at her hands on the sword. “I’ve never made it on purpose. Not once. It’s always come when I wasn’t trying. The ditch. The yard. The horse at Ashby.” She was quiet. “I tried. And it came.”

“What’s different?” said Gabe.

Petra thought about it. She was the kind of woman who thought about a question before she answered it, even when the answer was obvious; he’d liked that about her since the first summer.

“I wasn’t trying to make a wall,” she said. “I was trying to keep the hail off you.” She looked up at him. “All of you. Behind me. I was thinking about you, not the wall.” She paused. “When I think about the wall, it doesn’t come. When I think about who’s behind it, it does.”

Gabe didn’t say anything.

He stood there in the hail, in his coat, with the sledge lying on the road where he’d dropped it, and the offcut cold in his pocket against his thigh, and he thought about the thing he did. Wall. That was what he called it, because that was what it was: aura going into his skin, hardening him, so that things that hit him hit stone. He’d held a gate with it at the Lists for six glasses with Sto through the bracket. He’d turned round just now in the hail without thinking and put his back to it with his arms round two people, the way he’d turned round his whole life, because he was the biggest.

He’d always thought Wall was about him. About his skin. About being hard.

It wasn’t. Not when it mattered. When it mattered, it was about who was under his arms.

“I do that,” said Gabe slowly. “Turn round. Put my back to it. It’s what I’m for.” He looked down at Petra. “I’ve always thought it was about being hard.”

“It’s not,” said Petra. “It’s about being in front.”

“I know,” said Gabe. “I just found out.”

Petra looked at him for a long moment, the way she’d looked at Xav in the yard at Duskhollow the first morning.

“Then you’re nearly there,” she said.

He opened his window that night, by the fire, in a barn that turned out to be only a mile and a half on.

Ninety-six. It had been ninety-five since the Lists, since a final on the main sand at Valcrest against Niko, who’d been one tier below him and fought like three above; Gabe had won it by not moving, by standing in the middle of the sand while a spellblade’s edge rang off him like a bell off an anvil, and had come off the sand thinking that’s it, that’s what Wall’s for, being hard.

Ninety-six now. One point. For turning round in a hailstorm with his arms round two people and finding out that being hard wasn’t what it was for.

He didn’t mind it being one point. One point was honest. He’d spent a winter at Harl’s forge learning that you didn’t get a sword out of a bar in a day.

He closed the window and took the offcut out of his pocket and held it to the fire.

The frost in it moved.

He saw it. Very slightly. The little branch of it running through the grey, catching the firelight, shifting, the way the frost all down the blade on the wall had shifted when Declan put both hands on the grip. As if it had felt something. As if it had been listening, in his pocket, all day, to the hail on the drum of Petra’s wall and him saying it’s what I’m for, and had turned over in its sleep.

Gabe looked at it for a long time.

“What do you want to be?” he said. Very quietly. To his palm.

It didn’t say anything. It was a bit of iron.

He asked Alea, in the end.

Not on purpose. On the ninth day, on the downs, walking, she dropped back from the front to walk beside him for a while the way she did sometimes, when she was thinking and wanted somebody big next to her who wouldn’t talk. He didn’t talk. They walked. And after a mile she said, without looking at him, “You’ve got something in your pocket you keep holding.”

He’d forgotten she could hear things like that. Fox ears. She’d have heard him turn it over in his pocket a thousand times since the lake.

He took it out and showed her.

She looked at it in his palm for a long time. Her ears went forward and then flat and then forward again.

“That’s his,” she said. “That’s from the—“

“Offcut. Fourth day. Off the prow.” He closed his hand. “Nobody saw. I don’t know what it’s for. I’ve been carrying it a month waiting for it to tell me and it won’t.”

Alea walked beside him without saying anything for a while.

“What does he need,” she said, “that he’d never ask for?”

Gabe looked at her.

“That’s how you made mine,” said Alea. “Astra’s grip. You didn’t ask me what I wanted. You watched how I held a cup.” She looked at the road ahead, at her father’s back at the end of the line, a long way back, with the water. “And his. You watched him hold a cup. You didn’t ask.” She paused. “So don’t ask the iron. Watch him. What does he need that he’d never ask for?”

Gabe didn’t say anything. He looked back down the road at the man at the end with the water skins on his shoulder and the rod on the other.

The rod.

He’d looked at it every day for two years and never once looked at it. It was the rod. It was always there. Long and plain and grey with use, a fisherman’s rod, nothing, a thing a man carried. A cheap reel that turned. A line he’d seen go out across the Redmarch four hundred paces, John said, and across a lake to the far trees and through the clouds. And at the end of the line, a hook.

He’d seen the hook. Once, in the summer, on the jetty, when Declan was tying on. A small dull iron hook, ordinary, the kind you’d buy in a bundle of twenty from any chandler on any quay in the kingdom for a copper. Bent a little at the shank. Rusted a little at the eye. It had been the same hook, Alea said, since before she was born.

He’d cut a lake in half with that hook on the end of the line. He’d caught a horned thing a hundred paces off the Redmarch by the shoulder with it and reeled it in through the air. He’d never once, in all the years Alea could remember, changed it.

“His hook,” said Gabe.

Alea’s ears went all the way up.

“It’s older than me,” said Alea. “He’s never changed it. He says it’s a perfectly good hook.” She was quiet. “It’s a terrible hook, Gabe. It’s bent. I’ve told him. He won’t buy another one because he says the fish don’t mind.”

“The fish don’t mind,” said Gabe.

“I mind.”

Gabe opened his hand and looked at the offcut. A little wedge of grey-silver the size of the last joint of his thumb, cold, heavy, with frost in it. The bit of the star that hadn’t wanted to be the point of a sword.

It would be just enough. For a hook. A good one. A small one, not much bigger than the old one; you didn’t want a big hook for lake fish, you wanted a sharp one with a good eye and a barb that held. He could see it already, the way he’d seen Sto in the bar: the shank, the bend, the point. He’d have to draw it very fine. Star-iron didn’t want to be thin, Declan had said at the anvil, it wanted to be something you had to carry. But a hook wasn’t thin. A hook was the smallest thing in the world that had to hold the biggest thing.

And it would go on the end of his line. On the rod. The thing he took with him every time he left the lake and never left behind.

The sword was for the day he left the lake. The king had said so.

The hook would be for every other day.

Gabe closed his hand on it. It wasn’t cold any more. Or it was, and he’d stopped noticing, because his palm had gone cold with it.

“Harl’s,” he said. “When we get to Kingsferry. I’ll need Harl’s forge. And a very fine file.” He looked at Alea. “Don’t tell him.”

“I won’t.”

“He’ll know anyway.”

“He always knows anyway,” said Alea. “He won’t say. That’s how you’ll know he knows.” She looked at the offcut in his closed hand, and then up at him, with her ears forward, the way she’d looked at him on the shingle the morning he gave her Astra back with a new grip. “Make it say something.”

“Like Sto?”

“Like Sto,” said Alea. “On the shank. Small.” She thought. “He’ll never see it. It’ll be in the water. That’s why.”

They came into Ashby on the eleventh day.

Annis came out into the posting-house yard in her apron and looked at the ten of them, at the hail-cuts on Malcolm’s hands and the sunburn on everyone and the man at the back with the water, and said “You again,” and “Rooms,” and “Don’t let that one near the horses,” at Rui Xiang, who hadn’t done anything yet.

They had the big table. They ate. Declan sat down and ate, in the middle of the bench, between Deston and Azac, the way he’d sat down and eaten every night since PenguChi told him to. Gabe watched him do it. He’d watched him every night. It still did something to his chest, every time, the way it had done something to his chest the first night at the beech wood: a man who’d stood at the end of eleven tables and eaten alone out of the pot, sitting down in the middle of a bench with his bowl in both hands, close, and eating with the rest of them.

After, when the table had gone up and Annis had thrown them out of the yard so her boys could wash it, Gabe went out to the back of the posting-house where the forge was. Ashby had a little one, for the horses, a farrier’s hearth with a bellows and a battered anvil and a bucket; Annis’s boy did shoes on it. The fire was banked. Nobody was there.

He didn’t light it. He wasn’t going to make it here. It needed Harl’s, and the good files, and a week, and a winter’s worth of patience he only had at Harl’s.

He just wanted to look at it in the light of a forge.

He took the offcut out and held it in front of the banked coals, where the red light came through the grate. The frost in it caught the red and went faintly pink, branching, very fine.

He turned it. He looked at it from every side, the way the king had looked at the star on the dray, the way you look at a bar before you put it in the fire for the first time. Where the grain ran. Where the frost thickened. Where the shank would come out of it and where the bend would go and where, at the very end, very fine, the point.

And he saw where the letters would go.

Not Sto. Sto was his. That was Gabe’s word; it was on Gabe’s sword and on the hook he’d made for the fox girl before she went down the river that had caught a conger off the Gullhaven wall in front of Sorrel’s mother on the first cast, in the spring.

Something else.

He thought about it for a long time, standing at a banked farrier’s fire at the back of a posting-house in a town that had been the same for three hundred years, with a piece of a star in his hand. About Latin. About a man who’d put Latin on everything in his other life, in his statuses, his group names; on a black sword that was half a headstone now, ad astra per aspera; on Gabe’s own, audentes fortuna iuvat. About what you’d put on a hook that nobody would ever see, that would spend its whole life in the water at the end of a line, holding.

He didn’t know any Latin. Not really. He knew the two Declan had given him and the one on the sword and a few off the coins at Harl’s.

He knew one more. He’d seen it on a gravestone when he was eleven, and asked what it meant, and been told, and never forgotten it, and never once in his life had anything to put it on.

He closed his hand on the offcut, and put it back in his pocket, cold against his thigh, where it had been for a month.

He’d tell Declan the word when it was done. Or he wouldn’t. Alea was right: he’d never see it. It would be in the water. That was why.

He went back in. The posting-house was quiet. Most of them were asleep, in the long room upstairs with the beds in a row like a barracks; Deston, on his back, listening to something with his eyes shut and a small smile; Rui Xiang flat on his face; Petra, sitting up against the wall at the end with her sword across her knees, awake, the way she always was.

Declan wasn’t upstairs. He was in the yard, on the mounting block by the gate, with the rod across his knees, looking east the way they’d come.

Gabe went out and sat on the edge of the water trough beside him.

They didn’t say anything. They’d never needed to. Gabe had known him eleven years on Earth, from the time Gabe was thirteen and Declan was fifteen and too loud and kind to him when nobody else in the school was; and he’d sat beside him on a great many walls and kerbs and car bonnets at two in the morning not saying anything. And then he’d stood at the back of a funeral in a suit that didn’t fit and not said anything, and then three years of not having him to not say anything to.

He’d said one thing, the last night. On a call, at three in the morning, with Yan and Niko and Newish still on, talking about nothing for an hour, the way they did. Sleep, Declan. Seriously. And Declan had laughed and said okay and left the call and gone to sleep, and not woken up.

Gabe had thought about that every day for three years.

He didn’t think about it tonight. He sat on the edge of the trough beside a man on a mounting block with a rod across his knees, and the offcut cold in his pocket, and looked east with him at the dark over the downs.

“Good hail,” said Declan, after a while.

“Good wall.”

“Good wall,” Declan agreed. He was quiet. “You turned round.”

Gabe didn’t say anything.

“You always turn round,” said Declan. “At school. In the field at Kingsferry. At the Narrows. I watched you at the Lists. Everybody else gets down.” He was looking at the dark. “You turn round and put your back to it.”

“It’s what I’m for.”

“I know,” said Declan. “I’ve always known.” He turned the rod over on his knees, once, the way he did, so that the little reel caught the light from the posting-house windows and the line glinted, and at the end of it, hooked into the cork of the handle where he always hooked it when he was walking, a small dull iron hook, bent a little at the shank. “It’s a good thing to be for.”

Gabe looked at the hook for a long time.

It was a terrible hook. Alea was right. It was bent, and rusted at the eye, and it had cut a lake in half.

“Yeah,” said Gabe. “It is.”

He put his hand in his pocket, and closed it on a piece of a star, and didn’t take it out.
