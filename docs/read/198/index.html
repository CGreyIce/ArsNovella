The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 7: Myths

Chapter 198

First There

7,678 words · 33 min read

There and back. That was the whole race.

Ossie had made the rules up on the first morning, lying on his back in the mud of the yard with his freckles going red and his big feet in the air, wheezing. From the well to the north gate. Touch it; you had to touch it, flat hand on the wood, or it didn’t count. And back. First one to slap the well-stone was the winner, and the loser had to say you’re the fastest thing in Thornwall out loud, in front of whoever was watching, which was usually Hild, who never said anything, and the Joss lad from the farrier’s, who said everything.

Ossie had won most of them, at first. He’d been Drawn since he was sixteen, and Tiger had been a summoned boy with tiger ears at twenty percent who’d never once in his life been faster than anybody who was trying. He’d got there first, sometimes. He’d never once got back first. Ossie would come past him on the way home with his elbows going and his mouth wide open, laughing, and slap the well-stone, and lie down in the mud and say say it.

Then Tiger had written Go.

After that, Ossie never won again.

Not once. Every morning for the weeks there were left, Tiger got there first and got back first and stood over him at the well, and every morning Ossie lay in the mud and said you’re the fastest thing in Thornwall in a voice that meant it, and then, every single morning, sat up and wiped his face and said: “Tomorrow.”

He’d said it the morning Sergeant Dunmore’s ten rode out. Sitting in the mud with his hair full of it. Tomorrow.

There hadn’t been one.

Tiger’s own number had said ninety-nine since the thaw, and he knew what it was waiting for, the way you know which tooth is going to hurt. The same thing Go had been, the first time, only all the way. Not three paces. All of it. He’d tried it on the wall road every morning since the passes opened, and every morning he’d done three hundred paces in a blur and then his legs had gone to water from the hip down and he’d sat in the slush; and Hild had stood over him and said once a day, the way she had in the first year.

Tiger had never been up the north tower.

People thought it was because he was frightened. Bee thought so; she’d said so, to his face, in the first winter, the way Bee said things. You’re scared of what she’ll see. It wasn’t that. He wasn’t frightened of the rings. He’d seen them. He’d stood on the top of the pass at midnight in the first summer and looked down at them, all of them, hundreds, in their rings round the burned circle with their faces turned in, and something with Ossie’s face had called up to him from the bottom, Tiger! Come down! It’s warm down here!, and he’d have gone. He’d have gone down that mountain in the dark faster than anything had ever gone down a mountain, if Drew hadn’t put both hands on his chest.

He wasn’t frightened of the rings.

He just didn’t want to stand next to Hyvred while she looked.

Because she could see him. Ossie. In the third ring, eleven miles off, with his freckled face turned up toward the pass. She could see him every morning. She checked; she’d told Tiger she checked, once, the autumn after the pass, and then never mentioned it again, because she’d seen Tiger’s face. And Tiger couldn’t see him. Not from the tower. Not from anywhere. He had good eyes, tiger eyes, good in the dusk and good in the dark, but eleven miles was eleven miles. He’d stand at the top of the tower beside her and look north at a brown valley and a smudge in the middle of it and know that Ossie was in the smudge, standing there, and not be able to see.

He’d rather sit on the shed.

So he sat on the shed. On the ridge of the roof he’d built three times, that had stood two years now with nobody under it. With Ossie’s shirt on a nail inside, mended, and OSSIE’S in Bee’s chalk on the back wall. Facing north-east, because that was the way the shed faced, and he’d never once thought about turning round.

The morning after Drew stood in the road, there were no names.

Hyvred came down off the tower at the grey and went round the whole of Thornwall the way she had every morning since the thaw, every house and stable and kitchen, with her glasses up in her hair and her gold eye locked, and came back across the yard to the kitchen door where Drew was standing with his cup, and stopped.

Tiger was at the well. He saw her face.

“None,” said Hyvred.

Drew put his cup down on the step.

“None,” said Hyvred again. Her voice had gone very high and very strange. “Not one window. Not one, Drew. I went round twice. I went round the soldiers. I went round the chickens.” She laughed, and it came out wrong, half a sob. “It’s the first morning since the thaw. There’s nobody.”

And she put both her arms round Drew, which Hyvred did to everybody, all the time, loudly; only this time she did it quietly, with her horns knocking his ear, and held on, and Drew stood there in the kitchen door with his hands out to the sides and a face like a man who’d been handed a baby, and then put his arms round her too.

Tiger watched from the well. His tail was going. He couldn’t stop it.

None.

Joss held the horses for him, after.

He did every morning. He’d done it since the first spring, when he was nine and Tiger was new and the farrier had sent him across with a message and he’d stood in the stable door with his mouth open looking at Tiger’s ears. He was eleven now. Small for it. Brown as a nut, with a gap in his front teeth and a red wool scarf his mother had knitted him that he wore from the first frost to the last and slept in. He held the grey and the bay for the north patrol while Tiger checked their feet, and asked him about tigers.

He’d been asking about tigers since the first spring. He’d never run out.

“Do tigers swim?”

“Yes,” said Tiger. “Loads. They love it.”

“Do you?”

“Badly.”

Joss thought about this. “Do tigers climb trees?”

“Some do.”

“Can you?”

“I can climb anything,” said Tiger. “Getting down’s the problem.”

Joss laughed. The gap in his teeth. Tiger had the grey’s near fore up between his knees and was picking a stone out of it, and he looked up at the boy over the horse’s leg and thought, the way he’d thought every morning for a week, about a stable on the other side of the fort, and a boy standing in the door of it with his face lifted and smiling, and Hild coming from nowhere.

Joss didn’t remember it. Kei said most of them didn’t. He’d asked Tiger, the morning after, why everybody kept looking at him, and Tiger had said you had a funny turn, and Joss had said oh, and that had been that. All he said about it, ever, was that it had been warm.

“Is it true,” said Joss, “Drew sent it back?”

Tiger put the hoof down.

“Who told you that?”

“Everybody.” Joss shrugged. “Nessa at the chandler’s. She was there. She said he stood in the road and said not here and it went back up the valley like a ball.” He looked at Tiger with enormous seriousness. “Is it gone?”

Tiger looked at him. At the red scarf, and the gap in his teeth, and the frightened thing behind the serious face that he was being very brave about.

“Hyv says there’s nobody this morning,” said Tiger. “Nobody at all. First time since the thaw.”

Joss let his breath out.

“Good,” he said. And then, because he was eleven, and that was that: “The king’s coming to the farrier’s. This afternoon. Master Tull says. To make shoes.” He said it the way you’d say the king was coming to fly. “And I’m doing the bellows.”

He was, too.

Tiger went past the farrier’s in the middle of the afternoon, on his way to the wall, and stopped in the street.

The farrier’s forge at Thornwall was a low black shed at the bottom of the north lane, open at the front, with a horse-ring on the wall and a smell of hot iron and hoof that you could find with your eyes shut. Old Tull had shod the Western Watch’s horses for thirty years. He was a little bent leathery man with forearms like ropes and no patience at all, and he was standing now at the side of his own forge with his arms folded and his mouth slightly open, watching the king of Aurelan make a horseshoe.

The king had his coat off. His shirtsleeves rolled. He stood at Old Tull’s anvil with Old Tull’s hammer in his fist, a great grey man in a little black shed, and he was beating out a shoe from a bar of iron on the horn of the anvil with a light quick tapping sound like somebody knocking politely on a door, and talking to Tull without looking up, about the shape of a heel. And on the far side of the forge, at the bellows, with both hands on the handle and his whole small body going up and down with it, red scarf and all, his face lit orange by the fire and so happy it hurt to look at, was Joss.

“Steady,” said the king. Not looking up. “Steady, lad. Not faster. Steadier. You’re breathing for it. Breathe like you’re asleep.”

Joss breathed like he was asleep. The fire went white.

“Good,” said the king.

Tiger stood in the street and watched for a long time.

He’d hugged the king once. In Valcrest, in the yard of the Broken Lance, in front of everybody, so that Dennis had dropped his pen. It had been like hugging a mountain. A warm one. The king’s arms had gone round him very briefly and very hard, the way a smith’s would, and let go.

He thought about doing it again. He didn’t. The king was working.

He went on up to the wall.

He was on the shed when it happened.

The ridge of the roof, at dusk, with his knees up and his tail round his feet, facing north-east the way the shed faced. The Pollen Puffs asleep on the ridge-pole beside him. The yard going blue below. The smell of Cook’s supper coming across from the guardhouse, and the forge at the bottom of the north lane gone quiet at last, and the lamps coming on one by one in the town.

He heard the scream before he heard the words.

From the tower. Far up. The north tower, a hundred and twelve steps, that he’d never once been up; and a voice coming down off the top of it that he’d have known in two worlds. Hyvred. Not shouting. Screaming. The way she’d screamed down the stair on the fourth day, two of them, two.

“TIGER.”

He was on his feet on the ridge.

“TIGER. IT’S JOSS. HE’S OUT.” Her voice cracked in half. “HE’S IN THE VALLEY. HE’S IN THE VALLEY, TIGER, HE’S NEARLY AT THE—“

He didn’t hear the rest.

He was off the roof.

He’d find out later how.

The millstream. The little stream that came down off the east ridge and ran through the bottom of the town past the farrier’s and the laundry and went out under the north wall through an iron grating, into the valley. The bars of the grating had been there a hundred years. The ice had got into them this winter and bent two of them at the bottom, and nobody had noticed, because nobody at Thornwall could have got through the gap.

Nobody but a small boy of eleven. Who’d been sent home from the farrier’s for his tea, at the end of the afternoon, with a burn on his thumb and the king’s own hand on his head. Who’d been fine. Who’d had no window at all that morning, Hyvred said, nothing, not a crack, she’d looked, she’d looked at him specially, she’d looked at him in the stable with Tiger and he’d been fine. Who’d stopped on the way home, by the millstream, in the long low light. And lifted his face.

His mother thought he was still at the forge with the king; he’d have stayed there till midnight if anybody let him. Old Tull thought he’d gone home. Nobody missed him for three hours.

It hadn’t knocked, this time. It hadn’t opened a window a day early for anybody to see. It had come down the valley in the late afternoon, very quietly, the day after it had been sent back up it, and gone straight past every soldier on the wall and every name Hyvred might have looked for; and found the smallest door in Thornwall, and the smallest person who could fit through it.

And Hyvred had been looking at the pass.

She’d tell him that afterwards, with her hands over her face. She’d been watching the pass road. Every dusk, from the top of the tower, with her sight thrown eleven miles to the swept stretch at the foot of the pass and held there, narrow, the way you’d hold a lamp to one line of a page. She’d had her back to the town for an hour, watching the road; and before that she’d been down in the town, going round doors, with her back to the valley. And by the time she let her sight come back down the valley, out of habit, the way you’d glance back at a door you’d shut, there’d been a little frost of wrong light eight miles out on the valley floor. Small. Going north. Steadily. With its face lifted.

Eight miles.

He went through the yard.

Not round anything. Through it. Over the woodpile and across the top of the water-butt and past the kitchen door, where Cook dropped a pot, and past Drew, who was in the middle of the yard already, turning toward the tower with his face white. Tiger didn’t stop. He couldn’t have stopped. Something had come into his legs the moment Hyvred said Joss and it wasn’t going to let him.

The postern.

Old Pruett was in front of it, on his stool, with the bar across, the way he’d sat all day with his arms folded since Drew walked his twelve home, daring it. He saw Tiger coming across the yard and was on his feet with both hands on the bar before Tiger had got halfway. He couldn’t lift it. He’d lifted it yesterday, smiling, with a strength that wasn’t his; he couldn’t lift it today with his own. He got one end up out of its bracket, grunting, red in the face, and the bar swung down and hit the stones, and he dragged the postern open with his whole old weight hanging off the ring.

“Go on, lad,” said Old Pruett.

And Drew’s voice behind him. Across the yard. Not calling him back.

The one word. Tiger’s word. The one he’d written in the air in his first spring, sparring with Ossie, with Hild watching, before he’d known what it meant.

“Go.”

He went.

Eight miles. More.

Up the valley road in the last of the light. The sky still pale in the west over the shoulder of the Greyspine, and the valley floor already going dark under it, brown and black and silver with meltwater in every rut and hollow, and the cold coming down off the mountains with the dusk, and the wind in his face.

He’d never run like it.

He’d run every day of his life here. Round the walls till his legs gave out, every morning, the first spring. Up and down the wall stair. To the gate and back with Ossie. He’d run in the Gate trial at Valcrest in front of forty thousand people, round the end of the line faster than anybody could follow, and had heard them go oh all at once, the whole stand. He’d thought that was fast.

This wasn’t fast. This was something else. He wasn’t running; his legs were. He’d stopped being the one in charge of them somewhere around the postern. They went down the road under him, long and low, and the ground came up toward him in a grey blur and went away behind him, and the field walls went past, and then there weren’t any field walls, only the open valley going up into the dark, and the road down the middle of it, wet stone, shining a little in what light there was.

He didn’t think. He’d never been good at thinking. He was fast. That was all he was. That was all he’d ever been.

There, his legs said. There. There. There.

He saw the rings at two miles.

He’d seen them before, from the top of the pass, at midnight, from very high up and very far away: little points of wrong light round a black circle, like a drawing. He’d never seen them from the ground.

They came up out of the dusk at him like a town. That was the first thing. Like a town you’d see from the road at evening, a dark shape on the valley floor with the light going. Then not like a town. People. Standing. Hundreds of them. More than he could count. Standing in the meltwater, ankle-deep, black against the last light, all facing in toward the middle, toward the circle burned into the valley floor that he couldn’t see from here; ring after ring of them, round and round, like the rings in a cut log, like the rings a stone makes in a pond, perfectly still. Not one of them moving. Not one head turned. The wind went over them and moved their hair and their coats, and they didn’t move.

And beyond them, at the far end of the valley, at the foot of the pass, nothing.

Nothing he could see. Just the valley going on, and the mountains coming up out of it, and the throat of the pass. Hyvred said there was something there the size of a hill. He looked at the place where she said it was and saw the dusk and the mountains, and his skin went tight all the way down his back to the end of his tail, and he looked away.

And there, between him and the rings, on the open valley floor, off the road, in the meltwater, small, walking: a red scarf.

Joss was walking the way they all walked. He’d seen it on Corporal Dunn, and Mother Ivey, and on Drew at the shutters with his face lifted and nobody behind it. Steadily. Not hurrying. With his hands open at his sides and his face turned up and his small boots going into the water and out of it, splash, splash, splash, straight across the flooded field toward the outermost ring. As if he’d been called in for his tea.

Tiger was a mile off.

He ran.

Half a mile. The road went the wrong way, curving west round a low hump of ground, and he left it, and went across the fields after the red scarf, straight, through the water, through the mud, the cold of it coming up over his boots and soaking his trousers to the knee and not mattering, nothing mattering. A quarter. He could see Joss properly now. The back of his head. The scarf. The burn on his thumb, wrapped in a bit of rag, that the king had wrapped. He was singing. Tiger could hear it. A little tuneless song, very quiet, the way a child sings to himself when he’s walking somewhere he wants to go.

Three hundred paces.

And the ring. The outermost one. Close now. Close enough to see faces. A man in a green coat, a good one, a grain factor’s coat. A man in a broad hat. An old woman in a black widow’s shawl, with her stick. A boy in a cap. Standing in the water with their backs to him. Shoulder to shoulder, nearly. Not quite. There were gaps between them. Narrow ones. Lined up, ring behind ring, all the way in, the way spokes are lined up in a wheel.

Joss was walking toward one of the gaps.

Two hundred paces. Tiger was at the end. He knew it. He’d been at the end of his legs before, a hundred times, at the top of the wall stair in the first spring with his lungs on fire; he knew exactly what the end felt like. It felt like this. Like running in a dream. His legs were still going and the ground wasn’t coming any faster.

A hundred and fifty. Joss was forty paces from the ring.

He wasn’t going to make it.

He saw it the way you see the end of a race you’ve lost. Very clearly. All at once. The red scarf going into the gap between the green coat and the hat. The boy taking his place, somewhere in there, small, with his face turned up, the way the thirty-nine had walked down the pass in the autumn one at a time and taken theirs. Standing there. Every morning. In the water. Facing in. And Hyvred on the tower, checking, every morning, for the rest of her life. And Tiger, on the shed, facing north-east.

Two.

Two faces he couldn’t see.

He didn’t decide it.

He’d think afterwards that he should have. That there should have been a moment, like Drew had said there’d been in the road: a moment where he thought of something, or said something, a word, the way Thea had said you had to. Write it. One small line. There wasn’t. He wasn’t a writer. He’d never been a writer. He was fast.

His hands went to his belt on their own. Both of them. And came out with the short blades.

The pair he’d been given in the first month, the ones Hild had taught him with, because aura needed a real blade below the seventh tier and you couldn’t put it into your legs without something in your hands for it to come out through. He’d never understood that. He understood it now. He felt it come up out of the middle of him and go down, not into the blades, past the blades, through them, the way water goes through a wheel; down, into his legs, all of it, all at once, everything he had and some he didn’t.

And he thought, not in words: there and back.

Touch it. You have to touch it, or it doesn’t count.

And then back.

He was there.

He didn’t cross the hundred and fifty paces. He’d swear to that for the rest of his life. He was a hundred and fifty paces off with his blades in his hands and his legs gone, and then he was there, at the edge of the outermost ring, in the water, with the green coat’s back so close in front of him he could see the stitching on it, and Joss’s small boot lifting to step into the gap.

He got him round the middle.

One arm. The left, with the blade still in the fist of it, held out flat so it wouldn’t touch him. He got Joss round the middle the way he’d have got a falling cup and lifted him clean off the ground, red scarf and all, and the boy didn’t fight, didn’t even seem to notice, just hung there over his arm with his face still turned up toward the pass, smiling, singing.

And for one heartbeat, Tiger was standing at the edge of the rings.

He’d remember it. All of it. All his life. The cold of the water round his ankles. The smell: nothing. That was the worst thing. Hundreds of people a pace away and they didn’t smell of anything, not sweat or wool or skin, nothing; Hisui would’ve said it tasted of nothing. The green coat in front of him, so close. The hat. The old woman’s stick in the water. Not one of them turning round. Not one of them knowing he was there.

And the gap. The one Joss had been stepping into. Lined up with the next gap, in the next ring, and the next, and the next, all the way in, like spokes, like looking down a long corridor of people. Ring after ring after ring. Faces, in the dusk, all turned the same way, up, toward the pass.

And there. Ten rings in. Small and far, down the corridor of gaps, in the third ring from the middle. A freckled face. Turned up.

Os.

Something said his name.

Very kindly. From behind the rings. From the place at the bottom of the pass where Hyvred said there was nothing. A warm voice, from a lit doorway, saying oh, there you are, Tiger, the way Drew had said it said it, come in, come in out of the cold—

He was already going.

That was the thing he’d tell Drew. On the roof. Later. It asked me in, and I couldn’t answer, because I was already going. You couldn’t call something back that had already gone. You could only call something that was standing still.

Back.

Straight back. The same line. He didn’t turn round; you couldn’t, not like this, he’d find that out later, it only went straight, the way a thrown thing goes. Back along the line he’d come in on, with the boy over his arm, out of the gap, out of the edge of the rings, back across the water, the hundred and fifty paces, and more, all at once—

And stopped.

His legs went.

Not slowly. All at once. Like somebody had cut the strings. He went down on his knees in the meltwater a hundred and fifty paces from the rings with Joss in his arms, and the cold went up him to the waist, and he didn’t care. He held on to the boy. His legs were shaking. Not trembling; shaking, hard, the whole length of them, from his hips to his heels, the way they’d shaken at the top of the wall stair in the first spring after his twentieth time up, only worse, much worse, so bad he could hear his own knees knocking in the water.

The short blades were still in his fists. He couldn’t open his hands.

He knelt there, and breathed, and held on.

And there was a window in front of him.

[ Tiger · Tier 1: Drawn · Skills: Bolt ]

He looked at it for a long time. His eyes wouldn’t work properly. Bolt.

He didn’t think about it. He couldn’t. He’d think about it later. He’d think about it on the roof, and in the bunk room, and every morning for the rest of his life when he went down to the well; but now, kneeling in the water with his legs going and a boy in his arms, the only thing in his head was the thing Ossie had said, lying in the mud.

Say it.

“You’re the fastest thing in Thornwall,” said Tiger. Out loud. To nobody. His voice came out of him in pieces. “You’re the fastest thing in—“

He stopped.

He’d turned his head. Not his body; he couldn’t have moved his body. Just his head, over his shoulder, back toward the rings. Out of habit. The way Ossie used to look back over his shoulder at the gate, every morning, running home, to see how far behind he was.

The gap was still there. Lined up. Ring after ring after ring, in the last of the dusk, a long dark corridor of people facing in.

And at the end of it, small and far, in the third ring. The freckled face.

It had been turned up toward the pass. For a year and a half. Every morning. Hyvred had checked.

It wasn’t now.

It had turned. Not much. Hardly at all. If he’d been anyone else, if he’d had anyone else’s eyes, if it had been a hair darker, he’d never have seen it. A very small turn, the smallest there could be, the turn of a head toward a sound behind it. Not up at the pass. Not in at the middle.

After him.

The way you’d look sideways, at the start of a race, at the one beside you.

“Os,” said Tiger.

The face didn’t move again. The wind went over the rings. Nobody else turned. Nobody else had ever turned.

He knelt in the water and looked at it till the dark came down and he couldn’t see it any more.

Hild got there first.

Of course she did. On the grey, the north patrol’s grey that Joss had held that morning, with no saddle, at a flat gallop up the valley road in the dark; and behind her on the grey’s back, holding on round her middle with his eyes shut and the satchel bouncing on his shoulder and Hisui flat to his neck, Kei. Then Brannoch, and four riders, with torches; and up behind the last of them, holding on round the rider’s middle with his eyes shut, because he’d never once in his life been on a galloping horse, Drew.

Hild came off the grey before it stopped. She came across the water to him in three strides and knelt in it beside him with her one eye open, and put her hand flat on the boy’s chest, and then on Tiger’s.

“Breathing,” said Hild. “Both.”

Kei was there. On his knees in the water too, with the satchel open, a flask out, green thread. He held it to Joss’s mouth. Joss was still smiling. Still singing, very quietly, the little tuneless song. His face still turned up toward the pass, over Tiger’s arm, in the dark.

“Drink this,” said Kei. “Please. It’s for you.”

A body swallows when there’s something in its mouth.

Tiger watched it go in. He couldn’t see the frost; he never could. He watched the boy’s face instead. And after a while, a long while, the singing stopped. And the face came down, slowly, out of wherever it had been. And blinked. And looked up at Tiger from very close, in the torchlight, with the gap in his teeth.

“Tiger?” said Joss.

“Hey,” said Tiger.

“Why’m I wet?”

“You went for a walk.”

Joss thought about this. He looked round, slowly. At the dark. At the water. At Hild, and Kei, and the torches, and the horses.

“It was warm,” said Joss. Puzzled. “It was so warm.” He looked up at Tiger. “Did you run?”

“Yeah.”

“Fast?”

“Yeah.”

Joss looked at him for a long moment, in the torchlight, with enormous seriousness.

“Faster than you used to race Ossie?” said Joss.

Tiger opened his mouth.

He’d forgotten. That Joss had been there. Nine years old, in the door of the farrier’s, every morning, the first spring, watching them go past. That Joss had been the one who said everything. Ossie, he’s beating you, Ossie, RUN.

“Yeah,” said Tiger. “Way faster.”

“Ossie’d be cross,” said Joss, and yawned, enormously, and put his face in Tiger’s teal coat and went to sleep.

Tiger knelt there in the water with the boy asleep on him and Kei’s hand on his shoulder and Hild’s on his back, and didn’t say anything at all, because if he’d said anything it would’ve been he turned round, and he wasn’t ready.

Drew got there at last.

He came off the back of the horse at the road and across the field on foot, slow, so slow, splashing, with the torches behind him, and stopped, wheezing, bent over, his hands on his knees, and stood there in the water a pace off and looked at Tiger for a long time. At the boy. At the short blades, still in Tiger’s fists. At Tiger’s legs, still shaking, so you could see it through the wet cloth.

Then he looked up. At the window. It was still there. Tiger hadn’t shut it. He didn’t know how; his hands wouldn’t open.

Drew read it.

“Bolt,” said Drew.

“Yeah.”

“Did you get there first?”

Tiger looked at him.

“Yeah,” said Tiger. And then, because it was Drew, and he’d known him twelve years, and there wasn’t anything you couldn’t say: “And back. I got back first.”

Drew sat down in the water.

Just sat down, in his good coat, up to his waist in the cold meltwater of the valley floor, a hundred and fifty paces from the rings, beside him. And put his arm round him. And didn’t say not on your own. He’d said go.

“Thirty-four,” said Drew.

They carried him back on the grey.

Tiger couldn’t walk. He tried, and his legs folded under him like wet paper, and Hild picked him up without a word, the way you’d pick up a sack of grain, and put him on the grey’s back in front of Kei with the boy in his arms. Kei held them both on. Hisui, round Kei’s neck, said something that made Kei laugh, once, shakily.

“What’d he say?” said Tiger.

“He says you smell like a wet dog.”

“Tiger.”

“He says he knows. He says it’s worse.”

They went back down the valley road in the dark, with the torches and the horses and Drew walking at the grey’s head with his hand on its bridle, and the rings behind them, and nobody looking back. Tiger didn’t look back. He’d looked back once. He’d seen what he’d seen. He held on to the boy.

The postern was open when they got there. Old Pruett was standing in it with a lantern held up over his head, and the whole of Thornwall behind him, it felt like. Soldiers. Bee, with chalk on her hands and her face wet, who said “You idiot,” and then didn’t say anything else. Hyvred, who’d come down the hundred and twelve steps and run the length of the north lane and was standing by the postern with her glasses in her hair and both hands over her mouth. Corvin. Fenwick, without a report, for once. Old Tull, with his rope arms folded, and his face working.

And the king.

He stood just inside the postern. In his shirtsleeves still, from the forge. With Joss’s mother beside him, a small fierce woman in a dyer’s apron, blue to the elbow, who’d been holding on to the king’s arm, Tiger found out later, for the last three hours, without either of them seeming to notice.

She took the boy off the grey. She didn’t say anything. She took him in both arms, red scarf and wet boots and all, and held him against her, and turned and walked off up the lane with him toward home without a word to anybody, the way a cat carries a kitten; and Joss, half asleep, said over her shoulder, “Mam, Tiger ran,” and she said, “I know, love, I know he did,” and then they were gone.

The king looked up at Tiger on the grey.

Tiger looked down at him.

“Bolt,” said the king. He’d read the window too. Everybody had. Tiger still couldn’t shut it.

“Yes, Majesty.”

The king nodded slowly.

“I was on the tower,” said the king. “Your friend with the horns called it for me, every step. And at the end I felt it go. I could feel that much.” His voice was very quiet. “I’ve seen a great many men run, son. I’ve never seen anything go like that.” He was quiet. “There and back.”

“There and back,” said Tiger. His voice went. “That was the rules.”

The king didn’t ask whose rules. He just looked at him, for a long moment, the way he looked at things he was going to remember.

Then he said: “Somebody get this lad off that horse and into something dry before he falls off it,” in quite a different voice, and Hild did.

He couldn’t walk till the small hours.

He sat in a blanket by the fire in the long room, the green room, Drew called it now, for reasons Tiger didn’t understand, with his legs out in front of him on a bench and Cook’s soup in a bowl and everybody coming in and out to look at him. Bee sat on the end of his bench and drew a tiger on the hearthstone in one line without lifting the chalk, and it got up and ran three times round the room so fast you couldn’t see it and then lay down on his feet, and Bee said, “Don’t,” and he didn’t, though he wanted to. Hyvred came and sat on the floor beside him with her back against the bench and didn’t say anything, for a long time, which for Hyvred was a very long time.

“I was looking at the road,” she said at last.

“I know.”

“I had my back to the town. For an hour. Watching the road. I’ve watched that road every dusk for a week and it’s never done anything, it’s just swept, and I had my back to the town, and he—“

“Hyv,” said Tiger. “He’s home.”

She put her face on her knees.

“He had no window,” she said, muffled. “Tiger. He had no window this morning. I looked. I looked at him specially. It didn’t knock.” She was quiet. “It didn’t knock.”

Tiger didn’t say anything. He didn’t know what to say. He wasn’t Drew. He put his hand on the top of her head, between her horns, and left it there.

He didn’t tell her about the third ring.

He thought about it. Sitting there with his hand on her head. She’d see it herself, in the morning, from the tower. She saw everything. Or she wouldn’t, because she’d be looking at the road, or at the absence, or at the place where the gap was, and a face turned the width of a hair wasn’t something you’d see if you weren’t looking for it. Only he’d been looking for it. For a year and a half. Without being able to see.

He’d tell her. Not tonight.

In the small hours his legs came back, mostly, and he went out to his gate.

He couldn’t have said why. He’d sat on the shed every night for a year and a half; he went to the gate instead. The south gate. His gate. Across the yard and down the lane to the south wall, slowly, holding on to things, with the blanket round him, and climbed up the fighting step one stair at a time, and sat on the top of it with his legs over, where he’d sat to watch the king come up the road.

Two oak leaves. Posts as thick as a man. A fighting step. He’d built it first in Valcrest, for the Lists, and it had held the Gate trial against the champions with Gabe behind it, and then afterwards it had fallen over on its own in the night, the way everything he’d ever built had fallen over. And he’d come home and built it again, here, when the old south gate warped, because he couldn’t think what else to do with his hands. That was two seasons ago. It hadn’t fallen over yet. He kept waiting for it to.

He sat on top of it and looked south, at the downs, where the road went away toward the rest of the kingdom, dark under the stars.

Somebody was coming down the lane.

He knew who it was by the way the cobbles didn’t notice. The king, in his coat now, with no lantern, walking very quietly for a man so big, the ninth tier held in round him so carefully that the cats on the wall didn’t even get up. He stopped at the foot of the gate and looked up at Tiger on top of it. And then, for a long time, not at Tiger. At the gate.

He put his hand on the post. Flat. The way he’d have put his hand on a bar of iron to feel if it was cool.

“Who built this?” said the king.

“Me,” said Tiger.

The king went on looking at it. He walked along it, the whole length, slowly, with his hand on the wood. He looked at the joints. He crouched, with a sound in his knees, and looked at the foot of the post where it went into the ground. He looked at the hinges, which were the only bit of the Valcrest gate that hadn’t fallen over, and which Tiger had carried home in his pack, three weeks, because he couldn’t leave them.

“Good gate,” said the king.

Tiger laughed. It came out of him like a cough.

“Everything I build falls down,” he said. “Majesty. Everything. My whole life. Even at home. I built a house once, a whole house, and something came and blew the whole—“ He stopped. He didn’t know how to explain it to the king of Aurelan. “Everything I build falls down. The first shed. The gate at the Lists. It fell over the same night. The shed here fell down twice. Ossie’s shed.” His voice went. “This one’s going to fall down too. I keep waiting.”

The king didn’t say anything for a while.

“My father,” he said at last, “used to say a wall’s for keeping out.” He patted the post. “Anybody can build a wall. Stack it high enough and it’ll keep out whatever you like, till it falls on you. But a gate’s a different thing. A gate’s for letting your own back in.” He looked up at Tiger. “That’s all a gate’s for. You go out of it, and you come back through it, and it stands there in between, holding the space open. Doesn’t matter how long it stands. It only has to stand long enough for whoever went out to come home.”

Tiger didn’t say anything.

“You went out tonight,” said the king. “And you came back. With the lad.” He took his hand off the post. “There and back. That’s a gate, son. You’ve been building the same thing all your life. You just didn’t know it had to have somebody go through it.”

He stood there a moment longer, looking up.

Then he said, “Bed,” the way he’d said it to Hyvred, and went back up the lane in the dark, and the cobbles didn’t notice, and the cats didn’t get up.

Tiger sat on the gate a long time after that.

He looked south, at the road. Then he did a thing he’d never done, in two years and a half, on the shed or the gate or anywhere.

He turned round.

North. Over the roofs of Thornwall, over the north wall, past the dark bulk of the tower he’d never been up. At the valley. At the dark, where the rings were, eleven miles of it, that he couldn’t see. At the place in the dark where a freckled face in the third ring had turned the width of a hair, after him, the way you’d look sideways at the start of a race at the one beside you.

He didn’t know what it meant. He didn’t know if it meant anything. He didn’t know if it was Ossie, or the thing that had Ossie’s face, or something in between, or nothing, the wind, the dusk, his own eyes wanting it so much they’d made it up. He’d ask Hyvred. In the morning. When he was ready. He’d go up the tower with her, all hundred and twelve steps, and stand beside her while she looked, and ask.

But he’d seen it.

He sat on his gate, that was standing, and looked north into the dark, and said the thing Ossie had said every single morning of the first spring, sitting up in the mud with his hair full of it, the morning he’d lost.

“Tomorrow.”
