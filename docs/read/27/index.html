The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 1: Thirty-Four Winters

Chapter 27

Night Market

2,927 words · 13 min read

Kingsferry held its midwinter market on the longest night of the year, on the quay, and Deston had been dreading it for a week.

Not because of the market. The market was wonderful. Everyone said so: stalls from one end of the waterfront to the other, strung with lanterns and green branches, selling hot cider and roast chestnuts and ribbons and knives and little carved boats for children to float on the black water with candles inside. There were musicians on the steps of the customs house. There were jugglers and fire-eaters and a dancing bear that Calvin was already determined to see. Half the river came. Bargemen from upstream, farmers from the hills, merchants from the coast. Thousands of people, all crowded onto one long strip of stone between the warehouses and the water.

That was what Deston was dreading. Thousands of people. Thousands of faces. And every one of them, for him, wrapped in threads.

He’d been looking for six weeks now. Every face, in every street, every day, since the stranger in the good coat had stood in the infirmary turning his hat round by the brim with nothing in the whole world tied to him. He’d got good at it. He could stand on a street corner and let his eyes go soft and see the threads come up out of a crowd like light through water, and sweep across them, and find the gaps. The places where a person was standing and the light didn’t reach them.

He hadn’t found one. Not since the infirmary.

He wasn’t sure that was good news.

“We’re going,” said Rachael.

“I know we’re going,” said Deston.

“I’m saying it so you don’t try to talk me out of it.” She was sitting on the edge of the table in their rooms above the chandler’s, pulling on her boots. Gabe’s knife was at her belt, in a sheath Sabine had found for it. “It’s the midwinter market. Sister Maud says the whole infirmary goes every year. They set up a booth for people who get hurt.” She looked up. “I’m volunteering at the booth.”

“Rae—“

“With all of you there. And Sabine. And Emrys.” She stood up. “I’ve been inside for six weeks, Dest. Infirmary and home. Infirmary and home. With someone walking me both ways like I’m made of glass.” Her voice softened. “I know why. I’m grateful. But I’m going to the market.”

Deston looked at her for a long time. Then he looked at the thread between them: bright and gold and steady, thicker than it had been in the autumn, much thicker than on the first day in the hall. It was the thickest it had ever been, in fact. Six weeks of walking someone home every night did that.

“Okay,” he said.

They went as a pack.

That was Sabine’s word for it, and it was the right one. Rachael in the middle. Deston on her left, Gabe on her right, close enough to touch. Calvin a few steps ahead, weaving through the crowd, ostensibly looking for the dancing bear, actually watching everything. Dennis a few steps behind, with his hands in his pockets and his face pleasant, listening to everyone who passed. And Sabine and Emrys behind him, Sabine with her sword at her hip under her cloak, Emrys humming something under his breath that Deston could almost feel, like a low vibration in his teeth.

The market was everything everyone had said. The noise of it, the light: lanterns everywhere, strung between the stalls and hung from the masts of the barges and floating on the black water in their little paper boats. The smell of cider and smoke and roasting meat. Children shrieking. Somewhere a fiddle, and somewhere else a drum, playing two different songs that somehow fitted together.

Rachael’s booth was at the far end, by the customs house steps: a trestle table with a white cloth and a basket of bandages, with Sister Maud presiding and Brother Tamsin nervously folding linen. They got her there without incident. She took her place behind the table, and the pack spread out round her in a loose ring, close enough to see, far enough not to be obvious.

And Deston let his eyes go soft, and looked.

The threads came up out of the market like a storm.

He’d never seen so many. Thousands of people in a few hundred paces of quay, and every one of them tied to every other one they’d ever met, and to every one they’d brushed past tonight. Grey threads by the hundred, so fine and so many they were almost a fog. Gold ones running between families, between couples, between children and their parents, tugging and stretching as the children ran off and the parents called them back. A thick gold rope between two old bargemen arguing at a cider stall who’d clearly been friends for fifty years. A brand-new thread, bright and thin and shy, between a boy and a girl by the water, both pretending not to look at each other.

It was the most beautiful thing he’d ever seen. He had to hold onto the edge of Rachael’s table to stay upright.

And then he saw the gap.

It was by a ribbon stall, thirty paces off, near the edge of the water. A man in a brown coat, looking at ribbons. Ordinary. Middle-aged. Buying something for his wife, perhaps.

And standing in the middle of the storm of light like a stone in a river, with every thread in the market flowing round him and not one of them touching him at all.

Deston’s breath stopped.

He made himself keep looking. Made himself sweep on across the crowd, the way he’d practised, not fixing on the man, not letting him know. And there, by the cider stall, near the old arguing bargemen: another one. A woman in a shawl, smiling at nothing. No threads.

And there, by the steps of the customs house, not ten paces from Rachael’s table, leaning against the wall with his hat in his hands, turning it round by the brim: a man in a good dark coat, with a neat grey beard.

Three of them. In a triangle. With Rachael’s booth in the middle.

“Gabe,” said Deston, very quietly. His voice came out almost normal. “Three. Ribbon stall, cider stall, customs steps. The one on the steps is him.”

Gabe didn’t look round. He just shifted, very slightly, so that he was between Rachael and the steps. “Got it.”

Deston lifted his hand to scratch the back of his neck: the signal Sabine had made them practise. Two fingers. Trouble.

Behind them, he heard Sabine stop talking. He heard Emrys stop humming.

Then Emrys started humming again, a different note, lower and steadier, and the hair on Deston’s arms stood up.

It happened very fast, and later none of them could agree on the order.

Calvin said it started with the wind. He was the one furthest forward, by the water’s edge, and he said that suddenly, for no reason, he felt the wind turn: not the real wind, but the other one, the one he’d been feeling for months before it happened. A shift in the air, like a door opening in another room. He turned round, and the woman in the shawl was walking toward Rachael’s table, quite slowly, still smiling at nothing, and the air around her felt wrong, he said. Like the air in a cellar.

Dennis said it started with a lie. The man in the brown coat had left the ribbon stall and was coming through the crowd, and he passed right by Dennis, and as he passed he said “Excuse me” in a perfectly pleasant voice, and Dennis heard it and went cold all over. “It was the lie,” he said afterwards. “Two words. Excuse me. And every part of it was a lie. Like hearing a bell that’s cracked all the way through.”

Deston knew it started with the stranger on the steps putting his hat on.

He did it slowly. Deliberately. Settled it on his head and tugged the brim, the way a man does before he goes out into weather. And then he came down the steps, smiling, toward Rachael’s booth, and said in his warm, courteous, slightly accented voice:

“Forgive me. I don’t think we finished our conversation.”

Rachael, behind the table, went white. Her hand went to Gabe’s knife.

Sister Maud stood up. “This booth is for the injured. Unless you’re bleeding, sir, you can—“

“Oh, I’m not here for the booth,” said the stranger. “I’m here for her.”

And the three of them moved at once.

Gabe went for the stranger. He didn’t think about it. Deston saw him not think about it. He just stepped round the table and into the stranger’s path, big and solid and planted, the way Azac had stood in front of the thing in the Hollow, and the stranger walked into him and pushed.

Gabe went backwards like he’d been hit by a cart. He crashed into the table, and the bandages went everywhere, and Sister Maud shouted, and the crowd nearest them screamed and scattered.

And Sabine came over the wreck of the table with her sword out.

Deston had never seen her fight. She’d never needed to. He’d known she was Reaching tier the way you know a fact about someone, without feeling it. Now he felt it. The light came up off her blade, faint and shimmering, and ran a hand’s width past the edge. She swung it at the stranger, low and fast, not at his body but at his legs. He stepped back, too fast, much too fast for a man, and the light caught the hem of his good coat and sliced it clean through.

He looked down at the cut, and then up at Sabine, and stopped smiling.

Behind Deston, the woman in the shawl had reached the edge of the scattered crowd, and Emrys sang.

It wasn’t a song. It was one note, the low steady one he’d been humming, suddenly thrown out at full voice. And something came with it. Light. A great white bloom of it, like a lantern going off in your face, right in front of the woman in the shawl. She flinched back with her arm over her eyes, and made a sound that was not a sound a woman makes.

“Torch,” Emrys gasped, staggering. “That’s… my whole… that’s the whole of it. That’s everything I’ve got.” His face was grey. “Somebody else do something.”

The man in the brown coat was coming round the other side.

Calvin saw him first. Calvin, who’d been feeling the wind turn for months, and who felt it turn now, behind Rachael, where nobody was looking. He shouted something wordless and threw himself sideways, and caught the man in the brown coat round the knees, and they both went down on the cobbles in a tangle.

The man in the brown coat came up first. He reached for Rachael.

Rachael cut him.

She didn’t hesitate. Deston would remember that for the rest of his life. She didn’t stop, or think, or freeze. She had Gabe’s knife out, and the man’s hand came at her, and she cut it, across the back, the way she’d been taught to cut a bandage. Fast and neat and without fuss.

The man looked at his hand.

It wasn’t bleeding. Or it was, but not red. Something thick and black was welling up out of the cut, slow and steaming in the cold. And the skin around the cut was moving. Just slightly. Shifting, like the surface of water when something swims underneath.

He looked at Rachael, and for a moment his face wasn’t a face at all.

Then the city watch arrived.

They came running down the quay in a clatter of boots, a dozen of them, with halberds and torches, shouting. They’d heard the screaming, and seen Emrys’s light, and somebody had run to the watch-house. The crowd parted for them in a great surge, and in the surge, in the confusion, in the scatter of torchlight and running people and the shouting of the watch-captain, the three of them turned.

All at once, like birds turning in the air.

And went into the river.

Deston saw it. The man in the brown coat, and the woman in the shawl, and the stranger in the good dark coat with the cut hem: three paces to the edge of the quay, and over, into the black water among the little floating candle-boats, without a splash. Without a sound. The candles bobbed. The ripples spread. And then there was nothing.

The watch-captain ran to the edge with a torch and held it out over the water. The light moved on the surface. Nothing came up.

“Gone,” he said blankly. “They’re… they’ve gone. Nobody can swim in that. It’s freezing. They’ll drown.”

“They won’t,” said Deston.

The watch-captain looked at him. Deston didn’t explain.

It was Dennis who found the coat.

On the edge of the quay, by the steps, where the stranger had gone over. A good dark coat, with the hem sliced through. And on top of it, set down neatly, as if someone had taken it off and placed it there on purpose: a hat.

The coat was empty. The hat was empty. Dennis picked them up and turned them over and looked inside them, and then put them down again very quickly and wiped his hands on his trousers.

“There’s nothing in them,” he said. “No lining. No pockets. No label.” He swallowed. “They’re just… shaped like a coat. Like someone made a coat out of what they remembered coats looked like.”

Nobody touched them after that.

Sister Maud bandaged Gabe’s ribs, which were bruised but not broken, and Calvin’s knees, which were skinned to the bone, and Emrys’s pride, which was in tatters. She made Emrys sit on the customs house steps with a cup of cider until the grey went out of his face. He drank it in one go and asked for another.

“That was the whole of it,” he kept saying. “The whole of Torch. Everything I had. One flash.” He laughed shakily. “Twenty years of training, and it bought us about three seconds.”

“It bought us enough,” said Sabine. She was cleaning her sword with a rag, very carefully, and Deston noticed that the black stuff on it was eating into the cloth. “Three seconds is a lot. Ask anyone who’s ever needed three seconds.”

Rachael sat on the steps beside Emrys, with Gabe’s knife on her knee. She’d cleaned it already. The black stuff had come off the steel, but it had left a faint mark, like a shadow, that wouldn’t polish out.

“I cut him,” she said. She sounded surprised. “I actually cut him.”

“You did,” said Gabe. He was sitting on her other side, holding his ribs. “Clean, too.”

“I thought I’d freeze. I’ve thought about it for six weeks. What I’d do. I thought I’d freeze.” She turned the knife over in her hands. “I didn’t even think. I just…”

“You held,” said Deston quietly. “Like with the dockworker. You held.”

Rachael looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed, a small, shaky, astonished sound, and leaned her head against Gabe’s shoulder, and closed her eyes.

Deston looked at the threads.

He couldn’t help it. He looked at the six of them, Rachael and Gabe and Calvin and Dennis and Sabine and Emrys, sitting on the steps of the customs house in the wreckage of the midwinter market, bruised and shaking and alive. And the threads between them were brighter than he’d ever seen them. Thicker. Gold, every one of them, even Sabine’s, even Emrys’s, gold right through. As if something had been poured into them tonight, and set, and hardened.

He didn’t say anything. He didn’t need to.

They wrote to everyone that night. All six other groups. It took until dawn.

Deston wrote the last one himself, to Xav, and at the bottom of it he added a line he hadn’t put in any of the others.

Three of them. No threads. They walked into a crowd of thousands and nothing in the world was tied to them, and nobody noticed but me.

I keep thinking: how many more are there? In how many crowds? Standing in the middle of all that light, with nothing tied to them, looking like everyone else?

And who would ever see them, if they didn’t have someone like me, or Hyvred, looking?

Rae cut one. Clean. Gabe says to tell Azac he stood in front, and it hurt, and Declan would have been furious, and he’d do it again.

He sealed it, and went to the window, and looked out at the quay.

The market was gone. The stalls were packed away, the lanterns taken down. Only the little candle-boats were left, a few of them, still floating on the black water where the children had set them adrift. They’d drifted out into the middle of the river now, and were turning slowly in the current, very small and very bright.

Deston watched them for a long time, until the last of them had burned out.

Then he looked at the thread running north, upriver, thin as a hair, to Xav. It was still there. It always was.

He’d never been so glad to see anything in his life.
