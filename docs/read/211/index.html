The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 8: The Third Seal

Chapter 211

Down the River

9,145 words · 40 min read

The Patient Widow came up the Wren on the third day after the bell, low in the water with salt and iron and a crate of lamp glass for the chandler, and Haskett tied her to the quay below the mill and shouted at everybody until noon.

Alea heard him from the cabin. Everybody always heard Haskett from the cabin. It was a mile and a bit, across the bottom of the lake and down the mill road, and the wind had to be from the south, but when it was, his voice came over the water like a dog barking in another valley, and her father, on the jetty, would turn his head an inch and say Haskett, the way you’d say rain.

He said it that morning. Haskett. He didn’t turn his head.

She’d been packed for two days. The pack had sat in the middle of the table where she’d packed it, in the sun, through two suppers, with Astra across the top. Neither of them had moved it. He’d eaten round it. So had she. On the second night the red hen had got up on the table, which she was not allowed to do, and sat on it, and he’d looked at the hen and the hen had looked at him and he’d let her.

“Noon tomorrow,” said Alea, at supper. “He’s loading flour. He goes at noon.”

“All right,” said her father.

That was all, on the second night. She’d lain in the loft afterwards with the curtain drawn, the red one with the old council crest on it that Odette had made out of a tablecloth, and listened to him not sleep, downstairs, the way she’d listened to it for nine years. She knew the sounds. The chair. The door. The jetty, eleven boards out, where one creaked. The lamp. Then nothing for a long time, which was him reading the channel, or him sitting. Then the boards again, coming back. Then the chair again. Then, late, the sound she’d only ever heard in the last year: the pencil, on the wall, by the drawing.

She hadn’t got up to look. She’d look in the morning.

In the morning it said what it had said for three days. Twelve dinners. Gabe. East. And Fish, at the bottom, in his own hand. Nothing new. She’d stood in front of it with her boots in her hand and been glad and disappointed at once, the way you are when you open a letter and it’s only about the weather, and then she’d put her boots on and gone down to do her forms on the shingle, and he’d been there already, on the jetty, with five fish on the string, watching the water.

She did the forms. He watched the water. When she’d finished he said, without turning round, “Your weight’s going forward on the third.”

“I know.”

“Good.”

She knew because she’d been doing it on purpose. Because on a boat you’d want your weight forward. Because she’d been thinking about it for two days. She didn’t say so, and he didn’t ask, and that was how they said it.

He came down to the quay with her.

She hadn’t known if he would. She hadn’t asked. She’d put the pack on, and Astra over it, in the harness Gabe had fitted with the old saddle leather and the sharkskin and the fisherman’s knot, and stood at the door; and he’d got up from the table and come out after her and pulled the door to, and they’d gone up the path through the pumpkin beds and along the lake road together the way they’d gone up it a thousand times, to the market, to the hall, to the forge in the square.

He hadn’t brought the rod.

She noticed at the top of the lake road, where the stone wall began. She’d been walking on his left, the way she always did, so his right hand was free, and the right hand was free. Nothing on the shoulder. Nothing in it. She looked at it for a while, swinging, empty, a man’s hand, a fisherman’s, that she’d known all her life.

She’d never once seen him go up this road without the rod. Not to the market, not to the hall. Not to Rowan’s door in the autumn, all night. He’d carried it into the Lists. He’d carried it to the grave. He’d carried it into a hall full of lords in Valcrest and leaned it against the king’s table.

He’d left it on the jetty. She’d seen it, now she thought, leaning on the last post, where it leaned at night.

She didn’t say anything. He didn’t either.

The town had come down to the quay. She’d known it would; the town came down to the quay for everything now. Two years ago she could have walked through Wrenhaven at noon with a sword on her back and a pack on her shoulders and nobody would have looked twice, because nobody looked at her father’s daughter twice, because they’d been told not to by a big grey man in a mayor’s chain who’d stood in a doorway once for a season. Now the whole quay was full. The millers’ boys rolling the last flour sacks down the plank. Old Mabry with his duck. Edric the baker with flour to his elbows and a parcel in a cloth that was, it turned out, for her. The chandler’s girl with a bag of hard toffee, and the egg-woman with Robin, who wasn’t allowed near the edge any more and was holding his mother’s skirt in both fists and staring at Astra.

And Group 1, on the bollards, in a row.

Xola with Squiddles on her head gone a hopeful sort of pink. PenguChi with Waddles, who was standing on the very edge of the quay looking down at the water with deep suspicion, because he had history with this quay. Higu with her book. Gigichi with her hands in her pockets and her face set, which meant she’d been crying earlier and wasn’t going to again. And Thea, at the end, very tall, with her pink-and-violet hair tied back and a cord on her wrist, a plain blue one, that hadn’t been there yesterday.

“Maren knotted us each one,” said Thea, when she saw Alea look at it. “Not one of Hana’s. It doesn’t do anything. She said it was so we’d remember to write to you.”

“You can’t read my hand,” said Alea.

“No,” said Thea. “And you can’t read ours. Maren’s going to read yours to us at supper, and we’re going to tell her what to say back, all five of us at once, and she’s going to write it down in yours.” She was quiet. “It’s going to take forever. We’ll do it anyway.”

Higu didn’t say anything. She just held out the book, open, so Alea could see the page. On it, in Higu’s small neat pencil, a column of days, and beside each day a little drawing of the moon. Tonight’s was a circle with nothing in it.

“New,” said Higu. “The night you go. There won’t be one.”

“I know,” said Alea. “I’ve got the chart.”

“I know you’ve got the chart,” said Higu. “I’m telling you anyway. It’s dark tonight. Don’t be frightened of it. It comes back on the third night, a thin one, in the west, after the sun.” She shut the book. “Look for it.”

Alea’s throat did something. She let it. She’d learned that, in the autumn, at a grave. You could let your throat do things.

“I’ll look,” she said.

Gigichi got off her bollard. She came across, gruff and small, with her hands still in her pockets, and stood in front of Alea and looked up at her, because everybody looked up at Alea now, except her father.

“You okay?” said Gigichi.

“Yes.”

Gigichi looked at her for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the way she’d nodded at the jetty in the spring when Alea’s father had said here.

“Good,” she said. “Ask me again in the summer.” And went back to her bollard.

Maren was on the quay steps with her list. Bram behind her, big and grey and grinning, with his thumbs in his belt. Maren didn’t say anything at all; Maren never said goodbyes. She just looked at Alea the way she’d looked at her the first week, in the militia hall, when a fox girl of sixteen with a copper guild token had stood in the doorway and asked if she could train, and Maren had looked her up and down for a long time and said yes, and nothing else. She looked at her like that now. Then she looked past her, at the man at the back of the crowd with no rod, and something went across her face that Alea had never seen on Maren’s face before, and was gone.

Rowan came down last, with his chain on, and Odette beside him with her keys. Rowan put his big hand on the top of Alea’s head, the way he’d done when she was nine and he’d sat on the stairs with her every night of a season, and left it there for a moment, and took it away.

“Odette’s packed you something,” said Rowan.

“It’s only bread,” said Odette. “And the cheese. And a jar.” She handed it over, a parcel in oilcloth tied with the same red cord as the curtain. “Don’t open the jar on the boat. It’s for Sorrel’s mother. You don’t arrive at somebody’s house with nothing.”

“I wasn’t going to arrive with nothing.”

“I know,” said Odette. “Now you’re arriving with a jar.”

Haskett roared.

He’d been roaring all morning, at his boys and the millers’ boys and the flour and the wind, which was wrong, and the river, which was high, and a goose that had got onto the deck and been thrown off it and got back on again. He roared now, from the steering oar, in his coat like a tent with his cap pushed back, and what he roared was anybody that’s going had better be on my boat or in the river, I’m not particular, and the quay laughed, because the quay always laughed at Haskett, and then went quiet, because everybody on it knew who was going.

Her father was at the back.

Where he always stood. At the edge of the crowd, behind everyone, where nobody would look at him, which these days meant everybody did. Not close. A dozen paces off, by the corner of the mill, with his empty hands at his sides.

She went to him through the crowd. It parted for her, the way it parted for him.

“Watch the bar at Gullhaven,” he said, before she could say anything. “That’s where Sorrel’s dad went over.”

“Sorrel will watch it.”

“I know.” He was quiet. Then he put his hand in his coat, and took something out, flat, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with a twist of fishing line, and held it out.

She knew what it was before she took it. She knew from the size of it, and the weight, and the way he held it, the way he’d held it once in the autumn at the table, getting it out of the drawer. She untied the line anyway, in the middle of the quay, with everybody watching.

Paper. The thick cream kind, with the faint mark of a crown in it when you held it to the light. Six sheets. The paper the king had left on their table two autumns ago, the first time he came, with a note in his big square hand: for when you’ve got something to say. Her father had used one sheet in two years. She’d watched him use it. He’d written a word on it and sent it to a king.

“I’ve got four left,” he said. “Under the drawing.”

She tied the line back round it. She put it in the inside pocket of her coat, beside Higu’s chart. Over her heart.

“I’ll write,” she said.

“I know you will.”

She didn’t say will you come. She’d said, on the last plank of the jetty, in the spring, I’m never going to ask. She’d meant it then and she meant it now, standing on the quay with the king’s paper against her ribs and her father’s empty hand in front of her. It wasn’t hers to ask. It was his to decide. She’d learned that from him.

He didn’t say anything about coming either. He’d taught her that too.

She put her arms round him. On the quay, in front of everybody. She didn’t do it often, and never in front of anybody, and she felt him go still for a heartbeat, surprised, and then not; his arms came round her, both of them, and held on, with Astra’s hilt between them like a third person, the way the sword had been between them all her life.

“Go on,” he said. Into her hair. “He’ll go without you. He’s done it before.”

She went.

The Patient Widow was low and wide and tarred black, with one mast and a crosstree and a long steering oar off the stern, and she smelled of flour and tar and wet rope and, faintly, from somewhere in the bilge, of every fish that had ever ridden on her. Alea had seen her twice: once last spring, coming round the last bend with a man up the mast screaming at Haskett, and once in the autumn, on the last barge before the freeze, with a basket of six perch lashed to the stern for a town downriver. She’d never been on her. She stood on the deck at the bow, in the place where Haskett’s younger boy pointed her, there, miss, you won’t be in the road, and felt it move.

It moved all the time. That was the thing nobody had told her. She’d thought a boat would be still at a quay and move when it went, the way a horse does. It moved at the quay. Every boot that came down the plank, every flour sack that thumped into the hold, the deck went down under it and came back, a finger’s width, slow, like breathing. She put her feet the way she’d put them for two days, weight forward, and the deck came up under her toes and she had to give with her knees, and it went down and she had to give again.

Like waiting for a fish, she thought. Her own words. She’d said them to PenguChi in the first week, about aura, before she knew they were true. It’s lower. Like waiting for a fish.

Haskett’s boys cast off. The long black hull slid out from the quay into the current, and the river took it, and the quay went away.

She turned round. She’d told herself she wouldn’t, and she turned round at once, the way you do. They were all still there. Group 1 on the bollards, in a row, all standing up now. Rowan with his hand up. Edric with his floury arm up. Robin, with one fist let go of his mother’s skirt, waving it. Waddles at the very edge, looking at the water. And Thea, at the end, with her hand up, and the blue cord on her wrist catching the sun.

And her father. By the corner of the mill. Where he’d been.

He didn’t lift the rod. He hadn’t brought it. He lifted his hand. Just his hand, open, the empty one, up beside his face, and held it there.

She lifted hers back.

The river took them round the bend, past the willows and the last of the houses, and the mill went behind the trees, and he went with it.

She stood at the bow for a long time after that, with her hand still half up, until Haskett’s elder boy came forward with a coil of rope and said beg pardon, miss in a way that meant she was in the road, and she put it down.

The first night she didn’t sleep at all.

Haskett tied up at dusk at a willow bank below a farm, the way he did every night on the upper river, because the upper Wren was full of snags in the spring and he’d lost a barge to one when his hair was black. The boys made a fire on the bank and cooked something in a pot that was mostly onions. Haskett ate it on the deck with his back to the mast and his boots off and told Alea, unasked, the names of everything on the bank that she could see and several things she couldn’t, and which of the farmers along the next ten miles owed him money, and how much. Then he went to sleep, sitting up, in his coat, between one sentence and the next, and snored.

Alea lay on the flour sacks with Astra beside her and looked up.

There was no moon. Higu had said so. There was only the dark, and the stars, which were very many, and the sound of the river going past the hull, which was loud. She’d thought a river would be quiet. It wasn’t. It talked all night, against the black boards a hand’s width from her head, in a voice like somebody saying the same sentence over and over in another room and never quite finishing it.

She’d never slept anywhere without a jetty.

That was the thing she thought, at midnight, at one, lying there. She’d slept in an inn in Valcrest with Petra and a carter’s bench in Ashby and the ground on the road east and the floor of Aldous’s chapel and a bed in the infirmary at Cinderhold where her mother had worked. And every one of those nights he’d been within a shout. In the next room. Across the fire. At the eleventh merlon, on the wall, where she’d known exactly where to find him in the dark. And at home there’d been the jetty: the boards, the eleventh one that creaked, the lamp on the last post, which she could see from the loft window if she sat up. All her life she’d gone to sleep knowing where the end of the jetty was.

She didn’t know where anything was.

She sat up at two and looked north, back up the river, where the lake was, and couldn’t see anything but willow and dark. She thought about him on the jetty. Whether he’d lit the lamp. Whether the hens were sitting in their row. Whether he’d crossed anything off. Whether he was reading the channel, the way he did every night, and whether there was anything in it, and whether any of it was about her. Alea’s on the river. Thea would have written it. Somebody would.

She thought about the rod, leaning on the last post, with nothing in his hand.

Then she lay down again, and put her hand on Astra’s flat, the cold steel, near the grip, where the crooked letters were. Ad astra per aspera. His. And beside them, a little further down, four clean ones she couldn’t read either and didn’t need to. To Diwa. My love. Except they weren’t there; they were on the other half, on a knoll an hour from a fort, at the head of a grave. She’d gone and got them anyway, in her head. You could do that.

At some point she slept. She woke at the grey with her hand still on the steel and Haskett awake at the oar, looking at her.

“Your mother used to sleep like that,” said Haskett. “With her hand on whatever she was minding.”

Alea sat up.

“You knew her?”

“Brought her up the river once,” said Haskett. “The year they came to the lake. You were a little thing, asleep in a basket the whole way. Him at the bow with that great black thing of his rolled up in a blanket so it wouldn’t frighten the horses at the portage, and her on the sacks with her hand on a chest of her herbs. Didn’t sleep the whole way up. Sat there where you’re sitting.” He looked at the water. “Gave me a salve for my knee. Still got the pot.” He jerked his head at the stern locker. “Never used it. Didn’t want to use it up.”

Alea looked at him for a long time.

“I didn’t know that,” she said.

“No reason you would,” said Haskett. “Nobody asks the bargeman.” And roared at the elder boy, who’d let the bow come round.

She did her forms at dawn on the deck.

She fell over on the second. Not all the way; onto one knee, on the planks, with Astra’s weight going sideways on her back and the deck coming up where her foot had meant to go down. Haskett’s younger boy laughed, then stopped, because she’d turned round and he’d seen her face, and then started again, because she was laughing too.

“Feet like a heron,” said Haskett, from the oar. “Long and no use.”

“Again,” said Alea. Rhea’s word. She’d learned it at Cinderhold, in the yard, where it meant you’re not dead, so get up.

She did it again. She fell again, on the fourth. She got up. By the time the sun was off the water she could do the first three without falling and the fourth if the river didn’t bend, and the boys had stopped laughing and were watching, and Haskett had stopped saying heron and was saying nothing, which from Haskett was a great deal.

The deck moved. It was always going to move. You didn’t hold it still. You couldn’t. You went with it, the way you’d go with a pike, the way she’d gone with Sorrel’s hook in the final, and let it carry your weight where it wanted, and set your feet at the end of where it carried you.

She’d have to teach that to the sand at Valcrest. The sand didn’t move. She’d have to remember how to stand on something that didn’t.

On the third day they came to the Narrows.

Haskett told her about them for an hour beforehand, in a voice that got lower and slower the closer they came, until by the time the banks began to rise he wasn’t roaring at all. The Wren went into the hills there, he said, between two walls of grey rock, and ran fast and deep and narrow for most of a day, and a barge going down didn’t stop, couldn’t stop, and a barge going up hauled from the towpath on the ledge, a man’s width, cut into the rock on the west side by somebody’s grandfather. And two springs ago, going up, with ten people from another world on his deck and a sail full of a wind that shouldn’t have been there, he’d hauled through the Narrows at dusk, and they’d been waiting.

“On the ledge,” said Haskett. “And on the landing at Gallow Rock. Folk. Ordinary folk, they looked like. A woman with a basket. A boy in a cap. A man in a good hat.” He spat over the side. “Didn’t breathe right. Didn’t blink. Stood there in a row looking at the little dark one. The healer.”

“Rachael,” said Alea.

“Her.” Haskett looked at the rock going by. “They came for her. Right over the side. And she cut one, with a little knife the big lad made her, and it went grey where she cut it, like a stone does when it’s wet and then dries. And the big lad with the strange blade went over the side onto the ledge after them. And the one with the shield jumped off a rock six feet up onto the deck with the shield out in front of him like a door coming off its hinges. I’ve seen a lot on this river. I’ve not seen that.”

He showed her the place. Gallow Rock: a flat stone landing on the west bank above the fast water, with an iron ring in it for mooring and a path going up into the rock and away. Nothing on it now. Moss, and a heron, which looked at them and didn’t move.

Alea went to the bow while they passed it. To the place at the very front where, Haskett said, the sword-woman from Duskhollow had stood every night of that trip, awake, with her blade across her knees. She stood there with Astra on her back and looked at the landing, and the path, and the heron.

She didn’t feel anything. She’d half thought she would. A cold, the way Yan said he felt it at the grave. A wrongness. There was nothing. Only rock and moss and a heron and the river going very fast, so that the landing came and went in the time it took to breathe twice.

But Rachael had been here. With a little knife. And it had come over the side for her.

She thought about that at the bow until the walls of the Narrows opened out again and the river widened and slowed and went brown and lazy between low green fields, and Haskett began roaring again at the boys, at the fields, at a cow.

Duskhollow existed because of a hole in the ground. Xav had told her that, the first time she met him, at the top of the lake road with Gabe and Deston and a look on his face she’d later understood was what a person looks like after a month on a barge and a night on a landing at Gallow Rock: Duskhollow exists because of a hole in the ground, and everyone in it knows it. The town sat on a bend of the Wren with its back to the river and its face to the Hollow, a great grey arch of old stone at the end of the main street with the dark under it.

The Patient Widow came round the bend at sunset, and they were on the quay.

Group 6. All five, in a row, the way Group 1 had been on the bollards; she wondered if it was something the summoned did, standing in a row. Xav in the middle, dark and still, with his hands loose. Malcolm with a book. Rui Xiang beside him, talking, until he saw the barge and stopped. Azac at the end, the biggest, with his shield on his back out of habit and a grin all the way across his face. John with a light on the end of his finger, a little gold one, which he lifted as the barge came in, and which went up off his finger and hung over the quay like a lamp nobody had to hold.

And behind them, on the steps, with her arms folded, Petra.

Haskett tied up for the night. Haskett always tied up at Duskhollow; he had a cousin with a tavern. Mother Annick fed everyone, at her long table in the house where Group 6 lived, with the Hollow’s arch filling the end of the street outside the window like a moon that never set; she fed Alea three times, because Alea kept eating it, and the third time she put her hand on the back of Alea’s neck the way you’d check a child for fever and said you’re his girl, then and went back to the stove without waiting for an answer.

They all asked about him. She’d known they would. Rui Xiang asked fast and a lot, and Malcolm asked one careful thing at a time and wrote the answers down, and Azac asked about the hens, and John asked about the jetty, the light, the time, how long he sat out there. Xav didn’t ask anything. Xav sat at the end of the table with his chin on his fist and listened to all of it, and at the end, when the others had run out, he said, “Did he bring the rod? To the quay?”

Alea looked at him.

“No,” she said.

Xav was quiet for a while.

“Okay,” said Xav. And didn’t say anything else. But he looked at Petra, down the table, and Petra looked back, and something passed between them that Alea couldn’t read and didn’t need to.

Petra’s dawn class was on the gravel bar below the town quay, where the river went round the bend and left a long low tongue of stones out into the water. Alea had heard about it in letters, all winter, read out at the militia hall in Maren’s voice: the class Petra had run in the guild yard in the first spring, with wooden swords and a list, and moved to the bar when Group 6 came home from Valcrest, because the stones moved under your feet and she said that was the point.

Anyone can try me, Petra had said, the first morning. Once a morning. With a practice sword. I’ll answer with this. And held up a stick. A length of grey driftwood, the length of a short sword, worn smooth as bone, that she’d carried all the way from the shingle at Lake Ilse in the bottom of her pack.

Alea knew that stick. She’d watched her father pick it up off the shingle last spring, at the end of the jetty, and teach a sword-woman from the Redmarch with it, every morning for a month. Earlier. Not faster. Move while it’s still deciding to.

There were fifteen people on the bar at dawn. Group 6, and a dozen from the town: guild apprentices, a girl from the tannery, two of the Hollow’s guards in their grey, an old man with a hoe, because Petra didn’t turn anybody away. They did the forms together in a ragged line along the stones with the river going past at their heels, and then Petra stood at the top of the bar with the stick, and they tried her. One at a time. Once.

Nobody touched her. Alea watched seven people try, and the stick met every one of them a finger from their ribs and stopped there, without hurry, already where they were going to be.

One of them was a Hollow guard called Dunstan, in the grey coat, a broad slow man past forty who’d stood on the bar every dawn since the first spring and been Gleaming, Xav said, since before Alea was born. The stick met him like it met everyone. But when he went, the sheen on his blade went a moment ahead of him, leaning off the edge the way a dog leans on a lead, and Petra’s eyebrows went up a hair. Dunstan sat on the stones afterwards with his sword across his knees and looked at it as if it belonged to somebody else. Third time this month, said Malcolm, beside her, who counted everything. He cried the first time. Petra told him to stop looking at the sword and look at the whole thing. He said that’s what the fisherman says. She said yes.

Then Petra looked at her.

“Once,” said Petra. “With that.” She nodded at Astra.

“It isn’t blunt.”

“I know it isn’t blunt,” said Petra. “I’ve seen what it does. I’d still rather you came at me with what you’ll come at them with. The sand at Valcrest isn’t going to give you a practice sword because you’re tired.” She held up the stick. “Once.”

Alea took Astra off her back.

The gravel moved under her. Not like the deck; the deck moved all of a piece, up and down, breathing. The gravel moved in pieces, every stone its own, so that when she set her feet the left one went down a finger’s width and the right one didn’t, and she had to give with the left knee and not the right, and then the stones under the right one shifted and she had to give with that.

She stood still. She let it settle. She waited for the fish.

Petra waited too. She was very good at it. She’d learned it on a shingle beach from the best there was.

Alea went.

Not straight. Never straight; she’d never once in her life gone at anybody straight. Sideways, in, the first step from stillness covering twice the ground it should, the way it always had since she was sixteen and drew; and the gravel went down under her front foot the way the deck had gone down under it on the second morning, and she didn’t fight it. She went with it. She let it carry her weight forward and low, under where Petra was looking, under where the stick was already going to be.

The stick met her. Of course it did. It met Astra’s flat a hand’s width from Petra’s knee and stopped there, and Petra’s other hand was on Alea’s shoulder, lightly, the way you’d touch someone to say there.

But a hand’s width. Not a finger. A hand.

Petra looked at the stick on the steel. At the distance. Down at the gravel, at Alea’s front foot, sunk to the ankle in stones that had gone down under it and taken her with them.

“Huh,” said Petra.

“That was the boat,” said Alea. “I’ve been falling over on a boat for five days.”

“Keep falling over on it,” said Petra. She took the stick back. She didn’t smile; Petra hardly ever smiled. But her face did the thing it did instead, at the edges. “Earlier. Not faster. You know that one.”

“I know that one.”

“Good,” said Petra. And then, as Alea was putting Astra back on her back, very quietly, so that the others on the bar didn’t hear: “I’m walking east with him this summer. For the tenth. The sixth fish was mine; he sent it down the river in the autumn. Tell him I said so.”

“He knows.”

“I know he knows,” said Petra. “Tell him anyway. In that.” She nodded at Alea’s coat, at the pocket, at the shape of six sheets of paper in oilcloth over her heart. “Somebody should get a letter from you that isn’t about the weather.”

John showed her afterwards.

She hadn’t known he could. She’d heard about it, in Maren’s voice, in letters: John’s Look, the little lights that saw for him, that he could send ahead round corners and over walls; and then, in the autumn, the thing he’d done on this bar that had made Petra put her hand over her face: he’d shown them themselves. Afterwards. In the air. What his light had seen.

He did it on the quay steps, with the sun up and Haskett roaring at his boys to load the cousin’s barrels. He held up his hand, and the little gold light that had hung over the bar all through the class came back to it, and he did something with his fingers like a man unrolling a map, and there in the air in front of them, small, the size of a window, faint and gold and moving: the gravel bar. The river. A girl with a black sword and a woman with a stick.

Alea had never seen herself from outside.

She’d seen herself in water, in the lake, still, on a calm morning. In a polished pan, once, at Cinderhold. She’d never seen herself move. She watched the girl in the air set her feet, and wait, and go: sideways, in, low, with the stones going down under her front foot and her body going with them. Faster than she’d thought she was. Smaller than she’d thought she was. She watched the stick meet the black steel, a hand from the knee, and the other hand come down on her shoulder.

“Again,” said Alea.

John played it again.

She watched the feet. Only the feet. And on the third time she saw it, the thing her father had said to her the first morning of her life she could remember him saying anything about her forms at all, when she was thirteen and he’d come down to the shingle and watched her do the first one for a long time and said, without any expression at all, your weight’s on your heels.

It was. On the gravel, at the start, before she went. Five days on a deck with her weight thrown forward and her knees giving, and the moment she stood on something that didn’t move she’d sat back on her heels to wait, the way she’d done at thirteen, because waiting on your heels felt like waiting, and it wasn’t, it was just sitting.

“Again,” she said.

John played it again.

“You’ll wear it out,” said Rui Xiang. “He can’t wear it out. Can you wear it out? John, can you wear it out?”

“No,” said John.

“Again,” said Alea.

On the sixth time she stopped watching her feet and watched the stick.

She didn’t know why. Something at the edge of the little gold window, the way you see a fish turn at the edge of your eye and look and it’s gone. John slowed it for her without being asked; he could do that, it turned out, the way you’d slow a song. The stick came across, slow as a heron’s leg, and met Astra’s flat, and stopped.

And behind it, where it had been, a line.

Thin. Grey. Hanging in the air along the path the stick had taken, the way a cut stays in the skin of ice on a puddle after the stone has gone through. For a blink. Less. Then gone.

Alea looked at John. John looked at Xav. Xav, on the step below with his elbows on his knees, didn’t look surprised at all.

“Since the thaw,” he said quietly. “Four times. Only with the stick. John’s got all four.”

“Does she know?”

“No.”

“Are you going to tell her?”

Xav thought about it, the way he thought about everything, for longer than you’d think a person could. “What did he say to you,” he said, “when you were thirteen?”

“Your weight’s on your heels.”

“Did he tell you what to do about it?”

He hadn’t. Not once. Not in three years.

“He points,” said Xav. “He doesn’t hand it to you. Then it’s yours.” He looked out at the bar, where Petra was walking up the stones with the stick under her arm, alone, the way she always walked. “She’ll find it. If she doesn’t, I’ll point. Not yet.”

“Again,” said Alea, to John. And this time she watched both.

They came to Kingsferry two days later, in the afternoon, and the river opened out.

She’d known it would. She’d seen Kingsferry on Deston’s map, and in Haskett’s stories, and in the shape of everything that came up the river to Wrenhaven in a crate with Kingsferry stencilled on the side. She hadn’t known what opened out meant. The Wren had been a river all her life, brown and narrow and fast between willows, the width of a shout. Below the last bend above Kingsferry it stopped being a river. It got wide, and then wider, and the banks went away on either side until they were low grey lines with towns on them, and the water went flat and slow and brown and huge, the colour of tea with milk in it, with boats on it, dozens, hundreds, sails and oars and barges and a great tall ship with three masts going down the middle of it as if the river were a road.

And the city. On both banks, and a bridge between, and a bell tower on the far side going bong over all of it at the hour, so that every gull on the water went up at once.

Haskett tied up at the Rope Street stairs, which were his, he said; he’d been tying up at them since before the stairs. And Deston was on the top step.

Of course he was. He’d have heard her coming. Not her; her thread. He listened to people’s threads the way other people listened to music, with his head tipped, as if somebody were humming the gap in a song. He’d have heard hers coming down the river for days.

“Three days,” said Deston. Before she’d even got off the plank. Tall, taller than anyone, with his head tipped. “Since Duskhollow. Yours goes into his like a nail. It got louder the whole way.” He smiled. “Hello, Alea.”

“Hello.”

“Gabe’s at the forge. He’s been at the forge since you were at the Narrows. He’s making something he says isn’t for you.” Deston’s smile went wider. “It’s for you.”

It was a hook.

Gabe gave it to her at Harl’s forge on the south wharf, in the red light, with Harl watching from the bellows with his arms folded and an expression that said he’d been listening to this young man talk about a hook for three days. Gabe was enormous and soot-black to the elbows and very red in the face, and he held it out to her the way he’d held out the refitted Astra on the shingle two springs ago: as if it might be wrong, and he’d quite understand.

A fish hook. Big. Steel, not iron, as long as her hand, with a barb you could have hung a coat on and a ring at the top for the line. And on the inside of the curve, so small she had to hold it to the forge-light to read it, if she could have read it, which she couldn’t: a line of letters, cut very carefully with a fine punch.

“It’s for the sea,” said Gabe. “Sorrel says they’ve got fish there the size of you. That the hooks off the market stalls bend. This one won’t.” He looked at his hands. “I put something on it. Inside. You can’t read it. That’s all right. It’s not for reading.”

“What does it say?”

Gabe went redder.

“Sto,” said Deston, from the door. “It says Sto. Same as his blade.” He tipped his head. “Stand. He couldn’t think what else to put on a hook.”

“It’s a good word for a hook,” said Gabe, to the anvil.

Alea held it. It was heavy, and cold, and it fitted in her palm the way the grip on Astra fitted her hand, which was the thing the king had once said about Gabe’s grips: nobody taught you that. She closed her hand round it.

“It’s a good word for a hook,” she said.

Gabe looked up. Then he picked up his hammer, because he didn’t know what to do with his face, and went back to the anvil and hit something that didn’t need hitting, very hard, and Harl said oi.

“He’s practising,” said Deston, quietly. “Strikes. All spring. The king wrote to him. He’s going up to the lake in the summer, a week before the tenth, to strike for the king, and the king won’t tell him what for.” He was quiet a moment, watching Gabe’s back. “He’s never been so frightened of anything. He’s never wanted anything so much. It’s the same thing, with Gabe.”

Alea looked at the big man’s back. At the hammer coming down, and down, on a bar of nothing.

She knew what that was. She’d watched her father pack and unpack a bag.

Rachael was on the infirmary step.

Alea went to find her, because she’d have been ashamed not to, and because of the Narrows; she wanted to see the hand that had held the little knife. It was evening. The infirmary was a long grey building at the top of the river stairs with a step where people sat to wait, and Rachael was sitting on it in an apron with blood on the hem, not hers, eating an apple, with her arms folded round her knees.

She looked up when Alea came. She didn’t get up. Rachael didn’t get up for people. She looked Alea up and down the way she looked at everyone, flat and practical and a little bit unimpressed, the way she’d looked at the whole of the Lists from the fifth tier of the south stand.

“So you’re going to the sea,” said Rachael.

“Yes.”

“On your own.”

“Sorrel will be there.”

“Sorrel’s sixteen,” said Rachael.

“Seventeen now.”

“Oh, well, then,” said Rachael. She took a bite of the apple. It was a good apple, red, from the market. Not like the other kind. Alea had heard about the other kind. “Have you ever been on the sea?”

“No.”

“I have,” said Rachael. “On Earth. A ferry. Two hours. I was sick the whole time and a man in a vest told me to look at the horizon, and I told him where he could put the horizon.” She looked at the apple. “Look at the horizon. It works. Don’t tell him.”

Alea sat down on the step. Not close. A pace away, at the other end, where the stone was worn. She knew about Rachael. Everybody knew about Rachael. You sat near her, not on her.

They sat there for a while. The bell tower went bong for the hour and the gulls went up off the river.

“Haskett told me,” said Alea. “About the Narrows.”

Rachael didn’t say anything for a moment.

“Haskett tells everyone about the Narrows,” said Rachael. “He tells it like he was the hero. He was at the steering oar shouting sail at a boy up a mast.”

“He said you cut one.”

“I did.”

“With a little knife.”

Rachael put her hand on her belt. On the sheath there, the small plain one Sabine had found for it. She didn’t take it out. She just put her hand on it, the way Alea put hers on Astra’s flat at night.

“It went grey,” said Rachael. “Where I cut it. Like a wet stone drying.” She was quiet. “I think about it most nights. I didn’t, for a year. Now I do.” She looked at Alea, sidelong. “They all went north-west. Six weeks ago. All seven, the same minute. I felt them go. Everyone said good.” She took a bite of the apple. “I said that’s worse.”

Alea didn’t say anything. She didn’t know enough to say anything. She thought about Yan, at the grave, saying it isn’t her, and the cold under it lying down like a dog.

“Write,” said Rachael.

“You can’t read my hand.”

“Sabine can,” said Rachael. “Sabine’ll read it to me in a voice. She does voices; it’s awful. Write anyway.” She got up. She stood on the step with the apple core in her hand and the evening going grey round her, a small dry woman in a bloody apron who had once been the patient for twenty-six years, and looked down at Alea. “And when you get to Valcrest, and you’re in the final, and you look up at the stands and he’s not there—“

Alea’s ears went flat. She felt them go.

“—you look at the horizon,” said Rachael. “It works.” And went back into the infirmary, and shut the door.

Dennis found her a boat.

She hadn’t asked him to. She’d meant to find one herself, in the morning, on the wharf, the way her father would have, by walking along it and looking at the boats and the men on them and picking the one that looked as if it would still be there in a week. Dennis came down to the Rope Street stairs at supper with a list and a look on his face like a cat who has been very good, and said, “The Gannet. Coaster. Down to the mouth on the morning ebb and along the coast to Gullhaven with lamp oil and nails. Three days if the wind’s kind, six if it isn’t. Master’s a woman called Brisa who once threw a customs man off her deck, so she’s honest. You’re paying half what she asked, which is still twice what it’s worth, because I said you were my friend’s daughter and she said which friend, and I said you’ve heard the ballad, and she went white.” He handed her a scrap of paper. “That’s your berth. Don’t lose it, she’ll make you pay again.”

Alea looked at the scrap. She couldn’t read it. It was in Dennis’s hand, in English, and the shape of it slid off her eyes like rain off oilcloth.

“Thank you,” she said.

“Don’t thank me,” said Dennis. “I’m the treasurer. It’s my whole personality.” He sat down on the step beside her, closer than Rachael would have, and looked at the river going by, huge and brown and slow, with the lamps coming on along the far bank one at a time. “Are you all right? Going on your own?”

“Yes,” said Alea.

Dennis didn’t look at her. He went on looking at the river.

Everybody knew about Dennis. He could hear a lie. Not the way you’d know someone was lying from their face; he heard it, he said, a little ring on it, like a cracked cup when you tap it. He’d heard it in Kingsferry all winter, the whole city ringing with lies like a bell tower, and he’d loved it.

“And it doesn’t matter,” said Alea. She didn’t know why she said it. She said it to the river. “If he doesn’t come to Valcrest. To the Lists. It’s mine, this year. I don’t mind if he isn’t there.”

Dennis was quiet for a long moment.

“Mm,” said Dennis.

She looked at him. He was still looking at the river. His face was perfectly pleasant and gave away absolutely nothing, which was, she’d learned at the Lists, how he gave things away.

“That one rang,” she said.

“A bit,” said Dennis. Gently. “Only a bit. Most of it was true.” He stood up, and brushed off the seat of his good coat, which he’d sat down on a wet step in without thinking, and looked dismayed about it. “It’s allowed to ring a bit. Mine do. Everyone’s do.” He looked down at her. “Morning ebb. Don’t be late. Brisa won’t wait, and I’ve already told her you’re never late, so if you’re late it’ll ring.”

The Gannet went down the river at dawn on the ebb, and Alea stood at the bow.

There was nobody on the boat who knew her. That was the thing. Brisa at the tiller, broad and brown and silent, with a ring in one ear and a pipe she didn’t light. Three men on the sheets who’d looked at the sword on her back once, and at her ears once, and then never again, because they’d seen stranger things come aboard at Kingsferry and would again. A cat, asleep on a coil of rope, that didn’t care about anything.

Nobody on the quay to wave. She’d said goodbye at supper. Gabe had held out his enormous sooty hand and then, not knowing what to do with it, patted her on the head with it, like Rowan, and gone redder than she’d ever seen anyone go. Deston had said I’ll hear you all the way, which was the kind of thing Deston said, and meant. Rachael hadn’t come. Rachael had sent, by Sabine, an apple, red, from the market, with a note tied to it in English that Sabine read out in a voice that was, in fact, awful: Horizon.

So nobody. Just the river, getting wider, and the city going behind, and the bell tower going bong once more, faint, for the hour, and then not even that.

It was the first time in her life she’d been anywhere where nobody knew who her father was.

She stood at the bow and found she didn’t know what to do with her hands. Then she found she did. She put one on the hook in her coat pocket, Sto, and the other on the oilcloth packet over her heart, six sheets of cream paper with a crown in them for when she had something to say, and Higu’s chart folded beside it with tonight’s moon on it: a thin one, in the west, after the sun. The third night. Look for it.

The river went wide, and wider, and the banks went away until there weren’t any. The water went from brown to grey, and from grey to a colour she didn’t have a word for, green and grey and blue at once, moving all over, all the time, every bit of it its own, like gravel. The air changed. It got cold and wet and enormous and it smelled of something she’d never smelled in her life, salt and weed and something rotten and something clean, all at once, so strong she could taste it.

And then the boat lifted under her.

Not the way the deck of the Patient Widow had lifted, a finger’s width, breathing. A long slow lift, the whole length of the boat, up and up, as if something very large underneath had taken a breath; and then down, longer, and her stomach went with it and stayed behind.

“That’s the bar,” said Brisa, from the tiller. The first thing she’d said. “That’s the sea. Get your feet under you, girl, there’s three days of it.”

Alea set her feet.

Weight forward. Not on her heels. Knees giving. The deck came up under her toes, and she gave, and it went down, and she gave again, and it came up, and she was still standing.

She looked at the horizon.

It worked.

Out ahead, past the bow, past the last line of white where the river finished arguing with the sea, there was nothing. Nothing at all, all the way to the edge of the world, flat and grey-green and moving and enormous, and not caring, the way Sorrel had written it didn’t, in a letter that had come up a river and down a lake road and been read out to Alea at a table in Maren’s voice: it doesn’t care about you, that’s the first thing, and then the second thing is that’s why you can love it.

She didn’t love it yet. She was going to be sick.

But she stood at the bow with her weight forward and her hand on a hook that said stand, and looked at the edge of the world, and didn’t sit back on her heels, and for the first time in five days she didn’t look north.
