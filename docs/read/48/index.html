The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 2: Letters

Chapter 48

The Cold One

4,459 words · 19 min read

Yan had learned, that year, that you could keep hold of a soul.

Not the whole of it. Not like holding a hand. More like the end of a thread: a single strand of what it had felt like, the first time you touched it, which you could close your fingers round and keep. And afterwards, however far away it went, if you were quiet enough and listened hard enough, you could feel it tug.

He’d found it out by accident, in the first week, at the grave. He’d stood beside the healer’s cairn on the Redmarch and felt the old grief worn into the ground there, deep and smooth, like a step worn by a thousand feet. And under it, something else. Something cold, that had been done to someone. He’d pulled back from it so fast he’d nearly fallen over. But a little of it had come with him, caught on him like a burr on a sleeve. And it had never quite come off.

He could still feel it now, if he wanted to. Ten months later, an hour’s walk away, sitting on the wall of Cinderhold with his eyes closed. A small cold knot, far out in the red, under the flowers. Waiting.

He didn’t tell anyone about that. He’d told Aldous about the cold under the grave, in the autumn, and watched Aldous’s face, and decided that was enough. Some things you turned over on your own for a long time before you set them down in front of anyone else.

That was how Yan did things. It was how he’d always done things. You carried it on your own until you knew what it was.

He felt the cold one cross on a clear night in early summer, and it was nothing like the grave.

He was on the wall. He was often on the wall at night, now; he’d got into the habit in the winter, when the scouts came, and never got out of it. Suzu was wound loosely round his shoulders, scarf-sized, her head tucked under his chin. Below him the Redmarch stretched away under the stars, flat and dark and quiet, to where the line of the border ran somewhere out of sight to the east.

He felt the living as candles, now. That was what he’d told Salty, in the spring, and it was still the closest he could get. Every living thing a small warm light. The soldiers asleep in the barracks behind him: dozens of little flames, steady, low. Salty, among them, like a forge. Mashi, two candles side by side that kept almost touching. Maigo, since the spring, something green and growing that didn’t look like a candle at all. The night watch on the gate. The horses. An owl, somewhere in the chapel roof.

And out on the Redmarch, nothing. The red was dark to him, the way it always was. Dead ground. Hurt ground. A long cold emptiness, with only the faint old grief of the grave burning in it, far out, like a coal.

Then something came into it.

It came from the south-east, from the direction of the border, and it was moving fast. Not in bursts, the way the scouts moved, start and stop and start. Smoothly. Steadily. Like something that had a long way to go and no intention of stopping.

And it was cold.

Not the grave’s cold. The grave’s cold was old and still and sorrowful, a thing that had been done and was over. This was cold that was alive. Cold that moved. Cold that had a shape and a purpose and was carrying them both across the dark at the speed of a running horse.

Yan went rigid on the wall.

He’d felt something like it before. In the winter, among the scouts. The one that pointed. The one that counted the towers and the gate and the well. That one had been cold like this, under its skin, the way the grave was cold. But that one had been a candle-flame of cold. This was a bonfire.

Suzu had lifted her head off his shoulder. She was very still, her tongue flickering, her bright green eyes fixed on the south-east.

The cold thing came level with Cinderhold. Two miles south, perhaps three. Out on the red, where there was nothing at all. And it didn’t slow down. It didn’t turn toward the fort. It didn’t stop to look at the towers, or the gate, or the horns hanging over the gate. It went straight past, without the slightest hesitation, as if Cinderhold weren’t there. As if Cinderhold weren’t what it had come for.

Heading north-west.

Yan didn’t think about it. He just reached.

He reached out with whatever it was in him that touched souls, as far as he’d ever reached, further, out across the dark ground, until he felt the edge of the cold thing go by. Like brushing your fingertips along the flank of something huge in the dark. He felt it, for one instant: the whole cold shape of it, old and heavy and patient and very, very sure of where it was going.

And he closed his fingers, and kept a thread.

Then it was past. Then it was gone, north-west, into the hills, faster than any horse. He sat on the wall with his heart pounding and his hand clenched on nothing and Suzu wound so tight round his neck he could barely breathe.

He could still feel it. A thread. A thin cold tug, going away into the dark.

Aldous didn’t go back to sleep after Yan woke him. Nobody did.

“Nothing that size has crossed since the war,” said Aldous, in the chapel, at two in the morning, in his nightshirt and his old coat, with a candle. He looked old, suddenly. Older than Yan had ever seen him look. “Not since the morning after. The big ones don’t come over the line. They never have. They wait on their side for the king to send them.” He shook his head slowly. “If one’s come over on its own, it’s because someone’s sent it for something.”

“It didn’t stop here,” said Yan.

“No,” said Aldous. “That’s what frightens me.”

Yan wrote to the channel before dawn. He sat on the cold pew with his window open and Suzu in his lap, and wrote it as plainly as he could, the way Higu’s rules asked. Something crossed the Redmarch tonight. South of the fort. Not a scout. Bigger. Cold. He thought about what to say at the end for a long time. He didn’t want to frighten anyone. He wanted to frighten everyone, just enough.

Nobody be alone at night. Please. Anyone.

Then he went back out on the wall, and closed his eyes, and felt for the thread.

He followed it for seven days.

Not with his feet. He never left Cinderhold. He followed it the way you follow the sound of a cart on a road at night: by listening. Every night, on the wall, he’d close his eyes and go quiet and feel for it, and there it would be. Fainter each time. Further. But there.

North-west. Always north-west. It moved at night and stopped by day; he could feel it go still in the mornings, a cold knot lying low somewhere, and start up again at dusk. It wasn’t hurrying. It didn’t need to. It had a long way to go and it knew exactly how long, and it was covering the ground the way a wolf covers ground, steadily, without wasting anything.

He couldn’t tell how far. That was the worst of it. Direction, yes. The thread pointed, the way a compass needle points. But distance was beyond him. By the third night it was so faint he had to sit for an hour to find it at all, and even then he couldn’t have told you if it was a hundred miles away or three hundred.

Aldous got out his maps and laid them on the chapel table, and they drew a line on it. From the Redmarch, north-west. A long, straight, terrible line, across the kingdom, through the hills, past the edge of the map, toward…

Everything. That was the trouble. North-west of the Redmarch was everything. The western hills, and Thornwall beyond them. The capital. The river. Duskhollow. Kingsferry. Wrenhaven, and the lake country. Every posting except their own lay somewhere in that long wide wedge of the map.

“It could be going anywhere,” said Wendy, looking at it.

“Yes,” said Yan.

“It could be going to any of them.”

“Yes.”

He wrote to the channel every morning. Still going. Still north-west. Still can’t tell how far. He knew it wasn’t enough. He knew everyone was reading it and doing the same sums he was, looking at their own little dot on their own map and wondering. He wished he could give them more. He wished he could reach further.

He couldn’t. So he sat on the wall every night and held the thread, and didn’t let go.

The fort was on edge all week. That was when Salty broke the table.

It wasn’t anyone’s plan. It was the fourth night, and nobody had slept properly since the first, and the mess hall after supper had that tight, prickly feeling that comes over a room full of soldiers who’ve been waiting for something to happen for too long. People snapping at each other. Someone knocked over a jug and someone else nearly hit him for it.

And Rhea, who could feel a room going bad the way a farmer feels rain coming, got up from her bench and slammed her elbow down on the big oak table in the middle of the hall, and held up her hand, and said, very loudly: “Salty. Thirty to twenty-eight. Come and lose.”

The hall went quiet. Then somebody whooped. Then everybody was crowding round.

The arm-wrestling had been going on since the first week. Rhea against Salty, once or twice every few days, a running war that the whole of Cinderhold followed with the passion of people who had very little else to follow. Maigo kept the tally on the wall by the door in chalk, and ran the betting, and was, as far as anyone could tell, making a small fortune. Rhea had led since the beginning. Never by much. Never by more than three. But always ahead.

Salty sat down opposite her without a word. He was like that. He didn’t do speeches. He just put his elbow on the table, and took her hand, and looked at her.

“It was a fix,” he said.

“It was not a fix.”

“Last time. You leaned.”

“I always lean. Leaning is legal.”

Maigo, on a bench, held up both hands. “On my count,” he said. “Three. Two. One. Go.”

Salty won the first one. It took nearly a minute, and the whole hall was screaming by the end. Thirty to twenty-nine.

Rhea won the next one. Thirty-one to twenty-nine. She was grinning like a wolf.

Salty won the one after that, slowly, grinding, his face going red and the veins standing out in his huge arms. Thirty-one to thirty.

Then he won the next one. Thirty-one all.

The mess hall had stopped being a mess hall. It was a riot. Soldiers standing on benches. Soldiers standing on each other. Wendy at the front, very cool, with her arms folded and her mismatched eyes narrowed, not cheering, the way she never cheered, but not looking away either. Mashi beside her, chewing his thumbnail. Maigo on his bench, chalk in hand, not even pretending to be neutral, shouting SALTY SALTY SALTY. Even Aldous had come in from the chapel to see what the noise was and stayed, standing at the back, with the faintest smile.

And Yan stood by the door and felt it.

He didn’t understand what he was feeling, at first. He’d been holding the thread all evening, in the back of his mind, the way he’d held it all week: that faint cold tug, far away, north-west. And now, in front of him, something was getting in the way of it. Something bright. Something hot.

Salty’s candle.

It had always been big. A forge, Yan had called it, in the spring: not a candle-flame, a bed of coals. But it was never more than that. It sat in Salty steady and banked and enormous, the way a forge sits overnight, and nothing ever seemed to make it rise.

It was rising now.

“Last one,” said Maigo, hoarse. “Decider. Thirty-one all. Three. Two. One—“

Salty had told Yan once, in the winter, what aura felt like to him. Like trying to lift something that’s bolted to the floor. He could feel it there. He could get his hands round it. And it just wouldn’t come up.

Mashi had said, in the spring, when he finally does write, it’ll come out big and stupid and all at once.

Mashi had been right.

It came up in Salty the way a bonfire comes up when someone throws oil on it. All at once, from nothing, with a sort of whump that Yan felt in his own chest from across the room. One instant there was a forge, banked and steady. The next there was a furnace. Heat and light, roaring up from somewhere at the very bottom of Salty and pouring out through his shoulders and down his arm and into his hand.

Into Rhea’s hand.

Rhea’s arm went down so fast nobody saw it move.

And the table went with it.

It was a good table. It was oak. It was older than any of them, and three inches thick, and it had survived seven years of soldiers’ suppers and eight months of arm-wrestling. It split straight down the middle with a crack like a tree coming down in a storm, and the two halves fell away from each other, and Rhea and Salty went down between them in a heap of splinters and porridge bowls and Maigo’s chalk, with their hands still locked together.

For about three heartbeats, the mess hall was completely silent.

Then Rhea sat up out of the wreckage, with oak shavings in her hair and porridge down one side of her face, and looked at Salty, who was sitting up too, staring at his own hand as if it belonged to someone else.

“That’s not a win,” said Rhea. “That’s vandalism.”

The hall exploded.

Salty opened his window sitting in the wreckage of the table, with half of Cinderhold trying to slap him on the back at once. He looked at it for a long time. Then he turned it round and showed it to Wendy, without a word, the way he did everything.

Wendy read it. Her face did something complicated.

“Drawn,” she said. “You’re Drawn. You’re first tier.” She looked up at him. “You broke a table and went up a tier.”

“Yeah,” said Salty.

“That’s the stupidest way anyone has ever—“

“Yeah.”

“—in the history of this world, probably—“

“Yeah.” He was starting to grin. It spread across his blunt square face very slowly, the way things did with Salty, until it was enormous. “Big and stupid,” he said. “All at once.”

Mashi, beside Wendy, put his face in his hands and started laughing so hard he had to sit down on half of the table.

“I told you,” he said, muffled. “I told you. In the spring.”

Maigo was already at the wall by the door with a fresh lump of chalk, wiping out the tally. Under it, in big loopy letters, he wrote: SALTY 32, RHEA 31, TABLE 0. And under that, after a moment’s thought: FOURTH.

Aldous, at the back of the hall, was laughing too, very quietly, with his hand over his eyes.

“That,” he said, to nobody in particular, “was the mess table.”

Yan felt it, standing by the door in the middle of all that noise: Salty’s candle, a furnace now, roaring and settling and roaring again, filling the whole hall with heat. He felt Wendy’s beside it, cool and steady and bright with something that might have been pride. He felt Mashi’s two, side by side, closer than they’d been in the spring. Thirteen and thirteen, Mashi had said that morning, very pleased. Still not touching. But closer.

And he felt the thread.

Far away. Very faint. North-west, under all of it, still there. Still going. Cold.

He didn’t say anything. He didn’t want to spoil it. He just stood by the door and let Cinderhold be happy for an evening, with the smashed table and the porridge and Rhea demanding a rematch, right now, on the floor, I don’t care, and held on to the thread on his own, the way he’d held everything that mattered, all his life, on his own.

He thought about his mother that night, on the wall.

He did, sometimes, when it was very late and very quiet. Not often. It hurt too much to do often. But sometimes, on a night like this, with the stars very clear and something cold somewhere out there in the dark, she came into his head on her own, and he let her stay.

She’d be at work, probably, right now. Or asleep. He didn’t know what time it was on Earth. He didn’t know how long it had been. Nearly a year here. Nearly a year there, presumably. Nearly a year since he’d left for a weekend and not come back. Nearly a year of her ringing his phone, and it going to voicemail, and her ringing again.

His brothers and sisters wouldn’t have helped. They never had. They’d have said he’d run off. They’d have said typical. They’d have left her on her own with it.

He’d asked the Advisor, in the hall, the first day. How long would it take to reach Sun? The Advisor hadn’t answered. Yan had known, from the not-answering, that it was years. A lot of years. More than his mother had.

So he’d made a decision, that first night, lying on the barracks floor with Suzu round his throat. He’d decided he would reach Sun. Whatever it took. Not for the kingdom. Not for the demon king. For one woman, in a small flat, with a phone in her hand.

He hadn’t told anyone. Not even Salty. He carried it the way he carried everything. On his own.

Except that wasn’t quite true, and hadn’t been since the hall. Suzu knew. She’d known before he’d finished deciding it; it had gone down the cord between them the way everything did. And she’d known the other half of it, too, the half he wouldn’t let himself look at. On Earth, Suzu had been a drawing. If he ever found the door, and walked through it into his mother’s kitchen, he didn’t know what would walk through it with him. Whether anything would.

She’d put her head under his chin that night, on the barracks floor, and kept it there until morning. She hadn’t asked him to choose. She never would. That was the worst of it, and the best.

Thirty-five, said his window, when he looked. Up one, that week. Slow.

He closed it, and sat with Suzu on the wall, and felt for the thread, and held on.

On the seventh night, the boy from Valcrest asked what Ice had been like.

Yan was on the wall when it came in. He’d been reading the channel with half his mind and holding the thread with the other half, the way he did now every night. He read Hako’s four lines, and felt something in his chest go tight and warm at once.

He wrote first. He couldn’t help it. He carried me in CS. I carried him in Apex. The tournament. The final circle. And Ice is down. Tragic. The crowd is devastated. He was smiling as he wrote it, alone on the wall in the dark, with Suzu’s head tucked under his chin. He hadn’t thought about that in years. He’d forgotten how funny it had been. How Ice had narrated his own death with such enormous gravity that Yan had nearly lost the final fight from laughing.

Then the others came in. Maigo, shouting about the Machine. Gabe and the choir. The lines rose up the channel and slid off the top, and Yan read them, and laughed, and felt his eyes sting, and held the thread.

The thread was very faint tonight. Fainter than ever. It had been moving all evening, slowly, and then, an hour or so ago, it had stopped. Settled. Gone still, somewhere very far away to the north-west, in the way it went still in the mornings. Except it wasn’t morning. It was the middle of the night.

It had stopped somewhere. And it was waiting.

Yan felt his skin prickle. He set the channel aside in his mind, and closed his eyes, and went quiet, and reached for it as far as he could reach.

There. Cold. Still. Waiting. Watching something.

And then it was gone.

He didn’t understand, at first.

He thought he’d lost it. That had happened before, on the third and fourth nights, when it was very faint: he’d lose his grip on the thread for a moment and have to feel for it again in the dark. He felt for it now.

Nothing.

Not faint. Not far. Nothing. The thread ended in his fingers like a cut string. There was nothing on the other end of it. No cold. No shape. No tug. Just the end of it, in his hand, and then nothing at all.

It had gone out.

Not slowly. Not the way the dying go out, the way he’d felt soldiers go out in the infirmary in the winter, a candle burning lower and lower and guttering and finally going dark. All at once. In one instant. As if someone had reached out and pinched it between two fingers.

Yan sat on the wall with his eyes closed and his heart pounding and the cut thread in his hand.

Then he did the only thing he could think of. He reached out, as far as he’d ever reached, further than he’d reached on the first night, along the line of the thread, north-west, toward the place where it had gone out. He reached until it hurt. Until his head rang and his nose started to bleed and Suzu was hissing at him, frantic, wound tight round his throat.

He wanted to feel what had done it.

Something had. Something had been there, where the cold one was. Something had reached out in the dark and ended it, in a single instant, without a struggle, without a sound. Something strong enough to do to a bonfire of cold what you’d do to a candle-wick.

He reached and reached, and felt nothing.

No. Not nothing. That was the strange part. That was the part he’d turn over, on his own, for months. It wasn’t that there was nothing there. It was that there was too much. He reached out toward where the cold one had died, and what he felt wasn’t absence. It was a kind of enormous, quiet fullness. A stillness so big that it didn’t feel like anything at all, the way you can’t feel the air you’re breathing. Like reaching out a hand to touch something and finding it was all around you, and had been all along.

Like trying to see the sea from inside it.

He let go. He had to. He opened his eyes and found himself sitting on the wall with blood running from his nose and Suzu’s face pressed against his cheek and the stars very bright and very far above him.

The thread was still in his hand. Cut.

He didn’t tell anyone that night.

He wiped his face. He soothed Suzu, who was furious with him. He sat on the wall until dawn, holding the cut end of the thread, and turning it over, on his own, the way he always did.

Something out there had killed the cold one in one stroke. Something he couldn’t feel, not because it was too small, but because it was too big. Something that had been there all along, all the time, so large and so still that he’d never noticed it, the way you never notice the sea until you’re drowning in it.

And it had been somewhere north-west. Somewhere along that long wide wedge of the map that held every posting but his own.

He thought about the horns over Cinderhold’s gate. Only one man alive could have cut these off. He thought about Aldous, in the autumn, looking at the horns with his face gone white. He thought about the scouts, fourteen of them, coming to the edge of the red and seeing the gate and running.

He thought about the man who’d planted the flowers on the grave, and carried a sword across the Redmarch alone, like firewood.

He didn’t let himself finish the thought. Not yet. It wasn’t time. He didn’t know enough. He’d carry it on his own until he did.

At dawn, he opened his window and wrote to the channel. Plainly, the way the rules said.

[ Suzuyun: The cold one’s gone.
Suzuyun: I’ve been holding a thread of it all week. Last night, a little after one, it went out. All at once.
Suzuyun: Something killed it. One stroke.
Suzuyun: I couldn’t feel what. I tried. ]

The answers came quickly. Relief, mostly. Questions. Where? What killed it? Are you sure? Tiger: THANK GOD. Malcolm: Adding a page. Kei: Yan, are you okay? Hisui says your last line looks tired. Chris, careful: a little after one, which way? can you say how far? And Yan answered what he could, which wasn’t much.

He noticed something, after a while. He didn’t know why he noticed it. He noticed things like that; he always had.

Group 1 hadn’t answered.

Xola had. Oh thank goodness. PenguChi had. ☺ thank you yan. Even Gigichi had, good. go to sleep. But two people who always answered, who answered everything, who’d been awake half the night writing stories about a man they’d loved, hadn’t written a word.

Thea. And Higu.

Yan looked at that for a long time, sitting on the wall at Cinderhold with the sun coming up over the red. At the two names that weren’t there.

Then he closed his window, and went down to breakfast, and didn’t say anything to anyone. He’d carry that one on his own, too, for now.
