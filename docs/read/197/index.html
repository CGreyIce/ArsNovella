The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 7: Myths

Chapter 197

Not Here

9,062 words · 39 min read

There was a room at every tournament that nobody ever saw.

Not the stage. Not the casters’ desk, or the stream, or the bracket on the big screen with the lines going in toward the middle. Behind all that. A room with a door that said STAFF on it, or nothing at all, with a kettle and a tray of sandwiches that had gone curly at the edges and a few plastic chairs, where the players sat and waited before they went on.

The green room. It was never green. Drew had run eleven tournaments on Earth and not one of the green rooms had been green.

That was the room he’d liked best. He’d never told anybody. Everybody thought the job was the stage, the voice, the being-in-charge; and it was, some of it. But the real job was the room before. Ten people in plastic chairs who’d come to win and were suddenly, now that it was nearly time, frightened out of their minds. Somebody’s leg going. Somebody not eating. Somebody reading the same message on their phone over and over without seeing it. And you went in, with the tray, and you didn’t say don’t be nervous, because nobody in the history of the world had ever stopped being nervous because somebody told them to. You said what’s your mum called, is she watching, I’ll say hi on stream. You said is that the new mouse, does it double-click, mine double-clicks, it’s ruined my life. You learned every name. You learned what each one looked like when they were about to be sick and what they looked like when they were about to laugh, and you aimed for the laugh. You made it a room people wanted to be in. So that when the door opened and somebody said you’re on, they went through it as themselves.

That was all. It wasn’t much. Nobody ever saw it.

He’d been doing it at Thornwall for two years and a half. He hadn’t known that was what he was doing until the autumn, when somebody had opened a door on him.

It had come back to him in pieces, over the winter.

He hadn’t remembered at first. He’d stood at the shutters in the guardhouse with Tiger’s hands on his arms and asked why’s everybody looking at me, and meant it. It was a week before the first piece came, in the night, out of nowhere, the way a word you’ve been trying to remember comes when you’ve stopped trying: a doorway. Warm light. Somebody standing in it.

Then, over the snow, the rest.

It hadn’t been like being pulled. Tiger thought it had. Tiger had held on to his arms so hard they’d bruised, as if Drew had been on the end of a rope and something on the other end had been hauling. It wasn’t like that at all. It was the opposite of that. It was the kindest thing that had ever happened to him. Somebody standing in a lit doorway on a cold night, somebody who’d been waiting a long time, who’d known him all his life and loved him the whole time, saying, very gently, oh, there you are. Come in. Come in out of the cold.

And he’d wanted to. He’d wanted to so much that, even now, months later, lying awake in the bunk room with Tiger breathing on the other side of it, he could feel the shape of the wanting in his chest like the place a tooth had been.

He’d told Kei. Once. On the stool. Nobody else.

But he’d thought about it every night since. What it sounded like. What it said. How it said it. He’d turned it over and over the way you’d turn over a recording of a match you’d lost, looking for the moment. And somewhere in the winter he’d realised that he knew it now. That if he heard it again he’d know it the way you know a voice on a phone before it’s said its name.

He’d never told anybody that either.

Hyvred came down off the tower in the grey with a slate in her hand.

She hadn’t slept. Drew could see that from across the yard. She’d gone to bed when the king said so, because people did what the king said, and she’d lain there in the dark, he’d bet anything, not counting, and then got up before the sky was light and gone round the whole of Thornwall with her glasses in her hair, every house, every door, the way she had every morning for a week. She came across the yard toward the kitchen with the slate held against her chest and her tail very still behind her, which was how you knew.

“Kei’s asleep,” said Drew. He was at the kitchen door with a cup. He’d been there since before she went round; he’d been there every morning since she started. “First time in a week. Don’t wake him.”

She stopped.

“He sat up with the king,” said Drew. “Till late. And then the king went, and he lay down on his bed with his boots on, and Hisui says he went out like a lamp.” He held out his hand. “Give it here.”

Hyvred looked at him for a long moment, with the gold eye still locked, the red crosshair in it. Looking at him. At the edges of him. He knew what she was looking for. She’d looked at him like that every morning since the autumn.

“You’re fine,” she said. Very quietly. She hadn’t said it to anybody all week.

“I know.”

She gave him the slate.

Eleven names. In her round looping hand, in English, because she didn’t write anything else. Not one of them a soldier.

He read them twice. He knew all of them. Of course he did. He’d been running the room for two years and a half.

Annet, the baker’s wife. Ivo the cooper. Marta and Lise at the Bell. Bryn (the shepherd boy). Old Linnet, Spindle Lane. Fenna who does the shirts. Perrin the carter. Nessa at the chandler’s. Teo in the east stable. Merrow (charcoal, came in yesterday).

“Eleven,” said Hyvred. “Since yesterday it’s eleven. And they’re all town, Drew. None of the soldiers. Not one.” Her voice went thin. “It’s stopped asking the soldiers. It’s asking the ones nobody’s watching.”

They talked about it in the yard, in the cold, standing up, the way you talked about things at Thornwall.

Captain Brannoch wanted them in the cells. Not unkindly. Brannoch had been on the Redmarch and had scars down the side of his face like a ploughed field, and he did things the way you did them on the Redmarch, which was the way that kept the most people alive. Eleven names: eleven doors, eleven soldiers, eleven people walked to the cells under the gatehouse and the doors locked and a man outside each one.

“They’re not prisoners,” said Corvin.

“I know they’re not,” said Brannoch. “That’s why they’ll still be alive tomorrow.”

“And when the call comes, and they walk into the door of the cell, and keep walking into it?” said Corvin. He’d put his good coat on again, badly. “Over and over? With their hands? You’ve not seen it. You’ve seen Dunn on the wall, for a minute. It doesn’t stop because there’s a door.”

Hild said nothing. She stood with her one eye shut, listening, the way she did.

Fenwick had a report under his arm.

And the king stood a little apart from all of them, by the foot of the tower stair, in his plain coat, with his gloves in his hand. He’d come down to go up. Hyvred was meant to be taking him. He hadn’t said anything. He was listening the way Hild listened, with his whole big grey head a little on one side, and every now and then he looked at whoever was talking, and you felt it.

Drew waited till they’d both finished.

“Let me fetch them,” he said.

They looked at him.

“Don’t send soldiers,” said Drew. “You send a soldier to somebody’s door at dawn, with a spear, and say come with me, they think it’s the end of the world. They think their son’s dead. And then they’re frightened all day, and frightened people—“ He stopped. He didn’t know how to say what he meant, which was that frightened people had a door open in them already. That he’d felt it. “Let me go round. Let me knock. I’ll tell them what’s happening. And I’ll ask them to come and spend the day in the long room, with me, and some bread, and the fire lit. Not a cell. A room.” He looked at Brannoch. “And you put your soldiers outside it. As many as you want. Just not inside. And no spears.”

“And if they won’t come?” said Brannoch.

“They’ll come,” said Drew. “Everybody comes to a party.”

Brannoch looked at Corvin. Corvin looked at Hild. Hild didn’t open her eye.

The king said, from the foot of the stair: “You’re the one who ran the Lists.”

Drew turned. “Yes, Majesty.”

“Lucan’s Lists. The trials. With the bracket.” The king looked at him. A long, slow, steady look, like a man looking along a bar of iron to see if it was true. “Lucan wrote me about you. Four pages. He said you could make a room of forty thousand people go quiet by lowering your voice.” A pause. “He said he’d never once seen you stop smiling. He said it frightened him a bit.”

“It frightens me a bit,” said Drew.

The king’s beard moved. It might have been a smile.

“Go on, then,” said the king. “Fetch your eleven. Captain, give him his room.” He put his gloves on. “Young woman. A hundred and twelve steps.”

He went round the doors.

It took him the best part of two hours. He did it the way he’d have done it on Earth, if Earth had been a fortress town at the foot of a mountain range in another world with something standing at the bottom of the pass: one at a time, at the door, with his hands where they could see them. He told every one of them the truth. He’d decided that, going across the yard. You didn’t lie to the green room. They always knew.

Hyvred’s seen something. On you. The thing she saw on Corporal Dunn, and on Mother Ivey, and the Joss lad; only earlier. It might be today. It might be tomorrow. You might hear somebody, asking you to go north. Asking you in. I heard it, in the autumn. I know what it sounds like. I’d like you to come and spend the day in the long room, with me, so that if it asks you, there’s somebody there who knows.

Annet, the baker’s wife, at the back door of the bakery with flour to her elbows and four loaves in the oven, said “I’ve bread in,” and then looked at his face and said, “I’ll bring it.” Ivo the cooper, who was deaf in his left ear and suspicious of everything that happened on that side of him, made him say it twice and then said “Huh,” and put his coat on. The Dacey sisters at the Bell, Marta and Lise, who’d kept the inn together for thirty years and had argued every single day of the thirty, argued about whether to come, and then about who’d mind the inn, and then about whose fault it was, and came together, still arguing. Bryn, the shepherd boy, sixteen, went red to the ears that his name was on a list and that Drew knew it, and said he was fine, honest, and then, at the door, very quietly, “Is it the thing that had Ossie?” and Drew said “Yes,” because you didn’t lie, and Bryn came.

Old Linnet, at the top of Spindle Lane, brought her spindle.

Fenna, who did the garrison’s shirts and had done since her husband died in the first spring, didn’t say anything at all. She just took her shawl off the peg and came out and pulled the door shut behind her, and walked down the lane beside him with her hands folded, and Drew knew from the way she walked that she’d been waiting for somebody to knock on that door for two years.

Perrin the carter wanted to know if there’d be beer. Nessa at the chandler’s, who was fourteen, wanted to know if the king was really in the fort, and whether he was really as big as people said, and whether he’d come to the long room, and Drew said he was bigger and he didn’t know. Teo, in the east stable, who’d been standing in the next stall when the groom was called last week, had already packed a bag, as if he were going away. Drew made him leave it.

Merrow the charcoal-burner he found asleep in the Bell’s hayloft, black to the eyebrows, having walked in off the east ridge the night before with a load for the smithy. He sat up in the hay and listened to all of it with his black face perfectly still and said, “So that’s what I heard.”

Drew stopped.

“Last night,” said Merrow. “Coming in. Down the ridge track, in the dark. Somebody calling my name. Kindly. From up the valley.” He scratched his ear and a little black dust came off it. “I thought I’d dreamed it. I nearly turned round.” He looked at Drew. “I nearly did.”

“I know,” said Drew.

“You know,” said Merrow. Not a question. He looked at Drew’s face for a while longer, black-eyed in a black face in the hayloft dark. Then he got up and brushed the hay off and came.

He made it a green room.

It wasn’t hard. It was what he was for. The long room in the guardhouse, where they ate, with the long table and the benches and the big hearth at the end. He got Cook to light the fire and make tea in the big urn, and Annet’s four loaves came out of her basket still warm and went round with the good butter, and the Dacey sisters immediately started arguing about the butter. He sat them where they could see the fire and not the shutters. He didn’t open the shutters. He sat with his back to them himself.

Bee came in the middle of the morning, with her chalk, and drew dogs on the hearthstones for Nessa. Bee didn’t like children; she said so. She drew Nessa nine dogs, one after another, each in one unbroken line, and the ninth one sat up on the hearthstone and wagged at her, and Nessa shrieked, and Bee said “Don’t scream, he doesn’t like it,” and the dog, who’d liked it very much, lay down on Nessa’s feet.

Old Linnet spun. Ivo told Perrin, at length, on the deaf side, what was wrong with the new barrels the garrison had sent up from Ashby. Bryn ate five slices of bread and then sat very straight on the bench and tried not to look at the shutters. Fenna mended a shirt. She’d brought one. Teo sat in the corner with his arms round his knees.

And Drew talked.

He talked the way he talked at supper, the way he’d talked on stream for years and in green rooms for eleven tournaments: easily, about nothing, with his elbows on the table and his hands going. He told them about the Lists. About Lucan, with his forty-foot bracket and his voice like a trumpet with a cold. About the man who’d fallen off the Banner mound into the water-trough, and about forty thousand people in the stands at Valcrest going so quiet you could hear a fox girl breathe. He told them about the tournaments at home without saying where home was. He told the Dacey sisters he’d once run a tournament where two teams had turned up claiming the same slot and had argued about it for an hour, and the sisters both said well, whose slot was it, and he said it was both of theirs, actually, he’d double-booked it, and the whole room laughed, even Teo, in the corner, a little.

You aimed for the laugh.

Outside the door, Brannoch’s soldiers stood in the passage. Two of them. No spears. Drew had checked.

He watched them all, while he talked. Every face. The way he’d watched the green rooms. Who wasn’t eating. Who was holding a cup too tight. He watched for the thing Hyvred saw and he couldn’t, and didn’t see it, because he couldn’t see it, because he wasn’t her. But he watched anyway. He’d learned to watch people on Earth for the moment before. Here, he’d learned it twice.

Toward noon, Nessa said, from the hearth, with a chalk dog on her feet: “Tell the goose one.”

He stopped.

“Bee says there’s a goose one,” said Nessa. “About Lucan. She says you never finish it.”

Bee, on the hearthstones, didn’t look up.

“I never finish it,” said Drew, “because every time I get to the good bit, something happens.”

“What happens?”

“Last time,” said Drew, “I got called.”

The room went very quiet.

He hadn’t meant to say it. Then he found he didn’t mind. You didn’t lie to the green room.

“Here,” he said. “At this table. In the autumn. At supper. I was telling the goose one, and I got to the good bit, and I stood up and walked to that window.” He nodded at the shutters behind him, without turning round. “I don’t remember it. Everybody else does. Tiger does. Tiger’s never going to forget it as long as he lives.” He smiled. “So I’ve never finished the goose one.”

“Finish it now,” said Nessa.

“All right,” said Drew.

One night in the week of the Lists, in Valcrest, a goose had got into the sand.

Nobody knew how. Nobody ever found out whose it was. It was just there, at midnight, in the middle of the sand, under the forty-foot bracket, a large grey goose with a very low opinion of everybody, and it wouldn’t leave. The night watch tried. Two grooms tried. A boy with a broom tried and came back without the broom. And at last somebody went and woke Lucan, because Lucan was in charge of the Lists, and Lucan came down from his rooms at one in the morning in a purple dressing gown with his hair standing up, and Drew, who’d been up late with the bracket, came down too, because he wouldn’t have missed it for anything.

“And Lucan walks out onto the sand,” said Drew, “in the dressing gown, very slow, very grand, with his hands out, like this. The way he walks out for the crowd. And he uses the voice. You know the voice. The one he uses for kings.”

The Dacey sisters knew the voice. Everybody in the kingdom knew the voice, now, from the ballads.

“And he says,” said Drew. “He says: Madam.”

The room was laughing already. Nessa had her hands over her mouth.

“Madam,” said Drew, in the voice. “These are the Lists of Valcrest. Kings have stood upon this sand. Champions have—”

And stopped.

It was very faint.

He’d have missed it, if he’d been anyone else. Anyone who hadn’t heard it before. It was like the first note of a song coming through a wall from another room, so quiet you weren’t sure, so quiet you’d think you’d imagined it, except that you knew the song, you knew it in your bones, and your bones had already started to sing along before you’d decided anything.

There you are.

Behind him. Through the shutters. From the north.

Oh, there you are.

He kept his face exactly where it was. He’d had years of keeping his face where it was. He kept smiling at Nessa, at the hearth, with the chalk dog on her feet; he kept his hands where they’d been, out in front of him, mid-story, Madam; and inside, somewhere under his ribs, something in him turned toward the voice the way a plant turns toward a window, and he felt it turn, and he held it.

Not you, he thought. Very clearly. Not today. I’m working.

And then he felt it go past him.

That was the worst thing. That was the thing he’d remember. It went past him, the way a wave goes past a post. Into the room. And all round him, at the long table, on the benches, at the hearth, eleven people lifted their faces.

Not quickly. The way you’d look up from a book if somebody said your name.

Annet first. With a slice of bread in her hand and butter on it. Then Ivo, on the deaf side, as if he’d heard it on the side he couldn’t hear on. Then the Dacey sisters, together, at the same moment, stopping in the middle of an argument; the first time in thirty years, Drew thought, from somewhere very far off, that they’d both stopped at once. Bryn. Old Linnet, with the spindle going round and round in her lap and her hands gone still on it. Perrin. Fenna, with the needle in the shirt. Teo, in the corner, unfolding. Merrow, by the fire, black-faced, very slowly, as if he’d been expecting it.

Nessa.

They were smiling.

All of them. The same smile. Not the same face; eleven faces, old and young, red and pale and black with charcoal; the same smile on all of them, very gentle, very glad, like people who’d been out in the cold a long time and had just seen a lit window.

At the hearth, all nine chalk dogs stood up, and barked.

They stood up.

All together. Benches going back. Annet put her bread down on the table, carefully, the way you’d put something down that you meant to come back for. Old Linnet put her spindle in her lap and then, when she stood, it rolled off onto the floor and went on turning by itself on the boards and she didn’t look down. Fenna laid the shirt over the end of the bench with the needle still in it.

They turned to the north wall. To the shutters. And then, when there was nothing there but shutters, they turned to the door.

“Annet,” said Drew.

He was on his feet. He didn’t remember getting up.

“Annet. Ivo. Marta. Lise.” He said them in the voice. The room voice. The one that made forty thousand people quiet. “Bryn. Sit down for me. Nessa. Nessa, love, sit down, I haven’t finished.”

They didn’t hear him. He hadn’t thought they would. He’d been one of them. He knew exactly how far away his own voice would sound, from in there: like somebody calling from the bottom of a garden, while you stood in a doorway with the warm light on your face.

They went to the door.

The two soldiers in the passage were young. Brannoch’s lads, from Ashby, in their first spring. They’d been told to stop anyone coming out, and they put their arms across the door, and Annet walked into them.

She didn’t push. That was the thing. None of them pushed. She just walked, the way you’d walk into a wind, and when the boy’s arm was across her she kept walking, with her soft floury hands at her sides and the smile on her face, slowly, steadily, into his arm, as if the arm weren’t there. And behind her Ivo did the same, and Perrin, and the sisters, and the boy’s arm began to bend, and the boy’s face went white, because he understood, all at once, that the only way to stop her was to hurt her. To really hurt her. To put her on the floor and hold her there, and go on holding her, and then do the same to the next one, and the next, eleven of them, while they smiled at him.

“Let them through,” said Drew.

The boy looked at him over Annet’s shoulder. Crying.

“Let them through,” said Drew. “Don’t hurt them. It’s all right. Walk with them. Don’t let go of them, just walk with them. I’m coming.”

The boy let go.

They went down the passage. Out into the yard. Into the spring morning, the cold sun, the mud, the wet stones steaming. Eleven people walking across the yard of Thornwall in a loose slow line, smiling, with their faces lifted toward the north wall and the mountains behind it, and soldiers walking alongside them with their hands out, not touching, not knowing what to do.

And from the top of the north tower, a long way up, a voice he knew. Hyvred’s. Screaming.

“PRUETT. DREW. THE GATE.”

Old Pruett had kept the north postern for forty years.

It was a small gate, the postern. A door, really, set deep in the thickness of the north wall, oak and iron, with a bar across it as thick as a man’s leg and a little stone room beside it where Pruett sat all day on a stool with his pipe and let the woodcutters in and out, and the patrols, and the charcoal-burners coming down off the hills with their loads. He’d been there so long that nobody saw him. He was part of the wall. He was so much part of the wall that Hyvred, going round every door in Thornwall at dawn with her glasses in her hair, had walked straight past him, and nodded, and he’d nodded back, and she hadn’t looked.

He wasn’t on the list.

He was standing at the postern now. Drew could see him from across the yard. A little bent old man in a leather apron, with both hands on the great bar, lifting it, with a strength he hadn’t had in twenty years. And smiling.

The bar came up out of its brackets and went down on the stones with a sound like a dropped bell.

The postern swung open.

And beyond it, the valley road. Going north. Straight and grey and wet between the last walled fields, and then out into the open valley, the long brown floor of it steaming in the sun, all the way to the Greyspine, white on white, and the dark throat of the pass between the peaks.

Pruett walked through it.

The eleven followed him.

Twelve.

Hild was at the postern.

From nowhere. The way Hild was always from nowhere. She stood in the arch of the open gate with her one eye shut and her sword in its scabbard and her hand not on it, and Drew, coming across the yard at a run, saw her see them. Not with the eye. With everything else. Hear them, smell them, feel them in the stones. Twelve people walking at her, smiling.

She was the sixth tier. She could have put all twelve of them on the ground in the time it took to say their names. Drew had seen her move. She could have done it without hurting any of them very much.

And then they’d get up. And walk again. Tomorrow, or tonight. With bruises.

“Hild,” said Drew. “Don’t.”

She turned her head toward him, with her eye still shut.

“Let me,” said Drew. He didn’t know what he meant. He didn’t know what he was going to do. He only knew, the way you know in the last round when the plan’s gone and there’s no time to make another one, that he had to be in front of them. Not behind them. Not beside them, with his hands out. In front.

“Let me try something,” he said.

Hild didn’t move for a moment.

Then she stepped aside. Out of the arch. Against the wall.

“Go on,” said Hild. “I’m behind you.”

And something came across the yard behind him like a gust of wind, a blur of teal coat and brown ears, faster than anything in Thornwall, faster than anything in the kingdom, and Hild’s hand came out without her looking and closed on the back of its collar, and stopped it dead.

“Drew,” said Tiger. From the end of Hild’s arm. His feet still going. “DREW—“

“His,” said Hild.

Drew didn’t look back. He couldn’t. If he looked at Tiger’s face he’d stop.

He went through the gate.

He was slow. He’d always been slow. He’d told Tiger so once, at a postern gate with frost on the iron, I’m really slow, you’d have to wait for me, and it was true; he was the slowest of the five of them by a long way, a brawler, built for standing still and letting things come to him. He ran up the valley road in the cold sun with his lungs burning and the mud sucking at his boots, past Teo, past the Dacey sisters, past Ivo and Annet and Merrow, past Old Pruett at the front with his leather apron and his lifted face. None of them looked at him. He went by them as if he were a cart going by on the road.

Fifty paces past the postern, where the last field wall ended and the valley opened out, he stopped. And turned round.

And stood in the road.

They came up the road toward him.

Twelve. In a loose straggling line, the way people walk home from somewhere together, not in step. Old Pruett. Annet, with flour still on her arms to the elbow. Ivo. The sisters, side by side. Bryn, the shepherd boy, who’d asked is it the thing that had Ossie. Fenna with her hands folded. Perrin. Old Linnet, slow, very slow, on her bad hip. Teo. Merrow, black to the eyebrows. Nessa, last, fourteen, with a chalk dog trotting beside her, barking and barking and barking, and her not looking down.

Behind them, the postern, and Hild in it, and Tiger at the end of Hild’s arm. Behind that, the wall, and the soldiers on it, and the north tower, and two small figures at the top of it, one with golden horns and one much too big.

In front of Drew: nothing. Him.

Behind Drew: the valley. Eleven miles of it. The rings, which he couldn’t see. The thing at the bottom of the pass, which nobody could see. The pass road going up between its walls of snow, swept.

He put his hand in his coat pocket and felt the bladed knuckles there. Cold. The Thornwall smith had made them for him in the first month, because aura needed a real blade below the seventh tier, everybody said so, Hild said so; you couldn’t put it into a bare fist. He had them in his pocket every day. He’d hit a lot of things with them. Practice dummies. Hild’s practice sword, once, by accident. Things on the wall, in the dark, that weren’t anybody.

He took his hand out of his pocket. Empty.

He wasn’t going to hit anybody. Not these.

He stood in the middle of the valley road with his big bare hands open at his sides, and waited for them.

“Annet,” said Drew.

She was ten paces off. Then eight. Smiling.

“Annet. It’s Drew.” The room voice. Very low. “I know. I know what you’re hearing. Somebody in a doorway. Somebody kind. Somebody who’s been waiting for you all your life and loves you and wants you to come in out of the cold.” He breathed. “I heard it too. Here. In the autumn. I know exactly what it sounds like.”

Six paces.

“It’s lying,” said Drew.

Four.

She walked into him.

Not hard. The way she’d walked into the boy’s arm in the passage: slowly, steadily, smiling, as if he weren’t there. Her floury hands at her sides. Her soft warm weight against his chest. And beside her Ivo, against his shoulder, and Pruett, and then all of them, one after another, coming up the road and walking into him and not stopping, the way water doesn’t stop, the way the meltwater in the valley Hyvred talked about didn’t stop, going through the absence as if it weren’t there. Twelve people leaning on him, gently, patiently, smiling, walking.

His boots slid in the mud. A foot. Another foot.

He couldn’t hold twelve. Nobody could hold twelve. Not without hurting them.

And then it came for him.

From behind.

From up the valley. From the pass. Down the swept grey road, down eleven miles of steaming valley floor, through the place at the foot of the pass where Hyvred’s sight found nothing; and through him. Not past him, this time. Not like a wave past a post. Through. Into the twelve people leaning on his chest. He felt it go through him into them like a current goes through a wire, and he felt them answer it, all twelve, a little lift in each of them, a little yes, and their weight came on harder.

And it said his name.

Drew.

It was so kind.

Oh, Drew. There you are.

It was the doorway. The warm light. Somebody standing in it, behind him, at his back, who’d known him all his life. He only had to turn round. He only had to turn round and he’d see who it was. He’d been wondering all winter who it was. He’d lain awake in the bunk room wondering. I’ve been waiting for you, he’d thought, in the autumn, at the shutters. I don’t even know who I meant.

He could find out. He only had to turn round.

Come in, it said. Come in out of the cold. All of you. Bring them. Bring them with you. You’re so good with people. They’ll come if you come. Come in.

He nearly did.

He’d never tell anyone how nearly. Not Kei. Not Tiger. His shoulders had started to turn. His feet in the mud had started to turn. Twelve people leaning on his chest, and the warmth at his back, and the voice, and he’d started to turn round toward it with them, the way you’d turn round in a crowd to see who’d called you, and the smile was coming up onto his face from somewhere underneath, not his smile, the painting smile, the one with nobody behind it—

And he thought of the green room.

He didn’t know why. He’d think about it for years and never know why. Of all the things. Not Tiger, not the pass, not Ossie in the third ring with his face turned up. A room with plastic chairs and curly sandwiches, and ten frightened people, and a door with nothing written on it. And somebody, any minute, opening the door and saying you’re on.

And him standing in front of it.

That was the job. That was the whole job. You didn’t let them go through the door until they could go through it as themselves.

Not you, he thought.

He said it out loud.

“Not here.”

It was what he’d said to Tiger. On the top of the pass, in the first summer, at midnight, with his hands flat on Tiger’s chest and something with Ossie’s face calling up from the valley. Not here. We’re going home. It was what he’d said to him again at the postern that winter, with frost on the iron. It was what Gigichi had written into a hawthorn hedge at Wrenhaven, in English, the first spring. Every other time, he’d said it to somebody he loved, to keep them from the thing in the valley.

This time he said it to the thing.

He didn’t hold anybody back. He didn’t have to.

The call came through him. It came down the valley at his back with everything it had, kindly, the whole weight of that lit doorway, and went through him into twelve people. And somewhere under his ribs, where the wanting had sat all winter like the place a tooth had been, something that had been waiting there a long time woke up and did what Drew had always done.

It waited for the other one to commit.

And the call committed. All of it. All at once. Twelve people and him, everything it had.

And it answered.

His hands went into his pocket on their own. He didn’t decide it. The way a fighter’s hands come up. They went in and came out and the bladed knuckles were on them, both hands, cold, the short blades out over his knuckles; and he didn’t close his fists. He couldn’t. He held his hands open, palms out, flat against the air in front of him, fingers spread, the blades on the backs of them catching the sun, and something came up out of the middle of him and down his arms and into the steel.

And went back.

Along the same line.

He felt it go. He’d never felt anything like it in his life. Like a blow you’d taken full in the chest coming back out of you, the whole weight of it, exactly as hard as it had come in, along exactly the line it had come in on, and not one inch to either side. Through the twelve. Off his open hands. Up the valley. Up the road, eleven miles, the way it had come, like a ball thrown back to the hand that threw it.

The voice at his back stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. Like a door slammed in a quiet house.

Annet blinked.

She was leaning on his chest with her floury hands at her sides. She blinked again, and her face came down, slowly, out of the light, and she looked at him from about an inch away, and the smile went, and something else came instead.

“Drew?” said Annet.

“Hello, Annet.”

“What—“ She looked down at herself. At the mud. At the valley road under her feet, and the open fields, and the sun. “I’ve bread in,” said Annet, and burst into tears.

All round him. All along the line. Faces coming down. Ivo, blinking on his deaf side. The Dacey sisters, both at once, staring at each other; and then Marta said what are you doing out here and Lise said what are YOU doing out here and they were off. Bryn sitting down very suddenly in the mud of the road with his hands over his face. Fenna, standing perfectly still, with her hands still folded, looking north, up the valley, at the pass, for a long time, as if she’d been somewhere just now and was trying to remember where. Merrow, black-faced, saying very quietly to nobody: “So that’s what it was.”

Old Pruett, at the front, in his leather apron, looking round him in complete bewilderment, and then back down the road at his postern, open, with its great bar lying on the stones.

“Who,” said Old Pruett, “opened my gate?”

And Drew laughed.

It came out of him before he could stop it. A big helpless shout of a laugh, standing in the mud of the valley road with twelve people round him and his hands still up and open in front of him with the blades on, and he couldn’t stop. He laughed till he had to bend over. Nessa, at the back, with her face still wet, started laughing too, because he was. The chalk dog sat down in the road and stopped barking and looked at him with its head on one side.

His hands were shaking. He looked at them.

There was a window in front of them.

Counter.

He stood and looked at it for a long time.

Of course it was. He’d been a counter-fighter all his life. In every game he’d ever played. In every green room. You didn’t go first. You waited, and you watched, and you let the other one show you what they were going to do, and when they’d done it, when they’d put everything into it and couldn’t take it back, you gave it back to them. That was all. That was all he’d ever been good at.

He’d just never thought you could do it to a voice.

He waited. Standing in the road, with twelve people round him crying and laughing and arguing and sitting in the mud, he waited, very carefully, for the doorway to open again at his back.

It did. A little. Faint. A long way off. Somebody, eleven miles up the valley, trying again; a thread of it, coming down the road toward him, very gently, Drew—

And sliding off.

He felt it slide off. The way a punch slides off a good guard. It came at him, and something in him that hadn’t been there an hour ago turned it, without his asking, without his even having to think, and sent it back up the road the way it had come. Smaller. A tap. But back.

It couldn’t get in.

He stood there and understood it, slowly, the way you understand a scoreline you’ve been staring at.

It couldn’t get in any more.

Kei came out of the postern at a run.

In his shirt, with his boots unlaced and his hair down and the satchel already on his shoulder, the one with the flasks in it, five of them, with green thread round their necks. Hisui round his neck, head up, tongue going. He’d been asleep. Drew could see it on him; the first sleep in a week, torn off him halfway through. He came up the road fifty paces at a dead run with his hand already on the satchel’s buckle, and stopped.

And looked at them.

Twelve people in the road, crying and laughing and sitting in the mud. Old Pruett glaring at his gate. Nessa with a chalk dog. And Drew, in the middle of them, with his hands open and the blades on and a window still hanging in the air in front of him, laughing.

Kei’s hand came off the buckle.

He didn’t open the satchel.

He stood in the road with five doses on his shoulder that he didn’t need, and Drew watched his face do the thing it had done perhaps four times in two years and a half, and then do it again.

“Hisui says,” said Kei. His voice wasn’t steady. “Hisui says the air’s changed.”

“Yeah?”

“He says it tastes like somebody slammed a door.” Kei swallowed. “He says, from the inside.”

Tiger came last.

Hild let go of his collar, at the postern, at last. Drew saw her do it, from up the road. She opened her hand and stepped back against the wall, and Tiger came up the valley road toward him like something let off a string, a blur, faster than anything, and Drew braced for it the way you’d brace for a cart.

He didn’t hit. He stopped. A pace off. Dead still, in the road, the way Tiger was never still, with his ears flat and his blue-grey eyes very wide and the XX under the left one standing out against his face, which had gone white.

“You went out the gate,” said Tiger. “On your own.”

“I wasn’t on my own,” said Drew. “There were twelve of them.”

“That’s not what it means.” Tiger’s voice cracked down the middle. “You said. You said, at the postern. Not on your own. Not ever. If you go, I go. You said—“

“I know.”

“You don’t get to say it and then go out the gate on your own—“

“I know,” said Drew. “I know. I’m sorry.”

Tiger looked at him.

Then at the window. Still there, in the air, between them. Counter.

Then back at his face.

And then he did the thing he’d done at the shutters, in the autumn, with Drew’s face lifted to the pass and nobody behind it. He put both arms round him. Not the way Tiger hugged everybody, loudly, with a run-up. Very quietly. With his face in Drew’s shoulder. And held on.

Drew held on back.

He stood in the valley road with his best friend of twelve years holding on to him as if he’d been gone, and twelve people round him, and the pass at his back, swept, and nothing at all coming down it any more that could get in. And over Tiger’s shoulder, a long way back, at the top of the north tower, he saw the two small figures still standing there. The one with the horns had both hands over her face. The big one had his hand flat on his chest.

He walked them home.

That was the job too. Nobody ever saw that part either. After the final, when the stage lights went off and the crowd went home and the stream ended, somebody had to walk the players back to the green room and make sure they had their coats.

He walked Annet to the bakery, where the four loaves in the oven had burned black, and she stood in the doorway of her own kitchen and looked at them and laughed until she cried again. He walked Ivo to the cooperage, on his good side. He walked the Dacey sisters to the Bell, arguing the whole way about whose fault it was that they’d both left the inn. He walked Bryn up to the sheep-pens on the hill above the town, because Bryn wanted to see the sheep, and stood with him a while, and didn’t say anything, and Bryn said, at last, still looking at the sheep, “Was it the same voice? As took Ossie?” and Drew said “I think so,” and Bryn nodded, and said “It was lovely,” very quietly, and then, “I hate it,” and Drew said “Yeah.”

He walked Old Linnet up Spindle Lane at her own speed, which was very slow, and she held his arm, and at her door she said, “I’d have liked to see who it was,” and Drew said, “Me too,” and she patted his hand and went in.

He walked Fenna home last. She didn’t say anything the whole way. At her door she stopped, with her hand on the latch.

“It was Aled’s voice,” said Fenna.

Drew didn’t move.

“My husband,” said Fenna. “In the doorway. It was his voice.” She looked at the latch. “Two years. I’d forgotten what he sounded like. I couldn’t remember it. I’ve been trying.” She was quiet. “And it gave it me back. Just for a minute. On the road.”

“I’m sorry,” said Drew. “I’m sorry I stopped it.”

Fenna looked at him.

“No,” she said. “No, you’re not. And you’re right not to be.” She opened the door. “It wasn’t him. He’d never have asked me to leave the shirts.”

She went in. And then, a moment later, put her head back out.

“The goose,” said Fenna. “Madam. And then what?”

Drew looked at her.

“And then,” he said slowly, “Lucan bows. All the way down. In the purple dressing gown. To a goose. And says, Madam, the sand is yours.”

“And?”

“And it bit him.”

Fenna looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed. A small cracked laugh, the first, he thought, in a long time. And shut the door.

The channel went the way it always went.

He wrote it himself, that afternoon, sitting on the mounting block in the yard with the window open in front of him and his hands still not quite steady. He’d thought about what to put for an hour. In the end he put what he’d have put on stream.
