The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 3: The Anniversary

Chapter 73

Again

3,966 words · 17 min read

PenguChi was good at waiting.

She’d always been good at it. She’d never thought of it as a talent, on Earth. It was just how she was. Other people rushed; she didn’t. Other people got bored, or angry, or gave up; she sat there, calmly, with her hands in her lap, and waited, and usually, if she waited long enough, whatever it was came round to her in the end.

It had made her a good streamer, a little. People came into her chat when they were upset, sometimes, and she’d talk to them in her slow, steady voice about nothing in particular until they weren’t upset anymore. It had made her very bad at games where you had to be fast. And it had made her, here, in this world, the only one of the five of them on the hard path.

Aura took patience. Everybody said so. Maren, and Bram, and every soldier in the militia hall, and Alea, the first week: it’s lower than magic, and slower, and it took me three years. The other four had looked at the hard path and gone the other way, the way you would. PenguChi had looked at it and thought, I can wait three years.

It had taken her one. With help.

She’d been Drawn for a month now. The ninth of all thirty-five; the first of Group 1 to go that way. And every morning in the yard of the militia hall, with a practice sword in her hands and Waddles sitting on the edge of the water trough watching her with one eye closed, she was finding out what came after.

What came after was harder.

She hadn’t known Ice very well.

That was the thing she thought about, sometimes, when the others talked about him. They’d all known him better. Thea and Xola had known him for years. Gigichi had got subs from him, and a message once, you okay?, that she’d never answered. Higu had let him mod her chat. PenguChi had known him as a name in her chat, now and then, when her channel was very small. That guy who comes to stream and is good at Words on Stream.

He had been very good at Words on Stream.

She remembered that much. He’d come in when Thea raided her, the first time, and stayed, and played, and found words she’d never heard of, long ones, and when chat said that’s not a word he’d said it’s Latin. trust. And it had counted, somehow. Because it was also English, or because the game was generous, or because he’d got lucky. She’d laughed so much she’d lost the round.

That was most of what she had. A name, and some words, and trust. When he died, she’d been surprised, and sad in the way you’re sad about someone you meant to get to know, and written something kind under somebody else’s post, and gone on with her life.

And now she was standing in the yard of a militia hall in another world, in a body that had been a cartoon a year ago, with a penguin that had been a plush, Drawn, on the hard path. And every single thing she knew about aura, every lesson that had actually worked, had come up the hill in a fox girl’s mouth, from a man by a lake who’d never once spoken to her.

Don’t push it. Let it rise. Let it go down through your back foot. Call it on the out-breath. Stop keeping it. Give it away.

She’d known him least of all of them. She thought, sometimes, that she might be getting to know him best.

The king’s guard came up the hill on the morning after the sword got its name.

PenguChi saw her first. She usually saw things first, in the yard, because she was usually standing still. A tall dark woman in a plain dark coat, with a sword on her hip, walking up the hill road from the town at a steady pace, alone. Not hurrying. Looking at everything. The hedge, the gate, the yard, the hall. The window.

She stopped at the gate.

Maren had seen her too. Maren went very straight, which was saying something, because Maren was always straight, and put down the practice spear she’d been using to correct Gigichi’s grip, and walked to the gate.

“Kestra,” said Maren.

“Maren.”

They looked at each other. PenguChi had the sense of two cats meeting in a doorway. Both of them guides and guards, both of them with someone to answer to, both of them very good at saying nothing.

“The king sends his regards to the militia of Wrenhaven,” said Kestra. “He’d like to know if it wants anything.”

“The militia of Wrenhaven is well,” said Maren. “And wants for nothing. Thank him.”

“I will.” Kestra didn’t move. “I’d like to watch, if I may. Your training.”

Maren looked at her for a long moment.

“Why?” she said.

“Because I’ve read about them,” said Kestra, “for a year. In reports. And I’ve never seen them.” She glanced past Maren, across the yard, at the five of them, who had all stopped what they were doing and were looking at her. “I’d like to see them.”

She watched all morning.

She stood by the gatepost with her back against it, the way she’d sat on the cabin step, Alea said, all night, every night. And she watched. Not like Bram watched, who called out corrections. Not like Maren, who watched like a teacher, seeing what was wrong. Kestra watched like PenguChi did. Quietly. Seeing what was.

She had a small black book. She took it out sometimes and wrote in it, very briefly, and put it away.

Gigichi didn’t like it.

PenguChi could tell. Gigichi didn’t like being watched at the best of times, and being watched by a Reaching swordswoman from the king’s guard who wrote things down was not the best of times. She went red, and then pale, and then started doing her forms much too hard, and then, after an hour, stopped altogether, and marched across the yard to the gatepost, and stood there with her fists clenched.

“Are you writing about us?” said Gigichi.

“Yes,” said Kestra.

“What?”

“What you do.”

“What did I do?”

Kestra looked at her. Then she took the book out, and opened it, and read, quite flatly, from the page:

“When I came through the gate, the silver-haired one stepped in front of the small one with the creature on her head. Without looking. Before she knew who I was.” She closed the book. “That’s what you did.”

Gigichi stared at her.

Across the yard, Xola had gone very pink. So had Squiddles.

“I didn’t,” said Gigichi.

“You did,” said Kestra. “Your feet did. Feet don’t lie.” She put the book away. “It’s the first thing anyone’s done in this yard all morning that I’d want to write down twice.”

Gigichi opened her mouth, and shut it, and turned round, and walked back across the yard to her place, very fast, with her ears red. And didn’t do her forms too hard after that. PenguChi watched her go, and thought that the king’s guard had just done something to Gigichi in four sentences that Thea had been trying to do for a year.

Thea went to the gatepost at midday.

PenguChi didn’t hear all of it. Nobody did. Thea kept her voice down, and so did Kestra. But PenguChi heard some, because she was nearest, doing her slow drills by the water trough with Waddles watching.

“The channel,” said Kestra. “The thing you made. In the windows.”

“Yes.”

“I can’t see it.”

“No. Nobody born here can. It’s only in ours.”

“Thirty-five people,” said Kestra, “all over the kingdom. Talking every night. Across the whole of Aurelan. No couriers.” She was looking at Thea the way she’d looked at Gigichi. “The Advisor told the king about it in the summer. The king didn’t say anything for a long time.” She paused. “He reads about you twice. Every report Maren sends. He reads the parts about you twice.”

Thea had gone very still.

“Why?” she said.

“Because you did a thing nobody’s ever done, in a year, at the first tier,” said Kestra. “And then gave it away to thirty-four other people, for nothing.” She looked out over the town, at the lake. “He likes people who give things away. I think it’s the only thing he likes.”

PenguChi asked her at the end of the morning.

She hadn’t planned to. She’d been doing her drills by the trough all morning, the new ones, the ones that came after Drawn, and they hadn’t been working. They hadn’t been working for a month. And she’d been feeling Kestra watch her do them, from the gatepost, for three hours. And at the end of the third hour, when Maren called a break, PenguChi put her practice sword down on the edge of the trough, and walked across the yard to the gatepost, and stood in front of the king’s guard, and said:

“You’re Reaching.”

Kestra looked down at her. “Yes.”

“I’m Drawn,” said PenguChi. “A month. I’m stuck.” She said it calmly, the way she said everything. “Would you show me one thing?”

The yard had gone quiet behind her. She could feel the others watching.

Kestra looked at her for a long time. PenguChi didn’t look away. She was good at that. She’d spent a year looking at Alea’s face while Alea told her things her dad said, and not asking, and not looking away.

“One thing,” said Kestra.

She took PenguChi to the middle of the yard. She didn’t draw her own sword. She just stood there, an arm’s length away, with her hands at her sides.

“Strike,” she said. “At the post. The way you’ve been doing all morning.”

There was a practice post in the middle of the yard, an oak stump with a straw man on top, battered by a year of the five of them. PenguChi picked up her practice sword and stood in front of it. She let the aura come up. The way Alea had taught her. Not pushing. Rising, like water in a jar. Down through her back foot. And on the out-breath, she struck.

The sword hit the post with a dull thock. The aura went into it, she could feel it, a little warmth in the wood. Not much. The same as every time.

“Again,” said Kestra.

She struck again. Thock.

“Again.”

Thock.

“Again.”

PenguChi struck the post twenty times. Kestra said again twenty times, in exactly the same voice, flat and patient, and nothing else. No correction. No advice. Just again. The others had stopped pretending to take their break and were all standing round the edge of the yard, watching. Maren too. Bram had his arms folded and an odd look on his face, as if he’d seen this before.

On the twenty-first strike, PenguChi stopped.

She didn’t decide to. She’d lifted the sword, and let the aura come up, and started to breathe out, and something stopped her. Some small thing at the back of her head that said not yet. And she stood there with the sword raised and the aura risen and her breath half out, and waited.

She didn’t know what she was waiting for. She just waited.

And she felt it arrive.

The aura. Not rising, this time. It had already risen. Arriving. The way a wave arrives at a beach after you’ve watched it coming in from far out: not the water rising, the water getting there. It came down her arms and into her hands and into the grip of the practice sword, and she hadn’t pushed it there, and she hadn’t swung yet. It was just there. In the wood. Waiting with her.

She swung.

The post went crack.

Not thock. Crack. Like a branch breaking. The straw man on top jumped a hand’s width into the air and came down crooked, and a long split ran down the oak stump from the place where she’d hit it, and PenguChi stood there with the practice sword in her hands and her mouth open.

“Better,” said Kestra.

“You were swinging before it got there,” said Kestra.

They were sitting on the edge of the water trough. PenguChi didn’t quite know how that had happened. She’d been standing in the middle of the yard staring at the split post, and Kestra had come and taken her by the elbow and walked her to the trough and sat her down, and sat down beside her. Waddles had come and sat on Kestra’s boot. Kestra had looked down at the penguin on her boot for a long moment with no expression at all.

“Why do animals keep doing that,” she said.

“He likes you,” said PenguChi.

“Hens do it to the king. Penguins do it to me.” Kestra looked away. “It’s undignified.” She didn’t move her boot. “You were swinging before it got there. Every time. You’d let it rise, the way somebody taught you. Good. You’d call it on the breath. Good. And then you’d swing, the moment it was up. Before it had arrived.” She looked at PenguChi. “Rising isn’t arriving. You have to wait for it to get where it’s going. And then a little longer.”

PenguChi looked at her own hands.

“That’s what everyone says,” she said slowly. “Everyone who’s good. They all say the same thing, a different way. Wait.” She thought about Alea on the ice, the first week. It’s like waiting for a fish. She thought about a book Cursiffe had sent, with a chapter in it called On Waiting: Most people miss the first tug, because they are too busy pulling.

“Everyone who’s good learned it from someone,” said Kestra.

“Who did you learn it from?”

Kestra was quiet for a moment.

“A boy called Milli,” she said. “In the capital. I told him again for three weeks, every day. He never waited. He’s a cat. Cats don’t wait; they pounce.” She looked at the split post. “And one day he did. Waited. Just once. And I watched him do it, and I thought, oh. That’s what it looks like from outside.” She almost smiled. “You learn it twice. Once doing it. Once watching someone else find it.”

PenguChi didn’t say anything. She looked at the split post, and the crooked straw man, and the king’s guard on the edge of the trough with a penguin on her boot.

“Thank you,” she said.

“Don’t thank me,” said Kestra. “You did it. I only said again.”

Alea came up the hill at noon.

They heard her before they saw her. Or rather, they heard the town. Somewhere down in the market square, a long low murmur that went up into a buzz and then into something like a cheer, and came up the hill road ahead of her, like a wave ahead of a boat. And then Alea came round the corner of the road, walking up the hill, and they saw why.

She had the sword on her back.

It was strapped across her shoulders in a harness of plain leather that somebody, Garrow probably, had made in a hurry, with the black grip standing up over her right shoulder and the point hanging down past her left hip, nearly to the back of her knee. It was enormous. It was much too big for her. She was walking up the hill under it slowly, carefully, with her shoulders very square and her chin very high and her ears very straight, and her face doing its best to look as if this were perfectly ordinary, and failing.

Every child in Wrenhaven was following her up the hill road at a respectful distance.

She came in at the gate. She nodded to Kestra, who nodded back. She walked across the yard, under the sword, to the five of them by the trough, and stopped.

“Hi,” said Alea.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Xola burst into tears, and Gigichi said oh my god very quietly, and Higu said can I and had already started drawing it, and Thea just looked at the sword over Alea’s shoulder with her face very still. And PenguChi got up off the edge of the trough, and went over, and put her arms round Alea, sword and all, and held on.

“You did it,” said PenguChi.

“He did it,” said Alea, into her shoulder. “And the king. I just did the bellows.”

“You lifted it.”

“Three times.” Alea laughed, a little shakily. “I nearly fell over twice.”

“But not the third time,” said PenguChi.

Alea pulled back and looked at her.

“No,” she said. “Not the third time.”

They all held it. Or tried to.

Alea unbuckled the harness and lifted the sword down off her back, the way you’d lift down a sleeping child, and laid it across the edge of the trough, and let them. Thea put her hand on the flat of the blade and went very quiet, the way she did when she was Parsing something, and then took her hand away quickly. Gigichi tried to lift the grip end and couldn’t, and said a word Maren pretended not to hear. Higu drew it. Xola didn’t try to lift it; she just touched the pommel, very gently, with one finger, and Squiddles touched it too.

PenguChi got the grip off the trough. Just. With the aura up and arriving, the way Kestra had shown her, waiting for it, and her whole body shaking. She got it a hand’s width into the air and held it there for three heartbeats, and put it down again, very carefully.

“You’ll be able to swing that,” said Kestra, from the gatepost. “In two years. If you wait.”

“I can wait,” said PenguChi.

And then she saw the letters.

They were near the grip. Just above where the old dark leather began. Scratched into the black iron, crooked, rough, some deeper than others. One gone over twice.

AD ASTRA PER ASPERA.

She’d read them in the channel, in the spring. Cursiffe had posted them, the night he knelt in front of the sword in the capital: I think it’s Latin. And then Gabe, and Deston, and Xav, and John, one after another: Declan used to do that. Latin in everything. His statuses. His Steam groups. He never translated them.

It’s Latin. Trust.

PenguChi stood by the trough and looked at the four crooked words, and thought about a name in her chat when her channel was very small, finding words nobody had ever heard of, and being right.

“Does it have a name?” she said.

Alea looked at her.

“Astra,” said Alea. She put her finger on the second word, very lightly. “That one. The stars.”

“Did you name it?”

“He let me.” Alea’s ears had gone pink at the tips. “He said I could. I nearly called it something stupid.” She looked down at the black blade. “My dad says it means to the stars, through the hard places. Each word. He told me what each word meant.” She was quiet a moment. “The hard places are going back to my mum. The other half. This half’s going the other way.”

PenguChi looked at the second word for a long time.

“I know what it means,” she said quietly.

Alea looked at her. And PenguChi looked back, the way she’d looked at Alea’s face for a year while Alea told her things her dad said. Not asking. Not looking away. And neither of them said anything else. They didn’t need to.

Kestra left at the end of the afternoon.

PenguChi walked her to the gate. She didn’t know why. It just seemed like the right thing to do, after the post, and the trough, and the penguin. They stood at the gate together, and Kestra looked down the hill road at the town, and past the town, at the lake. At the lake road, and the cabin at the end of it, and the long sailcloth roof on the shingle with its thin line of smoke going up into the evening.

PenguChi watched her look.

She knew that look. She’d seen it every day for a year. On Thea’s face, at the upper window. On Higu’s. On Gigichi’s, when Gigichi thought nobody was watching. On her own, probably, in the water butt, if she’d ever thought to check. It was the way the five of them looked at the lake road. Not curious. Not hoping. Just looking. The way you look at a door you’ve decided not to knock on yet.

Kestra was looking at it the same way.

“What did you write about him?” said PenguChi.

She didn’t say who. She didn’t need to.

Kestra didn’t answer for a long time. She took the small black book out of her coat, and held it, and didn’t open it.

“Nothing yet,” she said.

“Why?”

“Because I don’t know what to write.” Kestra looked at the lake. “I’ve stood beside the king for twelve years. I’ve written down everything I’ve ever seen. And I’ve been sitting on that man’s step for a week, every night, and I’ve watched him fish, and work a bellows, and take a stone off a child, and sit on the end of a jetty for an hour after he cut his own sword in half.” She turned the book over in her hands. “And I don’t know what to write.”

PenguChi thought about it.

“Neither do we,” she said. “We just wait.”

Kestra looked down at her.

“For what?”

PenguChi looked at the lake road.

“For him to come up it,” she said.

Kestra didn’t say anything for a while.

Then she opened the black book, standing at the gate of the militia hall in the evening, and took out her pencil, and wrote something. Very short. PenguChi didn’t try to see what.

“Again,” said Kestra, putting the book away. “Tomorrow. At dawn. If your guide will let me.”

“She’ll let you,” said PenguChi.

“Good.” Kestra turned to go. And then stopped, and looked back, at the five of them in the yard behind PenguChi, and Alea in the middle of them with the black sword across the trough, and Waddles waddling across the yard toward the gate, toward her boot, with great determination.

“Don’t,” said Kestra to the penguin.

Waddles sat on her boot.

Kestra looked down at him for a long moment. Then she sighed, very quietly, and bent down, and picked him up, and put him in PenguChi’s arms. And went off down the hill road toward the town and the lake in the evening, with her hands in her coat pockets, not hurrying, looking at everything.

PenguChi stood at the gate with Waddles in her arms and watched her go.

Then she opened her window.

[ PenguChi · Tier 1: Drawn · Progress to Tempered: 11% ]

Eleven. It had been four that morning. It had been four for a month.

She thought about a man by a lake who’d never once spoken to her. About four lessons that had come up the hill in a fox girl’s mouth. About a fifth one, today, from somebody else, that turned out to be the same lesson again: wait. Everybody who was good said the same thing. Everybody who was good had learned it from someone.

She thought she knew, now, who most of them had learned it from.

Trust, she thought. And smiled, and went back across the yard to the trough, where the others were all trying to lift Alea’s sword at once, and failing, and laughing.
