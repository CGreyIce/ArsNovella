The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 11

Chapter 323

The Grammar of It

4,314 words · 19 min read

Thea had the words laid out on the militia-hall table like cards.

Not real cards. Paper. Higu’s paper, the good stuff, torn into squares the size of her palm, with one thing written on each in her own hand, in English, because she thought in English and always would. She’d been laying them out every night for a week, since Rachael’s eight steps came up the channel at two in the morning, and moving them round, and putting them back, and lying on her pallet in the loft afterwards looking at the rafters with her eyes open.

COME. The valley at Thornwall. Thirteen rings, every one of them that word written round and round in the oldest hand in the leaves, so tight it ached to read. She’d read it through John’s picture till her nose bled.

STAY. Astra. The black sword on Alea’s back. One word folded into star-iron thirty-two thousand times by two people who wanted to go home, saying it on its own for three hundred years. A holding word. She’d read it with Alea’s hand over hers on the grip.

THE HOLE. The middle of the valley, where a word like stay had been pulled out of a circle and left a mouth with nothing in it.

THE GREY. The tiny word on Lissa Fowler’s arm, written ten thousand times round the cut like a seam. An emptying word. The opposite of stay. The word you’d write on the inside of a cup to make it pour. And Dennis, a hundred miles off, kneeling by an old woman on the customs-house steps, had heard it ring as a lie: what you were won’t be again.

THE POUR. Not a word at all. Yan said. People. Hundreds of them, put out and kept, packed like coal; the cold under Diwa’s grave; the dark that came off the priest’s fingers like water off a ledge.

THE RING. The band at Cinderhold, forty miles across, being gone over every midnight all along it at once like a pencil going over a line; and a man’s-width gap in it where Salty had sat down. A ring’s only a ring if it’s closed. She’d written that herself, in the channel, and she’d been right.

And one more square, at the end of the row, on its own.

CLOSE.

She looked at it every night. She couldn’t read it.

That was the thing nobody understood, quite, when she said it. Higu understood. Higu always understood; she sat on the end of the table with her notebook and the moon chart and listened to Thea talk at midnight about grammar the way she’d have listened to Thea talk about anything, with her head on one side, writing nothing down.

“I can read all of them,” said Thea. “Every one. I’ve read them all. I’ve had them in my hands or through John’s pictures or with Maren’s fire going through them. I know how come is built. I know where stay goes in a circle. I read the grey on Lissa’s arm stroke by stroke for four nights till I knew it better than my own name.” She put her finger on the last square. “I can’t read this one.”

“You’ve never seen it,” said Higu. “Rachael’s in Kingsferry.”

“I’ve seen it. At Wrenhaven, in the spring, when she was doing her month at Odile’s. She closed a cut on a fisherman’s hand and I Parsed it while she did it.” Thea sat back. “It’s not that I can’t see it. It’s that it’s not in anything.”

Higu waited.

“Everything else is written,” said Thea. “In the old hand. The hall’s hand. The same hand as the circle on the floor in Solmere I woke up with my hand on. Come’s written. Stay’s written. The grey’s written, it’s the smallest writing I’ve ever seen but it’s writing, it’s strokes, it’s got a first stroke and a last one, you can follow it. That’s how Maren burns it. That’s how Rachael unpicked it.” She touched CLOSE. “This isn’t written. When she does it, I Parse it and there’s nothing to read. There’s no strokes. No hand. It’s just—“ She stopped. She didn’t have the word. That was the whole problem. “It’s just a cut closing. The way cuts close. I can see it happen and there’s nothing there telling it to.”

Higu looked at the squares for a long time.

“Then how does it work,” she said.

“I don’t know,” said Thea. “That’s what I’ve been lying on my back about for a week.”

Maren sent for her on the eighth morning.

A boy from the weir. Not Davy Marsh; Davy was fine, Davy had a pink spiral on his forearm and showed it to everybody at the mill. A different boy, Davy’s cousin, Tam, eight, who’d been in the reeds below the weir pool after eels, where Davy had got his, and found one. In the mud. Rusted at the barb, the way a thing goes that’s been in the water since the spring. He’d thought it was Davy’s. He’d thought he’d give it back.

The grey was a finger’s width up from the palm when his mother brought him in.

Maren was waiting at the Wrenhaven infirmary with her sleeves rolled up and her new hands bare. Odile was there, at the second bed, with the basin and the linen. And Thea came in from the militia hall at a run with her hair half up and stood at the end of the bed, and Maren looked at her over the boy’s head.

“I’d like you to read it,” said Maren. “While I burn it.”

Thea stopped.

“Not before,” said Maren. “Not after. While.” She had the boy’s small hand in hers, palm up. She didn’t look at it. She looked at Thea. “It runs. You know it runs. When the fire comes, the end of it feels it coming and goes, and I have to send the fire round from the other end to catch it, and I have to guess which way it’s gone. On Lissa I guessed right. On the miller’s boy I was lucky; it was only the edges. One day I’ll guess wrong.” She was very formal about it, the way she was about everything. “I can make the fire go to the word. I can’t see where the word’s going to go. You can.”

“You want me to tell you,” said Thea. “While it runs.”

“Yes.”

“Like Dennis,” said Thea slowly. “For Rachael. On the steps. Left. Down. There.”

“Like that,” said Maren.

Thea looked at the boy’s hand. At the grey, a hair wide, going round the hook-hole in the palm and up toward the wrist, round and round. At the boy’s face, which was very white and very brave.

She’d never read anything fast.

That was the truth of it. She’d read everything she’d ever read slowly. Lying on her back. Sitting at the cabin table with Gigi’s thumb on her wrist counting her pulse. Through John’s pictures with a blanket round her and her nose bleeding. She was the best reader in the kingdom, Oswin said; she could read things nobody else could see. And she’d never once read anything while it was moving. Everything she’d ever Parsed had held still for her.

Torch, Maren had said, at Wrenhaven, in the spring, with her own window open in front of her and her hands blistered from eleven burns. Torch isn’t more. It’s meant. It’s a flame that goes where you mean in a press of bodies.

Fast enough for when things don’t hold still.

“Okay,” said Thea.

She Parsed it.

She let her eyes go, the way she did, and the room went with them. The beds and the basin and Odile’s face and the boy’s mother at the door: all of it went flat and faint, like a picture on a wall, and what was left was what things were written in. Maren’s hands, warm, the old guides’ writing in them, seven words in the old order. Odile’s, softer, the healer’s warmth she said was nothing special and was. The boy’s hand. And in it, round the hook-hole, going up, the grey.

The word. She knew it. She’d know it in her sleep now. Every stroke of it the same, the tiny emptying word, written round and round in a line like a seam, ten thousand times, waiting. She found the first stroke in the hook-hole. She found the last one, a finger’s width up toward the wrist, at the end of the line, where it was going.

“Ready,” she said.

Maren sang the seven words. Low. On the seventh, this, the white thread came out of the end of her finger and went into the hook-hole.

The grey felt it.

Thea saw it feel it. She’d never seen that; she’d always read it still. She saw the whole word, all ten thousand strokes of it, go tight, the way a cat goes tight when a door bangs; and at the far end, the last stroke, a finger’s width up, she saw it start to go.

Not burn. Go. Up the arm. Writing itself as it went. Adding strokes at the front faster than the fire took them at the back, the way a word would if a word could run.

“Up,” said Thea. “Inside of the wrist. It’s going up the inside. It’s—“

She stopped.

It wasn’t going up the inside. She’d said where it was. By the time she’d said it, it wasn’t. It had turned. It was going round, round the back of the wrist, the way a seam goes round a sleeve; and she’d said up the inside, and Maren was sending the other end of the fire up the inside, and it wasn’t there.

“No—round, it’s gone round the back—“

Maren’s fire went round the back. The word went up. Thea saw it go.

She was too slow.

She was reading it. She was reading every stroke as it was written, the way she’d always read, carefully, completely, the whole word; and by the time she’d read where it was, it had moved. She was reading a word that was writing itself faster than she could read it, and she was reading it properly, and properly was too slow.

“Stop reading it,” said Maren.

Thea didn’t understand.

“Stop reading all of it,” said Maren. Her voice was perfectly level and her fire was still going and her hand round the boy’s was absolutely steady. “You’re reading every stroke. You don’t need every stroke. You know every stroke; you’ve read it a hundred times. It’s the same stroke.” She sent the fire round. “Don’t read the word. Read where it’s going.”

Thea stood at the end of the bed.

She knew the word. That was true. She knew every stroke of it. It was the same stroke, ten thousand times, like a seam is the same stitch. She’d read it so many times on Lissa’s arm that she could have written it herself, which was a thought she didn’t like and put away. She didn’t need to read the stroke. She’d already read it.

She stopped reading the stroke.

She let it go. All ten thousand of it. Let it be what it was, the same thing over and over, a seam, a line, furniture; Ysolde’s word, that Chris had put in the channel last summer, let the others be furniture. And she looked, instead, at the only thing in the word that was different from the rest of it. The front. The last stroke. The one that was being written now.

It was going up.

She could see it. Not where it was; where it was going to be. The way you can see where a thread’s going when somebody’s sewing fast, not by watching the needle but by watching the hand. The front of the word was a hand, writing, and she could see which way the hand was leaning.

“Up,” she said. “Inside. Now. There.”

Maren’s fire was there.

The front of the word ran into it. Into the white thread coming down from above the wrist, where Thea had said, exactly where the hand was leaning; and it had nowhere to go. Fire behind and fire in front. It went up in a thin cold thread of smoke, smelling of a cellar step. All of it. In a breath.

Under it, the boy’s arm was red. Hot. A raw red line a finger long, round the wrist, bleeding.

“Odile,” said Maren.

Odile put her hands on it. There you are, she said, very quietly, the way she always said it when a body answered; and it answered.

Thea sat down on the floor.

Not on purpose. Her legs did it. She sat on the infirmary floor at the end of the bed with her back against the bed-foot and her nose not bleeding, which surprised her, and her heart going so fast she could feel it in her teeth, and looked up at Maren.

Maren was looking at her own hands.

“Forty heartbeats,” said Maren. “And it ran. And we were waiting for it.”

“I was too slow,” said Thea. “At first.”

“You were reading it,” said Maren. “You always read things. All of them. It’s why you’re the best reader in the kingdom.” She looked at Thea over the boy’s head. “And why you’ve been thirteen to Torch since the winter.”

Thea sat on the floor and didn’t say anything.

She looked at it on the infirmary step, after, with the sun on her face and Odile inside telling the boy’s mother how to change the dressing in a voice that was nearly crying.

Sixteen. It had been thirteen since the winter. Since before the winter, really; she’d gone thirteen on the night she read the empty middle of the valley and it hadn’t moved since, not for anything, not for a week of nosebleeds on the grey or the whole of Oswin’s register or Ars’s sword or Rachael’s eight steps read forty times.

It had moved for not reading.

For letting ten thousand strokes be furniture, and looking at the one that was moving.

She thought about that for a long time on the step. She didn’t like it. She’d been a reader her whole life; it was the thing she was. And the window had sat at thirteen for half a year while she read everything perfectly, and gone up three in forty heartbeats the first time she didn’t.

Torch isn’t more. It’s meant.

She thought she was starting to see what Maren had meant.

She went back to the squares that night with it still in her head.

Don’t read the word. Read where it’s going.

She laid them out on the militia-hall table again. COME. STAY. THE HOLE. THE GREY. THE POUR. THE RING. And at the end, on its own, CLOSE. And she didn’t read them. She’d read them; she knew every one of them better than her own name. She looked at where they were going.

Come. It went toward. That was all it ever did; it pulled. Thirteen rings, all pointing in.

Stay. It held. It went nowhere, on purpose; it told a thing not to move.

The grey. It emptied. It told a person to pour out what they were. What you were won’t be again.

The ring. It went round. It told a place to be a circle, closed, with a middle.

She sat back.

They all told. Every one of them. Every word she’d ever read in the old hand was a word that told something what to do. Come here. Stay there. Pour out. Go round. The hall’s circle in Solmere told thirty-five people to come through a floor. The valley told four hundred to walk up a road. Astra told itself to stay thirty-two thousand times. The grey told a boy’s arm to empty, a hair a day, and the arm did, because it was told.

She looked at CLOSE.

It didn’t tell anything.

That was why she couldn’t read it. She’d been looking for the strokes, for the hand, for the writing that said close the way the grey said empty. And there wasn’t any. Because Rachael’s Close didn’t tell the cut to close.

The cut was already trying to.

You help what’s already there do what it’s already trying to do. Maud had said it. Rachael had said it in the channel, the night Xola healed Maren’s hands, and Xola had said then Maud’s right, tell her an axolotl says so. A cut wanted to close. A body wanted to be itself. A woman in a ring wanted to be Maud. And Rachael didn’t write anything on that. She didn’t order it. She put her hands next to it and listened for what it was already doing, and agreed.

That was all Close was. Agreeing.

Thea sat at the table and looked at the square with the word on it for a long time.

The old hand didn’t have a word for agreeing. It had never needed one. Every word in it was written by somebody who wanted something to do something it didn’t want to do. You didn’t need to write close on a cut. A cut would close by itself. You only needed to write the grey.

The cup and the closer are the same word with the sign turned round. She’d said that, at the steps, in the channel. She’d been nearly right. It wasn’t the same word. It was the same thing. A healer held. That was what a healer was; Rachael had said it on the Widow two springs ago: I’d hold whatever they put in me, and I’d hold it well. The grey took a healer’s holding and turned it inside out, made it hold the pour, made it a cup. And Close was the same holding, the right way round. Holding a body the way the body wanted to be held.

The grey was the only word they’d written for a healer. And it was written to undo the one thing a healer did that didn’t need writing.

If the mender’s in the ring, she can close up anything. Of course she could. Because the ring was all telling, every line of it; and a mender was the one thing in it that agreed.

And there was something else.

Thea picked up the square. She turned it over. On the back, in her own hand, very small, from the first night, she’d written where the word came from. Not the hall’s hand. The other one.

Window. Rachael · Tier 2: Candle · Skills: Mend, Close.

English.

Every skill name she’d ever seen in a window was English. Every tier. Spark, Candle, Lantern, Torch. Drawn, Tempered, Honed. Parse, Patch, Handshake. She’d known that since the first year, since before she knew why; Chris had a theory about it that he’d put in the channel a hundred times. And then, that first winter at the lake, with Patch open in her hands, a fisherman had shown her the first window from the inside, the oldest one, the one all the others came from: and it was a small boy’s, in this world, at six years old, naming the parts of a thing that had come up in front of him, in the only language he had that wasn’t the one he’d been born into here.

His.

The window spoke English because a boy had named it in English at six. Close was a window word. Rachael’s window had found it for her, out of the language the first window was written in, the way every window found a word for what you’d done.

The old hand had no word for closing.

His did.

She went down to the lake at midnight.

She didn’t take a lamp. She knew the road. She’d walked it in every weather for three and a half years, from the militia hall down through the alders to the shingle, the first time with her hand up to knock on a door that opened before she could. The bench was on the shingle, empty, a good two paces nearer the water than it had been in the spring. The lamp was lit in the cabin window. It had been lit every night for seven weeks; Alea’s, till the autumn.

They were at the near end of the jetty.

She saw them from the turn by the alders. Two shapes, at the landward end, by the first post and the ash. One on the shingle, small, with her knees up and her back against the post. Gigi. And one beside her, a little higher, in a seat.

A seat grown out of the ash’s roots, low against the post, with a back and an arm, facing south down the lake. Gigi had told them in the channel. She’d said it came up the night of the steps. he sat down first. it was the ash, not me. Thea had read it at the lake the next morning without asking, the way she’d promised not to, and then apologised to the seat, which was stupid, and Parsed it anyway, which was worse. It was written in nothing. It was just roots, doing what roots wanted, which was to come up where somebody needed somewhere to sit.

Agreeing.

He was in it. With the rod across his knees, not casting, looking south.

She came down the shingle. Gigi looked round. She didn’t say anything; she moved along the stones a little, which was Gigi for sit down. Thea sat on the first plank of the jetty, with her feet on the shingle, beside the seat. Near.

He didn’t look at her. He knew she was there.

They sat for a long time.

“I read it,” said Thea at last. “What Rachael did. The grammar of it.”

He didn’t say anything.

“Everything in the old hand tells something what to do,” said Thea. “Come. Stay. The grey, the ring, the hall. All of it. It’s a language for making things do what they don’t want to.” She looked at the lake. “Close isn’t in it. It’s not written in anything. It doesn’t tell the cut to close. The cut wants to. It just agrees.”

“Agrees,” he said.

“That’s the nearest word I’ve got.” She pulled her knees up. “And it’s a window word. Rachael’s window found it for her. Out of the first window’s language. Which is—“ She stopped.

He was very still.

“I’m not asking you anything,” said Thea. “I know you don’t. I just thought you should know.” She looked at the water. “They’ve got a word for everything. For making people come and stay and empty out and go round. They’ve never once in all of it had a word for closing. Because nobody who wrote any of it ever wanted anything to close.” She was quiet. “The only word for it in the whole world is in yours. Because the first window was a boy’s, and it was in his words.”

The lake was very still. The stars were in it, upside down.

He didn’t say anything for a long time. Long enough that Gigi looked at him, once, sideways, and then away.

Then he did something Thea had never seen him do. Not in four years. Not at the gate, or the hall, or the lake, or the night he showed her the first window from the inside with her hands shaking on Patch.

He asked her a question.

“Did she know?” he said. “Rae. When she did it. Did she know it was—“ He stopped.

“Yours?” said Thea.

“Did she know it would work.”

Thea thought about it.

“No,” she said. “Nobody did. She went in anyway.” She looked at him. “She asked first. Can I. And then she agreed with an old woman who wanted to be herself, for three hours, one stroke at a time.” She was quiet. “I don’t think she knew it was yours. I don’t think it matters. It’s hers now. It’s anybody’s who does it.”

He looked south, down the lake, toward the place where the Wren went out.

“Good,” he said.

And then, after a long time, so quietly that Thea wasn’t sure he’d meant anybody to hear it, not her, not Gigi, maybe not even the seat:

“Then that’s the first thing of mine that was ever any good to anybody.”

Gigi went home at one. Thea didn’t. She sat on the first plank of the jetty with her feet on the shingle, near enough, and he sat in the seat the ash had grown, and neither of them said anything else, and the lamp burned in the window behind them for a girl a long way south who’d be home before the leaves turned.

In the morning there’d be a new line in the channel from Thornwall. Kei, at seven: Tiger’s turned for us every dawn this week. it lets go for nine heartbeats now. we’re going to try again. on Ossie. And Rachael from Kingsferry an hour later, with her hands still not working: agree with him. that’s all. find what he’s already doing and agree.

But that was the morning.

Thea sat on the jetty in the dark and thought about a boy of six with a window in front of him, naming its parts in the only language he had; and about a window, years later, reaching into that language for a word for what a body does when it heals, and finding one there.

She didn’t Parse it. She didn’t need to. Some things you didn’t read.

You just agreed.
