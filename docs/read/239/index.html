Chapter 239

One Pour

7,136 words · 31 min read

At a quarter past six on the night of the tenth, two hundred miles from the grave, Thea’s medium line flickered.

She was at the long table in the militia hall with her window open, the way all thirty-five of them had their windows open that night. Nobody had said they would. Nobody had needed to. The channel had been nearly empty all day, the way it went quiet on the anniversary, every year, by a sort of agreement nobody had made: a line from Chris at dawn, logging, and a line from Rachael at noon, Thinking of you all, and a line from Wendy at five, going up now, and then nothing. Thirty-four people all over a kingdom sitting with their windows open and not writing in them, waiting, while twenty people walked out along a line of markers on the edge of a red plain to a black stone on a hill.

She wasn’t reading the channel. She was looking at the top of her window. At the line under her name and her tier and her bar that she’d found in the first spring and nobody else had ever noticed was there until she showed them.

Medium: one blade · parted · Astra

It had been one blade when she found it. Then, the autumn before last, the morning a king and a fisherman split a black sword on an anvil by a lake, it had changed, very quietly, without a flicker, to one blade · parted. And then, the week the fox girl named her half, Astra. Nobody knew why the summoning cared what a sword was called. Thea had stopped asking. She looked at the line most nights, the way you’d look at a clock that told you something about a house you couldn’t see.

It flickered.

Once. All the letters at once, the way a lamp flickers when somebody opens a door.

And then the whole binding rang.

She’d felt it before. Twice. At dawn in the spring, on the last morning of the old moon, when Niko’s short sword rang on a knoll and the stone rang back and something under it put its head down; she’d been asleep and it had woken her, sitting bolt upright in the loft with her heart going, and she’d lain there feeling the whole of the thing she’d built the channel inside of hum like a struck glass. And once last summer, on the night of the ninth, faintly, a long way off, when a girl laid a sword on the grass beside the stone it had been cut from.

This wasn’t faint.

It came up through her window into her, into Parse, which was always half open now, and into the place under Parse where she’d learned to feel the binding round the channel the way you feel the walls of a room in the dark. All fourteen rings. The thirteen full ones and the fourteenth with its right half blank. Humming. Not a sound. A ring, the way a bell rings, a long pure note going round and round the whole of the circle, through every ring, round and round, faster and faster, and out.

She sat at the long table with her hands flat on the wood and let it go through her.

“Thea,” said Higu. From the other end of the table. Quietly.

“It’s ringing.”

“I know. The table’s shaking.” Higu had her hand flat on it too. “Gigi, the cups.”

Gigichi, who’d been sitting on the bench by the stove with her knees up, got up and went round the table and moved the cups to the middle, one by one, because they were walking very slowly toward the edge on their own.

Xola had her hand over her mouth. PenguChi was standing in the loft doorway with the penguin at her feet, and the penguin had lain down flat on the boards with its flippers out, the way the hens had lain down at the lake the morning Ice showed them what was at the top.

It went on for a long time.

Thea counted it. She couldn’t help it; she’d been counting heartbeats every morning on the step for a year. A hundred. Two hundred. The note going round the rings, round and round. Two hundred and eighty. Three hundred.

Then it faded. The way a bell fades. Down and down, round the rings, slower, until it was going round so slowly she could feel each ring as it passed, the first and the second and the third, out to the thirteenth, and the fourteenth, the half of it that was full and the half of it that wasn’t.

And stopped.

And the register came up.

She hadn’t gone looking for it. That was the thing. Every night for a week she’d gone looking for it: lain in the loft with her eyes shut and Parse open all the way, and gone down, out of the channel, into the binding round it, the whole way to the back of it, where you’d keep a log, and found it there, fifty-six frames, and reached for the ticks, and swum. Every night. A handkerchief. Higu writing down the time.

Tonight it came to her.

When the ringing stopped, she felt it come up. The way a thing comes up out of deep water when whatever was holding it down lets go. The register, all fifty-six frames of it, from the back of the binding where it lived; up through the rings; up through the binding; up to just under the channel, just under the place where she was sitting with her window open, so close she could feel it without going down at all. As if a book that had been on the bottom shelf of a library for three hundred years had been taken out and laid open on the table in front of her.

She could see the frames.

Not the chalk. Not the copies. The frames themselves, in the binding’s own hand. A ring. A reference. Lines out. An arc. Fifty-six of them, all at once, in the dark behind her eyes, so close she could have touched them.

And on the arcs, the ticks.

Not strokes. She’d seen that on the sixth night, going down. Here they were what they were: small folded things, like the shape inside a seed, each one on its arc. And tonight, because of the ringing, or because the register was so close, or because of something she didn’t know, they weren’t quite folded. They were the way a seed is on the morning before it opens. Swollen. Very nearly.

She reached for the first one.

It swam.

The whole frame. The ring and the reference and the lines and the arc, sliding sideways the way they’d slid every night, the letters going off the lines and back; and the tick under her hand, nearly open, nearly, and then not, because she couldn’t hold the frame and reach for the tick at the same time; and her nose, hot, high up behind her face.

She let go. She came up.

“Thea,” said Higu.

“I’m all right.” She wasn’t bleeding. Not yet. “It’s there. It’s come up. It’s right there, Gu, it’s right under the channel, I can see all fifty-six without going down. And the ticks are—“ She stopped. She didn’t know how to say it. “They’re nearly open. Tonight. Because of the ringing. They’re nearly open, and I can’t hold it. It’s still above me.”

She tried again.

And again. She tried for four hours. Higu counted. Gigichi sat beside her on the bench with her hand on Thea’s wrist, the way she had in the loft. Xola made tea nobody drank. PenguChi sat in the doorway and didn’t say anything, and Thea could feel her not saying it.

Forty heartbeats. Forty. Thirty-one, because her nose went. Forty-two, once, the best she’d ever done, and the frame still swam, and the tick under her hand was still nearly, nearly, and not.

She sat back on the bench with Higu’s handkerchief on her face and her head against the wall and felt it there. Fifty-six frames, just under the channel, close enough to touch, with their ticks very nearly open. And the ringing had stopped hours ago. And she could feel the register beginning, very slowly, the way a thing does in deep water when nobody’s holding it up, to go back down.

Not fast. But going.

“It’s sinking,” said Thea.

Nobody said anything.

“It’ll go back down. To the back. Where it was.” She took the handkerchief away. “I don’t know when it’ll come up again. If it ever will. The ninth didn’t do this. Niko’s bell didn’t do this. It’s tonight. It’s the tenth. It’s both halves, at her, together, ten years. I don’t—“ She stopped. “I can’t read it on my own.”

Higu was looking at her across the table.

“You said that,” said Higu. “Last week. In the loft.”

“I know.”

“You said better eyes. Than one person.” Higu opened her book. She turned it round on the table so Thea could see the page, the way she’d turned it round in the grey light in front of the wall a week ago, which she almost never did. TODO: eyes. “So it’s more than one.”

“I don’t know how to do more than one,” said Thea. “I can’t. Handshake’s locked. That’s what it is, Gu. It’s the thing that holds two things that don’t know each other together. I did it for twenty heartbeats on the floor of this room and bled all down my chin. I can’t hold seven people. I can’t hold one.”

“You can ask,” said Higu.

Thea looked at her.

“You don’t have to know how,” said Higu. “Not first. You can ask first.” She closed the book. “Rule four.”

Rule four had been on the first page of the book since the first spring, the night of the channel, in Higu’s small exact hand, and thirty-four people had written it back to her that night, one after another, until it went off the top of the window. Nobody is alone.

Thea sat there for a long time.

Then she got up, and went out of the militia hall in her stockings, and ran across the square.

Maren lived over the guild office, across from the hall, in two rooms up a stair that smelled of ink. Thea had been up it a hundred times. She went up it now three steps at a time in the dark with her stockings slipping on the boards and banged on the door with the flat of her hand.

Maren opened it before the second bang.

She was dressed. Of course she was. She was a guide; she’d sat up on every anniversary of anything since the summoning with her guild coat on and her list on the table, in case. She looked at Thea in the doorway, at her stockings and her face and Higu’s handkerchief still in her fist, and didn’t ask anything.

The cord was on the table. The blue one with the white bead.

Maren put her hand on it.

Thea put hers over Maren’s. The way she had every night for a year. And felt it go warm under both their hands, and felt, at the other end of it, a long way off, three hundred miles south, in a room in a castle, a cat-eared girl sitting on a floor with seven cords round her wrists and the other half of this one tied round her thumb, awake.

Hana. Awake. Of course she was awake. It was the tenth.

“Say it,” said Maren. “Slowly. I’ll write it.”

“Tell Hana,” said Thea. She didn’t do it slowly. She couldn’t. “Tell her the register’s come up. The real one. The ringing brought it up and it’s sinking and the ticks are nearly open and I can’t read them on my own. Tell her it’s above me. It’s above one person.” She breathed. “Tell her I need—“

She stopped.

She didn’t know what she needed. That was the truth. She didn’t know how to say it, because she didn’t know how it would work, because she’d never done it, because it was locked.

“Eyes,” said Maren.

Thea looked at her.

“That’s what you said,” said Maren. Very quietly. “On the stair. Last week. Higu told me.” She was already writing. Not on paper. In her head, the way the cords wrote, in her small exact hand that Thea had never once been able to read. “Thea. Needs. Your. Eyes.” A breath. “Is that right? Every word?”

“Yes,” said Thea.

Maren sent it.

She felt it go.

Down the blue cord, warm, three hundred miles south, into Hana’s thumb. And she felt Hana take it, at the other end, the way you feel somebody take your hand in the dark. And hold it.

And then Hana did what Hana did. What she’d done four times since the bell in the spring, and every night on the road north from the Lists, and once at the Lists with her whole bar jumping: she sent on all seven at once.

Thea felt it through the cord. She’d never felt it from this end before. Hana, in a room in Solmere, holding seven cords; and the one line, Thea needs your eyes, going into her through one of them and out of her through all seven, the same moment, the way water goes into a fountain through one pipe and out of it in seven jets. Out to Oswin, in the next room, asleep over a leaf. To Aldous, on a knoll at the edge of the red, on a white cloth, with a lantern. To Emrys at Kingsferry. To Corvin at Thornwall. To Garrick at Duskhollow. To Lucan at Valcrest. And back up the blue one to Maren, and through Maren’s hand to Thea’s, so that she heard it come back to her in Hana’s voice, which was soft and a little hoarse and very steady.

Thea needs your eyes.

And the seven guides woke.

She couldn’t see them. She couldn’t feel them; not like that; not yet. She just knew, the way you know a house is waking up around you by the doors. Seven old men and women all over a kingdom, with their hands on seven cords, in the middle of the night, getting a line in their own handwriting from a girl in Solmere that said Thea needs your eyes.

And not one of them asked what it meant.

What happened next, she found out afterwards, in pieces. From the channel, and the cords, and a long letter Corvin wrote, and the things people said to her for a long time after, at tables, on roads, in the hedge.

Corvin climbed the tower at Thornwall. A hundred and twelve steps, at his age, in his nightshirt, with his cord in his fist. Hyvred was on the top, where she slept most nights now in a blanket on the leads, looking west at the seven threadless standing in the field beyond the thirteenth ring. He woke her. He didn’t know what to say. He held the cord out to her, as if she could read it, which she couldn’t, and said Thea, and Hyvred sat up.

Lucan went down to the west wall of the Lists. He didn’t have to go far; Chris was there, where he was every night, sitting on the sand under thirty-six gold boxes in the moonlight, with his window open and his book on his knee. Lucan stood over him in a dressing-gown and said Thea needs your eyes, in the voice he used for things he was going to cry about later, and Chris closed the book.

Oswin went into the next room. Hana was already standing.

Emrys at Kingsferry and Garrick at Duskhollow had nobody to wake. Their summoned were on a knoll an hour east of Cinderhold. They sat up with their cords anyway, Emrys wrote later, both of them, all night, holding them, in case it helped. Thea thought it had.

And Aldous, on the knoll, on his white cloth, read it out loud to a boy with silver hair sitting against a black stone; and down the slope, before he’d finished reading it, Deston was already on his feet.

She felt Deston first.

She’d never felt him. Not like that. She’d heard about it: the threads, the choir, thirty-five notes going out across the kingdom from the hall that he’d been listening to for three years. She’d read the channel line, in the spring, when he wrote Harmony under his name for the first time, at the Lists, with Calvin’s wind in Rachael’s hands through him. She’d thought she understood what it was.

She hadn’t understood anything.

He came up her thread. That was the only way she could say it. Two hundred miles east, on a knoll, a man standing up in the moonlight with his eyes shut; and something coming along the thread from him to her, the one that had always been there, that she’d never been able to see; the way you feel somebody pick up the other end of a rope you didn’t know you were holding. And then he was there. Not in the room. In her. At the edge of Parse, where the binding was. A note. Low and warm and a little rough, a baritone’s. Humming.

Hi, Thea, said Deston. Not in words. In the note.

She didn’t know how she heard it. She heard it.

Hana says you need eyes, said Deston’s note. I’ve got six people on their feet. They don’t know what to do. Neither do I. A pause, in the hum. I can put them in you. Like I put Calvin in Rachael. If you can hold them.

I can’t hold them, she said. In the note, somehow. It’s locked.

Yeah, said Deston. I know. I read it. Hold them anyway.

And he poured.

Hana came first. Up the blue cord and through Maren’s hand and through Thea’s, because Hana was already holding that end; and she came in soft and warm and a little hoarse, the way her voice was, and with her, the thing Hana could do that nobody else could: she carried what you meant. Keepsake. Token. A charm that carried a feeling, not words. Hana came into her and Thea felt, at once, what Hana felt. Which was frightened, and very steady, and glad.

Then Hyvred. From the top of a tower at Thornwall, through Deston, a long way; and she came in cold and clear and very far-sighted, the way she was, and with her, Scope. Range. She could hold the rings at eleven miles, face after face; she came into Thea and Thea’s sight went long. Not further. Longer. The way a telescope makes a thing longer without making it nearer.

Then Chris. From the sand under the west wall of the Lists. He came in careful and precise and slightly apologetic, the way he was, like a man stepping into a room where somebody’s working; and with him, a thing that wasn’t a skill, quite, that sat grey under his bar the way Handshake sat under hers. Trace. Seeing it in time. He came into her and Thea felt, for the first time in her life, when. The order of things. Which came first and which came after, laid out like beads on a string.

Then Xav. From the knoll. He came in very still, a lamp in a window, and with him Now, which felt what was about to happen; and Third, which was the thing he’d made a man move three times with on a shingle. Deciding. He came into her and she could feel, somehow, where a thing had been decided.

Then Yan.

He came in last. An hour after something on the knoll that she didn’t know about yet. He came in deep and slow, and less tired than she’d felt him in any line he’d written all year; and with him, the colours. Kin. Hue. And under them, grey, locked, a thing she didn’t have a name for yet but that he’d had in his hand for nine heartbeats that night, and knew the shape of.

And Deston. Under all of them. Holding the threads. Humming.

Seven.

She was holding seven.

She couldn’t. She knew that. She’d known it for a year.

She was a Candle. She could hold a flame on her palm and nothing else. She’d held a roof up from underneath once for twenty heartbeats and it had come apart in her hands like wet paper. She’d held a flame and a reading together every morning for a year and the best she’d ever done was forty-two.

She was holding seven people.

And the binding. And the register, just under the channel, sinking.

She waited for it to come apart.

It didn’t.

She didn’t know why. She found out later, or thought she did; she wasn’t sure she’d ever really know. It was that she wasn’t holding them. Not the way she’d held the flame. Not from the outside, closing on them, squeezing, hold it, hold it together, don’t let go. She wasn’t holding anything. Deston was pouring. Hana was carrying. They were coming in. And all she had to do, all Handshake had ever been, she understood, sitting there in Maren’s room with her hand over Maren’s on a blue cord, was to be the place where they met. To let seven things that didn’t know each other come into the same room and say hello.

The way she’d built a chat window, in the first winter, inside somebody else’s three-hundred-year-old program. Not by holding it. By making a place where thirty-five people could meet.

Oh, thought Thea.

And she reached for the register.

Frame one.

A ring. A reference, dense, unique, like a knot drawn by somebody who’d never seen rope. Nine lines out, each ending in its own mark. An arc on the right. Nine ticks on the arc.

She’d read it a hundred times. She read it now with seven people’s eyes.

And the first tick opened.

It didn’t swim. That was the first thing. It held, under her, the whole frame, steady, the way a page holds still when somebody’s got their hand flat on it; because somebody did. Seven somebodies. And the tick, the small folded thing on the arc, nearly open, nearly, for three hundred years, opened.

Like a seed.

Hyvred saw it first, because Hyvred saw everything first. It’s a line, she said, somehow, in the place where they all were. It’s not a mark. It’s a line. It goes out. It’s long. The tick wasn’t a stroke at all; it was the end of a line, the very end, the only part of it you could see from here, like the cut end of a rope going down into deep water. And with Hyvred’s Range Thea followed it. Out. Along the arc. Out past the edge of the frame. Out of the fourteenth ring, through the blank half, the half that wasn’t blank, here, in frame one, three hundred years ago.

Out. A long way.

It’s going the way the line came in, said Hyvred. Look. The lines out. They come in from somewhere. That’s where they start. And the tick goes back out. The same way. Along the same line. Back.

Then Yan. It’s got a colour.

The tick. The line that was a tick. Thea saw it through him: it wasn’t grey, the way it was in chalk, the way it was in the binding’s hand. It had a colour. A small warm one. Gold, going a little red at the edge. The colour, Yan said, of somebody who’s very frightened and very glad at once.

It’s a person, said Yan. Very quietly. Deep and slow. That’s a person’s colour. On the line. Going.

Then Deston. Under all of them. It’s got a note. The line hummed. She heard it through him. Not one note: two. A note going out, along the line, getting fainter. And at the far end of it, so far off she could only just hear it through Deston’s ears, a different note. Not this world’s. Not any of the notes Deston had heard in three years of the choir. A note from somewhere else, that the first note went into, and stopped.

It arrived, said Deston. It went out and it got there. Somewhere. It’s a different key. It got home.

Then Chris. After. The line out, in the frame, first: a person coming in. And the tick, after. A long time after. Days. Months. It’s in order, said Chris. They came in, and then later, they went. It’s a log. That’s all it is. In and out. It’s a log.

Then Xav. Decided. The tick had been decided. Not by the circle. By the person. Xav could feel it, the way he felt a man about to move: a choice, made, at the moment the tick was written. They chose it, said Xav. Every one. Somebody stood there and chose to go.

And Hana. Carrying it. The thing the tick meant, under the colour and the note and the order and the choice; the feeling. Thea felt it through her, all at once, the way you feel a whole song from its first chord.

Home.

That was all. That was what it carried. Not a word. The thing under the word, that everybody has, that’s the same in every language in every world: the feeling of a door opening onto a room you know.

Frame one. Nine lines in. Nine ticks out.

Nine people. Who came in through the circle, three hundred years ago and more, from somewhere else, and stayed a while, and then stood on the arc that was the blank half of the fourteenth ring, and chose, and went back out along the line they’d come in by, frightened and glad, and got there.

Some went home, thought Thea.

She didn’t say it. Not yet. She’d said she wouldn’t until she could read it.

She went on.

Frame two. Four in. Four out.

Frame three. Seven and seven.

Down the register. Frame after frame. Thirty-seven of them. A ring, a reference, lines in, and on the arc, a tick for every one; and every tick opening under seven people’s eyes like a seed, into a line going back out, with a colour on it and a note and a time and a choice and a feeling. Thirty-seven summonings. Two hundred and nine people, Chris counted, afterwards, from the log of it in his head, because he couldn’t not. Two hundred and nine lines in. Two hundred and nine ticks out.

Every one of them went home.

Every colour was different. Yan said so. He didn’t stop saying it, all the way down, very quietly, the way you’d say names at a graveside. Blue. That one’s blue. Pale. That’s somebody old. That one’s red, she’s angry, she’s going anyway. That one’s two colours. Every note at the far end was a different key, Deston said; they hadn’t all come from the same somewhere. Some of them had come from very far. Some of them, Hyvred said, had come in along lines so long she couldn’t see the start of them even with Range; and gone back out along them, the whole way.

And every one of them had chosen. Xav felt each choice. Some of them had taken a long time. Some of them hadn’t.

And every one of them had felt it. Home. Hana carried it, two hundred and nine times, and Thea felt Hana start to cry somewhere in Solmere about halfway down and not stop and not let go.

Frame thirty-seven. Five in. Five out.

Frame thirty-eight.

Three lines in.

Three marks where they started: somewhere else, a long way off, along lines Hyvred could follow nearly all the way back. Three colours, Yan said: one green, one grey-blue, one a sort of brown. Three notes, Deston said, in three different keys. In. Chris could see them come in, in order, one, two, three, on the same day.

And the arc. The fingernail curve on the right. The blank half of the fourteenth ring, drawn smaller.

Nothing on it.

Thea had stood in front of the chalk of it more nights than she could count. Nothing. A small ring on a wall with three lines and an empty arc.

She looked at it now with seven people’s eyes.

And it wasn’t nothing.

It was worse. It was something. The arc was there. The way home was there, in frame thirty-eight, the same as in frame one, the same shape, waiting. And on it, where the ticks should have been, Hyvred saw, very faint, the place where the lines out would have started. Three places. Like three doors in a wall that had been drawn and never cut.

And no line went through them.

The three colours, Yan said, were still on the lines in. All the way in. They’d come in, and they’d stayed in. They hadn’t gone back out. Their colours were on the lines in, very faint, like a stain on a cloth that’s been washed a great many times; and then they stopped, somewhere inside, the way the colours under the markers on the red stopped. The places where a light had been.

They didn’t choose, said Xav. Very quietly. There’s nothing to feel. They didn’t choose not to. There wasn’t anything to choose.

The arc’s empty, said Chris. There’s no tick because there was no way through. The door’s drawn. It’s not cut. A pause, in the place where they were. Somebody stopped cutting it.

And Hana, carrying it.

Not home. The other thing. The thing under the word, that everybody has, in every language in every world. The feeling of standing in front of a door that’s drawn on a wall, and putting your hand on it, and it being a wall.

Thea felt it go through her. Through all seven of them. And she felt the seven of them, in the place where they all were, all at once, go very still.

Frame thirty-nine. Nothing.

Frame forty. Nothing.

Eighteen of them. Down to fifty-five, Aldemar’s two, two lines in, two colours, a red and a gold, Yan said, very faint, very old; and nothing on the arc. Nothing to feel. Nothing chosen. The door drawn, and not cut.

And then fifty-six.

Thirty-five lines in.

Like a dandelion clock. Thirty-five colours. Yan named them all. He didn’t mean to; it just came out of him, deep and slow and quiet, the way the names had come out of a man on the east wall of Cinderhold last summer, twenty-five of them, looking up. Gold. That’s Gigichi. Blue, that’s Rachael. Thea’s is—

He didn’t say what hers was. She didn’t ask.

Thirty-five notes, Deston said. All the same key. Thirty-five lines in, in order, Chris said, all at once, the same moment, a stone floor in a hall in Solmere on a late summer morning three years and seven days ago.

And Hyvred followed them back.

Out. The way they’d come in. Along thirty-five lines, so long she couldn’t see the end of them, through the gap in the rings, out of the fourteenth, out of the world, and on. And on. And Thea, through Hyvred’s eyes, saw them go. Thirty-five threads of grey going out of the world in the same direction, all together, like the grey threads Deston had seen going out of every one of them since the first spring and called the road home; and at the far end of them, so far off that even Hyvred could only just see it, not a place. A knot. Thirty-five threads going into one knot, very small, very tight, very far away.

A room, Thea thought. Thirty-five rooms. A flat with the curtains open and a gas bill on the table. A desk in the dark. A call still going.

And on the arc of frame fifty-six, nothing.

Not the nothing of thirty-eight. Not a drawn door. Not there wasn’t anything to choose. Xav felt it, and said so, very quietly, in the place where they were: It’s not that nothing happened. It’s that it hasn’t happened yet.

The arc was there. The way home was there, drawn, the same shape. And on it, where thirty-five ticks would go, thirty-five places where a line could start. Not cut. Not yet.

TODO, Thea thought.

She didn’t say anything. She held it. Seven people’s eyes and the binding and the register and frame fifty-six, the dandelion clock with its thirty-five lines and its empty arc, steady, under all of them, for as long as they needed to look at it.

She didn’t know how long. Higu did.

When she came up, she was breathing.

That was the first thing she noticed. Before the window. Before anything. She was sitting on Maren’s floor, not on the chair, she didn’t know when she’d got onto the floor, with her back against Maren’s bed and her hand still over Maren’s on the blue cord, and Maren kneeling beside her with her other hand on Thea’s back; and she was breathing. She was breathing.

She’d been holding seven people and a binding and three hundred years of a register, for longer than forty heartbeats. Much longer. And she’d been breathing the whole time.

A Lantern holds a spell steady, Maren had said, a year ago, while it does something else. A Lantern can hold a flame and fall asleep.

Afterwards, she found out the rest.

That Hana’s bar had jumped, carrying all seven at once, two hundred and nine times home and forty-seven times the other thing, and was at ninety-eight; and that she’d sat on the floor of the room in Solmere afterwards with Oswin and Edwy and the Warden, who’d come up the stairs in his nightshirt holding the pink bead, and cried for an hour, and then laughed, and then slept on the floor with seven cords round her wrists and her tail round Edwy.

And on the west road, a long way from the lake and a long way short of Thornwall, a little after midnight, on the night of the tenth, a big grey horse stopped walking.

Nobody had pulled it up. The man on its back had felt something go through him, through the whole of the ninth tier, the way you feel the earth move under your feet in a forge when somebody drops a big casting. He sat there in the dark, in the middle of the road, with the reins loose in his hands and a king’s guard on a bay a length ahead turning round in her saddle, and an empty dray creaking to a stop behind him with eight pale oxen in the shafts.

He didn’t know what it was. He knew what it felt like.
