The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 9: The Other Front

Chapter 260

The Gate

6,002 words · 26 min read

The iron tells you when it’s ready.

His father had said it every day of Severin’s childhood, at the forge in Brakewater, with his thumb out of the way. You didn’t decide when the iron was ready. You watched the colours, and you trusted them, and when it was ready it told you, and then you didn’t wait. If you waited, it went past ready into something else, and you’d lost it.

Severin had thought about that every morning for sixty days at Thornwall, standing on the tower at dawn with his back to the mountains. Not yet. He’d borrowed the words from a man by a lake and they’d fitted his mouth well enough. Not yet, to Brannoch, who wanted to march up the valley and burn the rings. Not yet, to Corvin, who wanted the town moved south. Not yet, to a boy with quick hands who wanted to count holes with his fingers. He’d been watching the colours.

At noon on the sixty-third day, the girl with the gold eye came down the tower stair two steps at a time, which she hadn’t done since the spring, and stopped in the forge door with her hand on the frame and her tail straight out behind her, and said: “It’s coming.”

He put the hammer down.

It had started at dawn, she said. She’d counted at dawn, the way she always did: thirty-one at the fourteenth place, the same as the night Rui Xiang came back. The rings upright, turned round, facing the town. The thing in front of them on the near side, facing the tower. The dog asleep at its edge.

And then a little later, while she was eating Malcolm’s porridge on the tower roof with Bee, the dog got up.

“It stretched,” said Hyvred. “Like a dog does. Front first, then the back. And then it just stood there, by the thing, waiting. And then the thing moved.” She was holding the doorframe very hard. “Not like before. Before, it was a bit nearer, and then it stopped. This time it didn’t stop. It’s coming down the valley. Slowly. Like a cloud’s shadow coming across a field when there’s no wind. And they’re coming behind it.”

“The rings.”

“All of them. All thirteen. Walking.” She swallowed. “Walking in their rings. Like a crowd coming out of a church. Not hurrying. And the curve. The thirty-one. Walking on the left, all in a line. Like furniture walking.”

He took his apron off.

“How far?”

“Four miles in four hours. A bit less.” She’d thought about it; he saw her do it. “It’ll be at the fields by dusk.”

By dusk. Hild had said it would be. It likes the dark side. He hung the apron on its nail by the forge door, where Agnes hung hers, and looked at it for a moment. A leather apron, burned in a hundred small places. He’d worn one like it every day of his life until he was twenty, and most days since he’d come here.

“Tell John,” he said. “Now. Every window. So they all know.” He looked at her. “Can you?”

“He’s already on the roof,” said Hyvred. “He’s waiting for you to say.”

“I’ve said.”

She went. Two steps at a time, back up a hundred and twelve, the tail going behind her round the turn of the stair.

Agnes was standing by the second hearth with the tongs in her hand. Sixty, with hands like his. She’d made sixty shields with him in sixty days and complained about every one of them.

“Majesty,” said Agnes.

“Bank the fires,” he said. “Both. And then go to the Bell. They’ll want hot water.”

“I’ve a hinge in that fire.”

“Leave it,” said Severin.

She looked at him for a long time. Then she put the tongs down, and took off her apron, and hung it on the nail beside his.

He walked the town.

He’d done it every evening since he came, after supper, the long way round, the way he’d walked the camp every evening on the Redmarch when he was young and frightened all the time. You walked it so they saw you walk it. You walked it so that if anybody had a question, they could ask it to your face in the street instead of lying awake with it.

He walked it now in the low sun, in his coat, with Kestra a length behind him, and the town was doing the order.

The children first. They were being counted into the granary by name, the way the order said, at the big door on the square, by Corvin with his book and the schoolmaster with his and Malcolm with his, all three, checking each other. Small ones in coats, holding hands, in a line; bigger ones carrying the small ones. A girl of about eight with a cat under her arm that nobody had told her she couldn’t bring. They went in out of the cold sun one at a time, and Corvin said each name, and the schoolmaster said it back, and Malcolm wrote it down and said yes; and on the granary wall beside the door, in the light from the square, a running dog drawn in one line of white chalk ran forever toward the corner of the building, and two more sat either side of the door with their chalk tongues out.

He’d put his thumb in the crack in the old granary hinge two weeks ago. The new one was on now. He’d hung it himself.

Petra was in the square.

She was standing in front of the granary door, with her back to it, in the middle of the cobbles. Not doing anything. Very straight, with a plain sword on her hip and her hands at her sides, the way she’d stood at the south gate in the sleet when he said you came. She didn’t look at him as he went past. She was looking up the street that went north from the square, toward the north gate, which you couldn’t see from here because the street turned.

“Majesty,” said Petra.

“How many?” he said.

She didn’t ask what he meant.

“Eighty-four,” said Petra. “So far. Corvin says a hundred and six.” She didn’t move. “I can feel every one of them come in through the door behind me. Like stones going into a bag.”

“Can you hold it with that many?”

“I’ve never held it with that many.” She looked at him then, very steadily. “I don’t think that’s how it works. I don’t think it gets harder.” She looked back up the street. “I think it gets heavier.”

He nodded, and went on.

The long room. Everybody who’d been called, the thirty-three and the ones who’d come since, forty-one now, going in at the guardhouse door at a walk with blankets over their arms; Annet with her bread; Old Pruett grumbling that he should be on his postern and somebody else had it and they’d better not leave the bar off. Drew at the door, with his chest bound under his jacket, saying every name as they went in, every one, without looking at a book. Evening, Annet. Evening, Ivo. Mother Ivey. Joss. A boy with tiger ears at his shoulder. A big young man from Duskhollow in the doorway beside them both, filling it, with his arms held a little out from his sides, the way he stood when he was getting ready to hold something up.

The young man with the snake was inside. Severin could hear him through the door, saying something quiet to somebody about a cup, and the snake’s silence.

The Bell, with Agnes going in at the side door and the healers from Kingsferry already there with their sleeves rolled. Water in every house, buckets on every step. Lamps lit in every window though it wasn’t dark yet, all down the long street, the way the order said, so that whatever came in would come into light.

And the north gate.

Hild was in front of it.

Not on the wall. In the gateway itself, inside the gate, in the arch, with her back to the great iron-bound doors and her sword drawn, point down, resting on the cobbles, her two hands on the pommel and her head a little on one side. Twelve of Brannoch’s soldiers behind her in two ranks with the grey-black shields on their arms and staves in their right hands. More on the wall above. Brannoch on the gatehouse roof. Xav, on the wall by the postern, with Old Pruett’s great bar across the little iron door behind him, who looked down at the king as he went past and lifted one hand, a little, and put it back on the parapet.

Severin stopped in front of Hild.

“Majesty,” said Hild. She didn’t open her eye.

“You’ll have the doors behind you.”

“For a while.”

“And when they go?”

“Then I’ll have the arch.” She was listening north. “It’s a good arch. Old. Narrow. You could hold it with a door.” The good eye opened, and came round to him, grey and steady. “That’s what I am. The girl in the square is the street.”

“I know,” said Severin. “I watched you.”

“I know you did,” said Hild. “You breathe through your nose when you’re watching something you like.” Something happened at the corner of her mouth. “Go up, Majesty. It’s coming to you. Not me. I’m just the door it’ll knock on.”

He went up.

The gatehouse roof was flat, with a parapet, and a stair down inside the wall on the town side. He’d stood on it once in the spring, the first week, and looked north up the valley and seen nothing, because there was nothing for him to see; he didn’t have the eye. He stood on it now in the last of the light with Brannoch on his left and Kestra a length behind and looked north again.

The fields went away from the foot of the wall, cut to stubble, white with frost, flat for half a mile to where the valley floor began and the swept road went up it, dark and straight, toward the mountains. The sun was behind the mountains already. The sky over them was red going to brown. The frost was blue.

He couldn’t see anything.

He could feel it.

It had been a pull since the spring. A draught, on the road, the morning he rode in. A pull in the shape of things, the way you feel a draught in a room before you see which door’s open. He’d felt it every day since, a little, the way a smith learns not to hear the hammers in the next forge. He’d stood on the tower in the spring and opened his whole ninth tier at it and felt it look at him, and look at him, as long as it takes a polished edge to run from straw to blue, and turn away.

This wasn’t a draught.

This was the door coming off its hinges.

It came down the valley toward him in the dusk and he felt it come the way you’d feel a cart coming down a lane toward you through the soles of your boots, before you heard it. Every step. Not steps; it didn’t have steps. Every hair nearer. And it wasn’t facing him. He’d have liked it better facing him. It was facing past him. Over his shoulder. Through the gatehouse and the long street and the roofs, to the tower in the middle of the town where a girl with a gold eye stood with her back to it and a boy with lights beside her.

It wants the eyes, he thought. He’d thought it in the spring. He’d known it in the spring. It had looked at her first.

“Majesty,” said Brannoch, very quietly.

He looked where Brannoch was looking.

People were coming across the fields.

Out of the dusk. Out of the blue frost at the end of the valley, where the swept road came off the valley floor into the stubble; walking. Hundreds of them. Not in a line, not in a column, not the way soldiers come. In rings. He could see the rings, from up here, in the last light, the way you’d see the rings in water: a great round slow crowd coming across the frosted fields toward the north wall at a market-day pace, with its breath going up off it in a single faint haze.

Coats and shawls. Smocks. A woman in an apron. A man with a beard with frost in it. An old woman. A boy.

And soldiers.

He heard Brannoch make a sound beside him. Not a word. The sound a man makes when he’s been hit somewhere he didn’t know he could be hit.

Nine of them. In the second ring, near the front, walking together, the way the ten of them had walked out together in the first spring along the shepherds’ track toward the passes; in Western Watch grey gone pale with two years of weather, with their swords still on their hips and their faces turned up toward the north wall. Toward the gatehouse. Toward Brannoch.

“Dunmore,” said Brannoch.

A sergeant, broad, grey-whiskered, at the front of the nine, walking easily, with his hands at his sides. He didn’t look up. None of them looked up. They walked across the fields toward the wall with their faces turned toward it the way you’d turn your face toward a fire.

All along the wall, the soldiers were saying names.

Severin heard them start. One, at the east end, a young voice: that’s Lewin. That’s Col Lewin, he had the bunk over mine. Then another. That’s my uncle. That’s Uncle Rhodri, in the blue. Mam. Mam, it’s Mistress Tibbet from the bakehouse. That’s Pell. That’s Ossie Pell, there, in the third, look— All along the north wall, in the dusk, soldiers in grey with the king’s shields on their arms saying the names of the people walking toward them, quietly, the way you say the names of people at a funeral.

He’d written it in the order. You’ll know their faces. He hadn’t known what it would sound like.

And in front of them all, in front of the first ring, with the frost going dark round it in a curve as it came: nothing. A place where the frost stopped. Coming across the fields at the pace of the slowest walker in the crowd behind it, the way a man walks at the front of a funeral.

He couldn’t see it. He didn’t need to.

“Light the gate lamps,” he said.

They came to the wall at full dark.

Not all at once. The way water comes up a beach. The first ring came to the foot of the north wall and stopped, with their faces turned up toward it, close enough to touch the stone, and stood there; and the second ring came up behind them, and stopped; and the third, and the fourth, until the whole of the ground under the north wall from the east end to the west was a crowd of people standing in the frost with their faces turned up, silent, breathing.

Silent. That was the thing. Hundreds of people at the foot of a wall and not one sound. Not a cough. Not a shuffle. Just breath, going up into the lamplight from the gate.

And then the ones at the front put their hands on the wall.

All at once. Like one person. They lifted their hands and laid them flat on the stone, the way you’d lay your hands on a horse’s flank to calm it; and leaned.

He felt the wall take it.

He’d built walls. Not this one; this one was older than the kingdom. But he knew what a wall felt like under weight, the way he knew what an anvil felt like under a hammer, and he felt it now through the soles of his boots on the gatehouse roof: hundreds of people leaning on the north wall of Thornwall with their whole weight, and the people behind them leaning on them, and the people behind them, thirteen rings deep. Not pushing. Leaning. The way the tide leans on a sea wall. Patient.

The gate groaned.

He heard it go. Below him, in the arch, the great iron-bound doors, with the bar across them that took four men to lift: the bar took the weight of the crowd pressed against the outside of the gate, and the wood round its brackets made a long low sound like a ship’s timbers in a swell.

Come, said the call.

He’d never heard it. Not with his ears. Not the way Kei did, or the long room, or Drew. He’d felt it as a pull. He heard it now, the way you hear thunder in your chest before your ears catch up, and it wasn’t a pull any more. It was a voice the size of the valley, at the foot of the wall, all round, from every one of the hundreds at once and from the place in front of them where the frost stopped: one word, over and over, low and heavy, the way a tide comes in. Come. Come. Come to this.

Behind him, across the town, in the long room behind the guardhouse, he felt forty-one people stand up at once.

And he felt Drew.

He’d felt Counter go past him in the spring like a thrown hammer. It didn’t go past him now. It came up in the middle of the town, round the long room, like a bell being struck and held; and the call hit it and went back. Not all of it. A part. Enough. He felt the boy take it, wave after wave, on his open hands; and give it back.

And still it came, round the boy, round the long room, over the roofs, into every house in Thornwall. Into everybody. The ones who’d never been called. The ones who’d been fine. Into the Bell and the forge and the bakehouse and the granary.

And it was facing the tower.

He opened the door.

He’d opened it all the way three times in ten years.

On the Redmarch, in the last year of the war. On the tower in the spring, for a girl with a gold eye. In the long room at the balance, with his hand on a boy’s back, give it back, I’ll lean. And now. You didn’t open it often. You couldn’t. It wasn’t a thing you did; it was a thing you were, all the time, a little, going out from you whether you wanted it or not; and opening the door was letting all of it go out at once. All of it. Every year of the ninth tier and the eighth under it and the seventh under that, back down to a boy at the forge in Brakewater with his thumb out of the way.

He stood on the gatehouse roof in the dark with the lamps lit below him and opened it.

And put himself in front of the tower.

Not his body. He didn’t move. He stood exactly where he was, with his hands on the cold parapet and Kestra a length behind him; but he put the rest of himself, all of it, everything that went out from him, on the line between the place in the fields where the frost stopped and the tower in the middle of the town. Like standing in a doorway. Like standing between a forge and a child. Let it see what’s standing in front of you.

It saw.

It turned. A hair. Off the tower. Onto him.

He’d felt it look at him once before, in the spring, from eleven miles. It was at the foot of the wall now, a stone’s throw off, in front of its crowd. He felt it look at him the way you feel the heat of a furnace when somebody opens the door with you standing in front of it: all at once, all of you, from the front, with no edges. It didn’t push. It didn’t strike. It just faced him, with the whole of itself, as if he were the only thing in the world that was there.

And then it leaned.

Not on the wall. On him.

He knew what it was doing. He’d known since the spring, standing on the tower; he’d felt it try a hair, then, and stop. It wasn’t trying a hair. It leaned on him the way the crowd leaned on the wall, the way the tide leans on a sea wall, slowly, all of its weight at once and then a little more, and a little more; patient; not in a hurry; as if it had all night, and all winter, and all of whatever came after.

He leaned back.

It was like holding the tongs. That was the only way he had of thinking about it, afterwards. Holding a bar of iron at heat on the anvil in the tongs, with both hands, the whole weight of your arms and your back and your legs behind it to keep it dead still; while the striker swings. And the striker swings again. And again. And you don’t move. You can’t move. If you move, the hammer comes down on your hand. And the striker never gets tired, and never stops, and the iron never gets cooler; and you hold.

He held.

He didn’t know how long. Somebody told him afterwards that it was an hour before the gate went. It felt like a winter.

He heard things. He couldn’t look at them; he couldn’t look at anything but the place in the fields where the frost stopped, and he couldn’t even see that. But he heard them, the way you hear a forge yard round you when you’re at the anvil with the bar in your tongs, and you know without looking which hammer’s which.

He heard the bar on the north gate crack.

A long sound, wood, splitting along the grain. Then a short one. Then the doors. Not falling; there wasn’t room for them to fall, with the crowd against them. They opened. Inward. Slowly. The two great iron-bound leaves pushed back on their hinges by the weight of hundreds of people leaning, a hand, two hands, a foot; and the crowd came through the gap the way water comes through a sluice.

And met Hild.

He heard her wall go up. He’d have known it anywhere now, after the yard: a sound like water coming off a weir, and then a deep soft boom as the first of them walked into it. And another. And another. He heard the wall give, the way she’d said it would, and come back, and throw them. Bodies going back into the crowd in the arch. A thud. A grunt of breath going out of somebody who didn’t feel it. Then the boom again, and the give, and the throw. And again.

He heard the soldiers behind her. Hold. Hold. Push. Brannoch’s sergeant, hoarse. The flat thock of a shield rim meeting a chest, and the scrape of boots on cobbles going backwards, and a stave cracking down across somebody’s shoulder with a sound like a branch going in a frost; and nobody crying out. Nobody. Not the man the stave had hit, whose collarbone Severin had heard go. Not anyone. The only voices in the arch were the soldiers’, and Hild’s, once, very calm: left, Owen, left, he’s coming round.

The turned didn’t fight. That was the thing he heard, and couldn’t stop hearing. They didn’t strike. They didn’t draw the swords on their hips. They walked into the wall, and the shields, and the staves, and took the blows without a sound, and leaned, and when they were thrown back they came again at the same pace, at a walk, with their faces turned past the soldiers toward the tower, and their hands out, flat, to lean on whatever was in front of them.

Don’t kill them if you can help it. He’d written it. He heard sixty men in grey trying to help it, in the dark, against their own neighbours, who didn’t feel anything and didn’t get tired.

He held the tongs.

It was the grating that went.

He felt it before he heard it. That was the worst of it, later; he’d never be able to explain to anybody why. He’d made those bars. Six of them, square, as thick as a big man’s thumb, at the second hour of the morning in the spring with Old Tull grumbling on the bellows, and set them himself in the stream bed under the north wall, knee-deep, with his hands. You didn’t stop knowing a piece of iron because you’d let go of it. He was holding the whole of himself in front of the thing in the fields, and somewhere under that, at the east end of the north wall where the little stream went out under it into the valley, he felt his own bars bend.

Like a burn in his hands.

He couldn’t look. He couldn’t move. He said, through his teeth, without turning his head: “Brannoch. The grating.”

Brannoch went. He heard him go, down the gatehouse stair at a run, shouting for the reserve.

He heard the soldiers at the grating. Later, a long time later, he’d ask, and they’d tell him, and he’d know it the way you know a weld you didn’t see made by the shape of the seam. Six of Brannoch’s men, in the stream, knee-deep in the black water inside the wall where it came under the arch, with their shields up against the bars and the bars bending in toward them. And on the other side, in the water, in the dark, the turned. Not many; the stream arch was low and narrow. But they’d come down into the water and waded to the grating and laid their hands on the bars the way they’d laid them on the wall, and the ones behind had laid their hands on them, and they leaned.

The bars went one at a time. He felt each one.

Hold, somebody was shouting. A young voice. Hold it, hold it, push—

A trooper called Huw Bevan was at the end of the line.

He knew Huw. That was the other thing. He’d known him sixty days. A tall boy of nineteen with big ears who’d carried shields from the forge to the guardhouse every morning since the first ones were cool enough to carry, two at a time, one on each arm, and stopped in the forge door every morning to say thank you, Majesty as if the king had made them for him personally, which in a way he had. Huw had held the end of Fenwick’s chain, as a boy, when Fenwick chained the valley. Huw’s father had walked out in the first year, up the shepherds’ track from Coldwater, and was in the fourth ring.

He’d stood in the forge door that morning, with the last two shields, and said: Majesty. If it’s my da.

And Severin had said: Then push, son. And tell him his name.

Huw was at the end of the line, in the water, with his shield against the last of the bars and his shoulder behind it, pushing, saying his father’s name; when the last bar went, and something came through the gap that wasn’t a neighbour.

Low. Under the arms of the turned, in the water, where they couldn’t see it. Grey, the colour of a wet stone. Long arms.

It came up out of the black water inside the grating in the space where the last bar had been, between the turned and the soldiers, the way an otter comes up out of a river; and the soldiers had their shields up and locked against the neighbours, against the turned, against the people they’d been told to push and not to cut. They had their staves in their right hands and their swords in their sheaths. Every one of them was doing exactly what the order said.

It was at the end of the line. Where Huw was.

Its long arm came round, low and fast, out of the water, the way every soldier on the Redmarch had seen one come a hundred times; and Huw, who had his shield against his father’s neighbours and his face turned toward them and his father’s name in his mouth, didn’t see it come.

The back of its hand took him across the side of the head.

Severin heard it. From the gatehouse roof, sixty paces off, through the stone, through the dark, through the whole of himself held in front of the thing in the fields. A sound like an axe going into a wet log. Short. Heavy. Final. And then a splash, a big one, a tall man going into knee-deep water sideways all at once with his shield still on his arm.

And nothing.

No shout. No cry. Huw didn’t make a sound. The turned didn’t. The thing in the water didn’t; it was already turning, low, toward the next man, with its long arms swinging, hush, hush, like a scythe in long grass—

“Kestra.”

He’d never sent her. Twenty-two years. She’d ridden a length behind him for twenty-two years and stood a length behind him at every table and every gate and every forge and he had never once sent her away from him, because that was what she was for, and they both knew it.

He heard her go.

Not down the stair. Over the inside of the parapet, onto the wall-walk, along it, east, at a run; and off it, a storey, into the stream. He heard the water take her. He heard her sword come out.

He heard the thing in the water scream.

The sound every soldier on the Redmarch knew, like a fox in a trap with a man’s lungs. High and rattling and furious, under the arch, in the dark, so loud that for a moment the whole north wall went quiet under it. And a heavy wet sound with it, and another splash, smaller; and the scream going away, under the water, out through the gap in the grating, into the crowd of the turned on the far side, who didn’t move to let it through, and didn’t need to. And gone.

Kestra’s voice. Very calm. Arm. Off at the elbow. It’s gone back. A pause. Get these bars up. Get anything up. Get the cart from the laundry.

And then, quieter, from the water: Majesty. It’s Bevan.

He didn’t move.

He couldn’t. He was holding the tongs. The thing in the fields was leaning on him with all its weight and a little more, and a little more, patient; and if he moved, if he turned his head, if he let one hair of himself go down off the gatehouse roof into a stream at the east end of the wall where a boy was lying in the black water with his shield on his arm, it would be through. Off him. Past him. Onto the tower. Onto the eyes.

He stood on the gatehouse roof and held, and didn’t go down.

He said the name instead. Out loud, into the dark, to nobody, with his teeth together, because it was what he’d told them all to do, and because a man should have his name said when there’s nobody near him who can say it.

“Huw Bevan,” said Severin.

They got the bars up. A laundry cart, upended, wedged into the arch of the stream with its wheels in the air; two doors off the farrier’s; the reserve, standing in the water behind them with their shields locked, not looking at the thing they were standing beside under the water, which was a boy they’d known.

Kestra came back up onto the gatehouse roof a long time later. Soaked to the waist. With her sword in her hand and the black stuff on it that wasn’t blood, hissing a little in the frost. She came and stood a length behind him, where she’d always stood, and didn’t say anything.

He felt her there. He’d always felt her there.

“I sent you,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Twenty-two years.”

“Yes,” said Kestra. “You should have done it sooner.” She was quiet. “I’d have been there before it came up.”

He didn’t answer that. There wasn’t an answer to that.

In the arch below him, Hild’s wall boomed, and gave, and threw. In the long room, a boy held the call off forty-one people on his open hands and gave it back, and gave it back. On the tower, behind him, a girl with a gold eye stood with her back to the north and her eyes shut, looking at it anyway, and a boy with lights stood beside her and didn’t send anything, because the king had said every window at noon and once was enough until there was something they could do. And at the foot of the north wall, all round, thirteen rings deep, the turned leaned on the stone with their hands flat and their faces turned up, and breathed, and didn’t make a sound.

And in front of them, the thing leaned on him.

It wasn’t in a hurry. That was what he understood, standing there, in the second hour, with his hands on the parapet and the iron in his tongs and the striker swinging. It hadn’t come down the valley to take the town tonight. It had come down to lean. To find out how much a wall could take, and a gate, and a grating; and a boy with his hands open, and a woman with one eye, and an old smith with the door open. It was finding out how much. The way it had found out at the balance. It knows how much, he’d said then, with his hands shaking.

It was learning again.

It would go on learning, he thought, every night, at dusk, until one of them broke. Or until the snow came.

Behind him, across the town, something shut.

He felt it the way you feel a door shut in a house behind you when you’re standing at the front of it. Not a sound. A change in the air. He couldn’t turn round. But he knew what it was, and where; he’d watched it go up in the guardhouse yard on a frosty morning with six children standing behind it.

There were turned in the lanes, then. Through the grating, before the cart went up; a few, at the east end, by the laundry. Walking up the street toward the square, at a market-day pace, with their faces turned toward the tower. And between them and a granary with a hundred and six children in it, in the middle of the cobbles, with a running dog in chalk on the wall behind her, a woman from a ditch had put up a wall across the square from one side to the other. Flat. Grey. The colour of slate.

He felt it go down into the stones and stay.

It wouldn’t give. She’d said so. Not while they’re there.

Severin stood on the gatehouse roof at Thornwall with the door open and his whole self leaning on the dark, and a boy in the water at the east end of the wall, and the first night of it not yet half over; and held the tongs.

The iron would tell him when it was ready. It always had. He’d just never stood this long at the anvil, waiting to hear it.
