Chapter 120

The Night Before

4,147 words · 18 min read

There was a hill a few miles north of Valcrest that the carters called the Last Look, because it was the last place on the north road where you could see the city before the farms and the walls and the market gardens closed round you and you were in it.

They camped on it on the sixteenth night.

Maren had wanted to push on. Maren had a list, and on the list the sixteenth night was a posting house at the north gate, with beds, and stabling, and a wash. But the dray had thrown a shoe at noon, and the miller’s cart had a wheel that was talking, and by the time Bram had seen to both the sun was going down red over the plain and the city was still three hours off. So they’d come up the Last Look in the dusk, slowly, and stopped at the top, and nobody had said anything for a while.

Ars stood a little way down the far side of the hill, with the buckets at his feet, and looked at it.

He hadn’t seen Valcrest in fourteen years.

It hadn’t changed. Cities didn’t, much, in fourteen years. It lay on the plain in the last of the light like something spilled: a wide low sprawl of roofs inside a wall, and roofs outside the wall, and roads going into it from every side like the spokes of a wheel. Towers. The great dome of the chapel where Emrys had sung at midwinter with two hundred voices round him. And in the middle of it all, bigger than he remembered, a long oval of pale stone like a bowl set down on the city: the Lists. The sand inside it would be raked by now. The stands would be swept. Somewhere under them a red-cloaked man who wept easily would be counting paper horns.

The lamps were coming on. One at a time, and then a street at a time, and then all of it, until the whole city lay on the dark plain glowing like a coal in a grate.

He let himself feel it.

He’d been holding it off for two days. Since the downs. He’d felt it gathering, the way you feel weather gathering in your knees, and he’d kept it at arm’s length, the way he’d kept everything at arm’s length for eight years. He’d carried the water. He’d taken the third watch. He’d taught the morning class on the side of the road, and watched Alea move first against Petra on a wet verge and nearly win, and not thought about it.

He thought about it now.

The city was full of candles. Thousands. Tens of thousands. The whole plain round it was speckled with them, farmhouses and inns and carters’ camps, the way the sky’s speckled with stars if you look long enough. Most of them were nothing to him; just warmth, just people, just the low general glow of a great many lives being lived close together. Like the noise of a market heard from a hill.

But some of them he knew.

He knew five that had been in the city for a year and a half. He’d never felt them before. They’d always been too far away, south, at the bottom of a kingdom. But he’d read every word they’d written for a year, and listened to the shape of each of them in every line, and he found that he knew them the moment he reached for them, the way you know a voice on the phone. One steady and careful and always keeping count. One bright and hot, like a forge with the door open. One cold. Cold right through, the way ice is cold, except at the very middle, where it wasn’t. One that sat a long way off from everyone else, the way a man holds an angle, and waited, and was patient, and was sure. And one that ticked. Fast and light and exact, like a clock that was very pleased with itself.

He knew five more that had come in from the north-west yesterday, in the middle of four hundred people and two hundred horses and eleven wagons of somebody’s luggage. He’d never felt those either. He’d never felt anything of them at all but names in a channel. One that pulled at the people round her without meaning to, like a tide pulls, and pushed it back down every time. One with ink on it. One soft and warm and full of animals. One that was cross with everything, and loved it all. And one that was steady and patient, and asked the plain questions nobody else would.

Five more that had come in from the west this afternoon. A hard bright one that had stood on a shed roof all winter. One that ran things. One that buzzed, faintly, out of spite. One with a small cold coil wrapped round it, talking. And one that saw: he could feel it looking, from inside the city, the way you feel somebody watching you across a room.

And in the middle of them all, like a mountain on a plain, one that wasn’t a candle at all.

Severin.

He’d felt the king arrive yesterday. Everybody on the hill would have felt it if they’d known what they were feeling: a weight, coming down on the city from the north-west, settling. Like a great bell being set down on a floor. Ascendant. The only one alive. Ars had felt it once before in his life at this distance, the morning the king came down the lake road in the autumn with a roll of smith’s tools, and the hens had gone mad with joy.

And beside the king, close, the way it had always been close, a thin tight candle that burned very steadily and very carefully, as if it were afraid of going out.

Halvard.

Ars stood on the side of the hill with his hands at his sides and looked at that one for a long time.

The channel was very loud that night.

He sat down on the slope below the camp, a little way off, where the hill fell away toward the city, with the rod across his knees, and opened his window, the way he had every night for a year. The way you check your ammo. It scrolled up past him faint and quiet in the dark, two hundred lines at a time, and he read it.

Ars put his hand over his mouth.

It didn’t help. It came up out of him anyway, on the side of the hill, in the dark, with the city glowing below him: a laugh, a real one, loud and sudden and helpless, the kind he hadn’t laughed since a forge by a lake in the autumn with a king at the bellows. It went out across the slope and up the hill behind him, and in the camp at the top somebody sat up in their blankets, and somebody else said what, and Calvin said he’s reading the channel in a voice like a man watching a sunrise.

Ten thousand paper fishing rods. In every window. On the chapel.

Just in case.

He laughed until his eyes ran. Then he sat there with the rod across his knees and his hand over his face, the way a king had sat in a small hall over a cold bowl of porridge, and didn’t laugh any more, and didn’t do anything else either, for quite a long time.

He read it all. Every line. The way he always had.

He didn’t write anything. He never had.

Alea came down the hill at midnight.

He felt her coming the way he’d felt her coming down the stairs in the cabin every night of her life when she couldn’t sleep: the candle he’d know if he were blind and deaf and a hundred miles off, coming toward him through the dark, slow, a little reluctant, the way you go toward something you’ve decided you need. She had her blanket round her shoulders. She’d left Astra in the camp, which meant she’d thought about bringing it.

She sat down beside him on the slope without a word and looked at the city.

“I can’t sleep,” she said, after a while.

“I know.”

“Everyone else is asleep. Even Calvin. Calvin’s asleep holding the kettle.” She pulled the blanket tighter. “Petra’s asleep with her sword on. Like a soldier.”

“She is a soldier.”

“I know.” Alea was quiet. “The Fledgling Lists start on midsummer morning,” she said. “First round at the third hour. Drew says there are a hundred and forty of us. Cadets. Guild kids. Noble boys with private masters. A girl from the coast who fights with two hooks.” She stopped. “I’ve never fought anyone I didn’t know.”

“You fought the girls. In the first winter.”

“I knew them. I knew them before I met them. You told me.” She looked at her hands. “I’ve never fought anyone who didn’t know who I was. Who wasn’t being careful of me. Not once. Not in my whole life.”

He didn’t say anything.

“I’m frightened,” said Alea.

“Good.”

She looked at him.

“You’d be stupid not to be,” he said. “A hundred and forty of them. All of them better than they look.” He turned the rod in his hands. “Your mother was frightened every time she went into an infirmary tent. Every single time. She’d stand at the flap with her hands together and her ears flat, and count to three in her head, and then she’d go in and be the best healer on the border for twelve hours.” He looked at the city. “Be frightened. Then count to three.”

Alea was quiet for a long time.

“Are you?” she said.

“Frightened?”

“Of tomorrow. Of them.” She nodded at the city. At the lamps. “Twelve, Deston said. Twelve who still think you’re dead.”

He thought about it.

He thought about eight years on the end of a jetty. About a cheap notebook and a stair and a window that wrote itself into thirty-five other windows and never said his name. About supper in the snow, and five girls at the door with lanterns, and Gigichi shoving a lamp at him so she wouldn’t have to look at his face. About a song on a lake road with a gap in it. About every reason he’d ever had for waiting, laid out on the boards one by one on a midwinter night like fish on a slab, and every one of them turning out to be the same fish.

“I was,” he said. “For eight years. Of their faces. Of what they’d see. Of being the thing everyone was waiting for, and not being enough.”

“And now?”

He looked at the city, glowing on the plain. At the candles in it he knew. At the mountain in the middle, and the thin careful flame beside it.

“Now I’m frightened,” he said slowly, “that they’ll be happy.”

Alea stared at him.

“That’s stupid,” she said.

“I know.”

“That’s the stupidest thing you’ve ever said. And you once told the king the hens were misunderstood.”

“They are misunderstood.”

“Dad.”

“I know,” he said. “I know it’s stupid. But I know what to do with people who are angry with me. I’ve done that for thirty-four years. I know what to do with people who are frightened of me, or want something from me, or want me to go away. I’m good at all of that.” He breathed. “I don’t know what to do with twelve people being happy at me all at once. I’ve never had to. Not since a call at two in the morning in a room in Malacca, with a fan going, saying goodnight.”

Alea didn’t say anything for a while.

Then she leaned sideways, slowly, the way she’d leaned against him on the jetty when she was small, and put her head on his shoulder, between her ears and his coat, and left it there.

“Count to three,” she said.

He laughed. It hurt, the good way.

“Did you bring the good knife?” said Alea, later.

“Yes.”

“Show me.”

He took it out of his coat. The good knife. Plain horn handle, a blade worn thin with twelve years of sharpening, the knife he cleaned fish with and cut rope with and had once, memorably, used to take a splinter out of the king’s thumb. He held it up so that the light from the city caught the edge.

Alea looked at it for a long moment. Then she grinned. He could feel it, against his shoulder.

“First time ever,” she said.

“It is not.”

“It is. Every time we’ve ever gone anywhere. The grave. The market at Caddow when I was ten. You always forget the good knife.” She sat up. “Mum used to pack it for you. She told me. She said you’d forget your own head.”

He looked at the knife in his hand.

“She packed it,” he said. “Every time. The first year we were together, I went to the garrison for a week and came back and found it in my pack and didn’t know how it had got there. I thought I’d done it.” He turned it over. “I found out about four years later. She’d been doing it the whole time. She never said.”

“So you’ve never packed it yourself.”

“I packed it myself this week,” he said. “For the first time in my life.” He put it back in his coat. “I checked it four times.”

“I know,” said Alea. “I watched you. I was going to put it in if you forgot.” She pulled her blanket back round her shoulders. “I’ve been doing it since I was twelve. You never noticed.”

He looked at her.

She looked back at him in the dark, with her mother’s face, and her mother’s ears, and her mother’s habit of deciding whether he was worth the trouble.

“Go to sleep, Alea,” he said.

“You go to sleep.”

“I’m going to.”

“You’re not.” She stood up, with the blanket round her. “You’re going to sit here all night and look at the lamps, and then in the morning you’re going to walk into the city with no sleep and everyone’s going to be happy at you and you won’t know what to do.” She sniffed. “I’ll be beside you. In case you forget how to stand up.”

She went back up the hill.

He watched her candle go all the way to the top, and settle, and go quiet, the way it had gone quiet at the top of the stairs every night of her life.

The three of them came down an hour after that.

He’d known they would. He’d felt them lying awake in their blankets at the top of the hill, side by side, not talking, the way they’d lain on the cabin floor for six weeks. Gabe first, too big to be quiet. Then Deston, carefully, as if the dark were a thread he didn’t want to snag. Then Xav, who made no sound at all.

They sat down round him on the slope. Gabe on one side, Deston on the other. Xav a pace below, on his heels, the way he’d sat on the end of the jetty in the spring with the rod in his hands, waiting for a fish that was never going to come.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

“Tomorrow,” said Xav, eventually.

“Tomorrow.”

“Okay,” said Xav.

Gabe was looking at the city. At the Lists, the great pale bowl of it, glowing faintly in the middle of the lamps.

“Sleep, Declan,” he said.

“I’m going to.”

“Seriously.”

“I listened to you once,” said Declan. “Look where it got me.”

Gabe made a noise that was very nearly a laugh, and put his arm round Declan’s neck from the side like a falling wall, the way he had at the fire the night the king’s letter came, and didn’t take it away again.

“Twelve,” said Deston quietly.

“I’m sitting down,” said Declan. “Look.”

“You’ll have to stand up eventually.”

“Not till the morning.”

Deston was quiet for a moment. Then, very softly, under his breath, he hummed the first bar of the middle part. Just the first bar. And stopped. And didn’t go on.

Nobody went on. It didn’t need it. They all knew the rest.

They went back up in the end, all three. Gabe last, with a hand on the top of Declan’s head as he got up, heavy, the way you’d put a hand on a post to steady yourself. And then he was on his own on the side of the hill, with the rod across his knees and the city on the plain below him and the camp asleep above, the way he’d been on his own on the end of a jetty every night for eight years.

It didn’t feel the same.

He opened his window. Out of habit.

He looked at it for a long time.

It had said fifty-six on the jetty, the night the king’s letter came. Somewhere on the road south it had moved. He didn’t know where. Not at the Sallow; he’d checked. Perhaps in a village with a goose. Perhaps in a common room at Pellow’s Cross, with a girl on the hearth singing about a horse called Midnight. Perhaps on a mounting stone in a posting-house yard at three in the morning, keeping a watch he’d asked for, with twenty people asleep round a fire in front of him and nobody needing him to do anything at all.

He closed it.

He put his hand in his coat, and found the stone.

Small and flat and white, from the lake shore. Old Mother Breck’s, from her stool in the square at dawn, sixteen days ago. She’d held on to his hand when she gave it to him, with her dry old fingers like paper, and pulled him down close, and said in his ear the thing nobody else had heard.

She’d have wanted to see this.

Mother Breck had been seventy-nine the summer Diwa stood on the end of a half-ruined jetty and said here. She’d brought them a pie, the first week, and stood at the door, and looked at Diwa’s ears and Diwa’s cold hands and the small fox girl hiding behind Diwa’s skirts, and said well, you’ll want feeding. She was the only person in Wrenhaven who’d ever said Diwa’s name to his face, after. Once, in the market, the first winter. Just the name. And then gone on buying her onions.

He turned the stone over in his fingers.

She’d have had me packed, he thought. The morning Calvin asked. She’d have been at the top of the lake road with the bags and the good knife and that look, and she’d have said “well?” And I’d have gone.

It took me a bit longer, Diwa. Sorry.

I’m going now.

Below him the city glowed on the plain.

He sat and looked at it. At the lamps. At the long pale bowl of the Lists, and the dome of the chapel, and the roads going in like spokes. Somewhere down there, in every window on the high street, there were ten thousand paper fishing rods, put up by a man who wept easily, at the word of a girl he’d last heard on a call at two in the morning, saying rude when he said love you guys, except Newish. Somewhere down there a girl who’d teased him about a name would be standing at the north gate in the morning, waiting for some friends. Somewhere down there a boy who’d held his far angle for a year and a half was saying it’ll be fine. Somewhere down there twelve threads were faded, and in the morning they wouldn’t be.

Somewhere down there a king was asleep, or not asleep, with a question he’d waited twelve years to ask.

And beside him, very close, the way it had always been close, a thin careful candle that was certainly not asleep. That had sat in a study every night for eight years, wondering. That had asked How is he at the bottom of every letter. That had sat down, at breakfast, in a small hall, when the king held up a single word.

Tomorrow, Halvard, he thought. You can stop wondering.

To the east, three days out, five candles on a road. Gold, all of them. One talking to sheep. One carrying something taller than himself in a blanket and calling it horseshoes. One with a white snake round his neck, counting to thirty-six.

And out, in the direction that isn’t a direction, one more. Thin and grey and thirty years old. Going out of the world toward a car-park wall. Not broken.

Big day tomorrow, Dad, he thought. Wish me luck.

The channel scrolled up past him in the dark, faint and quiet, slowing as the night went on. Tiger, one last time, in capitals. Hako, it’ll be fine. Chris, logging. Then nothing for a long while. Then, very late, a single line from Thea, who had written the same two words into a bonfire once, and who must have been lying awake at the top of the hill behind him looking at the same city:

[ 03:12 · Althea: Still here. ]

He read it.

He didn’t write anything. He never had.

He sat on the side of the Last Look with the rod across his knees and the white stone in his hand, and thirty-five candles spread out across the dark around him like seed, and one more going out of the world, and the lamps of Valcrest burning on the plain below. And waited, not afraid, for the morning.
