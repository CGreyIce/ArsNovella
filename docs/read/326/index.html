The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 11

Chapter 326

Race You

4,917 words · 21 min read

Tiger had turned at the bridge twenty-four mornings running, and every one of them he’d counted.

Not the turn. The after. He’d come down the swept road at full Bolt with his head down and his arms going, and three paces off the forty he’d plant his foot and turn, Back, in a pace, the whole run going into him and ringing like a bell the way it had the first morning; and he’d be going back up the road with his face over his shoulder before the sound reached the wall. And over his shoulder he’d count. Out loud, under his breath, the way Drew counted when Drew was frightened. One. Two. Three. How long the forty stood with their hands up in the air where nothing was, after he turned. How long the thing that held them let go.

Four, the first morning. Then six. Then seven for a week. Then nine.

Kei said it was learning. Not the thing. Them. Kei said the forty had been let go of every dawn for three weeks for a few heartbeats, and something in them was getting used to it, the way your arm gets used to a weight; and every morning they stood a little longer in the air with their hands up before the thing remembered and took hold again.

Hyvred said it was Ossie.

She said it from the tower every morning, after, in the channel. He pushed again. Along the top. When it let go, he pushed. She said the seam along the top of Ossie’s face, under his fringe, where it had been loose since the first race in the spring, was looser every morning by a hair. She said when the thing let go of the shoulder, the others went slack, asleep, the way you go slack when somebody stops holding you up; and Ossie went up. Pushed. From underneath. Like a man under ice putting his hands flat on it.

Eleven, this morning.

He’d counted it going back up the road with the wind in his ears and the wall coming up out of the grey in front of him and Drew’s voice behind him on the bridge saying it too, very quietly, the way Drew said things he didn’t want to have said. Eleven.

He slapped the oak post inside the arch. Flat hand. Tomorrow, he said, to the road.

And then he stood there with his hand on the post and his chest going and thought: no.

Not tomorrow.

“Tomorrow,” said Kei.

They were in the old wing, at the long table, all of them who’d be on the bridge. Kei with his book and the flask, the same size as last time, a thumb’s worth. Hisui round his neck. The two sisters: Agathe, square and grey and solid, and Wenna beside her with her hands on the table in front of her, pink and new and a little shiny where the nails were growing back. She kept looking at them. She’d kept looking at them for three weeks.

Hyvred. Bee with her chalk. Drew. Azac. Malcolm with his book. Nell Dunmore, who wasn’t going to be on the bridge for her own husband and had come anyway, and sat at the end of the bench with her big hands folded. And at the far end, by the door, a man Tiger hadn’t seen in three years and had known the second he walked into the Bell’s yard four days ago with a carpenter’s bag on his shoulder: big and quiet and grey at the temples, with Ossie’s eyes.

Aldo Pell. From Harrowford. Who’d come all that way in the first summer to find out what had happened to his son, and stayed to put a roof on a shed for a boy he’d never met because the boy had been his son’s friend.

Corvin had written to him. Tiger hadn’t known. Corvin had written in the week after the sergeant, your son is awake under it; we mean to try; and Aldo Pell had put his tools in a bag and walked from Harrowford, eleven days, because Ossie’s mother couldn’t do the road with her hip.

He hadn’t said anything since he came. He’d sat in the yard of the Bell on the mounting block and looked north, at the valley, the way Tiger looked at it from the roof of the shed.

“It’s eleven heartbeats now,” said Kei. “When Tiger turns. Eleven, and the flinch on top, four more. Fifteen.” He looked at his book. “Last time we had one.”

“It’s not long,” said Agathe.

“No,” said Kei. “But it’s not what we’re doing with it.” He looked at Wenna. “Rachael said. Agree with him. Find what he’s already doing and agree. Last time we went to the first stroke of the seam and unpicked it. That’s what you do on a half-turned woman in a ring. Ossie’s not half-turned. He’s been in there three years and the seam’s tight everywhere except one place.” He looked at Hyvred.

“The top,” said Hyvred. “Under the fringe. From the left temple across to the right. It’s loose there. Not open. Loose. Because he’s been pushing on it from underneath every morning since the spring.” She was quiet. “He’s not asleep like the others. He’s under the ice with his hands on it.”

“So we don’t open the seam,” said Kei. “We open where he’s pushing. We don’t unpick it; we let go of it, where he’s already making it give. And he comes up.” He looked round the table. “He does it. Not us. We just stop holding the lid down.”

“And the pour,” said Agathe.

“The pour comes out first,” said Kei. “It did on Wenna’s hands. It will again.” He looked at Agathe. “This time you’re closing behind before it’s out. Not after. As he comes up through, you close under him, so it’s got nowhere to go but out the top and away. And Wenna doesn’t touch the seam. She touches him. His hands. When they come up.” He was quiet. “Rachael said. Somebody between. Somebody keeping time. Somebody to bring warmth.”

“We haven’t got a Deston,” said Wenna.

“No,” said Nell Dunmore.

They all looked at her.

“You’ve got a town,” said Nell Dunmore. She didn’t unfold her hands. “Haven’t you.”

That was how they did it. Tiger would remember it all his life, the way he remembered the first morning Ossie raced him on the wall: the size of it.

Drew’s chain. That was what Nell meant, and Drew knew it before she’d finished. On the night of the count, last autumn, when the call came for the town, Drew had stood in the middle of the Bell’s long room with forty people holding hands in a ring round him so the call couldn’t take any one of them alone; and it hadn’t. Hands. Plain hands, holding. Body warmth, the oldest thing there was, the thing Maud had told Rachael in the first winter went out of a healer first.

You couldn’t send it down a thread without a Deston. But you could send it down an arm.

So at the first grey on the twenty-fifth morning, when Hild heard the feet like people coming home from market, and the forty came down out of the valley and stood across the road a pace off the far end of the millstream bridge with Ossie at the front, the whole town came out.

Not on to the wall. On to the road.

Out of the postern, in coats over nightshirts, the Watch and the town and the children, and down the swept road in a line, holding hands. A quarter of a mile of them. From the postern to the bridge. Brannoch at the postern end with his hand in Old Pruett’s. Then the Watch, nineteen. Then Olwen, who’d walked out toward the north gate once in the night and been walked back; then the Dacey sisters, and Annet with flour to her elbows, and Ivo, on the deaf side, holding somebody’s hand very hard. The schoolmaster. The children, in the middle, Corin among them with his chin up. Agnes, with soot on her hands. Hild near the bridge end, with Petra beside her. Group 6, spread through it without anybody saying where. And at the end of the line, at the near end of the bridge, holding the last hand: Nell Dunmore, with her other hand on Sister Agathe’s back.

On to the bridge, past Drew’s back, the healers went. Agathe with Nell’s hand flat between her shoulders and a quarter of a mile of Thornwall on the other end of it. Wenna beside her, with Aldo Pell’s hand on her shoulder; he’d asked, and nobody had told him no. Hyvred behind Drew, with her hand on Drew’s shoulder, barefoot, in Bee’s coat. Bee with her chalk. Kei with the flask. Azac, talking, low and steady: Drew’s on the bridge. The town’s on the road. Brannoch’s got the postern. Everybody’s here.

And Tiger at the well.

He’d crouched at the mark every morning for three years. He crouched there now with his hands on the cobbles and his ears flat and the whole town gone out past him on to the road, holding hands, and nobody left in the square but him and the well and the sound of the millstream; and Rui Xiang, a hand off his shoulder, where Rui Xiang always was.

“Eleven,” said Rui Xiang. “And four. Fifteen.”

“I know.”

“You’ll turn and you’ll come back,” said Rui Xiang. “And you’ll run it again. If they need more.” He was very quiet. “You said. As many times as they want.”

“I know,” said Tiger.

He looked at the road. At the line of people on it, a quarter of a mile, holding hands in the grey, and the bridge at the end, and the shapes on it, and past them, a pace off the last stone, forty more. One at the front with freckles and a grey watch coat with the collar turned up.

He went.

Bolt.

He’d never run like it. He’d run every morning for three years and he’d never once run down a road with a whole town standing at the side of it holding hands, and it did something to him. They didn’t shout. They didn’t make any sound at all. They stood in their line in the grey and watched him go past, and he felt every one of them, one after another, like running past a fence and touching every post; Pruett and the Watch and Olwen and the children and Corin and Agnes and Hild, faster and faster, a blur of faces at the side of the road, until he couldn’t see them, only feel them, and the bridge came up out of the grey, and Drew’s back, and the healers past it, and the forty.

Ossie.

Three paces off, he planted his foot.

Back. The whole run went into him and rang. He heard it go up the road behind him, the long low bell of it, and the town hear it; and he was turning, in a pace, his face going over his shoulder the way it always did, to look.

The forty let go.

He saw it happen. He’d seen it twenty-four times. Every head going a hair to one side, the way a sleeper twitches. Every pair of hands staying up in the air where nothing was. The thing that held them, letting go of the shoulder.

One, he counted, going back up the road.

Kei put the flask to Ossie’s lips.

Two.

The call flinched. He felt that too, even running; everybody did. The second let-go, on top of the first. Like a door that had been pushed open, and then somebody leaned on it from the other side.

Three.

“There,” said Hyvred. Her voice came up the road high and strange. “He’s pushing. He’s pushing now. The top. Left temple to right. It’s going—”

Four.

Wenna didn’t touch the seam.

Tiger didn’t see that. He was too far up the road by then, at full Bolt, going away, with his face over his shoulder and the bridge getting small. Hyvred told him after. Wenna didn’t touch the seam at all. She put her new pink hands out in front of Ossie, a hair from his face, the way Rachael had written, and listened; and found him. Under. Pushing. With both hands flat on the underneath of his own face, where it had been pressed down for three years like a flower in a book, pushing up along the top where the join was loose.

And she didn’t open it.

She agreed with it.

Five.

The top of the seam gave.

Hyvred said it went the way ice goes on a river at the thaw: not all at once, and not where you’re looking. A crack along the top under his fringe, from the left temple to the right, where it had been loose since the spring; and then not a crack. Open. And something came up through it.

Cold, first. The pour. Out of the top of the seam like cold out of a cellar when you open the door in summer; a white breath of it, going up into the grey. But this time Agathe was there. Agathe, with Nell Dunmore’s hand flat between her shoulders and a quarter of a mile of Thornwall on the other end of it, had her hands under the cold, at the jaw, at the chin, at the ears, all round the bottom of the seam where it was tight; and as the cold came out of the top she was closing the bottom. Close. A healer’s close, not Rachael’s, not anything with a name; just the thing every healer did with her hands when a wound wanted to shut. So the pour had nowhere to go but out of the top, and up.

It went up.

Into the air, over the bridge, white, like smoke going up a chimney on a still morning. And then not up. North. Along the swept road. Up the valley, toward the pass, eleven miles, the way it had come; going home to whatever had sent it, the way Aldous had said they would. They’ll go where they’re called.

Six.

And under it, coming up through the top of the seam like a man coming up out of a well, Ossie.

Seven.

His own face. Hyvred said. Not the patient one. Not the puzzled one. Pressed flat for three years and coming up, and the other face, the one that had been put on, going slack over it like a mask with the strings cut, and sliding, and not there. His own. Freckled. Thinner than it had been. Grey with three years under. And his mouth open, the way your mouth opens when you’ve been under water a very long time.

Eight.

Wenna took his hands.

He’d had them up in the air with the rest of the forty, open, flat, where nothing was. She took them. Both. In her new hands, pink and shiny where the nails were coming back; and his were cold, so cold, like holding fish; and she held them, and Aldo Pell’s hand was on her shoulder, and Tiger felt it, even at the top of the road, even running: the warmth going. Down a quarter of a mile of the town’s arms, hand to hand to hand, into Nell Dunmore’s palm, into Agathe’s back, along Agathe’s arms, into Wenna’s, into his.

Nine.

The thing tried to take hold again.

Hyvred saw it. From behind Drew, with her eye bleeding now, she saw it reach back for the shoulder, the way it reached back every morning at nine, at eleven, at whatever the count was. Reach for Ossie, to pull the seam shut from inside, the way it had pulled Sergeant Dunmore’s shut on Wenna’s fingers.

And there was nothing to pull.

The seam was gone. Not shut. Gone. Agathe had closed the bottom of it, and the top had opened all the way, and there wasn’t a join left to pull on; there was just a face. A boy’s face. Ossie’s. With nobody else’s on top of it.

The thing reached, and found nothing to hold, and let go.

Ten.

Ossie fell over.

Tiger stopped at the postern.

He’d never stopped there. He always went through, under the arch, to the post. He stopped at the postern with his hand on the stone and his chest going and looked back down a quarter of a mile of swept road with the whole town on it, holding hands, every face turned the same way, toward the bridge; and at the end of it, on the bridge, a knot of people kneeling. Wenna and Agathe. Kei. Bee. And in the middle of them, on the stone, somebody lying down in a grey watch coat.

And past them, a pace off the far end of the bridge, thirty-nine.

Standing. Hands down at their sides again. Facing the town. Thirty-nine, where there’d been forty, with a gap at the front like a missing tooth.

Hyvred said they didn’t move all day. They stood at the end of the bridge with the gap in them and looked at the town and didn’t do anything, and at dusk they turned round and walked home up the valley, thirty-nine, and the gap stayed where Ossie had been, all the way up the road, eleven miles. As if something in them was keeping his place.

But that was later.

Now Tiger stood at the postern and couldn’t move.

“Tiger,” said somebody. Rui Xiang, at his shoulder. He hadn’t heard him come. “Tiger. Go.”

He went.

Not Bolt. He couldn’t have. He walked. Down the swept road, past the town, past every one of them holding hands and not letting go, Pruett and the Watch and Olwen and Annet and the children and Corin, who looked at him going past with his chin up and his eyes enormous. Past Agnes. Past Hild, who didn’t look at him at all, and put her free hand on his back as he went by, once, flat, and took it away. On to the bridge. Past Drew, who had turned round, who was standing with his face to the forty and his back to the town for the first time in all the mornings, because the rule didn’t matter any more, and whose face was doing something Tiger had never seen it do.

To the knot of people on the stone.

Ossie was on his back on the bridge with his eyes open, looking at the sky.

Agathe had his head. Wenna had his hands, still, both of them, in hers. Aldo Pell was kneeling on the other side of him with his big carpenter’s hands not touching anything, held a little way off over his son’s chest, as if he didn’t know if he was allowed.

Ossie didn’t look at any of them.

His eyes were open and grey and moving, slowly, the way a newborn’s eyes move. Across the sky. Across the faces over him. Across his father’s, and past it, and on, without stopping, the way you’d look at the faces in a crowd in a town you’d never been to. Nothing in them caught. Nothing knew anything.

“Os,” said Aldo Pell.

Ossie’s eyes went past him.

The big man made a sound. Tiger had never heard a man make a sound like it. He put his hands over his face, the carpenter’s hands that had put a roof on a shed in the first summer, and knelt on the bridge with them over his face, and didn’t take them away.

“It’s three years,” said Agathe. Very quietly. Not to anybody. “He’s been under three years. He doesn’t—“ She stopped. “Give him time. Give him a minute. It takes a minute.”

Tiger knelt down.

He didn’t know why. He didn’t decide. He knelt on the stone of the millstream bridge by Ossie’s shoulder, in the place Wenna moved over to make, with his chest still going from the run and his ears flat and his hands on his knees, and looked down.

Ossie’s eyes came across the sky. Across the faces. Across Bee’s, and Kei’s, and Agathe’s above him.

Across Tiger’s.

And stopped.

Tiger didn’t breathe. Nobody on the bridge did. The whole town on the road didn’t; he could feel them not breathing, a quarter of a mile of people holding hands in the grey, holding their breath.

Ossie looked at him.

Not past. At. The way you look at somebody you know. Grey eyes in a thin freckled face under a fringe that needed cutting, looking at a tiger demihuman with brown ears and an XX under his eye, kneeling on a bridge with sweat on his face; and something in them catching, the way a hook catches. Slowly. And then all the way.

His mouth moved.

“Tiger,” said Ossie.

It came out wrong. Cracked and thin and much too quiet, a voice that hadn’t been used in three years except by something else. But it was his. It was the voice that had shouted Race you! along the top of the wall every morning of the first spring and lost every single time and never once minded.

Tiger opened his mouth and nothing came out.

Ossie looked at him a long time. Then his eyes went, slowly, the way a man’s eyes go when he’s remembering something from a long way off, down the bridge. Up the swept road. To the wall, a quarter of a mile away, grey in the grey. To the arch. To where, inside the arch, there was an old black oak post that two boys had slapped every morning of a spring a long time ago.

“Race you,” said Ossie.

He couldn’t stand. Of course he couldn’t. He’d been standing for three years, every day, in a valley and on a road, on legs that hadn’t been his; and now they were his and they didn’t know how. Agathe said it would be weeks. Months, maybe. He was thin as a lath under the watch coat, and cold right through, and he couldn’t hold his own head up for long.

Tiger carried him.

Not far. Not running. He picked Ossie up off the stone of the bridge the way you’d pick up somebody very light, and Ossie let him, and put his head against Tiger’s shoulder because he couldn’t hold it up; and Tiger walked. Up the swept road. A quarter of a mile. Past the whole town, still holding hands, still in their line, who didn’t let go and didn’t make a sound as he went by, only turned their heads, one after another, the way the forty turned their heads, except that every face that turned was crying.

Aldo Pell walked on his other side, with his hand on his son’s ankle, because he couldn’t reach anything else.

Past Corin. Past Annet and Ivo. Past Olwen. Past the Watch, nineteen, at attention, Brannoch at the end of them with his face set like a door. Past Old Pruett, at the postern, who had his hand on the new green bar that was the worst bar he’d ever had, and who stepped back to let them through.

Under the arch.

Tiger stopped at the post.

The old black oak post inside the arch of the north gate. He’d slapped it every morning for three years. He’d said Tomorrow to it. He stood in front of it now with Ossie in his arms and his own arms shaking, because he’d run a quarter of a mile at full Bolt and walked a quarter of a mile back with somebody, and Ossie was light but he wasn’t nothing.

“Can you,” said Tiger.

Ossie lifted his head off Tiger’s shoulder. It took a long time. He looked at the post.

Then he put his hand out.

Slowly. Thin and grey and shaking, the hand that had been up in the air for three years, open, flat, where nothing was. He put it out the length of his arm, which wasn’t far, and Tiger leaned, so it would reach; and Ossie put his hand flat on the old black oak post inside the arch of the north gate of Thornwall.

Flat. The way they’d always done it.

He held it there.

“First,” said Ossie.

Tiger laughed. It came out of him like something breaking, a sound he hadn’t made since the count; and it went on, and it turned into the other thing halfway through, and he didn’t stop it. He stood in the arch with Ossie in his arms and Ossie’s hand on the post and laughed and cried at the same time, and behind him, on the swept road, a quarter of a mile of Thornwall finally let go of each other’s hands, and the sound they made coming up the road toward the gate was like nothing Tiger had ever heard.

“You won,” he said, when he could.

“I know,” said Ossie. Very faint. His eyes were closing. “First time.”

He slept for two days in the old wing, in the bed by the north window where the lamp was never put out. Agathe sat with him. Wenna sat with him. His father sat with him and didn’t speak and held his hand when Agathe said he could. Tiger sat on the floor by the door with his back against the wall and his knees up and didn’t go anywhere.

On the second evening Ossie woke up and looked at the ceiling for a long time. Then at the lamp. Then at the man in the chair beside the bed, big and quiet and grey at the temples, with his eyes.

Tiger watched him look.

It took longer than it had on the bridge. Much longer. The grey eyes going over the face, slowly, the way you’d read a letter in a hand you used to know. The jaw. The ears. The hands.

“Da,” said Ossie.

Aldo Pell put his head down on the edge of the bed and didn’t lift it for a long time.

On the fifth morning Tiger ran.

Not to the bridge. Thirty-nine stood there, with their gap, and he didn’t want to turn for them that morning; Kei said they’d need him again, all of them, one at a time, for a long time, and he said he knew, and he’d do it, every morning, as many as they wanted. But not that morning.

That morning he ran from the well to the post. The old race. The first spring’s race, the one he’d run with Ossie along the top of the wall and down and across the square, before the valley, before any of it. Not Bolt. Just running.

And at the post, in the arch, on a stool Agnes had brought out of the forge, in a blanket, with his father on one side and Wenna on the other, Ossie was sitting. Thin and grey and wrapped up, with his fringe cut, holding his head up by himself for nearly a minute at a time now. Watching the square.

Tiger came across the cobbles and slapped the post. Flat hand.

Ossie reached out from the stool, slowly, the length of his arm, and put his own flat hand on the post an inch below Tiger’s.

“Tie,” said Ossie.

“You were sitting down,” said Tiger. “You didn’t run. You were already here.”

“Still counts,” said Ossie.

Tiger looked at him. At the thin hand on the black oak under his own. At the face under the cut fringe, with nobody on top of it.

“Yeah,” he said. “Still counts.”

“Tomorrow,” said Ossie.

And Tiger, who’d said it to an empty road every morning for three years, heard it said back for the first time, and stood in the arch with his hand on the post and couldn’t answer at all.
