The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 1: Thirty-Four Winters

Chapter 14

Fish

2,680 words · 12 min read

The fish started arriving in the second week, and by the end of the second month nobody in the militia hall could remember what dinner had been like before them.

Alea brought them wrapped in wet leaves in a basket over her arm: two, usually, sometimes three, lake trout or silver perch still cold from the water. She’d put them down on the kitchen table without any ceremony at all and say, “From the fisherman,” and go out to the yard to find PenguChi, and that was that.

“Does he know you’re giving his fish away?” Xola asked, the first time.

“He gives them to me,” said Alea. “I just don’t eat them all.”

“That’s not an answer,” said Higu, who had started keeping a list, Thea suspected, of all the things Alea said that weren’t answers.

“It’s a fish,” said Alea. “It doesn’t need an answer. It needs a pan.”

So the fish went in the pan. And somewhere between the second week and the end of the second month, Alea stopped being a visitor and became something else, the way water stops being in a glass and starts being in the floor.

She sparred with PenguChi every morning in the far corner of the yard, and PenguChi, who’d been at one percent for a month, crept up to three, then five, then nine, slowly and stubbornly, the way PenguChi did everything. She sat with Xola on the wall in the afternoons and taught her the names of every bird on the lake, and Xola taught her words from Earth that she would then use completely wrong for days. She stopped Gigichi from being alone by the gate, mostly by sitting next to her and saying nothing. She ate supper with them more nights than not.

And she watched Thea. Thea noticed that. Alea watched her the way you watch a pot you think might boil over.

“She’s not a fledgling,” Higu said one night, quietly, when Alea had gone home. “Not really. Nobody that good at sixteen is a fledgling anything.”

“She’s good at aura,” said Xola. “That’s all.”

“She’s good at not answering things.” Higu looked at Thea. “You see more than we do. What do you see when you look at her?”

Thea had been wondering when someone would ask.

“She says she doesn’t have a window,” she said slowly. “But when she’s near me, mine… shifts. A little. Like it recognises something.” She shook her head. “I don’t know what that means. I could be imagining it.”

“You’re never imagining it,” said Gigichi, from the corner.

It was the first thing she’d said to Thea, directly, in three weeks.

Thea looked up. Gigichi didn’t look back. But she didn’t look away either.

It rained at the end of the second month, the hard, cold, sideways rain the lake country was apparently famous for, and on the second night of it the roof of the long room started leaking directly onto Thea’s bed.

Then onto Gigichi’s.

Then onto the floor between them, in a steady rhythmic drip, like a clock nobody had asked for.

“Bram,” said Maren, from her own dry corner, without opening her eyes.

“I’m not going up on a roof in this,” said Bram, from his. “I’m forty-six. I’ve got a knee.”

“Then someone else will go up.”

Nobody volunteered. Water dripped. Xola had pulled her blanket over her head and was pretending to be asleep. PenguChi actually was asleep, with Waddles on her chest, apparently untroubled by the fact that her left foot was in a puddle.

Thea sighed, got up, and went to find the ladder.

When she got to the top of it, soaked through in about four seconds, with the rain driving into her face and a bag of spare slates over her shoulder, she found Gigichi already on the roof.

They stared at each other through the rain.

“Your bed was leaking too,” said Gigichi, eventually, by way of explanation.

“So was yours.”

“Yeah.”

There was a long pause, filled with rain.

“I’ll hold the slates,” said Gigichi. “You’ve got better hands for the nails.”

So Thea did the nails, and Gigichi held the slates, and the wind tried twice to take them both off the roof. The second time, Gigichi grabbed Thea’s arm and held on, and they both ended up flat against the wet slates, gasping, and then, for no reason at all, laughing. Laughing so hard Thea couldn’t get her breath, lying on a roof in another world in the pouring rain, with the girl she’d sworn not to speak to holding her sleeve.

“I’m sorry,” said Gigichi, when they could talk again. She said it to the slates, not to Thea. “About Earth. About all of it. I wasn’t pulling my weight. I know I wasn’t. I was…” She stopped. “I was really tired, for a really long time, and you were always so fine, and it made me feel worse, so I stopped trying. That’s not your fault. I made it your fault. It wasn’t.”

Thea didn’t say anything for a moment. The rain ran down her face.

“I wasn’t fine,” she said at last. “I was just better at hiding it.”

Gigichi turned her head and looked at her.

“I’m still angry,” said Thea. “A bit.”

“Yeah. That’s fair.”

“But I’m on a roof with you. So.”

“So,” Gigichi agreed.

They fixed the leak. It took an hour. When they climbed down, soaked to the bone and shivering, Maren was standing at the bottom of the ladder with two blankets, and she looked from one of them to the other with an expression that, on anyone else’s face, Thea might have called relief.

Thea had been reading other people’s windows for a month by then.

They let her. That had surprised her at first. But they’d promised, the last night in the castle, to tell each other everything, and somehow that had come to include show. So in the evenings after supper she’d sit with one of them, Xola or Higu or PenguChi, and once, finally, Gigichi, and they’d open their windows, and she’d look underneath.

Every window was different on top. Different names, different numbers, different shapes of progress: Thea’s own at sixty-four, Higu’s at thirteen, Xola’s at eleven, Gigichi’s at ten, PenguChi’s at nine and climbing like a tide. Xola’s had a faint watery shimmer to it that Thea was fairly sure meant her water path was close to showing. Higu’s changed with the moon. PenguChi’s was slow and deep and steady, like PenguChi.

But underneath, at the very bottom of the lattice, where the structure began, they were all the same.

Not similar. The same. The same root shape, the same first lines, the same strange nested beginning. As if every window she’d ever looked at had been copied from one original and then filled in differently. Like a template. Like the way, in code, you’d build a hundred things on top of one thing, and every one of them would carry a little piece of that first thing inside it forever.

And at the very bottom of every one of them, there was a reference. A line that pointed away, out of the window, toward something older. She couldn’t follow it. It went nowhere she could see. But she knew what it was.

Every window inherits from a first window, she wrote to Chris, that night, by the light of a candle stub. Ours, the guides’, everyone’s. They’re all built on top of one original. Somebody had the first one. Everyone else’s is a copy.

The Advisor said it started with a single man. I think he meant that literally.

She looked at the letter for a long time before she sealed it.

Then she thought about Alea, who said she didn’t have a window, and who made Thea’s own window shift whenever she came near, the way a compass needle shivers when you bring it close to iron.

She didn’t put that in the letter. Not yet.

Thea caught Alea alone at the water butt, on the last day of the month, while the others were inside arguing about the fish.

She hadn’t planned it. She’d just come out to wash charcoal off her hands, and there Alea was, filling a bucket, her tail swaying, humming something under her breath that Thea didn’t recognise. And Thea’s window, which she hadn’t opened, shifted again: that faint, familiar shiver, like a compass needle when you bring it near iron.

“Can I ask you something?” said Thea.

Alea’s ears went up, then carefully back down. “You can ask.”

“You said you don’t have a window.”

“I said most people here don’t.”

“You said I don’t really have one.” Thea dried her hands on her skirt. “I remember. I remember most things people say. It’s a bad habit.” She hesitated. “When you’re near me, mine moves. Like it knows you. I’ve looked at four other people’s windows this month, and it doesn’t do that for any of them. Only you.”

Alea set the bucket down. She didn’t say anything for a long moment. Her tail had gone very still.

“I’m not going to make you tell me,” said Thea. “I just wanted you to know that I know something’s there. So you don’t have to keep pretending it isn’t. Not with me.”

Alea looked at her. She had very sharp eyes, Thea thought. Sharp, and kind, and much older than sixteen.

“Someone I love told me once,” Alea said slowly, “that a secret isn’t only yours. It belongs to everyone it would hurt if you told it.” She picked up the bucket again. “Some of mine aren’t mine to give away yet.”

“Okay,” said Thea.

“Okay?”

“Okay.” Thea shrugged. “I’ve got one too. A line in my window I can’t read. Sealed, like a door with no handle.” She smiled a little. “When you’re ready, maybe we can trade.”

Alea stared at her. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed: short and surprised and a little wet.

“Maybe,” she said. “Maybe we can.”

She went back inside with the bucket. Thea stayed by the water butt for a while, with her clean hands and her window shivering faintly, and thought that this was the first time since she’d come to this world that someone had told her a true thing about a secret instead of a lie.

Alea almost told him on a Tuesday.

It was evening, and the rain had finally stopped, and the lake outside the cabin was very still and very dark under a clearing sky. Her father was in his chair by the fire with a book open on his knee. She was on the floor by the hearth, mending a tear in her practice tunic, very badly.

She had been carrying it for six weeks.

Three years. Every time Xola laughed, she heard it. Every time Thea said his old name around the fire, or Higu told a story about him from her old streams, or PenguChi said he was so good at word games in that soft, fond, faraway voice. Three years. They’d lost him three years ago, and they were still sad about it, and they talked about him as if he’d just walked out of the room.

And he was here. By the fire. Reading.

“Dad,” she said.

He looked up.

She opened her mouth. She had the words ready. She’d practised them on the walk home, on the lake road in the dark: Dad, they think you died three years ago. Dad, it’s only been three years for them. Dad, your father might still be alive.

He was looking at her, patient, waiting. The firelight was on his face. There were lines on it she could remember appearing. She could remember the night, seven years ago, that a lot of them had appeared all at once.

“Nothing,” she said. “I tore my tunic again.”

He looked at her a moment longer than he needed to. Then he said, “Use smaller stitches,” and went back to his book.

She used smaller stitches. Her hands weren’t quite steady.

Ars had known since the second day.

It wasn’t hard. Alea had never in her life been able to keep a secret from him for longer than about a week, and she’d only been keeping this one for two days when he noticed she was coming home with the faint, familiar shimmer of five Initiates’ mana clinging to her like woodsmoke. He didn’t need to follow her. He didn’t need to ask Rowan. He had stood at the end of the jetty on her first evening back and felt her walk up the lake road, and with her, faintly, the memory of where she’d been.

So the next morning, when he came back from the water, he took the two best fish out of his catch, and wrapped them in wet leaves, and put them in the basket by the door.

And when Alea left for “the guild,” she took the basket with her. And when she came home that night, it was empty.

They’d been doing it for six weeks now. Every morning, two fish in the basket. Every evening, an empty basket by the door. Neither of them had ever said one word about it.

He’d thought about saying something. Of course he had. He’d thought about it on the jetty at dawn, and in the garden, and at night, lying awake listening to her breathe on the other side of the wall. Alea. I know where you go. It would have been easy.

But she was her mother’s daughter. Diwa had never told him anything until she was ready, and then she’d told him everything at once, all in one breath, usually at the worst possible moment. Alea was the same. She’d tell him what she was carrying when she knew how to carry it. Or she’d decide not to, and that would be hers to decide too.

And if she was going to sit around a fire every night with five girls from a life he’d lost, and come home with their laughter clinging to her like woodsmoke, then the least he could do was make sure they were fed.

So: two fish. Every morning.

He watched her now, over the top of the book he hadn’t been reading, as she bent over her tunic with her ears flat and her tail very still. She’d been about to tell him something. He’d seen it in her face. Something heavy. Something that had been sitting in her for weeks, and that she’d looked at his face and decided, at the last moment, that he couldn’t hold.

He wondered what it was.

He didn’t ask.

Later, after she’d gone to bed, he went down to the jetty with the net that needed mending, and sat at the end of it in the dark, and worked by feel. He did that most nights now. He couldn’t have said why. There was something about the rhythm of it, knot after knot, the cold of the water below and the stars coming out above, that settled something in him he hadn’t known needed settling.

And lately, sometimes, mending the nets in the dark, he’d felt the world go very quiet around him. Not silent. Quiet. As if the lake and the trees and the air itself had stopped for a moment to listen to him tie a knot.

He didn’t know what that was either.

He tied the knot, and the next one, and the one after that, and above him the sky cleared and cleared until there were more stars than he could count, the same stars he’d been looking at for thirty-four years. They were still the wrong ones. He’d stopped minding a long time ago.

Up on the hill, in the militia hall, five girls were drying their blankets by the fire and arguing about whether the fish had been perch or trout. He couldn’t hear them. He didn’t need to.

He knew exactly how it sounded.
