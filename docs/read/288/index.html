The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 10: The Sword's Country

Chapter 288

Resurgam

5,532 words · 24 min read

He knew Gabe’s walk from a mile off.

He’d known it from further, once. Across a school hall, at choir, in another life; off a coach in Malacca, the year before he died. A big young man coming toward him with his shoulders forward and his hands in his pockets like somebody trying to look smaller than he was and failing. He’d know it in the dark. He’d known it on the shingle at the lake the first time, through a face that wasn’t Gabe’s face any more than his own was his.

He knew it now. On the deck of a salt barge coming up the lake out of the mouth of the Wren in the evening, a mile off, with the sail full of a wind that was blowing the wrong way for anybody but Calvin. A big shape in the bow with its hands in its pockets.

Ars sat on his stool on the last plank with the rod across his knees and watched it come.

He’d been watching since noon. Higu had said four days, in the channel, four days ago; and Calvin’s wind, he’d learned, was as good as Calvin said it was. He’d come out after the midday meal and sat down and not fished. Alea had come out an hour later and sat on the plank beside him with her boots over the water and Astra’s grip over her shoulder and not said anything. On the bench by the lake road, Gigi had been sitting since she’d finished her breakfast, with her cup and her watch, and the new leaves along the back of the bench gone from the size of a fingernail to the size of a thumb.

Nobody had said anything all afternoon.

The Patient Widow came up the last mile of the lake with the evening going gold behind her and the water flat, and swung in toward the jetty, and Old Haskett on the steering oar shouted something at his boys about the posts. And Ars saw them all, then, along the rail. Deston, in the middle, with his hand up to shade his eyes. Calvin in the bow with his eyes shut, holding the wind, the kettle on his hip. Dennis with something folded in his arms. Rachael at the stern with her arms folded. A woman he didn’t know, with a sword across her knees, eating an apple. And Gabe.

Gabe took his hands out of his pockets.

He didn’t remember getting up. He was on his feet on the last plank with the rod in his hand when the barge’s side came in against the posts with a soft bump; and Gabe was over the rail before Haskett’s boys had the lines on, the way Gabe had always got over things, all at once, big; and on the jetty, and coming down it, plank by plank, toward him.

He stopped two planks off.

He was wet. His coat was still stiff with river water at the hem, and there was a graze along one cheekbone, and he was bigger than Ars remembered, though that wasn’t possible; three and a half years in Harl’s forge and the Lists. He stood on the jetty with his hands at his sides and looked at Ars.

“Hi,” said Gabe.

“Hi.”

“I’ve got something for you.”

He put his hand inside his shirt. And brought out a twist of oilcloth, damp, and held it out on his palm, and didn’t open it.

Ars looked at it.

He knew what it was. Of course he did. He’d read every line of it in the channel all winter, in the dark on the step: eleven files, thirty-two thousand, seven letters, the eye’s done, the point’s done. A finger-length of star-iron off the prow of the sword on his wall, the one he’d made at this jetty with the king holding and the boy tapping where. He’d known what Gabe was making since the autumn.

He hadn’t known what it would look like. Lying on Gabe’s palm on the end of his jetty, in a twist of wet oilcloth, with the light going.

He put the rod down on the plank. He took the oilcloth off Gabe’s hand.

Inside it was a hook. Cold. With frost in it, deep down, if you turned it to the light. A shank, an eye, a bend, and a barb so fine you could have hung a hair on it. And down the shank, cut so small he had to hold it up to the last of the light to read it, in capital letters, the old square ones off a stone:

RESURGAM.

He stood there.

“I picked it off a gravestone,” said Gabe. “When I was eleven. I don’t know whose.” He put his hands back in his pockets. “Thea said the star wasn’t written in anything. When you made it. That it was the only thing she’d ever read that didn’t say anything.” He looked at the hook in Ars’s hand. “It says something now.”

Ars didn’t say anything for a long time.

He knew the word. He’d known it in the other life, and kept it, the way he’d kept all of them. Alea. Astra. Resurgam. Bits of a language nobody spoke any more, that he’d liked because nobody spoke it. He’d put them in his statuses and his group names and on the hilt of a sword, and a handful of people in two worlds had ever known why.

I shall rise again.

“Bro,” said Gabe, very quietly, when it had gone on long enough. “Say something. I filed it for three months.”

“It’s good,” said Ars.

His voice came out wrong. He let it.

“It’s very good, Gabe.”

Gabe’s face did the thing it had always done, in every school hall and kitchen in another life, when somebody he loved said something nice to him: it went red from the neck up and tried to look somewhere else and couldn’t find anywhere.

“Okay,” said Gabe. “Okay. Good.”

He didn’t put it on the rod. Not then. He wrapped it back in its oilcloth and put it inside his own coat, flat, against his chest, beside the letter that had come by the road at ice-out, where he’d kept the letter for three weeks; and the two of them lay there together, the paper and the iron.

Then the others were off the barge. Deston, who stopped at the foot of the jetty and looked at him for a long time and didn’t say anything, which from Deston was louder than shouting. Calvin, who said hi, Declan and the kettle’s still going and held it up as proof. Dennis, with a small net folded tight in his arms, knotted at the corners, carrying it the way you’d carry something that wasn’t yours. Rachael, who stood at the end of the jetty with her arms folded and looked at him and nodded, once, the way she had the first time, two springs ago, on the landing stage, and that was all, and it was a great deal. The woman with the sword, who was Sabine, who looked him up and down with a mercenary’s eye and said so you’re the lake.

And they all looked at him. All five. The way people look at a man when they’ve brought him something up a river for three weeks and don’t know yet if he wants it.

It’s yours to hear first, the letter had said. We’ll bring it at ice-out, all of us. Nothing’s wrong.

“Come up,” said Ars. “There’s soup. Alea’s made too much. She always does when people are coming.”

“I don’t always,” said Alea.

“You always do,” said Ars.

They ate first. That was right; you didn’t do anything on an empty stomach that you could do on a full one. He’d learned that on the Redmarch at fifteen, from a sergeant who’d have hit him for saying it any other way. Alea had made too much, and Calvin had brought a sack of Kingsferry bread, and Dennis had brought a round of cheese wrapped in muslin that he said had cost more than the boat, and Group 1 came over from the town at dusk with a pot of something of Maren’s and stood in the yard and said hello and went away again, all of them, without being asked. Xola went to hug Rachael and stopped a step short, remembering, and bowed instead, very low, with Squiddles bowing on her shoulder; and Rachael stood there with her arms folded and her face trying not to do anything and said okay, okay, that’s enough. Higu looked at Deston, and Deston looked at Higu, and something passed between the two of them that Ars didn’t follow and didn’t try to. Thea came last. She stood in the cabin door and looked at the table, and at Alea, and at him; and at the place on his coat where the hook was, though she couldn’t have known it was there.

“Tomorrow,” said Thea. To Alea. “If you want. I’ll be at the militia hall.”

“Tomorrow,” said Alea.

And Thea went. And the door shut. And it was the seven of them in the cabin, round the table, with the lamp in the middle, and the fire, and the sword on Garrow’s pegs on the wall, and the hens asleep in the window, and nobody saying anything.

Gigi was on her bench. He could see her through the window if he turned his head. She’d said she’d be sitting on it. She was.

“Okay,” said Deston.

They did it the way they did everything. He’d watched it from the outside all winter, in the channel, in the gaps between what they said; he’d known they were doing it, even if he hadn’t known what it was. Each of them had a piece, and each of them put their piece on the table, one at a time, and Dennis said true at the end of each one. It was very orderly. It was the most orderly thing he’d ever seen five of his friends do, in two worlds.

Gabe went first. The folds. Harl’s blade over the back door, black, folded five times, five’s a master. The sums on the anvil in soapstone: five folds is thirty-two layers, fifteen is thirty-two thousand seven hundred and sixty-eight. It doubles. Smiths at home did that. A long way north of where Gabe grew up, for a thousand years. Somebody from home made it.

“True,” said Dennis.

Deston went next. A cord from Solmere, in the night, three weeks before the river opened. Cursiffe, in the archive, in a dead king’s little purse book, with Oswin reading the old court hand out loud. They asked again today, in the margin, for nine years. And then not. They have stopped asking. I did not think I would grieve that. I do. A forge in the north yard. A star that wouldn’t take the fire from anybody but her. A striker called Tam Ashe who burned both his hands and wouldn’t stop. A man who folded it fifteen times a night and counted out loud in a language nobody here knew. A woman who wrote on it before every fold, a few strokes and a little loop at the end like a girl finishing her name.

To hold the mouth. The one in the leaves. As long as we can.

Two horses. Bread for forty days. A long sword-belt. The road east. And a king who’d had their places laid at table for ten years, and had them taken away, and told his household they’d gone home.

Deston read it off a sheet in Cursiffe’s small careful English. His voice didn’t change. His hands did.

“True,” said Dennis. “Cursiffe believes it. Oswin read it to him. I’d say the book does too.”

Ars sat at the end of the table with his hands flat on the wood and didn’t say anything.

Then Calvin. The wind, on the mast of the Widow, at dusk in the snow; and on it, from across the river, an old woman singing. And Dennis, after him, because it was Dennis’s as much as Calvin’s: twelve afternoons on a coil of wet rope on the south wharf, learning to tie a mesh-knot. The last well. Barley and goats and looking at the floor. A man called Emmet Rook with a broken leg in a well-house. Forty years from up the Sallow.

And the song.

Calvin sang it. He hadn’t meant to; he said so after. He’d meant to say the words. But when he got to it he found he couldn’t say them without the tune, because they’d only ever come to him with the tune, on the wind; and so he sang it, very quietly, in English, at the cabin table, with his hands round a cup:

One hill, and one nail, and one ring round the hill. Two rings, and two hands that hold the nail still.

All of it. The three rings and the three nights and keep your back to the cold. Four hands. And they’re coming from the west. And then the last verse; the one a girl had sung with a fever in Maud’s infirmary, holding the hand of a woman who’d told her she was from up the Sallow.

Six rings, and no hands, and a boy from the west. He pulled out the nail, little one, and the hill gets no rest.

Nobody said anything.

Dennis told the rest. The girl at the well in a village two days north of the last one, at dawn, six or seven years old. The boy coming out of the black, out of the east, where nobody comes from, on his own. A soldier’s grey coat. The Nail on a strap over his shoulder, dragging, so that it cut a line in the ground behind him all the way. His hands in rags, bleeding through. The dogs going under the houses. The priest who walked east afterwards, all the way in, a month, and came back and sat down in the doorway of the well-house and put his hands flat on the ground and wept, and told them the hill was empty.

“True,” said Dennis. At the end of it. Very quietly. “All of it. She believes every word.”

And then he stopped. And looked at the folded net in his lap, the one he’d carried up the river like a thing that wasn’t his.

Ars sat at the end of the table.

He’d known since the jetty. Since ice-out. Since the letter in the big square hand, we found the other half of the story. He’d known since before that, if he was honest: since the night on the ice when Alea had written tell me where you found it on the back of the list, and he’d told her, the whole of it, for the first time in his life, and seen the rings in the grass in his head while he told it, and known them. The rings on the rise. The rings in Hyvred’s valley. The ring under Diwa’s grave that Yan could feel. All the same drawer.

He’d known. He hadn’t known this.

Two people. From home. Who’d asked a king for nine years to send them home and been told no, every day. Who’d stopped asking. Who’d learned to read a language nobody could read, and found something in it, and gone to a forge, and made a thing to hold it, and walked east with it, as long as we can, and put it in a hill on the far side of the black where nobody goes, and walked round and round it holding it till they died. And the grass had grown in rings where they walked.

He’d walked up that hill at nineteen.

He’d been happy. That was the thing he’d told Alea on the ice, and it was still the thing. He’d been nineteen and at Cinderhold four years, and Tempered nearly to Honed, and Diwa had had a baby at the end of the summer before in the infirmary, a small furious fox girl with her mother’s ears, and he’d been so happy he couldn’t sit still in it. So he’d walked out of the east gate one morning before the bell with a horse and some bread and not told anybody. To see. Because he could. Because he’d never been anywhere he could walk out of before, in either life, and the black was there, and nobody went there.

And he’d found a hill. Green, in a black land. With no birds. With the grass in rings round it, and spokes. And a sword in the top of it where the spokes met, point down, with a bare iron grip, waiting.

And he’d pulled it out. It had taken a day. It had come the way a nail comes out of old wood. And the ground had breathed out, once, very cold, from a hole the width of his hand, and he’d never looked in.

He’d dragged it home. All summer. It was somebody’s, Diwa had said, in the infirmary door, with the baby on her hip.

“I pulled it out,” said Ars.

Nobody said anything.

He hadn’t said it loudly. He’d said it the way he said most things, to the table, with his hands flat on the wood. But it went round the room the way a dropped plate goes round a room, and stopped everybody.

“I pulled it out,” he said again. “I didn’t know what it was. I didn’t ask. I was nineteen and I was happy and I walked into the black because it was there and I found a sword in a hill and I pulled it out.” He looked at his hands. “And something breathed out of the hole. And I went home.”

Alea’s hand was on his arm.

He didn’t know when she’d put it there. She was sitting beside him on the bench, where she always sat, on his left, with Astra’s grip over her shoulder; and her hand was on his forearm, flat, not holding it, just there. She didn’t say anything.

“They held it,” said Ars. “Two of them. From home. Three hundred years. They walked round it till they died and the grass grew in their footsteps, and then it held on its own, for however long, with nobody walking. And I walked up at nineteen with a horse and pulled it out like a fence post.” He was quiet. “And I carried it into a war. And eight years after I pulled it, the rise—“

He stopped.

He couldn’t say that one. Not yet. Not at a table, with Diwa’s daughter’s hand on his arm. The rise, an hour from Cinderhold, where the general had lit the fires so he could see it from the wall; and the ring on it, burned into the ground, with lines; that Yan had knelt beside with him, a year and a half ago, and felt. The same shape. Different drawers, he’d thought, all those years. They weren’t.

He didn’t know. He didn’t know if it was the same thing. He didn’t know if what had breathed out of a hand-wide hole on a green hill at the far side of the black when he was nineteen had walked west across the red, eight years later, and found a fox girl from the infirmary on a rise an hour from the fort. He didn’t know.

He wasn’t going to know tonight.

“I don’t know what it was holding,” he said. Instead. “Nobody does. Yeva doesn’t. The king who paid for the coal didn’t. They didn’t tell him. The mouth. The one in the leaves.” He looked at Deston. “Thea might, one day.”

“Thea might,” said Deston.

“Not yet,” said Ars.

He asked them, then. Near the end. When the lamp had burned down and the fire was low and Calvin had put the kettle on again because nobody knew what else to do.

“Keep it off the channel,” he said. “For now. Please.”

They looked at him.

“Not because it’s a secret,” he said. “It’s not a secret. You found it. Cursiffe found it. An old woman on a wharf has been singing it to herself for forty years. It’s not mine to keep.” He turned his cup on the table. “But I want to know what to say first. Before thirty people ask me what I’m going to do about it. I don’t know what I’m going to do about it.” He was quiet. “I’ve never known what to do about anything until I’d sat with it for a while. I’d like to sit with this one.”

“Rule three,” said Calvin.

They all looked at him.

“Don’t show them your whole window,” said Calvin. “That was always about him. About them. About the Advisor.” He looked at Ars across the table. “It’s about you now. You get to not show it. Till you want to.”

“Rule three, turned round,” said Deston, slowly.

“Rule three, turned round,” said Gabe.

Rachael, at the end of the table, with her arms folded, hadn’t said a word all night. She unfolded them now. She looked at Ars down the length of the table with her sharp face in the low light, the way she’d looked at him on the landing stage the first time, two springs ago, arms folded, letting him look.

“You didn’t know,” she said.

“No.”

“You didn’t ask.”

“No.”

“Nobody told you there was anything to ask.” She put her hands flat on the table, the way he had his. “Nobody told us anything either. A man in grey healed me in the first five minutes and I thought he was God, and three of my friends thought he was the villain in a book, and we were all wrong, and none of us asked him the right question for three years, because we didn’t know what it was.” She looked at him. “You were nineteen. You found a sword. Sometimes you find a sword.”

Ars looked at her.

“I’m not saying it’s nothing,” said Rachael. “I’m saying you don’t get to carry it on your own. That’s all. It’s not allowed. We came up a river.” She folded her arms again. “That’s all. I’ve said it.”

It didn’t ring. He saw Dennis not hear it ring. He’d have known; you could see it on Dennis, the little flinch, when one did.

“Thank you, Rae,” said Ars.

Dennis gave him the net last.

He’d held on to it all night. On his knees, through the soup and the folds and the cord and the song. He held it out across the table now, folded tight, knotted at the corners.

“She said give it to whoever’s at the end of the river,” said Dennis. “We didn’t tell her who. Rule three. She guessed there was somebody.” He put it down on the table in front of Ars. “And she said: tell him an old woman at the last well said a song back to him. He’ll know what it means or he won’t.”

Ars looked at the net.

He didn’t pick it up. He sat looking at it, on the table, in the low light. A small net. A good one; every knot the same. The kind you’d use for something small, close to the shore.

“There was a girl,” he said.

They waited.

“At a well. On the way back. I don’t know which well; there were a lot of them, at the edge, little places, a few houses each. I didn’t stop at any of them.” He was looking at the net. “Everybody ran. When I came through. The dogs, and the people. I didn’t blame them; I could feel what was on the strap, the way things looked at it. Everybody went inside and shut the doors.” He was quiet a long time. “There was a girl at one of them who didn’t. At dawn. Small. With a bucket. She stood by the well with her bucket and watched me go past, all the way, and didn’t run.”

Nobody said anything.

“I’ve thought about her,” said Ars. “Sometimes. I never knew why she didn’t run.”

Dennis’s face had gone very still.

“She died in Kingsferry,” said Dennis. Quietly. “The end of the short war. Thirteen. Carrying water for them at the crossings, and a horse kicked her, and they brought her down the river to Maud’s. Yeva sat with her. Held her hand. She sang the new verse in the fever.” He swallowed. “Yeva buried her. In the paupers’ ground behind the rope-walk. With a stone. With a name on it that isn’t hers, because she never knew it.”

Ars didn’t move.

He sat at the end of the table with his hands flat on the wood. The fire ticked. In the window, one of the hens woke and said something and went back to sleep. And Alea’s hand on his arm closed, for the first time all night, round his wrist, and held it.

“It might not be her,” said Deston. Gently. “There were a lot of wells.”

“It might not be,” said Ars.

He picked up the net. He held it in both hands on the table.

“Tell her,” he said, after a long time. “When you go back. Tell her I heard it.” He stopped. “Tell her the boy from the west heard it. And that he’s sorry. And that he still doesn’t know why the girl didn’t run.” He looked at the net. “And that he’d like to.”

“I’ll tell her,” said Dennis.

It wasn’t a question, so it couldn’t ring. But Ars saw Dennis’s face, and knew that if it had been, it wouldn’t have.

They slept where they fell. That was what the cabin was like when it was full; it had been like that the first spring they came, with the five of them on the floor in a row, and the king’s visit, and the week of the star. Calvin on the floor by the fire with the kettle. Dennis in the chair. Rachael on Alea’s bed, because Alea said so, and Alea in the loft with the hens’ overflow. Sabine outside, on the step, with her sword across her knees, because she said she was a guard and that was where guards went, and also because she wanted to see if it was true about the lake at dawn.

Gabe went down to the shingle. He said he’d slept in a forge for three and a half years and couldn’t sleep under a roof without an anvil. He lay down on the stones at the foot of the jetty with his coat over him, a hand from the young ash with its four leaves now, and was asleep before Ars had finished saying goodnight.

Deston stayed up. He sat at the table with his hands round a cold cup and didn’t say anything. Ars sat across from him. They’d done that before, in another life, more than once: two people at a table late, not saying anything, for as long as it took. It was the same. It was exactly the same.

“You okay?” said Deston, eventually.

“No,” said Ars.

Deston nodded.

“Good,” he said. “That’s the right answer. I’d have worried about anything else.” He finished his cold tea. “We’re here a while. Haskett wants a week for the rudder. Dennis wants a month for the cheese. I want—“ He stopped. “We’ll be here a while.”

“Good,” said Ars.

Gigi was still on her bench.

He went out to her at the last hour before dawn, when the cabin was asleep and Deston had finally put his head down on his arms on the table and gone. He took two cups. He walked up the yard and along the lake road in the dark with the cups in his hands and the hook and the letter against his chest, and the frost on the grass, and stopped at the bench.

She was awake. Of course she was. She had her coat round her and her watch in her fist and the leaves along the back of the bench were black in the dark, and she looked up at him.

He gave her a cup. She took it. He sat down beside her on the bench, on the smooth grey root, the shape of somebody sitting. It was warm. Warmer than the night.

She didn’t ask.

That was the thing about Gigi. He’d known it since the first autumn, when he’d asked her you okay a hundred times and she’d never answered, and then answered with the mess, and then asked him, at the gap in the hedge, for the first time anybody had. She asked once. And then she waited. And she never asked twice in a night, because that would be asking for the answer you wanted.

“I’ve heard it,” he said.

She didn’t say anything.

“The rest of it. They brought it up the river.” He looked at the cup. “It’s still no. I wanted you to know I’ve heard it. That’s all.”

“Okay,” said Gigi.

They sat there.

“It’s a good bench,” he said, after a while.

“Shut up,” said Gigi.

He nearly laughed. He didn’t, quite. But something in his chest that had been held since ice-out went loose, a hair, the way a line goes loose when the fish lets go.

“I mean it,” he said. “It’s a good bench. It’s the best bench I’ve ever sat on.”

“It grew,” said Gigi. “I didn’t do anything. I just kept sitting on it.”

“I know,” said Ars. “That’s how it works.”

He put the hook on at dawn.

On the end of the jetty, on the last plank, with the lake grey and flat and the mist lying on it in sheets and the first birds starting in the reeds. Not on the new rod. On the old one; the one he’d had since before Alea could walk, that Garrow had re-whipped twice and Bram once and that had a crack near the butt he’d been meaning to do something about for ten years. He took the oilcloth out of his coat and unwrapped the hook and held it up to the grey light.

RESURGAM.

He tied it on. His hands knew how; they’d known for twenty years. A clinch knot, five turns, wet it, pull. He didn’t bait it. He’d never baited a first cast. He didn’t know why; he never had.

Behind him, at the foot of the jetty, Gabe was awake. Sitting up on the shingle with his coat round his shoulders, beside the ash, watching. Not saying anything. Alea was in the cabin door with her hair everywhere and Astra over her shoulder. Sabine was on the step with her apple. On the bench along the lake road, very small in the mist, somebody was sitting.

He cast.

The way he always cast. Not hard. A long easy line going out over the grey water, unrolling, and the hook at the end of it a bright point in the mist for a moment, a bit of fallen star with a word on it; and then it went in. A small sound. Barely a sound. And it went down, into the dark under the grey, the way everything goes down into the lake, slowly, turning; and the line went down after it; and was still.

He stood there.

He thought about two people from home walking round a hill till they died. About a woman with a little loop at the end of her word. About a king laying places at a table for ten years and then telling everybody they’d gone home. About a girl at a well with a bucket, who didn’t run. About a boy with a horse, happy, walking into the black because it was there.

About what had breathed out.

I shall rise again. Off a gravestone. Gabe hadn’t known whose.

He didn’t know, either, standing on the end of his jetty in the mist with the line going down into the dark, whether it was a promise or a warning. Whether it was about the thing in the hill, or about the two who’d held it, or about him. He thought it might be all of them. He thought most words worth cutting into iron were.

He waited. The line was still. The mist moved on the water.

Then he drew it in. Slowly. Hand over hand, the way you do when there’s nothing on it. The wet line coming up out of the dark, and the water running off it, and at the end of it, coming up out of the lake into the grey light, cold and bright and empty, the hook.

It came up. That was all. It went down into the dark and it came up again.

He held it in his palm, wet, with the lake running off it, and looked at the word.

At the foot of the jetty, Gabe stood up.
