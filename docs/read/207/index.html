The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 7: Myths

Chapter 207

The Knoll

8,976 words · 39 min read

Salty knew weights.

Everything had one. That was the first thing this world had taught him, before tiers, before aura, before the red. A sack of meal. An anvil, which was as much as a horse. A black sword the length of a man, which was more than anything had a right to be, until something in his chest opened up and took it. You picked a thing up and it told you what it was. You couldn’t lie to a weight.

The night had one.

He felt it come up the slope. Not the way Yan felt things, or Maigo. He didn’t have that. He just stood on the south side of the ring by Rhea with his greatsword up in both hands and the forge coming up in him and felt the whole knoll go heavy under his feet, the way a floor goes heavy when a lot of people stand on it at once. A hundred and sixty-one of them at the bottom of the slope, leaning. Four big ones coming up through them. Forty soldiers in a ring. A chaplain singing flat. Wendy behind him by the cairn with a glowing arrow drawn.

Heavy. All of it. He could feel exactly how heavy.

“Three,” said Yan.

Salty didn’t take his eyes off the slope.

“Three coming,” said Yan, from the middle, slow and calm. “South, west, north. One’s staying back.” A pause. “The big one. It’s sat down at the bottom. It’s watching.”

“Sat down,” said Rhea.

“Like a dog,” said Yan.

“I hate that,” said Rhea, and lifted her sword, and the light came up along the edge of it, thin and silver.

They walked through the fire.

He’d known they would. He’d seen it two summers ago. They didn’t jump it and they didn’t go round. They came up the slope at a steady heavy trot, the three of them, as tall as the scouts and twice as deep through the chest, their grey hides hanging off them loose and creased like wet canvas, horned heads down, arms so long the knuckles brushed the grass. When they got to Aldous’s fire they walked into it. Salty heard it on them: a hiss, like fat thrown on a griddle. And a smell came up the slope that wasn’t burning meat, colder than that, a cellar with a candle just pinched out in it. They came out the other side smoking, with the fire still stuck to their hides in patches, and kept coming.

The south one came straight at Rhea.

“Ring!” Teague was roaring it, the way he had two summers ago. “Ring! Shields!”

The shields came up. The spears came down over them, the old ones’ spears, eight of them on the south side, levelled over the shoulders of the front rank in a long bristling line, the way they’d drilled it all Saturday a hundred paces from the gate. And the south one hit the line.

Salty felt it in his feet. The whole ring rocked, and somewhere on his left a shield-rim cracked against a helmet with a sound like a dropped pan.

Three spears went into it, deep: one in the chest, two in the thick of the shoulder. The ash shafts bowed. Two of them snapped, crack, crack, and the old men holding them went down on their knees with the broken ends in their fists. It didn’t stop. It leaned into the rest, and the men behind them went back a step, two steps, their heels gouging long brown skids in the green, and it raised one long arm over the shields—

And Rhea cut it.

She went in under the arm, low and fast, the way she did everything. The silver line on her sword went in at its ribs and came out at its back, and the grey hide opened behind the edge like a sack slit with a knife, and the black stuff that wasn’t blood came out of it in a sheet and smoked where it hit the grass. It screamed, high and thin, like wind in a crack: the sound one of them had made two summers ago, going over Rhea’s head on the end of a line. And the arm came round anyway, the back of it, the whole long weight of it, the way it had two summers ago, and caught her across the left side.

She went ten feet. She’d been low, or it would have been twenty. She hit the green on her shoulder inside the ring and rolled over the arm underneath her, and Salty heard it go: a short wet snap, like green wood over a knee.

She came up on one knee. Her left arm hung from the shoulder with the hand turned the wrong way round. Her sword was in her right.

“AGAIN,” said Rhea.

Salty went in.

He didn’t decide. He never did. The thing was half through the spears with Rhea’s cut across its ribs and its arm still coming back from the swing, and there was a space, the size of a man, between its arm and its side, where it had opened up to hit her. And he was in the space, with the forge roaring, and he swung.

Two summers ago his sword had stuck. He remembered. He’d swung the best swing of his life into the north one, just above the hip, and it had gone in a hand’s width and stuck there, and he’d stood there pulling on it like a man pulling a cart out of mud, and it had hit him and broken his ribs. He’d thought about that every day since. In the forge. With the hammer. You don’t leave it in.

He didn’t leave it in.

The greatsword went into its side under the arm. He felt the hide give, and the meat under it, cold and dense, and the blade went in a hand’s width, two, and slowed, the grey closing round the steel the way wet clay closes round a spade. He felt it start to grip. And he pulled: with his legs, down through his heels into the green and back up through all of him, the way you take a shoe out of the fire with the tongs, in one movement, before the iron’s had time to stick.

It came out with a sound like a boot coming out of a bog, and the black came after it in a rope, all down his forearms, so cold it burned.

And Rhea was there beside him, one-armed, grey in the face, and her sword went in after his. Into the same hole. Silver. To the hilt.

The south one stood there for a moment, very still, with Rhea’s sword in it and the old men’s spears in it and Salty in front of it with black running off his elbows. Then its knees went. It sat down on its haunches on the green, heavily, like a very tired dog, and leaned over sideways, slowly, and the ground took its weight with a thump that came up through Salty’s boots. The fire on its hide went out. It didn’t move again.

Nobody cheered.

Salty had thought, once or twice, lying awake, that if it ever happened, if they ever brought one of those down on their own, without him, somebody would cheer. Nobody did. Teague just stood there on the south side with his split shield on his bad arm, breathing, looking at it. And the old ones looked at it. And one of them, Jory Platt, with his three fingers on his spear, said very quietly, “That’s one,” the way you’d say it at a graveside.

“Ring,” said Teague. Hoarse. “Close it up. Ring.”

The ring closed up.

“West!” said Yan. “West, on Suzu!”

Salty turned.

The west one was through the fire and through the shields on the west side and halfway across the green toward the cairn, and Suzu had it. He saw her go. A long white shape, low on the green, faster than anything that size should move, going for its legs, the way she had two summers ago, and wrapping round them, once, twice, and pulling. It staggered. Its long arms went out for balance. And the old men on the west side went into it with their spears while it was off balance, all of them at once, the way they’d been waiting nine years to go into something.

It tore free.

Salty saw it happen. It reached down with one long hand, almost lazily, and closed its grey fingers round the white body wrapped round its knees. He saw the fingers sink in. He saw Suzu’s scales pucker under them and something dark come up between them. It hauled her off its legs in one long unwinding heave, a man dragging a mooring rope off a post, swung her once, and let go.

She hit the cairn side-on, against the foot of the black stone, with a sound like a wet sack dropped on a step. Two of the children’s stones went skittering out across the green. She lay among the rest of them and didn’t move.

Yan made a sound Salty had never heard Yan make.

Then Wendy’s arrows came down on it. Three. Out of the dark above the ring, glowing, falling straight down out of the air where she’d held them, the way she’d learned in the second winter, into its back and its shoulders, one after another after another, thock thock thock. And Aldous turned on his knees at the edge of the ring and lifted his free hand toward it, still singing, and the fire on the edge of the green went up, behind it, in a great roaring sheet that Salty felt on his face from forty paces, and the old ones drove their spears into it from the front and pushed.

Back. Toward the fire. Step by step, with its arms going and its horned head down, back across the green, the old men leaning into the spear-shafts with their heels in the grass and their teeth bared, not shouting, not making a sound, back and back, until it was in the fire.

It went down the slope burning.

Salty watched it go. Out through Aldous’s fire on the west side and away down the dark slope, on fire all over, stumbling, through the line of scouts at the bottom, which parted for it, and out onto the red beyond. It got perhaps fifty paces. Then it lay down.

It kept burning, out there in the dark, for a long time.

“That’s two,” said Jory Platt.

Yan was on his knees by the cairn with Suzu in his lap and the scythe dropped on the green beside him.

She’d gone small. Not by choosing. The way she went small when she was hurt, when she didn’t have anything left to be big with. Scarf-sized. Limp. White, with something dark on the white along one side where the hand had closed. Her green eyes were open. She was looking at Yan.

Yan had his hand on her. Flat. On the dark place.

Salty knew what he was doing. They all did. Two summers ago, on the walk home from the grave, Yan had told them in the chapel, very calmly, the way he told them everything. I made her. She couldn’t eat the cold ones; there was nothing in a demon she could take. And when she was hurt, and there was nothing else, she could take some of his. It came back. He said it came back.

Salty watched Yan’s face go grey.

Not pale. Yan was always pale. Grey. The colour of the grass outside the green. It went out of him slowly, as if somebody were drawing the colour off him with a cloth, and into his hand, and into the small white snake under it.

Suzu was fighting it. Salty could see her fighting it. Her head was turned away, her mouth open, hissing at him. She tried to bite his wrist. He didn’t move his hand.

“Yan,” said Wendy. Sharp.

“I know,” said Yan. Very quietly. “I know. She’s telling me too.”

He didn’t take his hand away until the dark place on her side had gone white again. Then he did. And sat there on the green with his back against the cairn and his eyes shut and his face the colour of ash, and Suzu went up his arm onto his shoulder and round his neck and lay there, small, with her head against his jaw, and Salty heard her. He couldn’t hear her. Nobody could but Yan, and Kei’s snake. But he heard her anyway, the way you hear somebody being furious in another room.

“Twenty heartbeats,” said Wendy. To nobody. Flatly. “He gave her twenty heartbeats’ worth.”

She’d counted it.

Of course she had.

And while everybody was looking at the west one, the scouts came in.

He should have seen it coming. He’d seen it before. They didn’t want the people. They’d never wanted the people. Three big ones through the fire in three places, and the fire low and broken where they’d gone through, and the scouts had been standing at the bottom of the slope in their long line all that time, perfectly still, leaning. Waiting for the gaps.

They came through the gaps.

Bursts. Step-step-step and stop. Tall, thin, grey, faceless, bent forward, through the low places in the fire on the south side and the west and the north, where the big ones had broken it, and onto the green.

And the green held them.

Salty saw that. He’d remember it all his life. The nearest came through the low place in the fire ten paces from him: taller than any man and thin as a rake, bent forward from the hips, with a smooth grey nothing where a face should be, smoking at the shins where it had stepped through the flames. It put one long foot down on the soft short grass with the white and blue flowers in it, and the grass took hold. Roots came up out of the green, thin and pale and very fast, and went round its ankle three times and pulled tight. He heard them creak, like a mooring rope taking a boat’s weight. It stopped dead, mid-stride. It leaned against them, and they creaked, and held.

All round the knoll, wherever a scout put a foot on the green, the same.

Two summers ago Maigo had made them stop for a heartbeat. On his knees on the red, with his hands flat, stay, stay, stay, and a heartbeat was all he’d had.

It wasn’t a heartbeat now.

Salty looked at the ash. Maigo was sitting against it, where he’d been all night, with his root foot flat on the green and his face screwed up behind his sunglasses and his lips moving. Stay. Stay. Stay. And the tree over him with every leaf still pointed east, and the roots coming up out of the green all round the knoll, everywhere the scouts put a foot, holding.

Two hands, Maigo always said. Three, with the spike.

The old ones went into the stuck scouts with their spears. Jory Platt took the one in front of Salty: two steps and a long low thrust with his whole weight behind it, three fingers and all, in under where its ribs should have been. It folded over the shaft without a sound and hung there, held up by its own ankles, until he put his boot on it and pulled the spear out. The archers in the middle with Wendy shot the rest. They went down one after another, grey and silent, with roots round their ankles, and didn’t get up.

But there were a hundred and sixty-one.

And roots can only hold so many at once.

Some of them got through.

Not many. Six. Seven. Salty never knew how many exactly; Wendy would know. They came through the low place on the east side, past Niko’s gap, where Niko took one and then another and was standing in his gap with his short sword going in and out like a needle, very calm, holding, and they went round him. Past the old men. Past the archers. In bursts, step and stop, and they weren’t stopping for the roots any more because Maigo was holding so many he’d run out of hands.

To the cairn.

To the stone.

They put their hands on it.

Salty saw it from the south side. Their long grey fingers on the black iron, and the iron burning them, the way it had burned them two summers ago, smoke going up off their hands where they touched it. They didn’t let go. They never let go. Six of them. Seven. All round the black stone, with their hands on it, their long arms wrapped round it, the smoke coming off them.

And they pulled.

Maigo screamed.

Salty had never heard Maigo scream. He’d heard him shout, all the time, about everything. He’d heard him cry, on the walks out to the grave the first spring, though Maigo didn’t know he had. He’d never heard him scream. It came out of him against the tree, high and raw, with his root foot jammed down flat in the green and his whole small body arched off the bark.

“It’s moving!”

The stone moved.

Salty saw it. Not much. A finger’s width. The black iron slab rocking in the cairn, the stones round its foot shifting and clicking, the children’s stones, the scorched one, the white one at the head, and the whole black stone lifting. Up. Out. A finger’s width. Two. The way a nail comes out of a board when you get the claw of the hammer under it and lean.

“The spike,” Maigo was screaming. “It’s coming out, they’re pulling it out, it’s under, it’s—it’s lifting its head, Salty, it’s lifting its head—”

Salty didn’t know what lifted its head. He didn’t need to. He’d felt what was under that stone once, through his hands, in the frost, the second winter, carrying it the last stretch. Not felt. He didn’t feel things that way. Weighed. He’d known, carrying it, the whole way, that it was heavy because it knew where it was going. He’d known when it went into the hole like a key that it was right. He didn’t know what it was keeping shut.

He knew it was keeping something shut.

Three fingers. Four.

He dropped his sword.

He crossed the green in six steps.

He didn’t remember them afterwards. He remembered the sword hitting the grass behind him, that heavy dull sound, and then he was at the cairn, on the east side, the back of the stone, where the scouts were, and he put his shoulder into the nearest one the way he’d put his shoulder into one two summers ago, low, at the hip, with all of him behind it. It was like running into a bundle of cold sticks. Something in it went with a sound like dry kindling snapping, and it folded away from the stone and went down. He put his fist into the next one’s long grey wrist where it gripped the iron, and felt the wrist give under his knuckles, and the hand came open.

And he got his arms round the stone.

Round the black iron. From behind. From the east, with his chest against the plain back of it, the side without letters, and his arms wrapped round it as far as they’d go, his hands meeting on the other side, on the front, on the west face, where he knew without looking the letters were. The black iron cold and smoking under his forearms where the scouts had had their hands. The other scouts still on it, all round him, pulling, their long arms over his, their grey hands next to his hands on the iron, smoke coming up between his fingers.

He held it.

And the bear sat down on it.

He didn’t decide that either. It was just what happened. It was what always happened. Every time he picked a thing up, or held it, or bore it, the weight of it went off his joints and sat on his aura instead, and the thing went heavy.

He’d been cursing it for a week. The skillet. Every time he tried to toss it, it went heavy in his hands, like a stone, and dropped. It’s the bear, he’d said, red to the ears, with the noodles on the hearth and the whole forge laughing at him. I can’t move what I’m carrying. You can’t toss a thing if you’re carrying it.

You couldn’t.

And nobody else could either.

The stone went down.

He felt it go. All at once, under his arms. The way the skillet had gone down onto the fire-bed with a sound like a dropped cartwheel. The black iron went heavy. Heavier than a horse. Heavier than the anvil. As heavy as a black sword the length of a man had been on the first day, when he’d put his hands on it in the courtyard and nearly not been able to lift it at all. As heavy as that, and then heavier, because he was holding it down. And it went down. Back. Into the cairn. The finger’s width, the two, the three, the four. The stones round its foot clicking back into place. The spike going back down into the old hole in the ground like a key going home into a lock.

Clunk.

He felt it seat. He’d felt it seat once before, in the frost, putting it in. It felt exactly the same.

The scouts pulled.

Nothing happened.

They pulled, all round him, seven of them, with their long arms over his and their burning hands on the iron, and the smoke coming up, and nothing happened. The stone didn’t move. It didn’t rock. It didn’t so much as click. It was as if they were trying to lift the knoll.

You can’t move what the bear sits on. Oen had said that, on the anvil, with his chin on his fist, bored. You told me. Yesterday.

Salty knelt there on the green with his arms round the black stone and his cheek against the cold iron and started to laugh.

“Ring on Salty!” That was Yan. From somewhere. His voice was thin, grey, still calm. “Salty’s anchored. Ring on Salty!”

The old ones came.

He didn’t see them. He couldn’t see anything but black iron and a long grey arm across his shoulder. But he heard them. Boots on the green. Spears. The sound a spear makes going into one of those things, which isn’t like anything else, a sort of dry thud, like putting a fork into a loaf. The scouts on the stone round him going down, one by one, letting go, falling away. Teague’s voice, hoarse: Ring. Round the stone. Ring.

And then there was a ring round him. He could feel it. Shields. Backs. Old men standing round him on the green with their spears out and their backs to him, facing out, the way you’d stand round a fire in the cold.

He stayed where he was.

He couldn’t do anything else. He knew that. He’d known it since he got his arms round it. He couldn’t swing what he was bearing. He couldn’t swing anything. His sword was on the grass ten paces behind him where he’d dropped it, and it might as well have been in Solmere. He couldn’t turn round. He couldn’t fight. He could kneel here with his arms round a black stone and hold it down, and that was all he could do, the same as two summers ago with the sword in his arms in the middle of the broken ring, going come on, then.

He stayed where he was.

The thing about carrying, Salty thought, kneeling there, with his cheek on the iron, was that people thought it was about walking.

It wasn’t. He’d carried a lot of things, out here. A black sword across the red, three hundred paces at a time, with Wendy counting. A slab of black iron in the frost, the last stretch, the whole way, not putting it down, don’t count. A hammer that had been a king’s father’s, all the way east, to put in a man’s hands. A seed, from the end of the world, in a rivet tin, that weighed something. And all of it, everybody thought, was walking. Getting a thing from here to there.

It wasn’t. Mostly it was standing still with it. Mostly it was just not putting it down.

That was most of what carrying was.

He knelt there and didn’t put it down.

The night went on.

He found that out about holding a thing: the night doesn’t care. It goes on at the same speed whatever you’re doing. He knelt in the middle of the knoll with his arms round the stone and the ring of old men round him, and the fight went on outside the ring, and he couldn’t see most of it. He heard it. He felt it, in his knees, through the green.

He heard Aldous’s song falter, and stop, and start again. Lower. The fire going down to a few low guttering patches, and then coming back up, not as high, when Aldous started again. It happened twice. The second time Aldous’s voice was hardly a voice at all. Just a thread. Salty didn’t know how he was still doing it. He was fifty-four, and he’d sat up all night once already this year, in the winter, over fourteen hundred names.

He heard Wendy. Calling. Left. Two. Gap on the north. Her voice flat and steady, the voice she used on a call, counting. He heard her arrows. The soft hiss of them going up, and the silence while she held them in the air, and then the thock when she let them fall. He counted them, after a while, the way she would have. He couldn’t help it. She’d done that to him, over the years.

He heard Niko. Not his voice. Niko didn’t say anything. He heard the short sword. In and out. And the little scuff of Niko’s boots stepping back into his gap after every one, as if he’d never left it.

He heard the scouts. Not a sound they made; they didn’t make any. The sound the green made under them. A sort of tearing, very soft. The roots coming up out of the grass, and taking hold, and holding. Over and over. And under it, from the north edge of the knoll, outside the fire, very low, so low he could only just hear it under everything, Liraun.

Singing.

He didn’t know what. It wasn’t Aldous’s song. It was lower. It went on and on, the same few notes, not a tune exactly, more like the noise you’d make to a horse in a storm. It’s all right. It’s all right. I’m here. To the grass, he thought. To the young green on the knoll and on the riverbeds, that had never in its life been walked on by anything like this, and didn’t know yet what to be afraid of, and was finding out.

She sang to it all night.

She didn’t fight.

He saw the elves once.

It was the only time he looked away from the stone. Somebody on the north side shouted, Wendy shouted, sharp, north, the north one, it’s going round, and he turned his head as far as it would go with his cheek on the iron, and looked.

The north one. The third big one. It had come through the fire on the north side with the others, an hour ago, two hours, and he’d thought Rhea’s lot had driven it back, and they had. Back out through the fire onto the grey grass outside the ring. And it had gone round.

He saw it in the firelight. Going round the outside of the ring, low, along the north edge of the knoll, past where the green stopped. In the open, on the grey grass.

Past the elves.

They were standing exactly where they’d stood all night. Three tall shapes in lichen-coloured coats, a few paces apart, just outside the fire, just outside the green. The scouts had flowed round them all night like water round stones in a river. Not touched them. Not stopped. Just gone round, the way you’d walk round a post. Salty had seen that and not understood it and not had time to think about it.

The north one went round them too.

It went past Tavrin close enough to touch. A pace. Less. Huge and grey and horned, with Rhea’s cut across its flank and three of Wendy’s arrows in it, trotting low along the edge of the fire with its long arms swinging, and it went past the old elf so close that its shoulder nearly brushed her coat. And she didn’t move. She stood there with her stave planted and her winter-ice eyes on it as it went by, and her face like birch bark in winter, and didn’t move at all.

And Oen moved.

Salty saw him. The young one. He’d stood there all night with his arms folded so hard the cords stood out in them, and when the big one went past Tavrin he went. One step. Toward it. His arms coming unfolded, his long hands opening, his ears flat against his head, and something in the set of him that Salty knew, because he’d seen it in the mirror. The way you go when you’ve decided before you know there’s anything to decide.

Tavrin’s stave came across his chest.

That was all. She didn’t look at him. She didn’t say anything. She just put the long grey stave across his chest, level, the way you’d put an arm across a door, and he hit it. And stopped. And stood there with it across him, shaking.

And the big one went on past them. Round the north side. Toward the place on the north-east of the ring where the fire had gone lowest, behind the tree, behind Maigo, who was feeling everything at once and couldn’t feel it coming.

Toward Wendy.

She was on the north-east side of the cairn. Salty could see her if he turned his head the other way, and he did. She’d moved round there an hour ago, to shoot over the low place in the fire, and she was standing on the green with her bow, with the soft blue light of a held arrow above her, and the archers round her, and her back to the north.

And she’d seen it. Because Wendy always saw things first.

She was turning. Bringing the bow round.

He saw her quiver. He couldn’t help seeing it. It was nearly empty. Two arrows. Maybe three. She’d been shooting all night.

The thing was ten paces from her. Coming the way the one two summers ago had come, the way the one in Valcrest had come, Ina had said. Not running. Gathering itself. Low. Its long arms out wide on either side and its horns down and every line of it bent toward her.

Salty was six paces from her.

Six paces, with his arms round a black stone.

His hands opened.

They were already opening. He felt them do it. The way they’d opened on the grip of the black sword two summers ago on the red, before he knew there was anything to decide. His fingers coming off the iron on the west face. His weight coming back, onto his heels, out from under the stone. The bear starting to get up. And the stone, under his arms, going light, the way it did the moment he let go of anything, light, and the scouts still on the far side of it, three of them that the old men hadn’t got to yet, feeling it go light, and pulling—

“DON’T YOU DARE.”

Wendy.

He’d never heard her shout like that. Not on Earth. Not here. Not at him. She didn’t look at him. She didn’t take her eyes off the thing coming at her. She just shouted it, over her shoulder, at the top of her voice, so that the whole knoll heard it and some of the old men flinched.

“Don’t you dare let go of it!”

He didn’t let go of it.

He did the hardest thing he had ever done in his life, in two worlds, and it was nothing. It was not moving. He closed his hands again on the black iron, on the letters he couldn’t see, and the bear sat down, and the stone went heavy, and went down, clunk, and the scouts on the far side pulled and nothing happened.

And he knelt there and watched it go for her.

Garth was there.

Salty didn’t see where he came from. Nobody did, after; Wendy said he must have been on the north side the whole time, with the archers, because Teague had put him there, because he was a recruit, and the north was supposed to be the quiet side. He was just there. Between Wendy and the thing. With his shield up and his helmet on very straight and his feet planted on the green, one in front of the other, shoulder-width apart, his weight on the back foot, exactly the way they taught you on the first day in the yard, exactly the way he stood every single time, so that Maigo had said so to the tree: He stands right.

He stood right.

The thing hit his shield.

Salty heard it from six paces: a deep wooden boom, like a door kicked in. The iron rim folded. The boards split from the boss to the edge, and the boy behind them went with them, off his feet, backwards into the air, his helmet coming off and spinning away into the dark. He landed flat on his back on the green three paces behind Wendy with a sound that went into Salty’s teeth: all the air going out of him at once, and something in his chest giving with it. He didn’t get up.

The thing came on through the pieces of the shield. Slower. It had had to stop for him.

But he’d stood there.

For as long as it takes a thing that size to go through a recruit who stands right. A heartbeat. A heartbeat and a half.

And Wendy had used it.

Salty saw her do it. He’d never seen anything like it and he’d seen her shoot ten thousand arrows.

There wasn’t time to draw. She’d had an arrow held in the air above her, glowing, the way she always did, and she let it go down, straight out of the air in front of her into the thing’s back as it came through Garth; it went in between the shoulders to the feathers with a thock like an axe into a stump. In the same moment she had another off the string, from the hip, from two paces, into the smooth grey place where its face should have been. Its head snapped back. And the last one, from one pace, so close the fletching brushed her knuckles going out, into the same place a hand lower.

It stopped. It was nowhere near dead. But it stopped, its horned head thrown back and both long arms coming up to claw at the two shafts standing out of its face, and its throat open to the sky.

And Rhea was there.

From the south. One-armed. Her left arm strapped across her chest with a strip of somebody’s cloak and her sword in her right, and the silver on it hardly a flicker now, she’d been fighting all night with a broken arm; and she came across the green behind it, and didn’t shout, and didn’t say again. She put the point in at the back of its neck, where the horns met the hide, and leaned her whole weight on the hilt, one-handed, with her broken arm pressed against her chest; and Salty heard the steel go in and in, grinding, like a spade going into gravel, until the crossguard was against the grey.

The thing’s arms dropped. It went down onto its knees, and then onto its face on the green at Wendy’s feet, and its horns dug two short furrows in the grass, and it lay there.

Rhea let go of the sword. She sat down on the grass beside it, quite suddenly, the way it had. And put her head between her knees.

“Three,” said Jory Platt, from somewhere, hoarse. “That’s three.”

Wendy was on her knees by Garth.

Salty watched her. He couldn’t do anything else. She had her hands on the boy’s chest, feeling, the way Rachael did, though Wendy didn’t have Rachael’s hands, and she was saying something to him, low and fierce. And Hesper was there, big and slow and old, with his canvas bags, getting down on the green beside her with a grunt.

Garth’s eyes opened.

“Was that right?” said Garth. Very faintly. To Wendy. “Did I stand right?”

“You stood right,” said Wendy.

“Teague said.”

“Teague was right.” She was crying. Wendy didn’t cry. Salty had seen her cry four times since he’d known her. “You stood exactly right. Shut up now. Hesper’s going to do your ribs.”

“Yes, ma’am,” said Garth, and shut his eyes, and Hesper started cutting his tunic off with a pair of shears, very gently, as if he were a large old man who’d cut a great many tunics off a great many boys on this plain and had never once, in nine years, got used to it.

She came over afterwards.

It was a long time afterwards. The fire was low. Aldous had stopped singing, and started again, and it was only a whisper now and the flames only knee-high. The scouts were standing outside it again, the ones that were left, perfectly still in their long line, leaning. At the bottom of the slope the fourth big one still sat where it had sat all night, like a dog. Waiting.

Wendy came through the ring of old men and knelt down on the green beside Salty, with her bow across her knees and her quiver empty, and didn’t say anything.

He had his cheek on the iron. He couldn’t turn his head far. He turned it as far as it went.

“You didn’t let go,” said Wendy.

“You told me not to.”

“I know.” She was quiet. “I didn’t think you’d listen.”

“Nor did I,” said Salty.

She looked at him for a long time. Her face was black with smoke except where it wasn’t, and there were two clean lines down it, and her headphones were still round her neck, silent, lavender, the way they’d been for two and a half years, and there was blood on them that wasn’t hers.

Then she put her hand in his pocket.

Salty jumped. “What—“

“Cook gave you two,” said Wendy. She took out the heel of bread. The good bread. He’d forgotten. “You haven’t eaten since lunch. You can’t let go.” She tore a bit off. “Open.”

He looked at her.

“Open,” said Wendy.

He opened. She put the bread in his mouth. He chewed it, kneeling in the middle of the knoll on the edge of the world with his arms round a black stone and the bear sitting on it and the smoke going up, and she fed him the whole heel, bit by bit, the way you’d feed somebody in a hospital bed. Neither of them said anything. Round them, the old men stood with their backs turned and their spears out and very carefully didn’t look.

“The other one’s for after,” said Wendy.

“After what?”

She looked down the slope. At the fourth one, sitting.

“After,” she said.

He talked to the stone, a bit, in the long cold part before the morning.

Not out loud. He wasn’t Maigo. In his head. The way you talk to a weight you’re carrying, on a long stretch, when there’s nobody to count for you. Come on, then. Nearly there. Not far now.

It talked back.

Not in words. In the way a weight talks back. You held a thing long enough and it told you what it was, more and more, the longer you held it. And he’d been holding this all night, with his chest against the back of it and his arms round it and his palms flat on the letters he couldn’t see, and it had been telling him.

It was a sword. That first. Under everything, under the slab and the spike and the letters, it was still a sword, and it knew it. It remembered. A thing that is made remembers being made, Tavrin had said on the forge step, with his angry hammer marks in her hand. He felt it now, through his palms, the way you’d feel the grain in a plank. The marks. Big ones. Patient ones. A smith who’d hit it a thousand times and never once too hard. The king. He’d felt them in the frost, carrying it, and not known whose they were until Garrow, walking beside the spike, told him. And it remembered being cut. One cut. Clean. On an anvil by a lake. He could feel the place where it had been one thing and become two, and how the cut had been made by somebody who loved it.

And under that, older, something else. Something he didn’t know and didn’t try to.

And it remembered being carried. That most of all. Across the red, on a man’s shoulder, like firewood. Across the red again, in a blanket, three hundred paces at a time. Up this knoll in the frost on Salty’s own back, not counting.

And it knew where it was. That was the thing. You could feel it, the way you could feel a nail that had gone in straight. It knew exactly where it was and it was exactly where it was meant to be. It had been heavy, in the frost, because it knew where it was going. It was heavy now because it had got there.

He’d told it that, in the autumn, with his hand on it. It was heavy. Because you knew where it was going.

“Not far now,” Salty told it. In his head. “Nearly morning.”

Nearly morning.

The sky over Kharvos went grey first. It always did. The mountains came up out of the dark as a black line against something a little less black, and then the line had an edge to it. And just above the edge, thin as a fingernail, the old moon came up. Very thin. Very bright. The last sliver of it, lying on its back over the mountains, the way it only did a day or two before it went altogether.

He saw it over the top of the stone.

He’d been kneeling there with his arms round it since, he thought, about midnight. He didn’t know. Six hours. Seven. The bear didn’t tire his joints; that was what it was for. But it tired him. The aura it sat on was his aura, and he’d been holding the whole weight of the stone on it, down, all night, and it had been like holding his breath with the whole of himself. He was shaking now. Not his arms. Further in. The forge in him had gone down to coals.

He didn’t let go.

Round him the knoll was very quiet.

He could see most of it, now, in the grey. He could see the ring of old men round him, thinner than it had been, men sitting where they’d been standing, men lying on the green with Hesper going between them with his bags. He could see Teague, on the south side, on one knee, with his split shield still on his bad arm and his head down. Rhea sitting by the third one, with her arm strapped across her chest and her sword across her knees, staring at nothing. Garth on a cloak by the tree. Collum beside him, holding his hand, white.

Nobody dead. Salty knew that. He’d heard Aldous say it, an hour ago, very quietly, going round on his knees. Forty-six. And then Hesper’s voice: forty-seven; you forgot me. And then, after a while, Aldous again, in the dark, as if he’d only just thought of it: forty-eight.

The fire was out. Aldous was on his knees at the edge of the green where it had been, with his book shut in his lap and his hands flat on the cover, swaying. His lips were still moving. No sound.

Maigo was against the tree, grey under the bark, his root foot still flat on the green, his sunglasses crooked. Yan sat against the cairn with Suzu round his neck and his eyes shut and his face like ash. Wendy knelt by Salty, with her empty bow, and one arrow.

One. He didn’t know where she’d found it. She had it in her hand, nocked, not drawn.

And on the east side of the ring, where the knoll went down to the riverbeds, in a dip in the edge of the green by a rock, Niko was standing in his gap.

He’d been standing in it all night. Salty knew that without having seen it. He had blood on his face from his nose, a lot of it, dried, and his short sword hanging from his hand, and he was standing exactly the way he’d stood at the start: weight on his back foot, eyes not on the slope but on the gaps in the slope.

And beyond him, down the slope, in the grey, the scouts.

They were fewer. Wendy would know how many. A hundred, maybe. Less. Standing in their long line at the bottom of the slope on the green threads of the riverbeds, perfectly still, the way they’d stood all night between the bursts. Facing the knoll.

And then, all at once, as the sky went from grey to the first thin edge of gold behind them, they leaned.

All of them. Together. The way grass leans when the wind comes over it. Every long grey body in the line tipping forward a hair further than it already was, toward the knoll, toward the cairn, toward the stone under Salty’s arms. He felt it. He felt it in the iron. As if the whole line had put its hands on the stone at once from a hundred paces away and pulled.

The stone didn’t move.

But under it, a long way down, something lifted its head.

He felt that too. He’d never felt anything like it. Down through the black iron, down through the spike, into the ground, and at the bottom of the spike, round the point of it, something small and dense and cold, moving. Not much. The way a sleeping dog moves when its name is said in another room. One ear.

“Salty,” said Maigo. From the tree. His voice was hardly anything. “It’s listening.”

“I know,” said Salty.

“Don’t let go.”

“I won’t.”

At the bottom of the slope, the fourth one stood up.

Salty saw it over the top of the stone. It had been sitting all night. Exactly where it had sat at the start, at the meeting of the two riverbeds, on the green, behind the line of scouts, like a dog at a door. It hadn’t moved once. Not when the others went down. Not when the third one died on Rhea’s sword. It had sat there and watched.

It stood up now.

It was the biggest. He’d known it would be. It was bigger than any of them, bigger than the ones two summers ago, heavier in the shoulders, with arms that hung down past its knees and horns that went back from where its face should have been in a long curve, like a ram’s. Grey. Faceless. Very still.

The scouts parted for it. The long grey line opening in the middle without a sound, like a curtain.

And it came up the slope.

Not trotting. It didn’t trot. It walked. Slowly. Up the east slope of the knoll in the first gold light, one heavy step and then another, with its long arms hanging and its horned head level, the way a man walks up a path to a door he already knows is going to open. Toward the cairn. Toward the stone.

Toward Salty.

“Big one,” said Yan. From the cairn. He hadn’t opened his eyes. His voice was thin and grey and dead calm. “East. It’s coming for the stone.” A pause. “It’s coming for Salty.”

Nobody moved.

There wasn’t anybody to move. Salty saw that. He looked round the knoll with his cheek on the iron and saw it all at once, the whole board, the way you see it at the end of a game you’ve already lost. Rhea on the grass with her arm strapped and her sword across her knees, getting up, trying to, and her legs not doing it. Teague on one knee with his head down. Aldous swaying with his lips moving and no fire. The old men, the ones still standing, too few, too far, on the wrong sides. The archers with nothing left. Maigo against the tree with his foot down, holding everything, holding the roots, holding the green, and nothing left over. Yan grey.

Wendy, beside him, with one arrow.

She drew it. To her ear. It glowed, faint, blue, in the gold light. She let it go.

It went into the big one’s chest. It didn’t stop. It didn’t slow down. It didn’t look at the arrow. It walked up the slope with the glowing arrow in its chest, one step, another, the way the others had walked through the fire, and kept coming.

Wendy lowered the bow.

That was all she had.

And he couldn’t let go.

He knew it. He knelt there with his arms round the stone and knew it the way you know a weight. If he let go, the stone went light. If the stone went light, the scouts at the bottom of the slope, leaning, all of them, would feel it. And whatever was under the spike with one ear up would feel it. And it would lift its head.

He didn’t know what happened then. He didn’t need to.

He couldn’t swing what he was bearing. He couldn’t stand up. He couldn’t turn round. He was kneeling on the green with his back to the east, to the slope, to the thing walking up it, and he could feel it coming through his knees, step by step, heavy, closer, and all he could do was kneel here and not let go.

If you go, I go.

He wasn’t going anywhere.

That was the thing. He saw it quite clearly, with his cheek on the cold iron and the sun coming up. He’d always thought it meant: if you go, I’ll come after you. Into whatever it is. And it did. It had. It had meant that, two summers ago, with the sword falling and his hands opening and fifteen paces between them.

But it meant this too. It meant: if you’re staying, I’m staying. If this is the place, this is the place.

Plant it where you are going to stay.

He stayed.

He shut his eyes and held the stone and felt the thing come up the slope behind him, step and step and step, and he didn’t let go, and he was not afraid, very much, or not as much as he’d thought, and he thought about Wendy’s hand in his pocket and a heel of good bread and a list of eleven things in a rivet tin, and he held on.

He heard Niko breathe out.

He didn’t know how he heard it. It was a small sound. A man breathing out, through his nose, slowly, the way you breathe out when you’ve been holding it for a long time. It came from the east. From the gap on the edge of the green, by the rock. Where Niko was standing.

Between the stone and the slope.

Salty opened his eyes and turned his head, as far as it would go, with his cheek on the iron.

He could just see him. Round the edge of the stone. Small. Thin. Standing in his gap with his back to them, facing east, facing the slope and the thing walking up it and the sun coming up behind it over Kharvos, gold, enormous, so that he was only a shape against the light. A small dark shape with a short sword hanging from one hand and blood dried on its face, standing in a gap he’d held all night, in the way.

He wasn’t moving.

He wasn’t getting out of the way.

He’d lowered the sword. Salty saw that, and didn’t understand it. The short sword hanging loose at his side, the point nearly on the grass. His other hand open. His shoulders down. His weight not on his back foot any more, the way it had been all night, but on both, even, easy, the way you’d stand at a stove.

As if he were about to toss something.

Beside Salty, very quietly, without looking away from him, Wendy said, “One.”
