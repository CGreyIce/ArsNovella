The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 8: The Third Seal

Chapter 230

Dense

6,536 words · 28 min read

It took a day to get it hot.

Severin had known it would. He’d known for fifty-eight years, in a way; since he was eight, standing barefoot in the forge yard at Brakewater watching his father heat it in the old hearth from dawn to dark with every bellows in the parish, and pull it out at nightfall, and look at it, and find it exactly as grey and cold and pitted as it had been in the morning. It’s not angry, his father had said, that night, sitting on the anvil block with his head in his hands. That’s the trouble. Iron gets angry in the fire. That’s how you know it’s listening. This doesn’t care.

It didn’t care now, either.

They had it in the hearth by the first light. That had taken an hour by itself; there was no pair of tongs on the lake shore that would hold it and no four men who could lift it, and in the end Ars had simply bent down, in his bare feet on the shingle, and put his hands under the near end of it, wrapped in wet sacking, and lifted, and walked it into the fire the way you’d walk a drunk friend through a door. One end on the hearthstones, deep in the coals. The other end on Garrow’s anvil, which eight men and the brewer’s dray had brought down from the square again in the dusk, with Garrow walking beside it the whole way, the way Severin had walked beside the star.

Then they’d built the fire round it.

Gabe built it. Severin let him. He’d watched the boy keep this hearth for two weeks now and there was nothing he could have told him. Coal from the Kingsferry barges, a whole cartload, that Dennis had sent up the river without being asked, with a note that said treasurer’s account and nothing else. Charcoal from the burner up the valley. And on top, last, a thing Severin hadn’t seen done since he was a boy: a layer of old horseshoes, rusted, a sack of them, that Gabe had begged off every stable in Wrenhaven the night before. Iron to feed the fire iron. His father had done that, for the hard jobs. Severin hadn’t thought anyone still did.

“Harl,” said Gabe, when he saw him looking. Red to the ears. “For the stubborn ones.”

“Harl’s right,” said Severin.

And then the bellows.

There were two pairs. Garrow’s great double bellows off the square forge, the size of a door, that took two men on the bar; and the old pair that lived under the sailcloth roof, that Ars used for hooks. Severin put the big pair on the hearth and set them going at dawn, and they did not stop, all that day, from first light to dark.

He hadn’t asked anybody to work them. He hadn’t needed to. They came down the shingle from the hedge in the first light, one and two at a time, the way they’d come down to watch the boy from Duskhollow make Ars move; and stood round the forge, and looked at the star in the fire, and at the bellows bar; and took turns.

All of them. The whole hedge, and Kestra, and Garrow, and a stream of Wrenhaven men coming down the lake road all day in ones and twos to take a turn on the bar and go back up. The tall one with the pink hair, who read things; Severin watched her read the star in the fire while she pumped, frowning, and stop, and read again, and say I can’t, it’s not written in anything, and keep pumping. The axolotl girl. The boy from the polytechnic who went invisible, half the time, so that the bar went up and down on its own and the Wrenhaven men crossed themselves. The penguin girl, who said nothing and pumped for an hour without stopping, and whose bellows-strokes, Severin noticed, were the steadiest of all of them; the fire liked her.

And under all of it, all day, the king.

He didn’t pump. He did the other thing.

He sat on Gabe’s bucket by the hearth with his father’s hammer across his knees and his hands open on his thighs, palms up, and his eyes half shut, and gave the fire what he had.

Not all of it. Not at first. You didn’t throw everything into a fire at once; his father had taught him that at six, at the bellows. Feed it. Don’t drown it. A little. Then a little more. The thing that had come to him in his father’s forge at thirty-nine, the twelfth plough blade on the anvil and the world going quiet and then bending: he let it come up through him, slowly, and go out through his open hands into the heat, the way you’d breathe on a coal to bring it up. Into the coals. Into the iron horseshoes on top of the coals. Into the fire round the end of the star.

He’d never done this. Not this way. He’d cut a chain on a gate with a Dominion swordsman once, and a black sword in half on this shingle, and both times it had been a blow: one, at the end, everything at once. This wasn’t a blow. This was the long way. Feeding a fire for a day, the way you’d sit with a sick man.

By mid-morning the coals round the star were white.

By noon the iron horseshoes had melted, and run down through the coals in bright threads, and the heat coming off the hearth was so great that the sailcloth roof over the forge had begun to smoke at the edges, and Garrow and two men from the square took it down and rolled it up and the forge stood open to the sky.

The star didn’t care.

Severin watched it, through the shimmer. The end in the fire had gone from grey to darker grey. That was all. The plum-bloom had burned off it, and under the bloom the metal was the same dense dark grey all through, pitted, melted, cold-looking even in the white heart of a fire that would have run a plough blade into a puddle in a quarter of an hour.

“It’s not getting hot,” said the axolotl girl, from the bar, in a small voice.

“It is,” said Severin. “It’s getting hot very slowly. From a long way down.” He didn’t open his eyes. “It’s been cold for a very long time. It’s been cold since before there was a Brakewater, or a kingdom, or anybody to name either. You don’t warm a thing like that up in a morning.”

“How long?”

“As long as it takes,” said Severin. “The iron tells you when it’s ready.”

He fed the fire. A little more.

Ars didn’t work the bellows.

He stood at the anvil end. All day. Not doing anything; there was nothing for a striker to do until there was something to strike. He stood at the far end of Garrow’s anvil, where the cold end of the star lay on the face of it, with his hands at his sides and his feet in the shingle, and looked at it. Like a man waiting for a fish.

Severin watched him, now and then, through the heat.

He’d watched him the autumn before last, on this shingle, cutting the black sword. He’d thought then that he’d never seen anybody stand so still. He’d thought, since, on the long nights, that he’d understood something about the man, standing like that; something about a lake, and nine years on the end of a jetty, and a bar that went up a percent at a time from catching fish. He’d thought, the autumn before last, that he recognised it. That it was the same thing that had happened to him in his father’s forge, in the winter, with the twelve plough blades. The slow way up.

He still thought so. But he watched Ars at the anvil end that day, still, all day, with his hands at his sides, and thought something else as well.

He was afraid of it.

Not of the star. Of what it was going to be. Severin could see it in the shoulders. The man had said okay to a boy from Kingsferry on the end of a jetty in the dark, and come up the shingle at dawn, and stood at the anvil all day; and every hour of that day his shoulders had been the shoulders of a man standing at the top of a road he’d walked down once, and swore he’d never walk down again, looking down it.

Severin didn’t say anything. There wasn’t anything to say. He fed the fire.

At about the fourth hour of the afternoon, Ars put his hand on the star.

On the cold end. On the anvil. Flat. The way you’d put your hand on a horse.

And it got hot.

Not all at once. Severin felt it. Everybody at the forge felt it; the penguin girl stopped pumping, and the tall one with the pink hair made a sound. It was as if something had turned over. Deep down in the dense grey heart of the thing, a long way down, something that had been lying one way for a very long time turned over and lay the other way; and the heat that Severin had been feeding into the fire since dawn, that had been going in and in and in and finding nothing to take hold of, found something.

The end in the fire went red.

Dull red, at first. A dark slow ember-red, coming up from inside it, the way a coal comes up when you breathe on it. Then brighter. Then orange, all along the end in the fire, and spreading, back along the star toward the anvil, toward the cold end, toward the man with his hand on it.

He didn’t take his hand off.

It came up under his palm. Orange, then yellow. Severin could see the heat in the air above it. Any other hand on the lake shore would have been burned to the bone. Ars stood with his palm flat on the star as it went yellow under it, and didn’t move, and didn’t take it off.

“It’s awake,” said Gabe.

He was standing at the hearth with the rake in his hand and his face shining with sweat and his eyes very wide. He wasn’t looking at the star. He was looking at Ars’s hand on it.

“It was waiting for him,” said Gabe. “It wasn’t asleep. It was waiting. I got it wrong.” He swallowed. “It didn’t know who for.”

Ars took his hand off.

He looked at his palm. It wasn’t burned. Severin had known it wouldn’t be. He looked at it anyway, for a long time, the way a man looks at his own hand when it’s done something without asking him; and then he closed it, and looked at the star, yellow-hot all the way along now, glowing in the evening light on the shingle like something that had just fallen.

“Tomorrow,” said Severin. He stood up off the bucket. His knees were very old. He’d fed a fire for a day, and he felt it in every bone. “It’ll hold the heat till morning now. It’s dense. It’ll hold it longer than any of us.” He looked at Gabe. “Bank it. Don’t let it go below yellow. We start drawing at first light.”

“What are we making?” said Gabe. “What shape?”

“You tell me,” said Severin. “That’s your job. Sleep on it. If you can.”

The boy didn’t sleep. Severin heard him at the forge all night, banking the fire, talking to it under his breath.

On the second day, they drew it.

Severin held. That was the first thing he’d decided, lying awake in the cabin bed that Ars had given up to him, listening to Gabe talk to the fire: he wouldn’t strike. Not this one. He’d held for Ars the autumn before last, and struck the cut himself, at the end, everything at once; and it had been right, then, because the black sword had been Ars’s already and it had needed somebody who wasn’t him to cut it. This was the other way round. This needed his hands on the tongs and somebody else’s on the hammer.

Gabe had made the tongs.

In the night, while he banked the fire, out of the four greased oak beams’ iron straps and a set of Garrow’s old ones and a whole bar of the Kingsferry coal. Long-reined tongs, a man’s height, with jaws as broad as a spade and shaped, Severin saw, to the exact curve of the star’s flank; because the boy had sat beside it on the first night with his hand on it in the dark, and remembered. They were the ugliest tongs Severin had ever seen. They were perfect.

So Severin held, with Gabe’s tongs, at the cold end.

And Ars struck.

With the big hammer. Gabe’s, from Harl’s, the sledge with the head the size of a loaf. He stood at the anvil with it in both hands, loosely, and waited for Severin’s word, and struck.

The first blow went across the lake like a bell.

Severin felt it in his hands, up the reins of the tongs, into his arms, into his teeth. A great dense ringing note, lower than any iron he’d ever struck in his life, lower than the bell in the castle tower at Solmere; it went out from the anvil across the flat morning water and hit the far hills and came back, and every gull on the lake went up, and in Wrenhaven, a mile off, Severin heard later, the hens in every yard in the town lay down.

The star didn’t move.

Not a hair. The blow went into it and the star took it and was exactly the shape it had been before. Severin looked at the face of it under the hammer and couldn’t see a mark.

“Again,” he said.

Ars struck again.

The same. The note across the lake. The gulls. Nothing.

“Again.”

Severin had known it would be like this, too. Dense, his father had said, the night he broke his best hammer on it, sitting on the anvil block. It’s so dense it doesn’t know it’s been hit. You’d have to hit it harder than a thing can be hit. And then, quietly, to the eight-year-old in the doorway: I’m not strong enough, Sev. Nobody is.

On the ninth blow, it moved.

Severin felt it. Through the tongs. Not a dent; nothing you’d see. A give. A tiny, deep, far-down yielding, a hair’s breadth, as if the whole dense weight of the thing had decided, at the ninth time of asking, that it would allow itself to be one hair’s breadth longer than it had been.

He looked up.

Ars was standing at the anvil with the sledge, breathing. Not hard. He’d struck nine times with a hammer Gabe could swing all day and he wasn’t breathing hard. But he’d felt it too. He was looking at the place where the hammer had landed, and Severin, who couldn’t see his face and never would, could see the rest of him, and the rest of him had gone very still.

“Nine,” said Severin.

“Nine,” said Ars.

“That’s not a strike. That’s a conversation.” Severin shifted the tongs. “Again. And don’t hit it harder. You’ll want to. Don’t. Hit it the same. It’s listening now.”

Ars struck.

And it moved.

They drew it for three days.

It was the slowest work Severin had ever done, and the hardest, and he’d have done it for thirty. Every blow across the lake like a bell. Every blow taking the star a hair longer, a hair flatter, a hair more like whatever it was becoming. Ars struck. Severin held. Gabe kept the fire and called the heats, and moved round the anvil with the little cross-peen, his father’s hammer, Severin’s father’s, that Severin had put into his hand on the first morning without a word; and touched the star with it where the next blow should go. Tap. Ars struck there. Tap. There.

That was Gabe’s job. Severin had told him on the first night: you’ll tell us both what it wants to be. And the boy did. He didn’t know he was doing it, half the time. He’d stand at the side of the anvil with the little hammer and his eyes half shut and his hand on the tong reins beside Severin’s, and feel; and tap. And Ars would strike where he’d tapped. And the star would go where Gabe had felt it wanted to go.

It didn’t want to be thin.

That was the first thing Gabe said, at the end of the second day, sitting on the shingle with his hands black to the elbow and his face grey with tiredness. “It doesn’t want to be thin. It won’t. If we try to draw it thin it’ll fight us all the way.” He looked at the star in the fire, glowing. “It wants to stay heavy. It wants to be what it is. Just longer. Just—“ He made a shape with his hands. A long broad flat shape, as wide as his hand was long. “Like the old one.”

“The black one,” said Severin.

“Not the same,” said Gabe. “It’s not trying to be the same. It’s not from the same place. But it wants to be that size. That weight.” He looked at Ars, at the anvil end, with the sledge leaning beside him. “It wants to be something he has to carry.”

Ars didn’t say anything.

So that was the shape. Severin had known it would be; he’d known since the spring. A slab. A great broad heavy slab of a blade, nearly the length of a man, a hand and more across, thick at the back as a man’s wrist; with a long grip for two hands, and no guard to speak of, and a blunt broad end with a point drawn on it like the prow of a boat. The kind of thing nobody in the kingdom had made a sword of in three hundred years, because nobody in the kingdom could lift it.

And as it drew, the frost came.

Severin saw it on the third day. In the face of the metal, as the star stretched under the blows and the pitted melted skin of it was beaten away and the clean grey under it came up to the light: the pattern. The same pattern that had been in the one cut his father made, a hand deep, fifty-eight years ago. Fine lines, crossing and branching and crossing again, like frost on the inside of a window on a winter morning. It hadn’t been only in the cut. It was all through it. And as the star drew out, the frost drew out with it, all along the length, so that by the evening of the third day the whole long grey-silver slab of it on the anvil, glowing orange in the dusk, was laced end to end with frost that the fire couldn’t touch.

The axolotl girl cried when she saw it. Severin didn’t know why. He didn’t think she did.

On the fourth day, Ars stopped.

In the middle of a heat. With the sledge up. He stood at the anvil with the hammer at the top of its swing and didn’t bring it down, and stood there, and Severin, at the tongs, felt the whole forge go quiet round them.

“What,” said Severin.

Ars lowered the hammer. Slowly. He set the head of it down on the shingle and stood with both hands on the end of the handle, looking at the blade on the anvil.

“I don’t want it,” he said.

He said it quietly. Not to Severin. To the blade.

Gabe, at the side of the anvil with the little hammer, went white under the black.

Severin didn’t say anything. He held the tongs. The blade glowed on the anvil between them, orange going to cherry, the frost all through it.

“I didn’t want the last one,” said Ars. “I picked it up on a hill because there was a war. I carried it because there was a war. I put it in the ground over her because I was done.” He looked at the frost. “I’ve been done for ten years. I’ve got a rod. I catch fish. I’ve got a list on the wall that says fish at the bottom in my own hand.” He was quiet. “And you’ve walked a star up the lake road on eight oxen and I’m standing here making it into the thing I put in the ground.”

The blade went from cherry to dark red. Losing the heat. Gabe didn’t move to put it back in the fire. Nobody moved.

Severin let go of the tongs.

He let the reins rest on the anvil, and the blade rest in the jaws, and he stood back from it, and looked across the anvil at the man on the other side of it, with his hands on the end of a sledgehammer, in the heat, at the end of the fourth day.

“I know,” said Severin.

Ars looked at him.

“I know you don’t,” said Severin. “I didn’t want the crown. I’d have stayed in Brakewater making plough blades till I was dead, if anybody had let me. I went Ascendant in a dead man’s forge making twelve plough blades for a farmer called Ashdown, and I wanted to stay there, and I didn’t get to.” He looked at the blade. “That’s not why we’re making it.”

“Then why?”

“Because it’s out there,” said Severin.

He didn’t say what. He didn’t need to. The tall girl with the pink hair, who was standing at the bellows bar with her hands still on it, went very still.

“Fifty-six frames,” said Severin. “Thirty-seven with ticks on the way home, and nineteen with nothing. And then thirty-five people who came through my Hall two years and a half ago, with nothing on theirs. Seven people standing in a ring outside the thirteenth at Thornwall, where a fourteenth would go, who don’t know their own names. A door in Duskhollow with a light under it the colour of nothing.” He was quiet. “Something cut a lake in half at dawn six days ago, with the first thing it ever learned, and I felt it at Pellow’s Cross. So did Kestra. So did the innkeeper. And somewhere, Ars, something else felt it too. You know it did. You held it in at the hills and you did it very well and something felt it anyway, because things like that always do.”

Ars didn’t answer.

“I’m not asking you to carry it,” said Severin. “I’m not asking you to pick it up. I’m asking you to finish it.” He put his hands back on the tongs. “So that it’s there. On a wall. Finished. If the day comes. If it never comes, it’ll hang on your wall till you’re dead and Alea will hang it on hers and nobody will ever lift it again, and I’ll be glad. I’ll be gladder than anybody.” He looked across the anvil. “But if it comes, I don’t want you going down that road with a fishing rod. Neither does he.”

He looked at Gabe.

Gabe was standing at the side of the anvil with the little hammer in his hand and his face black and white and wet, and his mouth shut tight.

Ars looked at him too.

For a long time. Long enough that the blade on the anvil went from dark red to the dull grey-red that meant back in the fire, now, or it’ll crack. Severin didn’t move. Neither did Gabe.

Then Ars bent and picked up the sledge.

“It’s going cold,” he said.

Gabe made a sound like something breaking, and grabbed the rake, and they got the blade back in the fire.

They finished it on the fifth day.

The last heat. The last blows. The point drawn on the broad end like the prow of a boat; the long tang for the grip drawn out of the cold end, thick as a man’s two thumbs. Gabe with the little hammer going over every inch of it, tap, tap, and Ars striking where he tapped, lightly now, the sledge hardly lifted, the notes going out across the lake higher and higher and clearer, until the last of them rang like a cup touched with a spoon.

Then the quench.

Severin had thought about the quench for three days. You couldn’t quench a thing like that in a trough. It would boil the trough dry and crack. You couldn’t quench it in oil; there wasn’t enough oil in the kingdom. He’d lain awake on the third night thinking about it, and then got up and gone and stood on the shingle in the dark and looked at the lake, and laughed out loud at himself, alone, for an old fool.

Ars carried it.

There was nobody else who could. He took the tang end in both hands, wrapped in wet sacking, and lifted the whole long glowing slab of it off the anvil, white-hot from end to end, laced with frost; and turned round, with it held out in front of him, level; and walked down the shingle.

Everybody stood back. The whole lake shore. All of them, and Kestra and Garrow and half of Wrenhaven come down the lake road to see the end of it. They stood back on the stones and watched him carry it, and nobody said anything.

Down the shingle. To the land end of the jetty, by the girl’s little tree. Out along the planks.

Severin counted them. Everyone did. Thirty-four. The planks smoked under the heat of the thing as he went, a thin grey smoke rising off each board after him, and the hens on the jetty went off it in a body, squawking, over the side onto the shingle, all except the big red one, who stood her ground on the last plank by the lamp and watched him come with her head on one side.

He stood on the end of the jetty with the white-hot blade held out over the water.

He stood there for a moment. Severin, on the shingle, couldn’t see his face. He could see the shoulders. And the shoulders were the shoulders of a man on the end of a jetty, on the end of nine years, with a thing in his hands he’d put in the ground once and sworn never to pick up again; looking at the lake.

Then he knelt, and put it in.

The lake screamed.

There was no other word for it. The blade went into Lake Ilse at the end of the jetty, white-hot, the length of a man, and the water round it didn’t boil; it exploded. A column of steam went up off the end of the jetty, white, roaring, higher than the cabin, higher than the hill behind the cabin, higher than the hills round the lake, straight up into the clear evening sky like a great white tree growing out of the water in a heartbeat; and the sound of it was a long tearing shriek that went on and on, and the whole surface of the lake for fifty paces round the jetty heaved and bubbled and went white, and a wave of hot water ran up the shingle in the other direction from the one it had run seven days ago, and the fish, Severin saw, the pike and the perch and the little silver dace, went away from the jetty in a great flickering rush across the shallows like birds leaving a tree.

And in the middle of it, on his knees on the last plank, invisible in the steam, Ars held it under.

He held it for a long time. Severin counted. It took a count of three hundred before the screaming stopped, and the steam thinned, and the white tree over the jetty broke up and drifted off across the water in great slow rolls, and the lake round the jetty went still.

He stood up.

He had it in his hands. Out of the water. Dripping. He turned round on the end of the jetty, slowly, in the last of the steam, and held it up, level, the way you’d hold up a fish you’d caught to show somebody on the shore.

It was grey-silver. All of it. The colour of the lake on a still morning before the sun’s on it. And all through it, end to end, from the blunt broad prow to the thick tang in his hands, the frost. Fine lines, crossing and branching, like the inside of a window on a winter morning; and in the evening light, as he turned it, the frost caught the sun and went white, and then silver, and then grey, and then white again, so that the whole long blade seemed to be breathing.

Nobody on the shingle said anything.

Severin found that he had his father’s hammer in his hand. He didn’t remember picking it up. He stood on the shingle with it in his hand and his eyes stinging and looked at the thing his father had dragged out of the Brakewater with every mule in the parish fifty-eight years ago, the thing that had broken his best hammer and lain under a sailcloth in the back of the yard for the rest of his life, dense, nobody’s strong enough, Sev; held up at the end of a jetty in the evening, finished, frosted, breathing, in the hands of the only man in two worlds who could hold it.

The iron tells you when it’s ready.

“There,” said Severin, to nobody. To his father. “There. It was ready.”

Gabe made the grip that night.

He wouldn’t let anybody watch. That was the only thing he’d ask for, all week. He took the blade into the forge, on its bench, when the hearth had gone down to a red eye and the stars were out, with a piece of black bog oak that Garrow had kept in the rafters of the square forge for thirty years waiting for something good enough, and a roll of plain brown leather, and his knives; and he sent everybody away, kindly, red to the ears, and they went.

Severin didn’t go far. He sat on the stones at the top of the shingle in the dark and listened. He couldn’t help it. He’d never been able to stay away from a forge at night.

He heard the knives on the oak, for hours. Small careful sounds. He heard the boy stop, often, for a long time. He knew what that was. That was a man holding a piece of wood in his hands and closing his eyes and thinking about somebody else’s.

In both hands, the boy had said, on the end of the jetty, the night the star came. Close. Like somebody’s going to take it.

At some time past midnight, Ars came down the slope from the cabin and sat on the stones beside Severin.

He’d brought two cups. Of the lake’s water; it was the only thing he ever had in the cabin at night. He held one out without looking, and Severin took it. Ars held the other one. In both hands. Close against his chest. Like somebody might take it.

Severin looked at the cup in his hands for a long time, and didn’t say anything.

They sat there and listened to the boy at the forge.

“Did you name the other one?” said Severin, at last. “The black one. When you found it.”

“No,” said Ars. “Nobody ever named it. The songs called it the black blade. I never called it anything.” He was quiet. “She named her half. Astra. She was right to.”

“And this one?”

Ars looked at the forge. At the red eye of the hearth, and the shape of the boy bent over the bench beside it.

“Not yet,” he said.

Severin nodded. He’d known he’d say that. He’d heard not yet from this man on this shingle before, the autumn before last, about a much bigger thing, and he’d learned, since, what it was worth. It was worth a great deal. It was a door left open.

In the morning, Gabe brought it up to the cabin.

The grip was done. Black bog oak, in two slabs, riveted through the tang, shaped and shaped again through the night until it wasn’t round, and wasn’t oval, and wasn’t anything Severin had a name for: a long grip for two hands, close together, a little flattened on the inside where the fingers went, a little swelled at the back where a palm would cup it, the way you’d cup a cup. Wrapped in the plain brown leather, in a long overlapping spiral. And tied off at the end, where the wrap finished against the pommel, with a fisherman’s knot.

Gabe stood in the cabin doorway with it held across both arms like a felled tree, and his arms shaking with the weight, and his face grey and black and red at once, and couldn’t say anything.

Ars took it from him.

With one hand. By the grip. And lifted it off Gabe’s arms as if it weighed what a rod weighs, and Gabe sagged in the doorway with the relief of it.

He stood in the middle of the cabin with it. Severin, at the table, with his cup, watched him. The whole of the lake shore was at the door, or at the window, or crowded on the step behind Gabe, looking in: the fox girl at the front, and the other two boys, and the five from the militia hall, and the five from Duskhollow, and the girl from the ditch at the back with her stick.

Ars put his other hand on the grip.

Both hands. Close together. Close against his body. The way he held a cup.

And the frost in the blade woke up.

Severin saw it. Everybody saw it. Not light; not the way the girl’s sword had gone silver on the sand at the Lists. The frost moved. The fine branching lines all through the grey-silver, from the grip to the prow, shifted, very slightly, all at once, the way frost on a window seems to move when you breathe near it. As if the whole long blade had felt the hands on it, both of them, close, and known them, and settled.

On the fox girl’s back, Astra hummed. Severin heard it from the table. A low note, in the black steel; and from the grey-silver in Ars’s hands, so low it was almost under hearing, a note that answered it.

Not the same note. A different one. The way two bells from different towers sound across a valley on a still evening: not the same, not tuned to each other, but each of them, somehow, the better for the other.

Ars stood there with it for a long time.

Then he looked at Severin.

“Not for now,” said Severin. “For the day you leave the lake.”

Ars didn’t answer.

He turned round, with the blade in both hands, to the wall over the table. Where the drawing was, on its nail: a child’s picture in blue crayon of a jetty with forty-one planks. And beside it, on the same nail, a little to the right, a list in Higu’s pencil, most of it crossed through now in his. Three words left standing: Twelve dinners. Gabe. East. And at the bottom, in his own hand, Fish.

Garrow had been in the night before. Severin had heard him, late, with a lantern and a hammer; and above the drawing and the list, set into the logs of the wall, there were two iron pegs now, black, plain, very strong, a man’s height apart.

Ars lifted the blade, and laid it on the pegs.

It lay there, along the wall, over the table. Grey-silver, laced with frost, the length of a man, over a blue crayon jetty and a list of next year. The pegs took the weight. Garrow had known what he was doing.

Nobody named it. Higu, at the window, had her book open and her pencil ready, and her pencil didn’t move. There wasn’t anything to write.

Ars stood looking at it on the wall.

Then he reached up, past the blade, to the list. And took Higu’s pencil from her, through the window, without asking, which he’d never once done; and drew a line, very carefully, through one word.

Gabe.

He gave the pencil back.

In the doorway, Gabe sat down on the step, all at once, the way a tree goes down, and put his black hands over his face, and didn’t take them away for a long time; and Deston sat down beside him, and put an arm round him; and Xav, on his other side, put a hand on the back of his neck and left it there.

The eleventh dinner was that night. Fish with onions. In butter, the way he’d made it in Oakes’s forge at Cinderhold, with a little of Salty’s paste and nothing else, in a skillet on the cabin hearth, because the forge on the shingle was cold for the first time in five days and Gabe was asleep beside it on the stones with the little cross-peen in his hand.

Severin ate it at the head of the long table in the girl’s hedge, under the stars, with the whole hedge round him and a king’s guard at his elbow, and a penguin, and the big red hen in the penguin girl’s lap.

It wasn’t anything. A piece of fish. Some onions, cooked down soft and gold. A bit of bread.

It was the best thing he’d ever eaten.

He looked down the table, past the cloths with the lace on them from a bridal veil, past the fox girl eating with her bowl held close in both hands, to the end; where the man stood, the way he always stood, by the place nobody sat in, watching them eat. With his hands at his sides.

Empty. No rod. It was on the jetty, by the lamp.

And on the wall of the cabin down the slope, in the dark, over the table, over a jetty in blue crayon and a list with one word crossed through, a long grey-silver blade lay on two iron pegs, laced with frost, breathing very slowly in the starlight that came through the window; and waited, the way it had always waited, only now it knew who for.

One left, Higu wrote that night, in the book, beside Twelve dinners. Owed.
