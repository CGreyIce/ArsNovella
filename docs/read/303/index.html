The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 11

Chapter 303

The Grey

4,494 words · 20 min read

The boy came in on a Tuesday, at the end of the afternoon, with his hand wrapped in his mother’s apron, and Rachael knew before she’d unwrapped it that it was going to be the worst thing she’d seen all year.

Not because of anything she could see. The apron was clean. The boy wasn’t crying. He was ten or so, a rope-walk boy, with the rope-walk’s tar ground into the creases of his knuckles and the rope-walk’s squint, and he sat on the end of Maud’s bench with his wrapped hand held out in front of him in the other one, the way you’d carry a bird, and he looked at it as if it belonged to somebody else.

That was what she knew from. The way he looked at it.

“Ned,” said his mother. She was thin and tired and smelled of the net sheds. “Ned Webb. It’s a hook. He’s had hooks in him before, it’s nothing, he’s had them in every finger, only it won’t—“ She stopped. “It won’t do anything. It’s been three days and it won’t go red and it won’t go hot and it won’t go right.”

Rachael pulled the other stool round and sat down in front of the boy, close enough to work and no closer. She’d been back in Kingsferry three weeks. Three weeks of the long ward and the river smell and Maud’s voice, after a month at a lake where the infirmary was four beds and a woman called Odile who cried at goodbyes. She’d come home cold and quiet and pleased with herself, and Maud had taken one look at her on the first morning and said you’ve been working, and put her on the bench.

“Hello, Ned,” said Rachael. “Can I?”

The boy looked at her. He had the face they all had in here, the ones old enough to have heard about the summoned: as if she might do something extraordinary, or might eat him. Then he nodded.

She unwrapped the apron.

It was a fish-hook cut, the kind she’d seen a hundred times. A good-sized hook, by the look of it, gone in at the heel of the palm and out again a finger-width on, the way they did when somebody pulled the line instead of cutting it; and then somebody had worked it out backwards, which was the right thing to do and the worst way to do it. A ragged little track across the meat of the hand. Three days old.

It should have been red. It should have been hot and puffy and angry, with a yellow edge and that sweet bad smell coming off it that meant she’d be cutting. She’d seen enough of them. Every child on the river put a hook in himself sooner or later, and half of them came in on the fourth day with a hand like a pudding.

It wasn’t red.

The edges of the cut were grey. Not black, the black that meant the flesh was dying; not the white of a burn. Grey. The colour of ash, or of a river under ice. A thin grey line along both lips of the wound, a hair wide, very even, as if somebody had drawn round it with a pencil.

She looked at it for a long time.

Then she put the backs of two fingers next to it, on the boy’s palm, not touching the cut. The palm was cold. Not cool; cold. Colder than a hand should be in the end of a spring afternoon in a ward with a fire going at each end.

“Does it hurt?” she said.

“No,” said Ned.

“Not at all?”

“No.” He looked at it. “It feels like it’s not mine.”

His mother made a small sound.

Rachael didn’t look up. She’d learned that on the first day, from Maud: you don’t look up at the mother. You look at the hand. The mother’s looking at you to see what you think, and you haven’t decided yet, and your face will tell her before you know.

“Where did you get the hook?” she said.

“A man give it me,” said Ned.

“What man?”

“On the customs-house steps. Sunday.” He was still looking at his hand. “He had a lot. In a box, all different, good ones, bright. He said take one, they’re for nothing. He was nice.” He thought about it. “He had a good coat on.”

Rachael’s hands stopped.

Only for a moment. She made them go on. She put the apron back over the hand, loosely, and sat back on the stool, and folded her arms, which was what she did with her hands when she didn’t want them to do anything else.

“Did he have a hat?” she said.

Ned looked up at her, surprised. “No,” he said. “No hat.”

“Okay.” She let her breath out. “Okay. Good. Can I try something?”

Mend was the first thing she’d ever been able to do, and she still did it the way Maud had taught her in the first winter: slowly, with both hands, with her whole attention, the way you’d pour water into a cup you couldn’t see the bottom of.

She put her right hand under the boy’s, flat, palm to palm, and her left over the top of it, not touching the cut, a hair above. And she let it go.

It went out of her the way it always went. Warmth first. Maud had said that in the first week, in this ward, with a soldier’s head in her lap: Healing takes from the healer. Not only mana. You. Warmth first, then strength. She felt it go out of her hands and into his, the warmth of her, the thing that made a hand a hand and not a glove; and she felt it find the cut, the way it always found the cut, the way water finds the low place in a floor.

And go in.

And not come back.

That was the thing she’d never felt before. When you mended, it went in, and the body took it, and did something with it: knitted, or warmed, or pulled shut, and you felt it happen, a sort of answer, like a dog’s tail thumping on a floor when you come in. The body always answered. Even the dying ones answered, a little, at the end; Maud said that was how you knew you were still talking to somebody.

This didn’t answer.

She poured, and it went in, and it went down into the grey edges of the cut and stopped there, and went nowhere. Like pouring water into sand. She felt it go and go and not fill anything. She poured more, because she didn’t know what else to do, because that was what you did, and felt her own hands go cold, and her wrists, and the cold start up her arms toward her elbows, and the grey line on the boy’s palm didn’t change at all.

She took her hands away.

She was shaking. She hadn’t noticed it start. Her fingers were white to the second knuckle and she couldn’t feel the ends of them, and the boy was looking at her with his face doing the thing that meant she’d frightened him, and she made her own face do something about it.

“It’s fine,” she said. “That’s just me. I’m always cold.” She put her hands in her armpits. “Give me a minute.”

Maud came over while she was sitting with her hands in her armpits.

Sister Maud had run the long ward for forty years. She was small and old and walked with a stick she used mostly to point, and she’d given Rachael her own needle case of black horn, forty years old, the spring Group 4 first went up the river, and said come back. She was the only person in Kingsferry who’d never once looked at Rachael as if she might eat somebody.

She looked at the hand under the apron. She lifted the edge of the apron with the end of her stick, and looked at it a long time, the way she looked at everything, with her mouth pulled in.

“Huh,” said Maud.

“Have you seen it before?”

“No.” Maud let the apron drop. “Forty years. Fevers and knives and burns and the black rot and the things that come up the river on the barges from the south. Never this.” She looked at Rachael’s white fingers. “It took your mend.”

“It took it and didn’t do anything with it.”

“No.” Maud leaned on her stick. “A wound does something. Always. It’s trying to do something, even if it’s the wrong thing; that’s all a wound is, a body trying. You don’t make anything, girl, I’ve told you. You help what’s already there do what it’s already trying to do.” She looked at the grey line. “That isn’t trying to do anything.”

“So what’s it doing?”

“Waiting,” said Maud.

Rachael looked at her.

“I don’t know,” said Maud. “That’s what it looks like to me. A thing waiting.” She turned her stick in her hand. “What have you got that’s not Mend?”

She had Close.

It had come to her at the Lists last summer, on the side sand, with Dennis’s forehead open and Deston’s hands on her and Calvin’s wind going through both of them. Close. The word she’d used for a year before she had it, in the channel, in the notebooks: healers close. Mend was warmth; it went in and helped. Close was the other thing. Close was the edges of a wound and her two hands telling them to come together, and they did. Fast. Clean. A cut that would take a week to knit, shut in a breath, with a pink line where it had been.

It cost more than Mend. It always had. She’d come off the side sand that day so cold that Gabe had put his coat on her, and she’d let him.

“I’ll close it,” she said.

Maud looked at her for a long moment.

“You know what I’ll say,” said Maud.

“Don’t close what you haven’t cleaned.”

“Everything you close over,” said Maud, “you close in.” She didn’t move. “That’s not dirt in there. I don’t know what it is. But it’s in there, and if you close it, it’s in there with a lid on.”

“It’s in there anyway.” Rachael looked at Ned, who was looking at his hand. “It’s been in there three days. If it’s waiting, it’s waiting with the door open. I’d rather it waited with the door shut.” She unfolded her arms. Her fingers had come back a little; she could feel the ends of them. “And if it’s under the skin I can watch it. I can see where it goes. Right now it’s on the edges and I can’t tell what it wants.”

Maud was quiet.

“All right,” she said at last. “Close it. And then you watch it. Every hour. And you write down every hour what it does, the way you lot write down everything, in your little books.” She pointed the stick at Rachael. “And if it goes past the wrist, you come and get me, and I don’t care if it’s three in the morning.”

“Can I?” she said to Ned again.

He nodded.

She didn’t put her hands under his this time. She put her two thumbs on either side of the cut, either side of the grey, on the cold skin of his palm, very lightly, and felt for the edges.

They were there. They always were. Even in the worst wounds, the edges were there, two sides of a thing that had been one thing and wanted to be one thing again; that was what Close found, every time, and why it was fast. It didn’t have to persuade anything. It only had to say now.

She said now.

And the edges came together under her thumbs.

She felt them do it. The two lips of the cut, ragged and grey-edged, drawing toward each other across the little track the hook had made, the way two halves of a torn page come together when you lay them flat; and meeting; and closing. The skin knitted over in a breath. A pink line across the heel of the boy’s hand, neat as a seam, where a ragged hole had been.

The cold came up her arms like water up a wick, to the elbows, past them, into her shoulders. She’d expected it. She held on.

She took her thumbs away.

Ned looked at his hand. He turned it over. He turned it back, and flexed it, and his face did the thing faces did when it worked, the thing that was the only reason she’d kept doing this through two winters of being cold to the bone every night; and his mother said oh, and put her hand over her mouth.

And Rachael looked at the pink line, and under it.

Under the new skin, a hair below it, very faint, the way you see a stone at the bottom of a clear stream: grey. A thin grey line, the same line, under the seam. As if somebody had drawn round the wound with a pencil and she’d closed the skin over the drawing.

It was still there.

It was still waiting.

“Thank you, miss,” said Ned’s mother. “Oh, thank you, miss—“

“Bring him back tomorrow,” said Rachael. Her voice came out perfectly ordinary. “First thing. And the day after. Every day.” She looked at Ned. “If it goes cold again, or it goes grey on top, or you feel anything funny in your hand, anything at all, you come straight here. Even at night. Bang on the door. Sister Maud doesn’t sleep anyway.”

“I heard that,” said Maud, from the next bench.

“You were meant to.”

She sat with her hands in a basin of warm water for an hour, afterwards, at the end of the long ward by the window, the one that had been a hole in the wall once, the night of the third hunter, and had glass in it now that Gabe had paid for. Brother Tamsin brought her the water and then more water and then tea, and didn’t say anything, because he was a little frightened of her and always had been, which she found restful.

The river went past outside the window. Brown, and fast with the end of the thaw, and full of boats. She could see the customs-house steps from here, if she leaned. The long flight of pale stone going down from the customs house to the water, where the boats tied up and the sellers sat and the children ran about under everybody’s feet. There was nobody on the steps now with a box of hooks. She leaned and looked anyway.

He had a good coat on.

No hat. That was something. She’d held on to that.

She’d been cut by the man in the hat once. No; she’d cut him. In the first winter, at the night market, with Gabe’s knife, in the sheath Sabine had found for it. She’d cut his hand, and something had come out that wasn’t blood, black, and his skin had moved round the knife like water.

And then again. At the Narrows, the second spring, on the Widow, in the gorge, with Xav and Petra and Azac’s shield and Malcolm’s sums, and the same hand, and the same place, and that time when it had come out it had been grey. Dry and grey. And the knife had come back grey, a hand’s width from the point, the colour of ash, and it had never washed off.

She took the knife out of her apron pocket. She’d carried it every day since. She laid it on the windowsill in the light, and looked at the grey on the blade.

Then she thought about the line under the pink skin on Ned Webb’s palm.

They were the same colour. They were exactly the same colour.

Gabe came at the end of the day, the way he did. He came in from Harl’s with his hands black and his face red and sat down on the bench opposite her at the end of the ward, near enough that she knew he was there and not so near she had to do anything about it, and waited.

She told him.

He listened the way Gabe listened, with his whole big body, his elbows on his knees and his hands hanging between them. When she got to the man on the steps with the box of hooks, his hands closed. When she got to the knife, he looked at it on the windowsill, his own knife, and didn’t touch it.

“Not the hat,” he said.

“Not the hat.”

“The hat’s on the river, though.” He looked at his hands. “I saw him. On the cliff at the Narrows. I told you.”

“You told me.” Two days after, on the Widow, at night, very quietly, as if he’d been carrying it in his mouth like a hot stone. He looked at you, Rae. All the way up the gorge. His head went round. “You said he was looking at me.”

“He was.” Gabe was quiet. “There’s more than one of them, isn’t there. Course there is. There was the apple woman at the Lists. There was the boy with the cap. There’s whatever the hat man takes his orders from.” He looked at the window, at the steps. “And now there’s a man with a box of hooks giving them away to kids.”

“To one kid.”

“That we know of.”

She didn’t say anything. That was the thing she’d been not thinking for an hour, with her hands in the basin.

“I’ll get Dest,” said Gabe, and got up.

Deston came after dark, with Calvin, who’d been on the river and smelled of it. Ned and his mother had gone home, to the net sheds at the bottom of Tanner’s Row, with a jar of Maud’s salve they didn’t need and instructions to bang on the door. But his mother had left the hook.

She’d kept it. She’d had it in her apron pocket the whole time, wrapped in a bit of rag, because the man had said it was good and you didn’t throw away a good hook. Rachael had asked her for it at the door.

It lay on the windowsill now beside the knife. A plain bright fish-hook, a big one, the kind you’d use for pike, with a barb like a cat’s claw. Perfectly ordinary. Rachael had looked at it under Maud’s lens and found nothing at all: no rust, no maker’s mark, no grey.

Deston didn’t touch it either. He stood at the windowsill with his hands in his pockets and his eyes shut, the way he stood when he was listening to threads, and his face did nothing for a long time.

Rachael had learned to wait for that face. She’d known him since she was seventeen. He’d been the one who brought Declan, the first time. She’d waited for Deston’s listening face across a lot of tables, in two worlds.

“Nothing,” said Deston.

He opened his eyes.

“Nothing,” he said again. “That’s the thing. It’s got nothing on it.” He looked at the hook. “Everything’s got something. Since the spring. Since the jetty, since I found out. Things that were made carefully, for somebody, by somebody’s hands: they’ve got a thread to whoever made them. A hair. The cups at the cabin. Gabe’s hook. Yeva’s net. Even a bought thing, if someone’s used it every day for years, it gets a little one, to them.” He nodded at the knife. “That’s got one. To you. And one to Gabe, because he made it.” He nodded at the fish-hook. “That’s got nothing. Not to whoever made it. Not to whoever gave it. Not to the boy, and he had it in his hand for a day.”

“So nobody made it,” said Calvin. Calvin asked everything straight out. It was the thing about him she liked best, and she’d never tell him.

“Somebody made it,” said Deston. “It’s a hook. Somebody bent it and filed it and put the barb on.” He was quiet. “Somebody with no thread to give it.”

Nobody said anything.

The river went past the window in the dark. On the customs-house steps, at the bottom, the lamps were lit along the water where the night boats tied up, and the children had gone home, and there was nobody sitting there with a box.

“Like the hat man,” said Calvin, at last.

“Like the hat man,” said Deston.

She watched it every hour, the way she’d told Maud she would. She wrote it down in the back of her notebook, the one she kept her anatomy in, the bones of the hand in Maud’s old Latin and her own neat English underneath.

Ned Webb, 10, rope walk. Hook cut, heel of R palm, 3 days. Grey edge, a hair. Cold. No heat, no pus, no smell. Mend: taken, no answer. Close: closed. Grey stays under the seam.

Hour 1: same.

Hour 2: same.

Hour 4 (his mother brought him back, couldn’t sleep): same. Hand cold. Says it feels “not his”. Says it feels “like it’s listening”.

She looked at that one for a long time after she’d written it.

Hour 12 (morning): grey under the seam, one hair wider. Toward the wrist.

She sat at the window with the notebook on her knees and the sun coming up over the river and looked at the hair she’d written down, and thought about what Maud had said. Waiting.

And she thought, because she couldn’t not, about a thing she’d said to Group 4 two springs ago, after the Narrows, with the knife in her lap and its grey edge. She’d said it very calmly. She’d been surprised how calm she was.

They want to empty me out and put something in. The way they did to her. Because a healer holds things.

A boy with a hook in his hand, holding something. A little. A hair’s width. Something put in through a cut, that didn’t go red and didn’t go hot and didn’t do anything at all except wait, and get a hair wider, toward the wrist.

That was a small thing. She knew that. A cut on a child’s hand. It wasn’t a woman on a hill in a circle with a host round her. It wasn’t anything like it.

But it was the same colour as her knife. And it was the same shape, if you squinted. Somebody making a hole in somebody, and putting something in, and leaving it.

She put it in the channel, because the channel was where you put the things you didn’t understand, so thirty-four other people could not understand them with you. She wrote it plainly. The hook, the grey, the mend that didn’t answer, the close that closed it in, the man on the steps with no hat and no thread. If anyone sees a cut go grey, don’t close it. Tell me first. She left out the rest.

The rest wasn’t hers.

She thought about that all morning, with Ned on the bench in front of her and the grey a hair wider and Maud’s stick tapping the floor behind her.

The rest wasn’t hers. She’d known it since the barge cabin. She knew what they’d done, roughly, on a rise on the Redmarch ten years ago. She knew it the way you knew the outline of a thing in the dark: what Declan had said, and not said, and what Deston’s face did when anybody said the healer died. She knew why Declan had stood with her on the shingle the first time they came up the river, and looked at her for a long time, and said nothing, and let her look back. She knew why he’d watched her, every day at the lake, the way you’d watch a lamp in the wind.

The other thirty didn’t.

Thirty people in seven towns, who’d read what she’d just written about a grey line on a boy’s hand. Who’d read it and think that’s horrible and poor kid and Rachael’ll sort it. Who didn’t know what it might be the start of, and didn’t know why they should be frightened, and didn’t know what a healer was for, to the people who’d made that hook.

They’d need to know. Soon. She could feel it coming, the way you feel a fever coming in the backs of your knees before you’re ill.

And it wasn’t hers to tell. It was his. It was the worst thing that had ever happened to him, and it was his daughter’s mother, and it was nobody’s business, and she’d never in her life told somebody else’s business without asking. She didn’t touch people without asking. She didn’t heal them without asking. She wasn’t going to start with this.

At noon, when Ned had gone home, she took a scrap of the infirmary’s paper off Maud’s desk, the rough grey stuff they wrote the doses on, and a pencil, and sat at the window.

She wrote Declan at the top. Then she sat and looked at it.

She thought about writing it all down. What she’d seen, the hook, the grey, the knife, why she was asking. He’d want to know why. Anybody would.

Then she thought: no. He wouldn’t. He’d know why. He’d know the moment he saw it, because he’d been watching her for two years as if he were waiting for exactly this, and the one thing she’d learned about him in two years, the one thing she was sure of, was that he’d rather be asked a small question straight than a big one sideways.

So she wrote the small one.

Can I?

She looked at it. Two words, in pencil, on the back of a dose slip. It was what she said to everybody before she touched them. It was the only way she’d ever known how to ask for anything.

She signed it R. She folded it twice and wrote Declan, the lake, by the post road on the outside, and walked it down to the post house on Harbour Street herself, in her apron, with her hands still cold and her knife in her pocket, past the customs-house steps.

There was nobody on them with a box of hooks.

She looked anyway. She’d look every day now. She knew that about herself, the way she knew she’d be cold tonight, and tomorrow, and every night until the grey under a ten-year-old’s palm either stopped or didn’t.

“Five days, love,” said the post clerk. “If the road’s dry.”

“Fine,” said Rachael, and went back up the hill to the ward.
