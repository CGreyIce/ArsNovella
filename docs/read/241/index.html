The Fisherman of Lake Ilse · Volume 9

Chapter 241

Looked Back

6,317 words · 27 min read

The first thing Hyvred did, every morning for three years, was open her eyes and look at the ceiling.

She’d done it on the first morning, on the stone floor of a hall in Solmere, before she knew where she was or how she’d got there or why thirty-four people round her were screaming. She’d opened her eyes, and there had been a ceiling. All of it. Every painted beam, edge to edge, corner to corner, the whole thing at once, without moving her head; and she’d lain there on her back on the cold stone with her glasses crooked on her face and cried, because on Earth she’d never once seen a whole ceiling in her life. On Earth she’d seen the world through a hole the size of a coin.

She’d done it every morning since. On the barracks ceiling at Thornwall, which was low and smoky and had a crack in the plaster shaped like a running horse. In tents on the road to Valcrest. In the loft of an inn. On the top of the north tower, most nights now, in a blanket on the leads, where there wasn’t a ceiling at all, just sky; and she’d open her eyes on the sky and look at all of it, edge to edge, and be glad.

The morning after the tenth, she opened her eyes on the sky, and didn’t look at it.

She looked at the parapet instead. The stone, a hand from her face, grey, with lichen on it. She looked at the lichen very carefully for a long time. It was the colour of old mustard and it grew in rings. She’d never noticed that.

She didn’t look west.

Corvin was sitting against the parapet on the other side of the leads, with his knees up and his surveyor’s coat round his shoulders and his cord in his fist.

He’d been there all night. She knew that without asking. She’d come up out of seven people’s eyes at about a quarter past midnight shaking so hard her teeth knocked together, and he’d had her by the shoulders, and he’d said her name four times, and then he’d put the blanket round her and sat down on the other side of the leads where she could see him and not said anything else. And she’d lain there and not slept and he’d sat there and not slept, and at some point the moon had come up, late and lopsided, over the hills to the east, and at some point it had gone down again.

He was thirty-six. He looked about sixty this morning.

“Morning,” said Hyvred.

Her voice came out all wrong. Small. She didn’t have a small voice; she’d never had one. On Earth she’d had a voice that people could hear across a convention hall over the top of a sound system, and she’d used it.

“Morning,” said Corvin. He didn’t move. “Did you sleep?”

“No.”

“Nor did I.” He looked down at the cord in his fist. A plain knotted cord of brown wool, one of seven; the other end of it was round a cat-eared girl’s wrist in Solmere, a long way south and east. “Hana’s asleep. Oswin says. On the floor. With all seven round her. Oswin says they were open all night and she didn’t let go of one.” He was quiet. “Thea’s Lantern.”

“I know.”

“You were in it. In the reading. Oswin says seven.” He looked at her. He’d been a royal surveyor for fifteen years before the Advisor made him a guide; he looked at things the way you’d look at a hill you were going to have to draw. “You were the one that could see far.”

“Yes.”

“And at the end.” He didn’t finish.

Hyvred sat up.

She did it slowly, with the blanket round her, and put her back against the parapet, and pulled her knees up the way he had. Her tail was cold. She tucked it in. Her glasses were in her hair, where she kept them, where she’d kept them for three years because she didn’t need them and couldn’t bear to put them in a drawer. She took them out and turned them over in her hands, round and gold-rimmed, and didn’t put them on.

“I looked the wrong way,” she said.

She told him. She hadn’t told anybody else and she didn’t, for a week, except Thea; but she told Corvin, on the leads, at dawn, because he’d sat there all night and because for two years before she came he’d been sending reports to the capital about tracks that didn’t belong to anything and a village where people stood wrong, and nobody had believed him.

She told him about the reading first, because it was easier. About being on the tower with Thea in her and Deston under her and Hana carrying everything, and Yan’s colours and Chris’s when and Xav’s chose. About following thirty-five lines back out of the world, all the way, with Range, further than she’d ever looked at anything, so far that the lines went into a knot at the end like a fist. About frame one, and the nine. About two hundred and nine people going home, gold going red at the edges, frightened and glad.

He listened. He didn’t write anything down. She’d never once seen Corvin not write something down.

“And at the end,” she said, “Deston let go.”

The threads had come apart. That was what it had felt like. Like the end of a song when the choir stops on the last chord and everybody breathes out at once. Thea first, then Hana, then the rest of them, each one going back down their own thread to wherever they were: a knoll, a wall, a castle floor. And Deston last, letting go of all of them, very gently, the way you’d put down a tray.

And she’d still had Range open.

She didn’t know why. She’d had it open for four hundred and six heartbeats, Higu said afterwards, longer than she’d ever held it, and when the others went it had just stayed open, the way your eyes stay open when you’ve been staring at something too long. And she’d still had a little of all of them in her. A heartbeat’s worth. Thea’s Parse, so that everything she looked at had parts. Yan’s colours. Deston’s notes.

And instead of letting it go, she’d turned her head.

Not along the line home. The other way. West. Past the rings in the valley, eleven miles up the pass road, that she’d watched every day for two years. Past the circle burned into the valley floor and the absence sitting at the foot of the pass and the seven threadless standing apart outside the thirteenth ring where a fourteenth would go. Past the end of the valley. Past the mountains. Past the edge of Corvin’s maps, which stopped, every one of them, at a line of peaks he’d never been able to cross.

Further than that.

“There was something there,” said Hyvred.

Corvin didn’t move.

“I don’t know what. I can’t—“ She stopped. She turned her glasses over. “It wasn’t a light. Everything’s a light, for me. People, horses, the fire in the guardroom, the rings, even the threadless, they’re lights with nothing in them, like lamps with the wick gone. The absence isn’t a light. It’s a hole where a light should be; I can see the edges of it. This wasn’t either.” She tried to think how to say it. “It was a shape. In the dark. Past the mountains. Very big. Bigger than the valley. Bigger than the mountains, maybe; I couldn’t see the edges. Where there shouldn’t be anything at all. And it was still.”

“Still,” said Corvin.

“Still like the absence. Not asleep. Waiting.” She swallowed. “And Thea’s Parse was still in me, so I could see it had parts. I couldn’t read them. But it had them. It wasn’t a mountain or a cloud. It was made. Or grown. Or—“ She stopped. “It was a thing. A thing with parts. Sitting in the dark past the end of your maps.”

He didn’t say anything for a long time.

“And it looked back,” said Hyvred.

She hadn’t known a thing without eyes could look.

The absence had looked at her once, in the spring, when the call came and the king stepped in front of her on the tower and opened his whole ninth tier. She’d felt it then. A pressure in her ears, and every one of the turned in the rings turning their heads at once with a sound like a field of barley, and a cold place behind her eyes. But it had been looking at the tower. At the town. At the king. She’d just been in the way.

This hadn’t been looking at the tower.

It had turned. The whole of it. The way the absence turned to face the pass; slowly, without moving, the way a compass needle turns. Toward her. Not toward Thornwall. Toward the small hot point of light on the top of a tower that had just been holding seven people’s eyes and still had a little of them in her. As if it had felt something go past in the dark, very far off, and turned its head to see what.

And it had seen her.

That was the thing she couldn’t stop feeling. Not that she’d seen it. That it had seen her. That for one heartbeat, past the edge of the world as anybody in this kingdom knew it, something the size of a mountain had turned toward a cow girl from another world in a blanket on a roof and known exactly where she was.

Then Corvin had had her by the shoulders, and she’d let go of Range, and it was gone.

“It knows where I am,” said Hyvred.

She said it very calmly. She didn’t feel calm.

“It knows what I am. The thing that sees. I think it’s known for a while. I think the absence looking at me in the spring was it, looking through the absence, the way—“ She stopped. “The way I looked through Thea. And last night I looked at it, and it looked at me, and now it knows I know.” She put her glasses back in her hair. Her hands were shaking. “I don’t want to look west again, Corvin. I don’t want to open Range and have it be there. I don’t think I can.”

Corvin looked at her for a long time across the leads.

Then he did a thing she’d never seen him do. He put the cord down on the stone beside him, very carefully, and took a stub of pencil out of his coat, and a fold of paper, the cheap grey surveyor’s kind he carried everywhere, and wrote. Not a report. She could see that. A short line, at the top of the paper, in his small careful hand, in the kingdom’s letters she couldn’t read.

“What’s that?” said Hyvred.

“The date,” said Corvin. “And Hyvred saw it.” He looked at the line. “I’ve been writing something is wrong in the west for five years. To the Advisor. To the court. To anybody. Tracks. A village where people stand wrong. Something in the valley. I never had anything to put after it.” He folded the paper. “Now I’ve got a girl on a tower who looked past the end of my maps and something looked back.”

“That’s not good, Corvin.”

“No,” said Corvin. “It’s the worst thing I’ve ever written.” He put the paper in his coat. “But it’s true. That’s all I’ve ever wanted. Something true to put after it.”

She wrote to Thea in the evening.

She’d meant to write to the channel. She’d opened her window three times during the day to do it, and three times closed it, because the channel was still full of the night before: two hundred and nine, and some went home, and Chris’s counts, and Hana’s tell Edwy, and everybody saying the same things to each other in thirty different ways, the way people do after something enormous. 209. Home. Lantern. Not yet. She’d read all of it. It was the happiest she’d ever seen the channel. She couldn’t put a mountain in the dark into the middle of it.

So she went down the tower to Corvin’s room over the guardhouse, and sat on the end of his bed with her tail round her ankles, and said it to him, and he said it into the cord.

It was a strange way to write a letter. She’d done it before, a few times, the way everybody did now. You said it to your guide; your guide said it into the cord; it went to Hana, three weeks’ ride south and east, round Hana’s wrist; and Hana sent it on, up the right cord, to the right guide; and it came out at the other end in the other guide’s hand, in the kingdom’s letters, and they read it to whoever it was for. So that what you said went through four people’s hands and two scripts and a cat girl’s wrist before it got there, and came out the other end, somehow, as what you meant.

Wendy had said, once, that cords carried what you meant, not what you said. Hyvred hadn’t believed her. She did now.

“Tell Thea,” she said. “Just Thea. Not the channel.”

Corvin waited with the cord in his fist.

“Tell her I kept Range open when Deston let go,” said Hyvred. “I didn’t mean to. And I looked west. Past the valley. Past the mountains. And there’s something there.” She stopped. She’d been trying all day to find a better way to say it and there wasn’t one. “Tell her I looked west and something looked back.”

Corvin said it into the cord. Slowly, word by word, the way Maren had taught all the guides to, since the spring: say it out loud, and stop when you don’t know why a word’s there.

Then he sat there with it in his fist, and they waited.

It took about as long as it takes to boil a kettle. Then the cord went warm, and Corvin’s face changed, the way the guides’ faces always changed when a strip came in, as if somebody had spoken in the next room. He read it out.

Thea says: I know. I felt you turn. I didn’t know what you saw. Thank you for telling me first.

A pause. The cord still warm.

Higu says: Page started.

Hyvred laughed. She didn’t mean to. It came out of her all at once, a short startled sound, the first time since the tower.

“What’s funny?” said Corvin.

“Higu,” said Hyvred. “She’s started a page. She’s got a book. She writes everything in it, and when something’s too big she gives it a page.” She wiped her eyes. “Ice had a page. For a year. And the slow clock. And the register. Now there’s a page for the thing in the west.” She shook her head. “She’ll have written WEST at the top. In capitals. And underlined it.”

The cord went warm again.

Higu says: WEST. Underlined. Go to bed, Hyvred.

She went to bed.

Not on the tower. In the barracks, in her own bunk, for the first time in a month, under the low smoky ceiling with the crack in the plaster shaped like a running horse; with Bee in the bunk below, pretending to be asleep, and the Pollen Puffs on the shelf over Bee’s head in a small round heap, glowing very faintly blue in the dark the way they did when they were worried.

“You came down,” said Bee. Not asleep.

“Yes.”

“You never come down.”

“I came down.”

There was a silence from the bunk below. Bee wasn’t good at silences. She was good at saying exactly what she thought, all the time, at whoever was nearest; she’d told the king, once, at the gate, in front of half the garrison, that his horse was too big for him. In all the time Hyvred had known her, at home and here, she’d never once known Bee to not say a thing.

“Is it bad?” said Bee.

“Yes.”

“Bad like the absence or bad like the call?”

“Bad like I don’t know what it is.”

The silence from the bunk below went on for a long time. Then the bunk creaked, and Bee’s face came up over the edge of Hyvred’s, in the dark, upside down, with her antennae bent against the slats of the bunk above and her wings folded flat down her back.

“Then I’m sleeping up there,” said Bee. “With you. Move over.”

“There’s no room.”

“There’s loads of room. You’re very thin for a cow.” And she climbed up, wings and antennae and elbows, and lay down on the edge of the bunk with her back to Hyvred’s front and her wings squashed between them, buzzing very faintly every time she breathed; and the Pollen Puffs came off the shelf one after another and drifted up and settled on the blanket over both of them in a soft glowing heap.

“Don’t look west,” said Bee. Into the dark.

“I wasn’t going to.”

“Good,” said Bee. “Then go to sleep.”

Hyvred lay there with her best friend’s wings against her chest and the bees on her feet, and went to sleep.

She didn’t look west for six days.

It was harder than it sounded. She hadn’t known how much of her day was looking until she stopped. On Earth looking had been work; you looked through the coin-sized hole at whatever you needed and that was all you got. Here, since the first morning, she’d looked at everything, all the time, because she could. She looked at the bread in the mess. She looked at the horses. She looked at the lights of people going about the fort, warm and small and moving, the way you’d watch fish in a pond. She looked at the valley, every morning, from the tower, for two years.

She didn’t look at the valley.

That was the hard part. You couldn’t look at the valley without looking up the pass road, and you couldn’t look up the pass road without looking north, and north from the tower the Greyspine went up in a long grey wall all the way to the edge of the sky, and west of that, past the last peak, past the end of Corvin’s maps, was the place where the dark had a shape in it.

So she didn’t go up the tower.

She did other things. She went round the town with Kei in the mornings, the way she had since the spring, looking at everybody’s lights for the faint cold at the edge that meant the call was thinking about them. She could see it a day or two early now; Kei had his doses ready. There wasn’t any cold. Not this week. The lights of Thornwall were warm and ordinary and a little tired, the way a town’s lights are at the end of a harvest.

“It’s quiet,” she said to Kei, on the third morning, outside the miller’s.

“Hisui says it’s not quiet,” said Kei. He had the green snake round his neck, the way Yan had his white one, and the snake’s head was up, its tongue going in and out, tasting the morning. Kei listened to it for a moment with his head on one side, the way he always did, as if somebody were talking to him quietly in a crowded room. “He says it’s thinking about it. He says the air’s different. Like before rain.” He listened again. “He says that’s a stupid way of putting it and he’s sorry, but it’s the only way he’s got.”

“Thinking about what?”

“The autumn,” said Kei. He looked up the street toward the north gate, toward the pass road beyond it, which you couldn’t see from here. “It came in the spring. Hisui thinks it comes twice. When the days and nights are the same.” He touched the snake’s head. “He says that’s in three weeks.”

Hyvred didn’t say anything.

“You didn’t look,” said Kei. Gently. He was the gentlest of all of them; people brought him things. They had since the first month, when a soldier with a dead brother had sat down next to him in the mess and not said anything for an hour and then said all of it. “You’d usually have looked by now. Up the road. To check.”

“I’m not looking north this week.”

Kei didn’t ask why. He nodded, and stroked the snake, and the snake said something Hyvred couldn’t hear that made Kei’s mouth twitch.

“He says that’s sensible,” said Kei. “He says he wouldn’t look either. He says he hasn’t looked north since the spring and he’s a snake.”

Hild found her on the fifth day.

Hyvred was in the yard behind the barracks, in the corner by the old well, where nobody went because the well had been dry for a hundred years and smelled of it. She wasn’t doing anything. She was sitting on the well’s rim with her glasses in her hands, looking at the stones of the yard wall, which were very ordinary, one after another.

She heard Hild before she saw her. You always did. Hild walked like she was listening to the ground, slowly, with her head a little on one side, the side with the eye; and she came across the yard and sat down on the well’s rim beside Hyvred without asking, the way she’d sat down beside her the first week, when Hyvred was still crying at ceilings.

She was fifty-two. She was the oldest of the seven guards and the strongest; she’d been Shaped since before Hyvred was born, the king said. She’d lost an eye in the war, the left one, to something nobody had ever been able to tell Hyvred about properly; there was a seam across that side of her face from the brow to the cheekbone, white, old, and under it, nothing. She’d learned to fight by hearing. The first thing she’d ever said to Hyvred, on the first day, in this yard, was so you’re the one who can see. And the second thing, a week later, was sight isn’t everything, girl. Close your eyes.

“You’ve stopped going up,” said Hild.

“Yes.”

“Corvin told me.” Hild didn’t look at her. She looked at the yard wall too, with her one eye. “Not what you saw. He wouldn’t. He said it was yours. He said you looked past his maps and something looked back, and you’ve not been up the tower since.” She was quiet. “That’s the first time in two years that tower’s been empty at night. The watch on the gate noticed. They asked me if you were ill.”

“I’m not ill.”

“I told them that.” Hild turned her head, finally. The good eye on Hyvred, grey and steady. “I told them you were doing what I did. After.”

Hyvred looked at her.

“After this,” said Hild, and touched the seam across the left side of her face. Not dramatically. The way you’d touch a scar you’d had a long time, to show where it was. “Twenty-six years ago. On the Redmarch, before they sent me west. A thing came out of the dark in the winter and took my eye and four of my squad, and I never saw what it was. I saw it coming. I saw it close. I saw it in front of me. And then I didn’t see anything on that side ever again.” She was quiet. “For a year afterwards I didn’t look east. I’d stand on the wall with my back to the red. I’d walk through the east gate with my eye shut. I thought if I looked, it’d be there. I thought it’d see me looking.”

“Did it?”

“I don’t know,” said Hild. “I never saw it again. Nobody did. It never came back.” She looked at the wall. “I think, now, it was just a thing. A big one. It came out of Kharvos in a bad winter, the way they do, and it took what it took, and it went back. I don’t think it knew I was there. I don’t think it knew anything.” She paused. “But I didn’t look east for a year. Because I was afraid it had seen me.”

Hyvred turned her glasses over in her hands.

“This one saw me,” she said.

“I believe you.”

“It’s not like yours. It’s not a thing that comes out of a pass in a bad winter. It’s bigger than the pass. It’s bigger than—“ She stopped. “And it turned. On purpose. Toward me.”

“I believe you,” said Hild again. “I’m not telling you it didn’t. I’m telling you what I did. I didn’t look north for a year. And at the end of the year I realised that I’d spent a year with my back to the thing I was supposed to be watching, and all the time I had my back to it, I didn’t know what it was doing.” She was quiet. “That was worse. Not looking was worse. It didn’t make it go away. It just made me blind on that side as well.”

Hyvred didn’t say anything.

“I’m not telling you to go up the tower,” said Hild. “Not today. Not tomorrow. That’s yours.” She stood up off the well’s rim, slowly, the way her knees made her now. “I’m telling you, when you do go up, you don’t have to look with your eyes.”

“What else is there?”

“Close them,” said Hild.

And walked away across the yard, listening to the ground.

She went up on the sixth night.

She didn’t decide to. She found herself at the bottom of the north tower at dusk with her blanket over her arm, the way she’d found herself there every evening for two years, and her feet were already on the first step before she’d thought about it.

A hundred and twelve steps. She counted them, because Wendy would have. The tower was the oldest thing in Thornwall, older than the walls, a squat round thing of grey stone that had been a watchtower on this road before there was a town to watch over; the steps were worn hollow in the middle by three hundred years of boots, and at the top there was a trapdoor, and the leads, and the parapet, and the sky.

She came up through the trapdoor and stood on the leads in the last of the light with her back to the north.

Facing south. Facing the town. The roofs of Thornwall going down the hill below her in the dusk, slate and thatch, with the smoke coming up from every chimney straight into the still air, and the lights coming on in the windows one at a time, and the lights of the people, warm and small, moving along the streets. The mill. The granary, with Bee’s chalk dog on its wall, a running dog in one line, that she’d drawn on the shortest day in the winter for Tiger and that nobody had washed off, because nobody would. Ossie’s shed, behind the north gate, the only thing Tiger had ever built in this world that stood, with a shape on its roof that was Tiger, sitting, looking north.

She looked at all of it. Edge to edge. The way she looked at a ceiling.

Then she shut her eyes.

She’d never done it on purpose. Not up here. Hild had made her do it in the yard, the first year, a hundred times, standing with her eyes shut while Hild walked round her and she said where Hild was; and she’d got good at it, a bit, the way you get good at anything you’re made to do a hundred times. But she’d never shut her eyes on the tower. The tower was for looking.

She stood on the leads with her eyes shut and her back to the north, and listened.

The town. Somebody calling a dog. A door. A cart, a long way down, on the road from the south. The wind, which was almost nothing, coming down off the mountains behind her, cold on the back of her neck. Her own breath. The faint dry tick of the tower’s stones cooling after the day.

And something else.

Not a sound. She didn’t know what to call it. She’d never felt it with her eyes open, because with her eyes open there was always too much to see. It was the thing Range did, when she opened it: the reaching. The long thin feel of her own sight going out from her like a line from a rod, out, and out, to wherever she was looking. Except that her eyes were shut, and she wasn’t looking at anything.

And it was still there.

Going out from her. Not ahead; she was facing south, toward the town. Behind. Out of the back of her head, somehow, out over her shoulder, the way you know without turning round that there’s a wall behind you. North. Up the pass road. Eleven miles. Into the valley.

She could feel the rings.

Not see them. Feel them. The way you feel the shape of a room in the dark when you’ve lived in it for years. Thirteen of them, round a circle burned into the valley floor, the turned standing in them, hundreds, still, the way they always stood. She could feel the shape of each ring, the way you can feel the shape of a coin in your pocket without taking it out. And the absence, at the foot of the pass, a hole in the shape of things, facing the pass, facing, not looking.

And outside the thirteenth ring, apart, the fourteenth place.

She felt them there. Standing. Not in a ring yet. In a short curved line, like the start of one.

Nine.

There had been seven. Since the spring. Seven threadless, standing outside the thirteenth ring where a fourteenth would go: a man in a torn long coat, an old woman with a stick, two dockers, a young woman with her hair in a net, a boy in a sailor’s jersey, and a woman with a market basket with apples in it. She’d counted them every morning since the spring, from the tower, with her eyes. Seven.

Nine.

Two more. Come in the last six days, while she had her back to it. She didn’t know who. She couldn’t see faces with her eyes shut. She could feel that there were nine of them, and that the curve they stood in was a little longer than it had been, and that it went, very slightly, the way the curve of the thirteenth ring went. Following it. As if the line of them were the beginning of something drawn round the outside of everything else.

She stood there with her eyes shut and felt it for a long time.

She didn’t feel west. She didn’t go west. She didn’t let the reaching go further than the valley; she held it there, at the rings, the way you’d hold a dog on a short lead. She could feel the edge of the valley where her reaching stopped, and past it, the mountains, and past them, nothing. She didn’t go into the nothing. She didn’t need to. She knew what was there.

But she could feel the valley. Without looking. With her back to it.

Sight isn’t everything, girl.

And then, because she was Hyvred, and because she had never in her life been able to leave a thing alone once she’d found it, she felt for Ossie.

She knew where he was. Third ring. North side. She’d watched him there for two and a half years, ever since the first spring, when Sergeant Dunmore’s ten went north into the passes and didn’t come back, and three weeks later a boy in a torn tunic walked in at the gate at dusk to report, not blinking, and then walked away north again as if he were remembering how. Trooper Oswald Pell. A freckled boy, nineteen, Drawn, standing in the third ring with his face turned up toward the pass, the way all of them stood. Except that in the spring, on the morning Tiger ran out to the rings and fetched Joss back by the middle and ran home, Ossie’s face had turned. A hair. After Tiger. The way you’d look sideways at the start of a race at the one beside you.

It had stayed turned. Every morning since. A hair. Toward the north gate, toward the town, toward the place where a tiger demihuman ran to the gate and back every morning at dawn because a boy he used to race had always lost.

She felt for him with her eyes shut.

There.

Third ring. North side. A small cold place in the shape of a boy, standing very still, the way all of them stood. She couldn’t see his face. She could feel which way it was turned.

Toward the town.

Further than a hair, now. She was almost sure. She’d had her back to it six days, and in six days it had gone round a little further, the way the hour hand of a clock goes round if you don’t look at it. As if every morning the thing that was wearing him felt a tiger run to the north gate and back, and every morning something left in the boy turned its head a little further to watch.

Hyvred opened her eyes.

The town was there, in front of her, in the dark now, with its lights. Ossie’s shed. The shape on its roof. Tiger, sitting there with his knees up and his tail hanging down over the eaves, looking north up the pass road, the way he sat every night.

She didn’t call down to him. She didn’t know what she’d say. He’s still turning. He’s turning more. She didn’t know if that was a good thing. She didn’t know if it was Ossie turning, or the thing wearing Ossie, watching the boy who kept running.

She’d tell him. Tomorrow. When she knew how to say it.

She opened her window before she lay down.

Forty-one. It had been thirty-six before the tenth. She’d felt it move, on the leads, when the seven came apart; she hadn’t looked until now. Five points in a night, from holding seven people’s eyes for four hundred heartbeats. Thea’s Lantern had cost a year and a half. Hana’s bar had gone to ninety-eight. Hers had gone up five.

She didn’t mind. She looked at it, and then at the line under it, Range, and thought about what it had done tonight with her eyes shut, which she hadn’t known it could do, which nobody had told her. Reaching. Without looking. Over her shoulder, the way you know there’s a wall behind you.

She thought: that’s new.

She thought: Hild knew.

She lay down on the leads in her blanket, facing south, with her back to the north and the mountains and the valley and the nine and the place past the end of the maps where a shape sat very still in the dark and knew where she was; and pulled the blanket up to her chin, and looked at the sky.

All of it. Edge to edge.

Then she shut her eyes, and felt the valley behind her, the way you’d feel a sleeping animal in the room; and didn’t go any further than that; and slept.

In the morning, she told Tiger.

She found him at the north gate at dawn, where he always was, at the end of the race. He’d touched the gate and turned round and was standing with his hands on his knees and his tail lashing, breathing, looking back down the street to the well where he’d started, the way he did every morning, as if somebody might be behind him.

Nobody ever was. He looked anyway.

“Tiger,” said Hyvred.

He straightened up. He looked at her, and at her face, and his ears went flat.

“What,” said Tiger.

“He’s turned,” said Hyvred. “Ossie. More. Not a hair now.” She didn’t know how to say the rest of it. “Toward the town. Toward the gate. I felt it last night. With my eyes shut. He’s turning a bit more every morning you run.”

Tiger didn’t say anything for a long time.

Then he turned round, very slowly, and looked out through the north gate, up the pass road, toward the mountains and the valley eleven miles off that he couldn’t see and she could feel; and Hyvred saw his hands close at his sides, and open.

“Is it him?” said Tiger. “Turning. Or the thing.”

“I don’t know.”

“Find out,” said Tiger. Not to her, quite. To the road. “Find out, Hyv. Please.”

“I’ll try.”

He nodded. He kept looking up the road. Then he did a thing she’d seen him do every morning for two and a half years and never once understood until now: he put his hand flat on the post of the north gate, where he touched it at the end of the race, and held it there, the way you’d put your hand on somebody’s shoulder.

“Tomorrow,” said Tiger. To the road.

And turned, and ran back down the street to the well, to start again.
