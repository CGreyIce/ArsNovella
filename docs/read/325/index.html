Chapter 325

The Thread on the Steps

3,389 words · 15 min read

Tell Dest thank you for writing it down as it came. He knew who was reading.

Deston had read that line more times than he’d read anything since the hall.

He hadn’t known. That was the thing he couldn’t tell anybody, not even Gabe. On the night of the steps he’d sat on the stone of the landing with his hand on Rae’s sleeve and four people’s warmth going down his thread into her, and every so often, when he could, he’d put a line in the channel. Short. Plain. She’s going up the steps. She closed it. Maud’s herself. He’d thought he was doing it for the thirty-four. For Higu holding the door, for Thea at the lake who couldn’t read it from there, for Yan on the knoll. He’d thought that was all.

He hadn’t once thought about who else was on the end of the line.

And Declan had read every word. On the end of a jetty a hundred miles off, at the near end, in a seat the ash had grown, with a girl on the stones beside him. Counting.

He knew who was reading. Declan had written that, in English, in a hand that was careful the way you’re careful in a language you haven’t written in thirty-eight years. As if Deston had known. As if he’d done it on purpose.

He hadn’t. But he’d thought about it every night since, on the infirmary roof, with his eyes loose, looking at the threads going out of Kingsferry in every direction like the spokes of a wheel. And he’d thought: next time I will.

The healers started coming at the end of the summer, a fortnight after the king’s cord.

By the river, the way the king had said. Before the autumn rains. Two from every order in the kingdom, on barges and lighters and the post-boat from the coast, with their bags and their aprons and their faces, which all did the same thing when they came up Tanner’s Row and saw the river-gate infirmary for the first time: a long low whitewashed building on a rise, with smoke coming out of the chimney at each end and a small rough iron bell on the doorpost with a rope pull low enough for a child.

Two from the temple at Ashby. Two from the college of physicians in Solmere, in their good grey coats, who’d been sent, Hana said in a cord, by Lord Vane himself. Two from the Sisters of the Long Road, who walked everywhere and had walked here from the downs. Two from the hospital at Caddow. Two from the fishing towns along the coast, who’d come in an open boat. One from Gullhaven, on her own, an old woman with no order at all who’d heard the crier on the quay and got on the next barge.

They all asked for her by name.

Deston watched it from the roof. He’d stopped going down to the ward in the day, mostly; there wasn’t room. Thirteen healers and Tamsin and the dock cases and Ned Webb’s mother, who’d started coming in every morning to roll bandages because, she said, she couldn’t sit at home with her hands empty and her boy whole. And in the middle of it, at Maud’s table by the window at the end of the long ward, a girl in a smith’s coat with the sleeves turned back, who couldn’t mend a cut finger.

She taught them anyway.

He’d known she would. He’d known her since she was seventeen. She’d never once in her life waited until she was ready.

She sat at Maud’s table with the dose book and the grey paper and the needle case, and the healers stood round her, and the dock cases came in by the side door, the way Maud had said, so they didn’t frighten the fevers. And Rachael didn’t put her hands on anybody. She told them where to put theirs.

Left. A hair. Down. There. Dennis’s words, from the landing. She used them. Don’t push. Listen first. Find what it’s already trying to do. Help.

And when a healer from Caddow with forty years in her hands put them on a docker’s grey palm and listened, and found it, and the grey went up as cold smoke under Emrys’s fire, and the cut under it went red and real and answered her Mend, she looked up at the girl at the table, astonished. And Rachael nodded, once, the way Maud used to, when a thing had been done right.

“That’ll do,” she said.

The six were in the back ward.

That was the other thing. Deston went down to look at them every evening, after the healers had gone to their lodgings and the ward was quiet. Sitting on the benches in a row, with their hands in their laps. Bet Ferrin from Mallow’s Ford, with her ferrywoman’s shawl. The boy in the fisher’s smock. The big woman. The bargeman, with the pike wound in his shoulder that had closed by itself, slowly, the way the turned closed, without anybody. The two men in brown. They ate when they were fed. They slept when it was dark. They didn’t speak. They didn’t look at anybody. They sat on the benches in the back ward of the river-gate infirmary and looked at the wall, where there was nothing, with their faces patient and a little puzzled.

The healers wanted to try.

Of course they did. They’d come a long way. They’d read Rachael’s eight steps, or had them read to them, and they’d heard the cord from Thornwall about the sergeant and his wife and the healer’s white hands, and they’d sat in the back ward and looked at six people who’d been somebody once, at a landing on the Wren, and wanted to put their hands on them.

Rachael said no.

“Not yet,” she said, at the long table, the night the Solmere physicians asked. “Thornwall did it in a heartbeat and it pulled shut from inside and took a woman’s hands. Halfway’s worse than not starting.” She looked at her own hands on the table. “They’re asleep under it. Hyvred says. All of them. The thing pulls the seam shut from inside unless somebody under it’s pulling the other way, and these six aren’t pulling. They don’t know there’s anything to pull toward.” She was quiet. “Thornwall’s going to try again. On a boy who’s awake. When they know how that goes, we’ll know more.”

The senior physician from Solmere, a careful man called Doctor Aske with ink on his cuffs, looked at her for a long time.

“With respect,” he said. “You can’t mend.”

“No,” said Rachael. “That’s why I’m not doing it.”

Deston went down to the steps on the forty-third night after.

Not alone. Nobody went anywhere alone; that had stayed, after. Calvin came, because Calvin went everywhere at night now, low over the river, as if he were still looking for something; and Dennis, because Dennis said he had nothing better to do and was lying, and they both knew he was lying, and Dennis said it was a very small lie and he’d allow it.

The steps were clean.

The fish market had scrubbed them the morning after, on their knees, with buckets, and gone over them again with sand, and then the women from the net sheds had come and done it a third time with lye, until the landing at the top was paler than the rest of the customs house and you couldn’t have found a mark on it if you’d tried. Nobody had left a hook on the bottom step since. Nobody had left anything. People walked round the landing now, the long way, by the side stair, without being told; and the ones who had to go up the main steps to the doors went up quickly, the way you’d walk past a place where somebody had died.

Deston walked up to the top and sat down on it.

In the middle. On the hub. Where an old woman had sat with her stick across her knees and a girl had knelt in front of her for three hours. The stone was cool and smooth and smelled of lye.

“What are we looking for,” said Calvin.

“I don’t know,” said Deston.

He let his eyes go loose.

Made things had threads. He’d learned that slowly, over four years, and then all at once, on a jetty, with Alea’s sword on a blanket and nothing coming off it at all. If somebody made a thing carefully, for somebody, there was a thread. A hair of one, mostly, fainter than a person’s; but there. From the thing to the one it was for. And back, faint, to the one who’d made it.

Gabe’s knife had one: to Rae, thick as a bootlace, and back to Gabe. Maud’s needle case had one so old and so many-handed it went back past Maud, past Merewen, past anybody he could follow, into a dark he couldn’t see the end of. The bell on the ward door had a new one, to the ward, very bright, and back to Gabe.

The hooks hadn’t had anything. Not a hair. He’d looked at every one of them for five weeks and found nothing at all; somebody with no thread to give it, he’d said, the first time, and he’d been right. The man in the hat had made them, or carried them, and the man in the hat had been nothing. Grey all the way through. No threads to anybody, ever.

But the man in the hat hadn’t drawn the ring.

Deston had thought about that every night on the roof. He’d watched it happen from the landing, or he’d watched the end of it. The ring had been drawn before any of them got there. In a dark that took the moonlight and gave none back, ten paces across, with lines going in like spokes and ruler marks along them, and twelve fires. And it had been drawn for somebody. Lit so it could be seen from one window. From the end of a long ward, from a table where a girl sat at night with a notebook on her knee. It had been made, very carefully, for her.

So it should have a thread.

He looked.

It took a long time. He’d known it would. The stone had been scrubbed three times, and the ring was gone; there was nothing on the landing now but lye and moonlight and the three of them. And a made thing’s thread was the faintest thing he could see even when the thing was there.

He looked anyway. The way Rae had listened, on this stone, to a word she couldn’t see. Not pushing. Letting his eyes go further and further out of focus until the landing went flat and pale and everything on it was just stone, and the only things left were the things that went between.

Calvin’s thread to him, warm, a little frayed. Dennis’s. Both of theirs to the infirmary on the hill, to Rae, thick and gold. Hundreds more, thin, going out from the landing in every direction, to the fish market and the net sheds and the north quay: every person who’d knelt on this stone with a bucket and scrubbed it and gone home.

And under all of that, in the stone itself, so faint that he thought at first it was a flaw in his own eye, a hair.

Not gold. Not any colour he’d seen on a thread before. It was the colour the ring had been: the colour of a dark that gave nothing back. A hair of it, lying in the stone at the hub where Maud had sat, finer than anything he’d ever followed. Scrubbed over three times. Still there. You couldn’t scrub a thread; he could have told the fish market that.

It went two ways.

One way, faint, up. Up the hill. Up Tanner’s Row, to the river-gate infirmary, to a window at the end of a long ward. To her. He’d expected that. A made thing’s thread went to the one it was made for. It was thinner than it should have been; it was fraying, he thought, the way a thread frays when the thing at the middle of it is gone. But it was there, and it went to Rae, and he didn’t like it, and he put it aside.

And the other way.

The other way went out.

Off the landing. Down the steps. Across the quay. Not toward the river, not up the Wren toward the lake, not east toward the red. West. Over the roofs of the warehouses behind the customs house, over the town wall, over the market gardens beyond it and the road that went up into the hills, and on. A hair of dark in the moonlight, going away from Kingsferry so straight it might have been drawn with a rule, up into the hills to the west, and over them, and further than he had ever in his life followed anything.

He followed it.

He didn’t mean to. He’d never been able to follow a thread that far; Deston’s threads went to people, and people were close, mostly, and when they were far the threads went thin and he lost them. This one didn’t go thin. It went on. Over the first ridge of the western hills and down into whatever was on the other side, and up over the next ridge, and on, and his eyes went with it, further and further, the way you’d follow a kite string up into the sky until the kite was a dot and then not even that.

He felt himself go.

Not his body. The rest of him. Out along the hair, west, the way he’d gone out along the threads on the night of Rae’s post, when thirty-four of them leaned on his. Except there was nothing leaning. There was nothing on this thread at all except the dark of it, and the straightness, and a sort of pull, very faint, at the far end, like a fish on a line a long way off, so far off you couldn’t tell if it was a fish or the current.

West.

“Dest.”

Somebody had his sleeve.

He came back the way you come back from falling asleep at a table: all at once, with a jolt, and the world too bright. He was sitting on the hub of the landing with his hand flat on the stone. Calvin had hold of his sleeve. Dennis was crouched in front of him with his face very close, and his eyes doing the thing.

“You stopped breathing,” said Dennis. “For a bit. I counted.” He didn’t let go of Deston’s other arm. “Forty. I was going to slap you at sixty. I’ve always wanted to slap you.”

Deston breathed.

“What did you see,” said Calvin.

He told them.

He told Rae in the morning. All of them, at the long table in the rooms above the chandler’s, the way they told each other everything now: Gabe and Rae and Dennis and Calvin and Emrys and Sabine, who was walking again with her ribs strapped and who sat on a stool by the door the way she always did.

“West,” said Rachael.

“West. Over the hills. Straight.” He spread his hands on the table. “Further than I’ve ever followed anything. Further than I could see the end of. It doesn’t go thin. And there’s a pull at the far end. Very faint.” He looked at Rachael. “And the other end goes to you.”

Gabe moved. Not much.

“It’s fraying,” said Deston quickly. “Your end. Since the ring went. I think it’ll go. I don’t think it’s anything now; I think it’s just what’s left. But I wanted you to know it was there.” He swallowed. “I’m not keeping things off the table. Not any more.”

Rachael didn’t say anything for a while. She sat with her hands in her lap, in the coat, and looked at the table.

“West,” she said again. “A household in the hills. West of here.”

Nobody said anything.

“That’s what he said,” said Rachael. “The first autumn. In the infirmary. With his hat in his hands. My master has a household in the hills, west of here.” She looked up. “Dennis said every word of it was a lie.”

“Every word,” said Dennis. Very quietly. “I’d have bet my share of the salt on it.” He looked at his hands. “I’d have lost the salt.”

He looked at his window that night, on the roof, with the healers’ lamps lit in the lodging-house windows all down Tanner’s Row and the bell on the ward door below him, and the steps at the bottom of the hill pale and clean in the moon.

[ Deston · Tier 2: Candle · Skills: Chorus, Harmony · Progress to Lantern: 52% ]

Fifty-two. It had been forty-seven since the spring. Since the night of the post, when thirty-four threads leaned on his.

He knew what had moved it. He’d followed a thread further than he’d ever followed anything, to somewhere he couldn’t see, and come back because somebody had his sleeve. Lantern: hold a thing steady while you do something else. He hadn’t been able to. He’d gone out along a hair of dark and stopped breathing for forty heartbeats. Next time he’d hold it and breathe.

Next time.

He looked west. He couldn’t see it now; he’d have to sit on the landing again, on the hub, with his eyes loose, for a long time. But he knew it was there. Going out of Kingsferry over the western hills like a line drawn with a rule, to a household that wasn’t a household, to a pull at the far end that might be a fish and might be the current.

He didn’t follow it.

He sat on the roof with his eyes on the steps, and thought about a man on a jetty a hundred miles north, reading every line as it came. And when the channel went quiet, at midnight, he wrote one more, short, plain, and didn’t think about who’d read it, because he knew.
