Live from Elsewhere · Volume 8: Somewhere Else

Chapter 199

Somewhere Else

3,453 words · 15 min read

The road down into Masmark took all night, and Ice was recognised four times before he got to the bottom.

The first was a woman in silver, at the third switchback, coming up as he was going down. She stopped on the road and looked at him. She looked at his shield, and the way he held it, low and a little out to the side. Then she put her fist in her other palm and bowed, very slightly, the way people bowed in a game with wind in its name, a long time ago.

“Tank,” she said.

That was all. Tank. As if it were his name.

He bowed back the same amount. She went on up.

The second was two men in green at a well halfway down, who said o7 together without looking up from their water, and he said it back.

The third was a girl in deep blue with a gold line pinned on her chest, at the gate in the outer wall at the bottom of the switchbacks. She saw him and her mouth fell open. She said “GM?” much too loudly, and then went bright red, and said, “Sorry. Sorry. Harlan said. Harlan told everyone. Sorry.” And ran off into the city before he could say anything.

The fourth was a man at a fish stall.

The fish stall was at the bottom of the north-west road, where it came down off the crater wall and met the first of the harbours. It was a cart. Just a cart, with a brazier on it and a canvas roof, and an old man behind it turning fish on sticks over the coals. Small silver fish from the lake, split and salted, crackling. The smell of them came up the road for half a mile. Every traveller coming down into Masmark by the north-west road walked into it like a wall.

The old man had a face like a boot and a hat like a smaller boot. He looked at Ice when he came down the last of the road at the first dawn, and kept turning his fish.

“Fish?” he said.

“Please.”

The old man gave him one on a stick. It was very hot and very good. Ice ate it standing up by the cart, looking out at the harbour. At the boats and the black water and the morning coming up over the far side of the crater, so far off it was blue.

“Three hundred years,” said the old man.

Ice looked at him.

“I’ve been here three hundred years,” said the old man. “Selling fish. Every traveller who comes down the north-west road, I’ve sold a fish to. Every one.” He turned a stick. “You lot are new. A year. Before you, it was only the others, the old ones, coming down this road. With their lamps.” He looked at Ice. “You’ve got a lamp.”

Ice looked down at his belt. At the plain lamp hanging from it, the one he’d carried since Fernwick.

“It’s just a lamp,” he said.

“They all said that,” said the old man. “The old ones. It’s just a lamp.” He gave Ice a second fish without being asked. “Then they went north and I never sold them another.”

Ice ate the second fish.

“Somebody came home,” he said. “From the north. On the longest night. To the round town.”

The old man stopped turning his sticks.

He looked at Ice for a long time over the brazier, with his boot face and his boot hat.

“Did they,” he said.

“An old woman. With a lamp. She walked behind the dark all night and lit the road.”

The old man didn’t say anything for a long time. Then he turned back to his sticks and turned them, very carefully, one at a time.

“Then I’ll sell them another fish,” he said. “If they ever come down this road.” He cleared his throat. “That one’s free. Your second. Don’t tell anybody.”

“I won’t.”

“And there’s somebody waiting for you,” said the old man, not looking up. “Been here since the second dusk yesterday. Sitting on the harbour wall. Hasn’t bought a fish.” He sniffed. “I don’t trust a man who doesn’t buy a fish.”

Ice looked along the harbour.

There was somebody on the wall. Sitting, with his back to the lake and his legs hanging down, looking at the bottom of the north-west road. In deep blue. Not much gold on him. Very still.

The fourth.

He didn’t know what Vane looked like. That was the strange part. He’d known him for two years, a long time ago, in a little game on a phone where everybody was a word and a number. He’d heard his voice, on calls at three in the morning, a few words at a time. He’d never seen his face.

He walked along the harbour to the wall and stopped a few paces off.

The man on the wall looked at him. He didn’t get up.

“GM,” he said.

His voice was the same. Very quiet. Dry. The kind of voice that had said a lot of true things in its life and not many extra ones.

“Vane,” said Ice.

Vane looked at him for a long time. At his face, which he’d never seen either. At the shield on his back and the sword on his hip and the plain lamp at his belt. At the arrow through the strap of his pack, with two grey feathers and one white.

“You came,” said Vane.

“You asked.”

“I don’t ask things.”

“I know,” said Ice. “That’s why I came.”

Vane didn’t say anything for a while. Then he moved over on the harbour wall. Not much. Enough. The way you’d make room for somebody on a bench.

Ice sat down beside him.

They sat on the harbour wall for a long time and didn’t say much. Ice had known they wouldn’t. Neither of them had ever said much. In two years of running a war against five thousand guilds, Vane had said maybe a thousand words to him, all of them useful. Ice had said maybe five hundred back.

They watched the lake. The boats going out. The sun coming up over the far rim, and the great ring of the city round the water coming out of the shadow one terrace at a time. The banners on the towers catching the light. The island in the middle, very small at this distance, with its tower.

“Harlan cried,” said Vane, after a while.

Ice looked at him.

“When he came back from the round town. In October. He came into Meridian’s Hall in the west and sat down and told it to all of us. The gate and the dap and you’re not running it? and not this one.” Vane was looking at the lake. “Then he went outside and cried for an hour. He told me not to tell you.”

“You’ve told me.”

“I don’t do what Harlan tells me,” said Vane. “I never have.” He paused. “He thought you’d quit. All of us did, except me. You went and played the market. For years. You stopped coming to the wars. We thought you’d gone soft.”

“I did go soft.”

“You didn’t go soft,” said Vane. “You went somewhere else.” He turned his head and looked at Ice for the first time since he’d sat down. “That’s what I told them. Every time. When they said the GM quit. I said the GM doesn’t quit. He goes somewhere else.” He looked back at the lake. “And then one day somebody sends me a clip. A tank on an oak floor, walking a bow into a crowd. And I watch it once and I know. That’s where he went.”

Ice didn’t say anything for a long time.

Somewhere else.

He’d said it to Salty by the fountain, with Meridian’s banner going away down the west road. Because it was somewhere else. And this is here. He’d meant: the old games, the black sky and the war and the guild with the stupid name. Those were somewhere else. And the round town was here.

And Vane had been saying the same words for years, the other way round. To a guild of ten thousand people on the far side of the map, every time somebody said the GM quit. As if he’d known all along that the somewhere else Ice had gone to was a real place, with a fen and a round town and sixty chairs. And that one day he’d find it.

“I’m not the GM,” said Ice.

“I know,” said Vane. “Harlan said. Kei. The one with the map.” He nodded. “It’s good. You were always better at the front than at the top. You hated the top. You used to log off at three in the morning and leave me the numbers.”

“You liked the numbers.”

“I did like the numbers,” agreed Vane.

Something happened at the corner of his mouth. Ice saw it. He’d never seen Vane’s face before today. He’d never known what it did. It did that.

The fish man came along the harbour at about nine with two fish on sticks and held them out without a word. Vane looked at them.

“I don’t buy fish,” said Vane.

“You’re not buying them,” said the fish man. “He is.” He nodded at Ice. “He’s had two free. He’s paying for yours.”

Ice paid for both.

Vane ate his slowly, looking at it as if he’d never eaten a fish before. Maybe he hadn’t, in here. He seemed like a man who forgot to eat.

“Aurelie wants to watch you,” he said, when he’d finished.

Ice looked at him.

“She’s heard about you for six years,” said Vane. “From me. Every time somebody said the GM quit, and I said he went somewhere else, and she asked who’s the GM. And I told her.” He put the stick down on the wall beside him, neatly. “She doesn’t believe half of it. She’s never said so. She doesn’t have to. She’s very good at not saying things. She’s better at it than me.”

“Okay.”

“She’s going to follow you round for a week,” said Vane. “She won’t say. She won’t come up to you. She’ll just be there. In the market, on the harbour, at the back of a Hall.” He paused. “She wants to see what you do when nobody’s asking you to do anything.”

Ice thought about it.

“I’ll notice,” he said.

“I know you’ll notice,” said Vane. “I told her you’d notice. She said good.” He looked at Ice. “She wants you to notice. She just doesn’t want you to do anything about it.”

“Okay,” said Ice.

Vane looked at him for a long moment.

“You haven’t changed,” he said.

“Harlan said that.”

“Harlan’s wrong about most things,” said Vane. “He’s right about that one.” He got up off the wall. He stood there on the harbour for a moment with his hands at his sides, in deep blue, not much gold on him, looking down at Ice. “Come and see the Hall. Meridian’s. It’s on the west rim. It’s very big. Aurelie won’t be there; she’s watching you from somewhere already.” He paused. “I’d like you to see it.”

Ice looked up at him.

I’d like you to see it. Vane, who didn’t ask things.

“Okay,” said Ice, and got up.

Masmark was the biggest thing he’d ever walked through.

He’d thought Carrow Cross was big. He’d thought Brackenford was big, in the first week, when it had two thousand eight hundred travellers on its board and he’d been in the top ten. Masmark was a city round a lake inside a mountain, and it took a day to walk round, Vane said. If you didn’t stop. Nobody didn’t stop.

They went round the north side of the lake toward the west rim, along the terraces. Streets of white stone stepping down to the water. Bridges over the places where streams came down the crater wall into the lake, running, all of them, Ice noticed, fast and loud. Markets on every terrace. Halls on every street. Banners on every Hall.

He’d never seen so many people. Not in the duel, not at the longest night, not on the south steps at the gate. Thousands of them, in every colour there was, all fifty and over, all with somewhere to be. Parties going down to the harbours with their packs. Guilds marching in step behind their banners, twelve at a time, twenty. A man in red with a ledger on his collar shouting prices at a crowd: Crimson Ledger, here too. A woman on a balcony painting a banner, very like Bee, with her tongue between her teeth.

People looked at him. Not all of them. Most of them had never heard of him. Masmark was too big for that, and he was one man in a plain shield walking beside one man in deep blue. But some of them looked. And some of them stopped. Twice more, on the long walk round, somebody said o7 as he went past. Once a man in black said tank and bowed. Once, a whole party of five in purple stopped dead in the street. One of them said, in a voice of total disbelief, “Murderers of the Universe?”, and the other four hit him.

Vane walked beside him the whole way and didn’t say anything.

“You knew this would happen,” said Ice, at the bridge over the third stream.

“I knew this would happen.”

“You didn’t warn me.”

“You’d have walked round it,” said Vane. “Like the goose.” He didn’t look at Ice. “I watched that too.”

Ice didn’t say anything. He felt the warmth come up the sides of his neck again. He’d stopped trying to stop it.

Meridian’s Hall was on the west rim, on a terrace above the biggest harbour in Masmark, and it was a castle.

Not a Hall. A castle. Grey stone, with towers at the corners and a great door in the middle and a courtyard behind it big enough for the round market. And over all of it, on the tallest tower, the banner. Deep blue, a long way across, with one gold line top to bottom.

Inside, there were hundreds of people. Meridian, in their blue, coming and going, talking, sitting at long tables in a hall with a roof so high it had its own weather. Not sixty chairs. A thousand. Two thousand. A wall at the far end with names on it, carved in the stone, in columns. Ice couldn’t read them from the door. There were too many.

Vane stopped in the doorway and let him look.

“Ten thousand,” said Vane. “Two hundred and twelve in Masmark now. More every day.”

Ice looked at it for a long time. At the thousand chairs and the wall of names and the weather under the roof.

“It’s very big,” he said.

“It’s very big,” agreed Vane. “I do the numbers for all of it.” He paused. “Aurelie does everything else.”

“Do you like it?”

Vane thought about it. Properly, the way he’d always thought about things, slowly, before he said them.

“I like the numbers,” he said. “I liked the twenty better.”

Ice looked at him.

“Twenty people,” said Vane, “and a war against everybody. And a GM who logged off at three in the morning and left me the numbers.” He was looking at the hall, at the thousand chairs. “I knew every one of the twenty. What they were bad at. Who’d stand next to who.” He paused. “I don’t know everybody here. I can’t. Nobody could. Aurelie does, nearly. She’s better than me.” He looked at Ice. “I liked the twenty better.”

Ice didn’t say anything.

He thought about sixty chairs at a long table in a round town. Under not round, on Sundays. A chair by the north door with an old woman asleep in it.

“Sixty,” he said.

“Sixty,” said Vane. “I know. I’ve seen the Roll. Harlan brought a copy.” He looked back at the hall. “That’s a good number.”

“It’s Kei’s number.”

“It’s a good number anyway,” said Vane.

He got a room that night on the north-west harbour, near the fish man. Small, over a boatbuilder’s, with a window onto the lake. He sat on the bed with the window open and the lake black and still below and the city’s lights all the way round it. He read the letters that had come.

He looked out of the window at the lake for a long time.

Then he took the arrow out of the strap of his pack, where it had been for fourteen days and one, and held it up on his palm by the open window. Over the black water, with the lights of Masmark all the way round.

It leaned. North and west, up over the crater wall, the way he’d come. Toward a grey thumb on a horizon he couldn’t see, and a round town, and sixty chairs. And a cold place at the end of a tunnel, where somebody sat two paces from where he usually sat, and called the dark for him.
