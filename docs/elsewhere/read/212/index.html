Live from Elsewhere · Volume 8: Somewhere Else

Chapter 212

Two N's

3,319 words · 14 min read

Chernn dipped candles in the corner of the Hart’s kitchen, and Kotori let her, because Kotori said two things that needed heat could share a stove.

She’d been doing it since January. A pot of wax on the back of the Hart’s big black range, beside Kotori’s kettles. A frame of wicks Joss had made her out of old iron, the size of a tea tray. A box of beeswax from Bee and tallow from the butcher on the east road. And every morning before the first dawn, while the round town was still dark, she’d stand in the corner of the Hart’s kitchen and dip.

Down, and up. Down, and up. Let it cool. Down, and up.

Mother Dipp had said it was like breathing. Chernn hadn’t believed her. It was like breathing.

She held the frame up to the window and looked at the candles. Forty of them, hanging in a row like a set of small white fingers. Straight. She’d got them straight in February. Mother Dipp had written back, when Chernn sent her one in a letter-parcel, Hm. Honest AND straight. Don’t get above yourself.

“Those are good,” said Kotori, from the kettles.

“They’re for Isa,” said Chernn.

Kotori looked at her.

“She doesn’t sleep much,” said Chernn. “At night. She sits in the chair and looks at the jar. And the jar’s light, but it’s small.” She took the candles off the frame one by one and laid them in the box. “I thought she’d like more light. While she waits.”

Kotori didn’t say anything for a while. She poured hot water over leaves and watched them open.

“What’s she waiting for?” she said.

Chernn thought about it.

“I don’t know,” she said. “I don’t think she does either. I think she’s just used to it.”

She took the candles to the banner room at the first dawn.

The room was nearly empty. It always was at first dawn. Ama Corrie at the far end with her broom, doing the Sunday chairs on a Tuesday because she felt like it. Ice asleep in the first of the nine with his feet on the rung and a cup on the table. Hyvred asleep beside him with her head on the table on her arms, holding his hand across it, both of them asleep. They’d come home yesterday, fourteen days from Masmark, ten of them. They’d been asleep, Kotori said, more or less ever since.

And at the north end, by the north door, in her chair, Isa Corrie. Awake. With the jar on the table in front of her and her bright black eyes on it.

She looked up when Chernn came in with the box.

“Tallow Green,” said Isa.

Chernn stopped.

She’d never told Isa where she was from. In the game. She’d never told anybody much. She’d come in through the south gate in December with Deston and two hundred candles and a stick, and Wendy had asked her who’d stand next to her, and she’d said him. And that had been that. People knew she was Deston’s. They knew she made candles. Nobody had asked where.

“How do you know?” said Chernn.

“The smell,” said Isa. “You smell of Tallow Green. Warm wax. My grandmother’s grandmother walked through it, going north. She said every window had a candle in it.” She looked at the box. “And one more for the road.”

Chernn stood in the middle of the banner room with the box of candles.

“On the longest night,” she said. “A candle in every window. And one more for the road.” She’d learned it on her first day, on a green, with a goose. “Mother Dipp said it.”

“Mother Dipp,” said Isa. “Is that still the name?”

“I don’t know if it’s the same one.”

“It’s always the same one,” said Isa. “In Tallow Green. There’s always a Mother Dipp.” She held out her root hands. “Show me.”

Chernn put the box down on the table in front of her, beside the jar. She took out a candle and gave it to her.

Isa turned it over in her fingers. She held it up to the light of the jar and looked down it, the way Malcolm looked down his stick, and Mother Dipp looked down a candle she was pleased with.

“Honest,” said Isa.

“And straight,” said Chernn. “As of February.”

“Hm.” Isa put it down. “Where’s the other one?”

Chernn looked at her.

“The one for the road,” said Isa. “She’ll have given you one. She always does. Tall, white, plain. The best she’s ever made.” She looked at Chernn with her bright black eyes. “She’ll have said whichever one needs it.”

She had it. Of course she had it.

She’d carried it since December, in her pack, wrapped in a bit of cloth from Mother Dipp’s apron. The tall white candle, plain, straight. The best Mother Dipp had ever made. And one more for the road. Which road? Whichever one needs it. That’s always been the way.

She’d thought about it on the longest night, at the windows, in Deston’s three, at Lv 9, with every window in the round town holding one of Mother Dipp’s candles. And she’d thought that’s the windows. But not the road. Not yet.

She’d thought about it at Chinese New Year, under Jared’s roof of lanterns. And at the gate yesterday, when the ten came home and Ice said everybody, today, and her, every day. She’d thought about it a lot.

She’d never known which road.

She took it out of her pack now and unwrapped it from the bit of apron and put it on the table in front of Isa. Tall, white, plain, straight. It looked like nothing next to the jar.

Isa looked at it for a long time.

“North,” she said.

Chernn looked at her.

“The boy with the jar,” said Isa. “He’s going to carry it back. To the hill. Some year.” She looked at the jar, glowing faintly through the clay. “Not this year. But some year. Before the longest night. He said so, on that stool, on Saturday.” She looked at the candle. “It’s dark out there. Past the last stone. Past where the lamps go. All the way to the hill. You walk a long way in the dark, carrying a light you mustn’t let go out. And there’s nothing to see by but the light you’re carrying. And you can’t use that. You mustn’t.” She was quiet. “My mother carried a candle. For the road. Every time she walked to the edge to look. So she could see her feet without using the lamp.”

Chernn didn’t say anything for a long time.

“So he’d need one,” she said.

“So he’d need one,” said Isa.

She found Suzu at the Hart that afternoon, at the corner table, with Kotori and a pot.

He looked up when she came in. He didn’t say anything. He never said much. Deston said he’d known him for years and Suzu had said maybe four hundred words to him in all that time, and every one of them had been worth it.

She put the candle down on the table in front of him.

He looked at it.

“Mother Dipp made it,” said Chernn. “In Tallow Green. The best one she’s ever made. She gave it to me when I left. She said one more for the road.” She took a breath. “I said which road. She said whichever one needs it.”

Suzu looked at the candle for a long time.

“Isa says it’s the north one,” said Chernn. “Yours. When you carry the light back to the hill. Some year.” She pushed it an inch closer to him across the table. “So you can see your feet. Without using the lamp.”

Suzu didn’t say anything.

He looked at the candle, and then at the jar, which he’d brought with him from Isa’s table because he always took it when he went out. Then at Kotori across the table, who’d put her cup down and was looking at him. Then at Chernn.

“Not this year,” he said.

“I know,” said Chernn. “Keep it till then.”

He picked it up. Tall, white, plain, straight. He held it in both hands the way he held the jar. Carefully. Like somebody carrying a light.

“Thank you,” said Suzu.

It was, Deston told her later, about the third time he’d ever heard Suzu say it.

She hit twenty on Thursday, on the high fells, with Deston singing.

She’d been nineteen and three-quarters for a week. The last bit before twenty was slow, everybody said; the game made you work for it. She’d gone up onto the high fells every day with Deston and a party from Moonlight Harvest. Gabe at the front, shouting about a phone game. Rachael behind, deciding who was helped first. And she’d hit the small angry white hares with her stick, and the bigger things with a sword Salty had made her before he went to Masmark. She’d stayed in time.

Deston always sang. Accord. Let the many move as one. It was meant for twenty people on a bridge, he said, or a line of sixty on the longest night. He’d used it on her for four months, on a chalk hill and a long road and the high fells. Just the two of them, mostly, like humming along with somebody in a kitchen. And every time something came at her, she’d known where it would be. Before it got there.

She’d asked him once, on the road, was that you?

It puts you in time with me, he’d said, and gone red.

On Thursday, at the second dusk, a Scree-Runner came down the slope at her. Small, the kind you’d take on your own at nineteen. And she knew where it would be, because Deston was singing, and she went there, and it was there. She hit it with Salty’s sword. Quite hard, for her.

She stood on the high fell in the wind and looked at it.

Deston had stopped singing.

“Twenty,” he said.

“Twenty.”

“You can sign the Roll,” said Deston. He said it very carefully, as if it might break. “At twenty. Kei’s rule. You can sign it. If you want.”

She looked at him. Six foot whatever of him, on a fell, in the wind, red to the ears. The man who’d walked five days to a candle village to tell her you’re slow.

“I want,” said Chernn.

Ama Corrie opened the Roll for her on the counter at the third dawn.

Everybody was there. They always were, for a signing. She’d watched them on streams. The nine, and Riverbank’s eight, last July. Thea’s six. Hana’s five, with Milli signing so close under Hana that the tails of his letters touched the tops of hers. Everybody in the round town came out into the Hall to watch, round the counter, in the light from the eye. And Isa in her chair, carried out by Tiger and Maigo, one on each side, very carefully.

The Roll was old. Not the oldest one. The oldest one was on the shelf behind the counter, the black book from the ruined hall, with Isa’s mothers in the back of it. This was the round town’s own. Ama Corrie’s. Heavy, worn soft at the corners, with sixty names in it in sixty hands. Kei’s at the top. Then all the others, down and down, page after page.

Ama Corrie turned to the last page.

Then she stopped. She turned back. Further. Back through the pages, past Hana’s five, past Thea’s six. To a page in the middle, last July. Moonlight Harvest’s page. Nine names, in nine hands.

And under Kai, a space.

Not a big one. A line. Just one line, empty, under Kai, before the next name. In a book where every other line was full.

Chernn looked at it.

“I kept it,” said Ama Corrie.

Nobody in the Hall said anything.

“The night the nine signed,” said Ama Corrie. “Last July. The tall one with the book. Kai. He signed, and then he looked at the line under his name for a long time, and didn’t say anything. And moved over, so the next one signed one line down.” She looked at Deston, standing behind Chernn, very still. “I didn’t know why. I don’t ask why. I just didn’t let anybody else write on it.” She sniffed. “The old book does that. Pairs. When two came who were going to be one, one day. They signed one under the other. Close. So they’d always be on the same page.”

Deston had gone completely red.

Chernn didn’t turn round to look at him. She didn’t need to. She could feel it from here, the red, coming off him like heat off Kotori’s range.

She looked at the space under Kai for a long time.

Then Ama Corrie held out the pen.

She wrote it small. In the space under his name, on Moonlight Harvest’s page, last July. Close. So close that the tails of her letters touched the bottom of his.

Chernn.

Two n’s.

Ama Corrie looked at it over her spectacles.

“Two n’s,” she said.

“Because Chern was taken,” said Chernn.

Ama Corrie looked at her for a long moment. Then she nodded, once.

“Good reason,” she said. And then, very quietly, the thing she said when something was kept: “Hm.”

The Hall went off.

Not like a firework. Not like the gate in January. Softer. Like the sound the round market had made under Jared’s lanterns when the wind dropped. Sixty people and a stool, all at once, letting out a breath. Gabe, of course, loudest, saying DEST, DEST, DEST. Hana crying. Leaka screaming into Curse’s sleeve. Rachael saying finally to nobody. And Ice, at the side, out of the way, near the stair. Holding Hyvred’s hand. His face doing the thing it had apparently been doing since the gate. Chernn had seen it on Tif’s clip. She hadn’t believed it until now.

Bee was already at the banner on its hooks under Ama Corrie’s stair. The first banner, the real one, the Seven Below’s. With a brush and a pot of gold. She painted a new dot, small, on the deep blue. And then a silver line from it, very short, to the dot that was Kai.

Sixty-one.

A letter came while Bee was still painting. To Deston, from Masmark. He read it out, because he couldn’t not, red to the ears.

Nobody said anything.

Chernn looked at the banner on its hooks, with Bee’s new dot drying on it. Then at the old black Roll on the shelf behind the counter, Isa’s, with three hundred years of mothers in the back of it. Then at Isa herself, in her chair, carried out by Salty, looking at Chernn with her bright black eyes.

“It answered,” said Isa.

He told her about the door on the steps afterward, in the evening, when most of the Hall had gone back into the banner room.

They were sitting on the south steps, the two of them, with the round market below and the fountain going. Kite in the gate. He’d been quiet since the signing. Deston, who liked talking to people and could talk to anyone, quiet for a whole hour.

“Vivian messaged,” he said, at last. “This morning. While I was in the egg.”

Chernn looked at him.

“The state said yes,” said Deston. “The consent. For the unit. Across the corridor from Dec’s.” He looked at the fountain. “She’d asked them to make it three months. They made it three. She said I asked twice. They noticed.”

“Vivian,” said Chernn.

“Vivian,” agreed Deston.

She thought about it. She’d seen the photos. A white empty room with nobody’s smell in it and a balcony door gone grey with dust, Malacca beyond it. A drift of old post on a corridor floor, picked up. A pot by a door with a very small cactus in it.

“A door,” she said.

“A door,” said Deston. “Opposite his. Where I can go home and still be there.” He didn’t look at her. He was looking very hard at the fountain. “And it’s— it’s got two keys. The lawyer said. They always give you two.”

She looked at him. At the side of his face, red, in the evening light. The man who’d walked five days to tell her she was slow, and held a frame of forty wicks for three days for a woman with floury arms, and put her in time with him on a chalk hill. Who’d left a line empty under his name on a page last July, and never said why.

She knew what he wasn’t saying. She’d known for a while.

“Ask me properly,” said Chernn.

Deston turned his head and looked at her.

“One day,” said Chernn. “Not today. Not on the steps. Not because there’s a door.” She looked at the fountain too. “One day. Properly. And I’ll answer.”

Deston didn’t say anything for a long time.

Then he laughed. Softly, on the steps, with his face still red. The way she’d only ever heard him laugh at her, on a green, in a candle village, when she’d held up a crooked candle.

“I will,” he said.

“I know,” said Chernn. “You always do.”

She took his hand. On the south steps of the Hall of Roads, in the round town, at Lv 20, with her name on a page from last July, in a space that had been kept for her. And she hadn’t known it.

Down in the gate, Kite looked up at them. Then away, at the road, very carefully, the way everybody in Constellation looked at the harbour.
