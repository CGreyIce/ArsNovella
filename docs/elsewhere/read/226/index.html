Live from Elsewhere · Volume 9: Properly

Chapter 226

A Bit

3,408 words · 15 min read

Hyvred had said six weeks, and it was nine, and she was furious about it.

“Nine,” she said, at the window of the room over Abel’s, at the first dawn on Monday. “I said six. I said. On the bridge, in May. People like him think a bit is about six weeks.” She glared out across the harbour at the north terrace, where the bridge over the third stream was, too far to see properly even with her eyes. “It’s been nine.”

“You were close,” said the swamp guy, from the chair.

“I was wrong.”

“Three weeks wrong.”

“That’s half again,” said Hyvred. “That’s fifty per cent wrong. I’m never fifty per cent wrong. I can see the whole harbour.”

He didn’t say anything. He was drinking his tea. But she saw his face do the thing at the corner of his mouth, and she turned round and threw a cushion at him. He caught it without looking, which made it worse.

She’d been different since Malacca. She knew she had. Bee said so, on their call, every night. You’re different. Not worse. Different.

She didn’t talk about it. She’d told Bee everything, of course, the night she got back, on the call, for three hours. But nobody else. Not Thea, not Xola, not Gigi. Not even Winter, who’d let her practise me on a riverbank until it was short enough. It was hers. It was the one thing in her life she’d done entirely on her own, from the first message to Gabe at eleven at night to the knock on the door, and she wanted to keep it that way.

But she thought about it all the time.

The bus window. Both sides of the road at once. All those trees at the edges that nobody had ever told her about. The lift, all four walls and the ceiling and the scratched metal and the little mirror. The long corridor, both ends of it, every door. The door at the end.

His face, when it opened.

She’d seen faces her whole life down a tunnel. One thing at a time, in a small round window in the middle of everything dark. She’d learned to turn her head a lot. She’d learned to listen. In here, in the game, she’d seen everything at once for the first time, edge to edge, and it had been like being born. Then the surgery, a year ago, and the edges out there too, wobbly at first, then less.

But she’d never seen him out there. Not once. Not his face, not the whole of him, standing in a doorway at the end of a corridor. With a cup of tea, and his feet, and both ends of the corridor, all of it at the same time.

She’d seen it now. And he’d stood there and let her look.

Okay. Look.

She thought about that most of all.

In here, nothing had changed. That was the thing. She’d thought it might. She’d worried about it on the bus going home. That she’d go back into the game on the Monday and he’d be different, or she would. That something would have been used up.

Nothing had. She came in a minute before him on the Monday, the way she always did, and sat on the windowsill. He came out of the dark left hand first, the way he always did, and looked at her.

“Hi,” she said.

“Hi.”

“Your corridor’s very long.”

“Yes.”

“Your auntie gave Chernn kueh.”

“She gives everybody kueh.”

“She didn’t give me kueh,” said Hyvred.

He looked at her for a long time.

“Next time,” he said.

She’d gone so red she’d had to look out of the window for a full minute.

The red cloths came down that week, one by one.

Marrok kept his word. That surprised everybody but Wendy. His clerks came down the lane on the Monday with their papers and their purses and went in and out of twelve doors, selling back twelve leases to the people who lived in them. For what he’d paid. Not a copper more.

The rope-walk was first. Hyvred watched from the window over Abel’s. The old man came out of his long low shed with his grandson behind him and stood on the step and looked at the red cloth on the latch for a long time. Then he untied it. Very carefully, the way you’d untie something you might want later.

He didn’t throw it away. He walked down the lane with it, past the netlofts, to the netloft at the end. Wendy was in the door. Hyvred couldn’t hear what he said. But she saw him give Wendy the red cloth, folded small. And Wendy took it, and looked at it, and then turned round and went inside.

When Hyvred went down to the netloft that evening, it was on the hook. The second hook, the one under the banner, beside the coil of rope. A strip of dark red cloth, folded, tied round the iron.

“He said for the gate,” said Wendy. “He said he didn’t know why.”

“I know why,” said Hyvred.

Wendy looked at her.

“So you remember,” said Hyvred. “How long it was in the way. Like Deston’s sticker.” She looked at it on the hook. “Somebody frames a sticker, somebody hangs a cloth. It’s the same thing.”

Sable Court trained on the bridge every evening. All of July.

She could see them from the window over Abel’s, if she leaned out. The north terrace was a long way off across the harbour, but her eyes were her eyes. The bridge over the third stream, old stone, with the water going under it white. And on it, twenty people in black with silver at the cuffs, fighting each other.

Not fighting, exactly. Training. Two at a time, on the bridge, while the others stood at either end and watched. Over and over, every evening, from the second dusk until the third dawn.

And always, every evening, one of the two was Lucan.

She watched them for a week before she understood what she was watching. Then she understood it all at once, on a Wednesday, with her elbows on the windowsill and her chin in her hands.

The one fighting Lucan was always doing the same thing.

Coming forward. All the time. Every beat. No rests. Sword, step, sword, step, never stopping, never waiting, like rain on a tin roof. And Lucan, in front of it, moving. Always moving, the way he did, like water over stones. Going round it. Through it. Learning where the gaps would be when there weren’t any.

Every evening. A different one of the twenty every time, so he’d never get used to any one of them. All doing the same thing at him.

The cellar.

The thing Val had written down with her pencil going through the paper. The thing nobody had seen but Riverbank and Kestrel and her. Fifty-one seconds with no rests in it at all.

They were doing it at him. All of July. So he’d be ready.

“I know,” said the swamp guy, when she told him.

They were on the harbour wall, at the second dusk, with fish. He was looking at the lake. She’d told him all of it, very fast, the way she told things when she was frightened: the bridge, the twenty, the rain on the tin roof, every evening, all of July.

“You know?”

“I’ve been watching.”

“From where?”

“The north terrace,” he said. “On the way back from the Undercliffs. I go past the bridge.” He ate some fish. “I stop for a bit.”

She stared at him.

“You’ve been watching him train,” she said. “For the fight. Against you. Every night. And you didn’t say.”

“You didn’t ask.”

She hit him.

He let her. He always let her. Then he looked at her with his face doing nothing at all, the way it did when something mattered.

“He’s good,” he said. “He’s very good. He’s better at it every night.” He looked back at the lake. “By the end of the month he’ll be able to stand in front of it. The thing from the cellar. He’ll find the gaps that aren’t there.”

“And then what?”

He didn’t answer.

The letter came on the Saturday morning.

Not to her. To him. But she was on the windowsill when it came, and he read it out loud without being asked. He did that now. Since the gate. He read her things.

He read it twice. Then he looked at her.

“Bring her,” said Hyvred.

“He said then she can watch,” said the swamp guy. “At the bottom of the lake. On the shingle. When I said Hyv.” He folded the letter. “He meant it.”

“I don’t like him.”

“I know.”

“I don’t want to watch.”

He didn’t say anything for a long time. He sat in the chair by the window in the room over Abel’s with the letter in his hand and looked at her. Not looking away.

“You don’t have to,” he said.

She knew he meant it. That was the worst part. He’d have gone on his own. He’d have stood on that bridge in front of half of Masmark and fought the best mover in the world. And she could have stayed in the window over Abel’s, or the netloft, or not come into the game at all, and he’d never once have said anything about it.

He never tells anybody what to do. She’d said that to Wendy, on the step, with the red cloths up the lane. He just stands where he is and lets them decide.

“I’m coming,” said Hyvred.

“Okay.”

“I’m still not watching.”

“Okay,” he said.

Half of Masmark was on the north terrace by the second dusk.

She’d known it would be. Lucan had written to him, but Lucan didn’t do things quietly. Everybody knew. The terrace above the bridge, the bank on both sides, the streets coming down off the rim, the roofs. Thousands of people. Masmark, the biggest city in the world, come to a bridge over a stream to watch two people talk.

And on the bridge, in the middle, on his own: Lucan.

Not moving.

She’d never seen him not move. Nobody had, except on the shingle at the bottom of the steps at dawn in March, sitting with Drew, talking about feet. He stood in the middle of the bridge with his hands at his sides. In black, with the silver at the cuffs. The water going under him white and loud. And every part of him perfectly still.

Sable Court stood at the east end of the bridge in a line, twenty of them, in black. At the west end, nobody.

The swamp guy walked onto the west end of the bridge, with her beside him, and stopped.

The terrace went quiet. All of it. Thousands of people. She could hear the water.

Lucan looked at him for a long time.

Twenty paces of old stone bridge between them. The light going gold, then red, off the lake. Behind the swamp guy, on the bank, she could see Constellation without turning her head. All twenty-two of them, at the front of the crowd. Kei with his map. Wendy with her hands out of her pockets. Salty with his black sword. Xav with his sketchbook shut, which meant he wasn’t going to draw this. He was going to watch it.

And further back, on the steps coming down off the rim: deep blue. Meridian. A small woman at their front. A tall still man beside her.

Further still, grey. VANTA. Kestrel at the parapet of the terrace above, leaning on her arms.

“Tank,” said Lucan.

His voice carried. The water was loud, but it carried.

“Who’d stand next to you?”

The swamp guy looked at him.

“Hyv,” he said.

Lucan looked at her.

She hadn’t expected that. She’d thought he’d look at the swamp guy, the way everybody did. She’d thought she’d stand there and be looked past, the way she’d been looked past her whole life by people who were looking at something in front of her she couldn’t see.

He looked at her. Properly. Across twenty paces of bridge. The way he’d looked at Kestrel on this bridge in March, when she said only one.

“Hyvred,” said Lucan.

She didn’t say anything. She couldn’t.

“You don’t like me,” said Lucan.

“No,” said Hyvred.

“That’s fair,” said Lucan. He was still not moving. “I’m going to ask him to fight me. Properly. On this bridge. In a week.” He paused. “And he’ll say yes, because I asked properly, and he always does. But you’re the one who’d stand next to him.” He looked at her. “So I’m asking you first.”

The terrace was so quiet she could hear somebody breathing on the bank behind her.

“Will you watch?” said Lucan.

She thought about it.

She thought about the cellar, and fifty-one seconds, and I couldn’t call it. About a harbour wall and before. About a guild of twenty all at the front, and nobody beside, and it was lonely. About a bridge in the dark in July, from a window over Abel’s, and twenty people in black taking it in turns to do the thing from the cellar at one man, every evening, so he’d be ready.

She thought about a door at the end of a corridor, opening. Okay. Look.

“I don’t like you,” said Hyvred.

“I know.”

“I think you’re going to hurt him.”

“I’m going to try,” said Lucan. “As hard as I can. For as long as I can.” He didn’t look away. “That’s what properly means.”

“I know what properly means,” said Hyvred. Her voice was doing something. She let it. “I asked him properly. At a gate. In January.” She swallowed. “I’ll watch.”

Lucan looked at her for a long moment.

Then he did something she didn’t expect at all. He put his hand on his chest, flat, over the black and the silver. And bowed. Not much. Just his head. To her.

“Thank you,” he said.

And then he looked at the swamp guy.

“Fight me,” said Lucan. “Properly. On this bridge. A week from tonight. At the second dusk.”

“Yes,” said the swamp guy.

That was all. Yes. Not okay. Not if you want. Not ask me after. Just yes, the way he’d said yes to Xav for nine years, every time.

The terrace let out its breath. Thousands of people, all at once. It went across the water like the bell had.

Lucan’s weight shifted. Left foot, right foot. The river coming back. He was moving again, a little, all over, the way he always did.

“I’ve been training,” he said.

“I know,” said the swamp guy. “I’ve been watching.”

Lucan stopped moving again.

“Watching,” he said.

“From the terrace. On the way back from the Undercliffs.” The swamp guy looked at the twenty in black at the east end of the bridge. “Every night. You’ve been doing the cellar at him.”

Lucan stared at him for a very long time.

Then he laughed. Short, out loud, surprised, over the noise of the water, the way he’d laughed in March.

“Of course you have,” he said.

They walked back along the north terrace in the dark, the two of them, with the crowd going away up the streets round them. Talking, all of them. Thousands of people, all talking about the same thing. A week. A bridge. A tank and a river.

She didn’t say anything for a long time. She held his hand. She looked at everything, edge to edge, the way she did. The crowd and the lamps and the lake and the bridge going away behind them in the dark, with twenty people in black still standing at the east end of it.

“Will you do the cellar?” she said at last.

“He’s ready for the cellar.”

“So you won’t.”

“I didn’t say that,” said the swamp guy.

She looked at him.

“You said you’d never needed a third,” said Hyvred. “In the cellar. Xav asked how many you’d got and you said I don’t know, I’ve never needed a third.”

“I’ve never fought Lucan properly.”

“You fought him on a bridge. In the wind game. You said.”

“Not properly,” said the swamp guy.

She stopped walking. In the middle of the north terrace, with the crowd going round them.

“Don’t stop waiting,” she said. “You promised. On the harbour wall.”

“I won’t,” he said. “Not with you.” He looked at her, in the dark, on the terrace, with his face doing nothing at all. “But I’m not going to wait for him.”

She didn’t sleep much that night. Out there, in her room, in Singapore, with all four corners of the ceiling.

On the desk, under the lamp, the note was still there. It had been there since the spring before last. ICE — 3,661 + one lesson. She’d stopped taking things off it after the gate. It had stopped being a number. It was just a thing on her desk now, in her own capitals, like Deston’s sticker or the old man’s red cloth. So she’d remember how long.

She looked at it for a long time.

Then she picked up a pen and wrote underneath it, very small. Not a number.

+ one bridge.

She looked at that for a while. Then she turned the lamp off and lay down and looked at the ceiling in the dark, all four corners. And thought about a door opening at the end of a corridor. About a man standing in it, letting her look.

I’ll watch, she’d said.

She would.
