The Captain

3,340 words · 15 min read

He couldn’t go and ask.

That was the first thing, and he said so, out loud, across the whole of Fernwick in the dark. Hollis was on the long bridge to the causeway, at the other end of the town. Ice was on a plank on the narrows, three hundred paces out along the north walk, with the old fen in front of him full of long dark shapes coming up out of the water. More of them now than before midnight. Bigger. If he left the narrows, the north walk went dark, and Sedge’s steps went dark after it, and then the town.

“I can’t leave it!” he shouted, over his shoulder.

There was a pause, across the water.

“I know you can’t, you great fool!” Hollis shouted back. “Why d’you think I said come and ask? I wanted to see if you’d try!”

He’d passed, apparently. He didn’t find out how until later.

The first hour after midnight was the worst of the night so far, and he’d find out later it wasn’t close to the worst of the night.

They came bigger. That was the thing that changed when the dawn went out. Before midnight the Unlit in the old fen had been tall and thin, heron-shaped, level twelve, and they’d come apart into black water at one good cut. After midnight the water bulged wider and slower. The things that stood up out of it were taller than the round houses’ eaves, with longer necks and heavier heads, and they didn’t come apart at one cut. They took two. Three. They leaned on Steadfast like a wall coming down.

He held. He didn’t know what else to do. He held the narrows, and turned what came, and cut on the beat. Deston’s count came out across the water from the Landing, steady, one and two and, and Ice put his back foot on the new pale plank and stood on it.

Kotori on her post, always somewhere behind and to the right, healing him before the cold had finished arriving. She was drinking, now, between heals. He’d given her ten of the Good draughts at half past eleven, the ones with moonbell in them, that he’d never sold to anybody. For the long bit, he’d said. She’d held them as if they were made of glass.

“Sorry,” she said, from the dark, every few minutes. “That one was late.”

“It wasn’t.”

“Sorry.”

Suzu in the water to his left. Xav behind whatever was looking at him. The north walk behind him, with its lanterns, fewer than there had been. And beyond them, the lit round houses of Fernwick on their stilts, and Sedge’s porch at the end of the walk with one old clay lamp burning on it, very small, very steady.

[ Fernwick · Lights: 312 ]

At ten to one, there was a lull.

It came the way the lulls came all night, without warning. The water in the old fen stopped bulging. The long dark shapes that were already up stood where they were in the shallows, heads down, swaying very slightly, like reeds. They didn’t come on. Waiting, or resting, or something else. Nobody knew. The fen went quiet except for Deston’s count, which didn’t stop. Deston never stopped.

Ice stood on the plank with his shield up and breathed.

Behind him, on the north walk, there were footsteps.

Slow ones. Old ones. Boots on boards, coming out from town. One of them landed a little heavier than the other. He’d have known the walk anywhere: a man who’d been hurt once, a long time ago, and had walked carefully ever since.

He didn’t turn round. He couldn’t. But he heard the footsteps stop, about three paces behind him, and he heard a man let himself down slowly onto the boardwalk with a grunt, and sit.

“Bridge?” said Ice.

“Bridge is fine.” Hollis’s voice, behind him, low and a bit hoarse from shouting. “Your daggers lad’s on it with my men. And the scythe. Told them I was going for a walk.” A pause. “They’re not coming at the bridge in the lull. Never have.”

“You said that like you know.”

“I do know.”

Ice stood with his shield up, facing the old fen, with an old man sitting on the boardwalk behind him in the dark.

“You’ve done this before,” he said.

“Twice.” Hollis’s voice didn’t change. “Once as a boy, in the town I grew up in, up in the hills. I was nine. My father kept the gate.” Another pause. “And once at a ford.”

The fen was very quiet. Out in the water, the long dark shapes swayed.

“You wanted to ask,” said Hollis. “So. Ask.”

“What was his name?” said Ice.

He didn’t know why that was the question. He’d thought about the other questions, all week. How did he hold it. How did he break them. But it was the name he asked.

Behind him, Hollis was quiet for a long time.

“Osric Fell,” he said.

He said it the way you’d put something down on a table that you’d been carrying a long way.

“Captain Osric Fell, of the Fen Militia, which was what they called us, because most of us were from round here. Nineteen of us. He was forty-one. I was seventeen.” The boards creaked as he shifted. “He was a big man. Not tall. Big. Like a door. And stubborn, like I told you. Stubborn as a stone in a river. He didn’t talk much. When he did, you listened, because it was usually the only thing that needed saying.”

Chat went quiet.

“There was a war in the hills,” said Hollis. “Before your lot were born. Doesn’t matter what about. They never do, after. There were more of them than us, and they came down out of the hills in the spring, toward the fen. And there was one place they could cross the river without going three days round. A ford. The Long Ford, at Ashwater, with stepping-stones like the ones at Brackenford. Only bigger, and wider, and the river deep and fast on both sides.” The boards creaked. “Fell said, if they cross there, they’re in the fen by night, and the fen’s got nobody in it but farmers. So we went and stood at the ford.”

“Nineteen of you.”

“Nineteen of us. Against a lot more than nineteen.” Hollis was quiet for a moment. “He stood on the middle stone. Like you on the plank. The rest of us on the bank behind him, and on the stones either side, where we could reach. He stood on the middle stone with a shield, and they came across the ford at him, and he held it.”

The water bulged.

Out in the old fen, all at once, the lull was over. The long dark shapes lifted their heads and came on, and the water behind them swelled and opened and more stood up out of it. Ice felt the first one coming before he saw it: the lean, toward the lantern behind him, toward Hollis sitting on the boards. He put his back foot down and Steadfast up.

“One. And two. And—”

It hit. He turned it. The cold went into his arm. He cut on three and it came apart. Another behind it.

Behind him, Hollis didn’t move. He sat on the boardwalk three paces back, in the lantern light, and kept talking. Steady. Low. As if the fight in front of him was weather.

“They came all morning,” said Hollis. “All afternoon. All night. Wave on wave, across the stones, into the water either side when they couldn’t get across the stones. And he held. He turned what came and he cut when it was off-line, the way you do. He never went forward. Never once. Never chased a man back across the ford, however much he wanted to, however good the chance looked. He stood on the middle stone and he held it.”

“On the first morning a man with an axe broke his arm. His shield arm. You could hear it go. I heard it from the bank.” The boards creaked. “He didn’t make a sound. Not one. He changed his grip, and put the shield up with the broken arm, and held. A day and a half. On a broken arm. And he never made a sound.”

Ice took the next one on the shield and turned it. The cold went into him like a river.

He didn’t make a sound.

“Do you know why?” said Hollis.

“Why he didn’t make a sound?”

“Why he didn’t make a sound.”

Ice cut. It came apart. He set his back foot again on the pale plank.

“Because if the front flinches,” he said, “everybody flinches.”

There was a long silence behind him. Long enough that he thought for a moment Hollis had gone.

“Who told you that?” said Hollis. His voice was strange.

“Nobody. I said it to my friend. On the first week. Deston. He asked why I didn’t make a sound at fifty.” He took the next one on the shield. “It just seemed true.”

Hollis didn’t say anything for a while. When he did, his voice was rough.

“That’s what he said. Word for word. On the first night at the ford, to a boy of seventeen who asked him the same thing.” He coughed. “I asked him. I was the boy.”

The second hour after midnight went past in the dark, and Hollis talked through all of it.

He told the whole thing. The first day at Ashwater, and the first night, which was the worst, in the dark, when you couldn’t see them coming across the stones until they were on you. He told how Fell had made them sing, on the bank, so that he’d know they were still there behind him. Nothing clever, old fen songs, the kind you sang while you cut reeds. Not for courage, Fell had said. For counting. If I can hear you, you’re there. How three of the nineteen had gone down in the river on the first night, and not come up. How the second day had come grey and wet. How Fell’s face had gone the colour of old candle wax from the arm, and how he’d held anyway.

And how Hollis had lost two fingers on the second afternoon.

“Caught a blade,” said Hollis, behind him, as if it were nothing. “Meant for his neck, while his shield was low. Came off the stone at him from the side. I was on the next stone and I put my hand up.” The boards creaked. “Didn’t think. You don’t. Two fingers. Didn’t feel them go till after.”

Ice cut, on three, and something came apart, and he didn’t look round. He couldn’t.

“He never thanked me,” said Hollis. “Not once, all his life. He looked at my hand, after, when it was over. He looked at it a long time, and then he looked at me, and he nodded. And that was it. That was the thanks. And it was more than enough.” He breathed out. “He was a stubborn old ass.”

At half past two, Ice was at forty-four per cent and the Unlit were level seventeen. He’d stopped counting how many.

“And the dawn,” he said, between blows. “On the second day. You said he broke them.”

“I said that.”

“How?”

Behind him, Hollis was quiet.

Out in the old fen, something big came up out of the water. Bigger than the others. Ice saw it rise and felt the boardwalk tremble very slightly under his feet, the way you feel a lorry go past.

“You held a day and a half,” said Hollis. “You turned everything they had. Every blow. You never went forward, you never fell back. You stood. And by the dawn of the second day, they’d been leaning on you for so long, so hard, that they’d forgotten how to do anything else.”

The big one came on through the shallows, head down, leaning toward the lantern behind Hollis.

“Every man in that army,” said Hollis, “was leaning on Osric Fell. Pushing. They’d been pushing a day and a half. Their whole weight was on him. On one stone, in the middle of the ford, with the deep water either side and the river running fast underneath. And at dawn, on the second day, they all came at once. Every man they had left. One last push, all their weight together, at one man on one stone.” A pause. “And he didn’t push back.”

The big one reached him. Its long dark head came down on Steadfast like a falling tree, and the cold went through him down to the plank.

He didn’t make a sound. He turned it, barely, and cut, and it didn’t come apart. It leaned harder.

“He stepped back,” said Hollis.

Ice’s back foot was on the plank. He felt the whole weight of the thing on his shield, leaning, wanting the light behind him. Kotori’s warmth came up through him from somewhere behind and to the right.

“Half a step,” said Hollis. “Off the middle stone, onto the next, behind him. And he pulled. With the broken arm. With the shield. Everything they’d put on him for a day and a half, he took it, all of it, and gave it back to them in one go. And it wasn’t a push. It was a pull. He let all that weight come forward, and he went with it, and he dragged it.” His voice dropped. “And the whole front of that army came forward off their feet. Off the stones. Into the river.”

The thing on his shield leaned and leaned.

“There was an undertow,” said Hollis, “below the Long Ford. There always had been. Everyone in the fen knew about it. Still water on top. A current underneath, that took you down and away and didn’t give you back. The river took them. All of them, the whole front. In the time it takes to say it.” A long breath. “And the ones behind saw it, and turned round, and went back up into the hills. And they never came down again.”

The fen was full of the sound of the water, and Deston’s count, and the cold.

“That’s how,” said Hollis. “That’s the bit nobody asks. Everybody wants the day and a half. They want to be the man who held. Nobody wants to know that the holding’s not the point. The holding’s how you get there.” He paused. “The point’s what you do with it, after. All that weight they gave you. All that cold. You don’t just stand there and take it forever. At the right moment, at dawn, you give it back.”

Ice turned the big one.

He didn’t know how. He just did it. He put everything on his back foot and slid the weight off the slope of the shield. Somewhere behind him in the reeds Xav came up, were you busy, and put both daggers into the back of its neck. It came apart, finally, into a wave of black water that broke over the narrows and soaked him to the waist.

He stood there, dripping, at thirty-one per cent, breathing.

Behind him, the boards creaked. Hollis was getting up. Slowly, the way he did everything.

“Here,” he said.

Ice couldn’t turn round. Hollis knew that. A hand came round from behind him, past his shoulder, into the edge of his vision: an old three-fingered hand, holding something out.

It was round. Iron. About the size of his palm, domed in the middle, with a flat rim pierced for rivets. A shield boss. Old and black and pitted, with a long bright scar across the dome where something sharp had hit it once, very hard, and slid off.

“His,” said Hollis. “Off his shield. They took the shield when they buried him, but I kept that. He told me to.” A pause. “He said, give it to the next one who does it like me. I said, what if there isn’t one? And he said, then keep it, you ugly boy, and stop asking stupid questions.”

Ice looked at it.

“I’ve had it thirty years,” said Hollis. “I’d stopped thinking there’d be one.” He held it out a little further. “Then a traveller walked into my yard with a sword and a shield and no idea what he was doing, and did a Falling Cut wrong two hundred times. And then one morning he did it right.”

Ice took it.

It was heavy. Heavier than it looked. It sat in his palm like it had been waiting there. He held it up against the middle of Steadfast, the drowned oak and the choir silver, where the plain round boss was. And without anyone telling him to, he knew what to do. It went on the way things did in here when they belonged: a click, a weight settling, iron finding wood.

[ Steadfast now bears Osric Fell’s boss. ]

And under it, quieter, a window he’d never seen before. Not gold, like the world first. Not red. Grey, like water.

He read it twice. Out in the fen, the water was bulging again.

“Hollis,” he said.

But Hollis was already walking away, back along the north walk toward town, slow and careful, one boot landing heavier than the other.

“Bridge,” he said, over his shoulder. “My lads’ll want me. You’ve got the rest of the night.” He stopped, once, a little way off, and his voice came back across the boards, rough. “He’d have liked you. He wouldn’t have said so. But he would.”

Then he was gone into the lanterns.
