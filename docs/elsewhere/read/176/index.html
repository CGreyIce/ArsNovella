Live from Elsewhere · Volume 7: Keep the Lamps

Chapter 176

Ninety-Four Cages

3,602 words · 16 min read

Ina took one of the old cages down to copy it, and Tiger watched him do it as if he were taking down a child.

“Careful,” said Tiger.

“I’m being careful.”

“It’s three hundred years old.”

“I know,” said Ina. “That’s why I’m being careful.” He had the cage in both hands, lifting it off the top of the iron post at the first lantern ring, very slowly. The wind came across the moor in the grey morning and pulled at his hair. “It’s been on this post since before anyone you’ve ever met was born. It can survive being looked at by a man with one horn for a week.”

“You’ll bring it back.”

“I’ll bring it back,” said Ina. “With ninety-four friends.”

Tiger watched him wrap it in sacking and put it in his pack. He looked at the empty post for a long time. Then he took one of his own lanterns out of his sack, a plain tin one from the market, and lit it, and set it on the post in the old cage’s place.

“Just till you’re back,” he told the post.

Ina didn’t laugh. He’d have been ashamed to laugh. He’d kept Emberly’s great forge alight for one Long Night in December with his mask on, and talked to it the whole time. He knew exactly how Tiger felt.

The old cage was beautiful.

He hadn’t seen it properly at the ring. You didn’t, outside, in the wind, with a lamp burning in it. On Joss’s bench behind the Hart, in the forge-light, with the sacking off, he could see everything. He sat and looked at it for an hour before he touched a hammer.

It was small. Smaller than you’d think, about the size of a man’s head. Iron, gone black and smooth with age, the bars thin and very even. Somebody had drawn every one of them out by hand, the same thickness to a hair. A little iron dish at the bottom for the oil. A ring at the top for hanging. And worked into the top of it, where the bars came together, a star. Five lines, the same as the stars on the waystones and the old hall and the banner’s edge. Not cut in. Forged in. Somebody had bent five bars into a star in the fire, while they were hot, and closed the cage round it.

“That’s not easy,” said Drew, looking over his shoulder.

“No.”

“I couldn’t do that.”

“Nor could I,” said Ina. “Yet.”

But it wasn’t the star he kept looking at. It was the hood.

There was a hood on one side of the cage. A curved plate of iron, like the hood of a cloak, covering about a third of the bars. It was riveted on, very neatly. When the lamp was lit inside, the hood would stop the light going out that way. All the light would go the other way, out through the open bars, in one direction.

Ina turned the cage round on the bench. He thought about how it had been sitting on the post at the ring. Which way the hood had faced. He’d lifted it off carefully. He’d noticed, without noticing, the way you notice things in a forge.

The hood had faced south.

He sat back on Joss’s stool.

“Drew,” he said.

“Mm.”

“The hood’s on the south side.”

Drew looked at the cage. At the hood. Then at Ina.

“So it shines north,” said Drew.

“Only north,” said Ina. “Nobody to the south can see it at all. You’d see a glow, maybe. You’d never see the flame.” He turned the cage again in the forge-light, slowly. “They didn’t want the people behind them to see it. They didn’t want them to look.”

“Why not?”

Ina thought about Maigo, at the long table on Sunday. They face the wrong way. The hollows on the backs of the waystones, facing north. And now the cages on the rings with their hoods to the south. Every light on that road built so only the north could see it.

“Because it wasn’t for them,” said Ina. “It was never for us. It’s for it.”

He knew what a lure was. He’d been one.

That was the thing he didn’t say to anybody. Not to Drew, not to Kei, not to the long table. He said it to himself, at Joss’s anvil, while he drew out the first bar of the first cage, the same thickness to a hair.

On the first Long Night, at Emberly, the great forge had been fed till it roared. Because that was what the town’s old line said. Feed the forge till it roars, and let them come to the hill. He’d been the one feeding it. Eleven hours, with Brannagh beside him saying four words an hour, and the oni mask on his face for the first time. He’d stood on the hill in front of the biggest fire he’d ever seen while the Unlit came up out of the plain toward it in their hundreds. Past the farms. Past the houses with the shutters closed and the children asleep. Up the hill. To him. Because he was standing in front of the light.

He’d had the best night of his life. He’d said so, on Kei’s call, at three in the morning, a bit hysterical. I’m having the best night of my life. It had been true.

He hadn’t understood, until Sunday, that he’d been doing what the old guild did. That Emberly’s forge and Grimmet’s shaft mouths were the same thing. And Selwick lighting the water, and Briarwick’s ditches outside the hedge. Every town on the old list had been given a small plain thing to do on the longest night. And every single one of them was the same thing.

Make a light. Somewhere you can keep it. And let them come to it, and not to the houses.

And the round town was the biggest. Seven roads. Ninety-four lamps on one of them, all facing north, like a finger pointing.

Everything comes here.

He drew out the second bar. And the third. It was the first thing in a long time he hadn’t had to make himself do. He’d said that about the great forge, in December. It was true of this, too. He noticed it, the way you notice the sun’s come out. Then he got on with the fourth bar.

There were six of them at the forge that week, and Joss, and they did it like a line.

Salty drew the iron. He’d been mining for ten months and he could find a seam blind. He went down the north-east tunnel in the mornings and came back with sacks, ordinary iron this time, grey, that shone. Drew and Slep drew the bars, one each side of the anvil, matching. Drew was quicker; Slep was more even. Neither said much. Jun Liang worked the bellows and sang, and nobody stopped him, because when he sang the fire did better. Nobody could explain that either.

Gabe closed them. He was the only one who could do the rivets for the hoods so they held, every time, first time. Because Gabe was an Anvil, and whatever an Anvil forged held. He stood at the end of the line with a little hammer, closing cages, talking the whole time about the duel, or the second heart, or SimpleMMO. He hadn’t stopped talking about SimpleMMO since Harlan.

“Five thousand guilds,” said Gabe, closing a hood. “Five thousand. And I was in one of them, Ina, I was in one of those five thousand, and he was at war with me. And I didn’t know.”

“Did he kill you?”

“I don’t know,” said Gabe, with deep feeling. “I asked him. He said probably.”

And Ina did the stars.

That was the hard part. Nobody else could do it yet, and Ina could only just. You took five bars, hot, and you bent them into a star in the fire, all at once, with the tongs and your hands. Then you closed the top of the cage round it before it cooled. If you were slow, it cooled and cracked. If you were rough, it bent wrong and you had a four-line star, or a smudge. He did his first one on Monday morning and it came out with six points, which wasn’t a star the old guild had ever used.

He did his second on Monday afternoon and it came out like a starfish that had been sat on.

[ Blacksmithing · Star · Grade 38% ]

He did nine more on Monday and none of them was right. He logged out at the second dusk, which was lunchtime for him in the UK, with his hands aching. He wanted very much to say he’d do the rest tomorrow. He knew if he said that he wouldn’t.

He came back at the third dawn, at what was four in the afternoon for him, after a cup of tea and half a packet of biscuits. He did eleven more.

On Tuesday, at the twenty-fourth try, it came right.

He didn’t know why. He’d done it the same way twenty-three times. On the twenty-fourth, something happened in his hands. A little looseness, the way Kei had once told him to feed the fire, nobody’s counting. He stopped trying to bend five bars into a star. He let them go where they were going. And they went into a star, five lines, perfectly even, the same as the old one on the bench beside him.

[ Blacksmithing · Star · Grade 74% · That one. ]

He held it up in the tongs. In the forge-light, against the old cage’s star, it looked very nearly the same.

Joss, at the back in his chair, made a small sound.

Ina looked round.

The old man was sitting forward, with his shaking hands on his knees, looking at the star in the tongs. His eyes were wet.

“My father’s father,” said Joss, “used to say he’d seen one of those made. Once. When he was a boy. An old woman from the north road, the last of somebody. Bent five bars in the fire with her bare hands.” He wiped his eyes. “I never believed him. I thought it was a story.”

“It’s not a story,” said Ina.

“No,” said Joss. “Seems it isn’t.” He sat back. “Do it again, lad. I want to see.”

Ina did it again.

Ice came to the smithy on Wednesday night, late, with a pack of Good draughts.

He didn’t say what they were for. He put them on the end of Joss’s bench beside the old cage, six of them in a row, bitter-green in their little bottles. Then he stood by the forge and watched Ina bend a star.

It came out at sixty-eight. Not that one. Close.

“They’re for the smiths,” said Ice. “Your hands. Kei says you’ve been at it nineteen hours.”

“Twenty,” said Ina. “Gabe counted.” He put the tongs down and flexed his fingers, which hurt in a way the game was very honest about. “You can’t drink a draught for your hands.”

“You can,” said Ice. “It’s just bitter.”

Ina drank one. It was very bitter. His hands stopped hurting.

Ice was looking at the old cage at the end of the bench. At the hood on its south side.

“It’s a lure,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Like Emberly.”

“Like Emberly.” Ina took up the tongs again. “I fed the forge till it roared, last year. On the hill. With the mask on. And they came up out of the plain to me, all night, past every house.” He bent the five bars in the fire. “I told Kei it was the best night of my life. On the call. At three in the morning. I sounded mad.”

“I know,” said Ice. “I watched Kei’s VOD.”

Ina looked at him.

“You didn’t sound mad,” said Ice. “You sounded like somebody standing in front of a light on purpose.” He considered. “I know what that sounds like.”

The star came out of the fire. Ina closed the cage round it before it cooled.

Ice nodded once, the way he did when something was right, and went back out into the night. He left the other five draughts on the bench. In the morning Drew found them and drank one, and Gabe drank two. Jun Liang tried one and sang about it, very sadly, for an hour.

By Thursday they had thirty-one.

They stood along Joss’s bench in rows, black and new, each with its hood and its star. Ina kept the old one at the end of the row, so they could all see it. It looked like a grandmother with her grandchildren lined up for a photograph.

Bee came on Thursday with the oil.

She’d organised it. Of course she had. Bee organised things; it was what she did when she wasn’t drawing. She’d gone round every brewer and chandler and farmhouse in the round town and on the seven roads, and bought, begged or traded for lamp oil. Newish had helped. They were both brewers, and it turned out brewers knew where everybody kept everything. She came into the smithy with a list as long as her arm. Behind her came a cart, driven by Hobb the carter, who’d brought turnips every eighth day for thirty years and was now bringing oil.

“Four hundred and twelve gallons,” said Bee. She was very pleased. “So far. Hobb says there’s more at Wester Ford if we ask nicely.”

“How long does a lamp burn on a gallon?” said Ina.

“I asked Ama Corrie,” said Bee. “She said about two nights.”

“And the Long Night’s how long?”

“A day and a half,” said Bee. “In the game. Thirty-five hours. It’s not a night, really. It’s two and a bit nights stuck together with no day in between.” She looked at the row of cages. “So we need a lot. And we need people to go and fill them. And we need—“ She stopped.

“What?”

“Malcolm says oil’s not enough,” said Bee. “He says the wicks won’t last the whole thing. Not all the way. Not without something else.” She waved her list. “He says he’s working on it. He says it’ll be March.”

“It’s always March,” said Ina.

“That’s what everyone says,” said Bee, and went to talk to Hobb about Wester Ford.

He took the first ones north on Saturday, with Hako and Maigo. The same three. Six cages each in their packs, and a jar of oil each, and wicks.

The first waystone was a mile out of the north door. A plain grey stone, waist high, with the star on its south face and the groove on top pointing north. And on its north face, where nobody ever looked, the round hollow Maigo had found. Cleaned out now, the old moss gone, the black soot crust inside it scraped back to the stone.

Ina took a cage out of his pack. He fitted it into the hollow. It went in as if it had been made for it, which in a way it had: three hundred years ago somebody had made a cage that size for that hole. He’d copied that cage to a hair. The hood faced south, toward the round town. The open bars faced north, toward the moor and the edge and the dark and the gold point coming home.

Maigo poured the oil. Hako set the wick.

Ina took out his short black wand. He held it up to the wick and said the one word under his breath, the word that did it.

“Kindle.”

The wick caught.

A small flame. Yellow. Steady. It burned in the little iron dish inside the new black cage in the hollow of the old grey stone. All of its light went one way, north, out across the moor in a thin bright line. And from where they were standing, on the south side, they could hardly see it at all. Just a warmth on the stone. A faint glow round the edge of the hood.

They stood and looked at it.

“Three hundred years,” said Maigo, at last.

“About that,” said Ina.

“And you can’t even see it,” said Hako. He sounded delighted. “We light the first lamp on the road in three hundred years and you can’t see it from here.” He walked round the stone, to the north side, out into the heather. And stopped, and looked back. “Oh,” he said, in a different voice. “Oh. Come and look.”

They went round.

From the north side, it was everything. The flame in its cage, bright and steady, gold in the grey. The star on top black against it. The light coming straight at you across the heather, the way the gold point came at them from the edge of the world. As if it was saying something. Here. This way. Here.

“It’s for you,” said Hako, softly, not in his documentary voice. “Whoever you are. It’s for you.”

Ina stood in the heather on the north side of the first lamp on the road in three hundred years. He looked at it for a long time. He thought about Emberly, and the great forge, and standing in front of it all night with the mask on while the dark came up the hill. Toward him. Because he was standing in front of the light.

He’d stand in front of this one too, if it came to that. He knew that, quite calmly. He’d stand in front of all ninety-four.

“Right,” said Ina. “Ninety-three more.”

They did twelve that day, and twelve the next. They walked north from stone to stone with Maigo making soup at every ring and Hako narrating everything. “The lesser-spotted Ina, approaching the waystone with caution.” Ina pretended to mind.

At the end of the second day they were at the first lantern ring. Tiger’s tin lantern was still burning on the empty post. Ina took the old cage out of his pack, unwrapped it from its sacking, and lifted it back onto the post where it had been since before anyone was born. Hood to the south. Bars to the north.

He lit it.

Then he took Tiger’s tin lantern down and blew it out, very gently, and put it in his pack to take home.

“Back where you belong,” he told the old cage. “With friends.”

And it was true. Behind them, down the north road, toward the round town, twenty-four new black cages were burning in twenty-four grey stones, one every mile. You couldn’t see any of them from here; they all faced north. But you could feel them. The way you feel somebody standing behind you, holding something up.

Hako lay on his back in the ring and looked at the sky.

“The road of lights,” he said. “Once thought extinct.”

“Conservation efforts,” said Maigo, stirring the soup, “are going really well, actually.”

Ina laughed. Out loud, on a moor, in the dark, with his hands aching and his mask at his shoulder. It surprised him, a bit, how easy it was.
