Live from Elsewhere · Volume 9: Properly

Chapter 219

Two Flames

3,842 words · 17 min read

The forty-third was the first one that didn’t lean.

Deston held it up to the window of the Hart’s kitchen at the third dawn and looked down it. The way Mother Dipp did, and Isa did, and Cherning did now. Closing one eye. Looking down the length of it to see if it stood up straight.

It did. Nearly. Two wicks, side by side, very close, coming out of the top. Neither of them pulling at the other. The wax smooth all the way down, a little thick at the bottom, where he’d lifted it too slow on the last dip. Not good. Not the kind of thing you’d put in a window in Tallow Green. But it stood up.

He’d got that window on the first night, in May. The night he’d written Mother Dipp six pages in very small writing and she’d written back four words and a bundle of wicks. You’ll want the fine ones. He’d been looking at that window every night since. Forty-two nights, forty-two candles, forty-two of them in the scrap bucket beside the range. Crooked, lumpy, thick, thin, one wick drowned, both wicks drowned. One that had somehow come out with the wicks crossed, like a pair of legs.

This one stood up.

Behind him, at the kettles, Kotori had been not saying anything for six weeks. She didn’t say anything now. But he heard her put the kettle down.

“Is that it?” said Kotori, to the kettle.

“I think that’s it.”

Kotori turned round. She looked at the candle in his hand for a long time. Then she looked at his face. And then she did something he’d never seen her do in eighteen months: she put both hands over her mouth and made a small sound and turned back round to the stove very fast.

“Sorry,” said Kotori.

“Don’t.”

“Thank you,” said Kotori, to the stove, much too loudly.

He’d been going to ask her on a Tuesday. He’d decided that in May. He didn’t know why. Tuesdays felt honest.

He’d been going to ask her in the round town. He’d decided that in May too, and then undecided it in June. Not on the steps. Not because there’s a door. She’d said that. She’d meant it. And the round town was all steps, and doors, and the Roll on the counter with her name under his on a page from last July, in a space Ama Corrie had kept.

That was the round town’s. It wasn’t theirs.

He’d thought about it for a week, dipping at night. And on the forty-third night, with a candle in his hand that stood up straight, he knew.

He found her at the first dawn in the banner room, at Moonlight Harvest’s end of the long table, on her chair. Ama Corrie had brought it in April, and nobody had sat on it but her. She was writing a letter. She looked up when he came in.

“Walk with me,” said Deston.

She looked at him.

“To Tallow Green,” said Deston. “Five days. Starting tomorrow.” He’d practised it. He still said it too fast. “If you want. I want to— there’s something I want to— I want to go back. To the green. With you.” He stopped. “If you want.”

Cherning looked at him for a long time.

She knew. He could see it. He’d known her a long time, and she’d never once in all that time been able to hide anything from her face, and she wasn’t hiding this. She knew where he’d been going at the third dawn for six weeks. Isa had smelled it on him. Kotori was terrible at secrets. Mother Dipp had probably written to her too.

She knew. And she wasn’t saying.

She was leaving him a gap. On purpose. So he could go first.

“Okay,” said Cherning.

They left at the first dawn on Wednesday, the two of them, down the south steps.

Kite was in the gate, with his hands at his sides. He looked at them. At Deston with his pack on, and his book, and something in his pack wrapped in a piece of cloth from an apron, very carefully. At Cherning beside him with her stick and her sword and a look on her face that meant she knew exactly what was in the cloth.

“Who’d stand next to you?” said Kite.

“Her,” said Deston.

Kite looked at Cherning.

“Him,” said Cherning.

“Fair,” said Kite. Then, after a moment, very quietly, which Kite never was: “Good luck.”

Deston went red to the ears.

At the bottom of the south road, at the milestone, a woman with two short curved blades across her knees and the bird cut off her collar looked up at them as they passed. She didn’t say anything. But she watched them all the way round the curve, the way Deston would learn later she watched everybody who went down that road. As if she were learning how.

Five days.

He’d walked it once before, in November, alone. Five days south-west off the Millrace road, past the White Hills, to find her on a green with a stick and a goose. He’d thought about what he’d say for five days, and when he got there, he’d said you’re slow.

This time he thought about it for five days too.

He thought about it on the Millrace road, with the river going alongside them, Cherning walking on the river side. He thought about it at the first night’s inn, where the innkeeper’s wife looked at the two of them and gave them the room with the window. He thought about it on the White Hills, the long chalk downs, where the giant figures were cut into the turf. The man with the staff. The horse with too many legs. And the woman with her arms up, white against the green, the one Gabe and Rae and Cal and Dennis had held the night for in May a year ago, when the Scourer came for her.

Cherning stopped below the woman with her arms up and looked at her for a long time.

“Gabe told me about her,” she said. “On a call. He cried.”

“Gabe cries at everything.”

“He said she was the only thing on the hill that looked happy,” said Cherning. She was looking at the arms, the long white arms going up into the sky. “He said all the others were just walking. Or standing. And she was the only one who looked like something had just happened.”

Deston looked at the chalk woman.

He’d never noticed. He’d seen the White Hills a dozen times, on the road, on Gabe’s stream, in Gabe’s long tearful calls. He’d never once noticed that she was the only one who looked like that.

“What happened?” he said.

Cherning thought about it.

“Something good,” she said. “Something she’d been waiting for.” She took his hand. “Come on. Mother Dipp hates it when you’re late.”

He thought about it the whole rest of the White Hills. What he’d say. He’d practised it. He’d written it down, the way he wrote everything down, in his book. Six pages. Like the letter to Mother Dipp. He’d crossed out five of them.

He sang, in the afternoons. Accord. It wasn’t for anything. There was nothing on the road to fight but the small angry hares, and she could take those on her own now. He sang because it put them in time. Because it was like humming along with somebody in a kitchen. And because when he sang, he didn’t have to find the words for anything else.

On the fourth night, at a hedge inn under the last of the White Hills, she said, over the soup:

“You’re quiet.”

“I’m thinking.”

“You’re never quiet,” said Cherning. “You talk to lamp-posts. Dec says.”

“I’m thinking about what to say.”

She looked at him across the soup for a long moment, with the candle on the table between them burning in its holder. One wick. Steady.

“You don’t need much,” she said.

They came over the last hill into Tallow Green on Sunday at the second dusk.

The green was very round and very flat, with the pond on it, and the ring of low cottages with their thatched roofs all the way round. The chalk hill behind, white in the dusk. And every window lit. Little yellow flames in every window, dozens of them, steady in the still evening. The whole place smelling of warm wax.

It hadn’t changed. It never would, he thought. Tallow Green didn’t change. There was always a Mother Dipp.

And the goose.

It was standing by the pond. The same one, or one exactly like it. Looking at them both with deep suspicion.

Cherning stopped at the top of the green and looked at it.

“Oh, no,” she said.

The goose took a step toward them.

“It remembers me,” said Cherning. “It remembers. I hit it with a stick in November and it remembers—“

“Everything here remembers,” said Deston. “Dec says.”

“Ice walked round a goose.”

“Dec is very wise,” said Deston, and Cherning laughed. Out loud, at the top of the green, the way she’d laughed in a doorway in Malacca at a framed sticker. The goose stopped, and considered them. Then it turned round with enormous dignity and walked into the pond.

Mother Dipp came out of her long low cottage at the far side of the green as they came down. Enormous. Floury to the elbows. A voice like a pigeon.

She looked at the two of them.

She looked at Cherning, at Lv 24 now, with a sword Salty had made her and a stick she still carried, because it was how she held things. She looked at Deston, six foot whatever of him, red to the ears already, with his pack on. With something in the pack, wrapped in a piece of her own apron, that she’d sent him the wicks for.

She didn’t say anything at all.

She turned round and went back into her cottage and shut the door. Very loudly. So loudly the candle in her own window jumped.

And all round the green, in every window, behind every little yellow flame, a face appeared. And then went away again very quickly, the way people do when they’re pretending not to look.

Cherning put her face in her hands.

“Everyone knows,” she said, through them.

“Mother Dipp told them,” said Deston. “She shut the door so they’d know not to come out.” He was so red he could feel it in his ears. “It’s— it’s the custom. Isa said. She said in Tallow Green, when somebody’s going to ask, everybody stays inside.”

“Isa told you?”

“Isa tells everybody everything,” said Deston. “Slowly.”

He’d been going to wait for dark. He didn’t.

He couldn’t. He stood on the green at Tallow Green in the last of the second dusk, with the pond and the goose, and every window lit. And every window with somebody in it pretending not to be. Mother Dipp’s door shut. And he found he couldn’t wait another minute, not one, after six weeks of nights in a kitchen and five days on a road.

He took off his pack.

He knelt down on the green, on the grass by the pond, and opened the pack and took out the cloth. He unwrapped it. She was watching him. She hadn’t moved.

The forty-third candle. Two wicks, side by side, close. Standing up.

He held it out.

She took it.

She held it up, the way you did, and looked down it with one eye shut. She looked for a long time. He’d seen her look down a hundred candles like that, in the corner of the Hart’s kitchen, at the first dawn. He’d never once seen her take this long.

“It’s thick at the bottom,” said Cherning.

“I lifted it too slow.”

“It’s a bit lumpy on one side.”

“I know.”

“It’s honest,” said Cherning. Her voice had gone very odd.

He’d brought a light. A coal from the Hart’s range, in a little iron box Joss had made him, that kept a coal for a day. He opened it. The coal was still red. He lit a spill from it, a long thin one, and held it out, and she held the candle, and he lit the first wick.

It caught. Small. Yellow. Steady.

He lit the second.

It caught. Beside the first, very close, the two flames leaning in toward each other a little, the way flames do when they’re close. Not pulling. Just leaning.

Two flames, one candle. On the green at Tallow Green, at dusk, with every window watching.

He’d written six pages. He’d crossed out five. He’d practised the last one on the road for five days, in his head, while he sang.

He didn’t say any of it.

“Cherning,” said Deston. “Will you marry me?”

She didn’t wait for the candle.

Nobody had that long. Isa had said so. The one being asked just says it. Before it’s burned down.

She didn’t even wait for him to finish. He’d got to marry and she was already saying it. Over the top of him, the way she’d said I want on a fell in the wind, when he said you can sign the Roll.

“Yes,” said Cherning.

And then, because he was staring at her and hadn’t moved, and the two flames were going in her hands, she said it again. Louder. So the whole green would hear, through every window, behind every flame.

“Yes.”

All round the green, every face came back to every window. All at once. And nobody pretended any more.

Mother Dipp’s door opened.

She stood in it, enormous, floury, with her arms folded. She looked at the two of them on the green by the pond. At Deston on his knees with an iron coal-box in one hand. At Cherning holding a candle with two flames on it, and crying, and laughing, and doing both, the way she did both.

“Both lit?” said Mother Dipp.

“Both,” said Cherning.

“Hm,” said Mother Dipp. And then, to the whole green, in a voice like a pigeon: “Well? Come out, then.”

They came out. All of them. Out of every cottage round the green, with candles in their hands, because in Tallow Green you didn’t go anywhere at dusk without one. Old men and children and the woman who kept the bees and the man who rendered the tallow. Thirty of them, forty. They came out onto the green and stood round in a ring, not too close. Every one of them with a candle. And they didn’t say anything. They just stood there, holding their lights, round the two of them by the pond.

Deston was still on his knees. He’d forgotten to get up.

Cherning sat down beside him on the grass. With the candle in both hands, the two flames going.

“Now we watch it,” she said. “Isa said. You sit and watch it burn the rest of the way down. Together. Because it’s nice.”

“It’ll take hours.”

“I know,” said Cherning.

So they sat on the green at Tallow Green, the two of them, and watched it burn. The village round them in a ring with their candles. The windows lit. The chalk hill white behind. The goose came out of the pond, after a while. It stood four feet off, looking at them with deep suspicion. And then, to everybody’s surprise, sat down on the grass. As if it, too, had decided this was worth seeing.

“There’s a box,” said Deston, after a long time. When the candle was halfway down, and most of the village had sat down too, on the grass round them, with their own lights.

“In the unit,” said Cherning. “From Singapore. The one you wouldn’t let anybody open.”

He looked at her.

“I saw it,” said Cherning. “On the counter. Taped. You moved it every time I went near it.” She was looking at the two flames. “Gabe kept trying to look in it. You hit his hand.”

“You knew.”

“I thought it might be,” said Cherning.

“It is,” said Deston.

He’d had it since April. Since the week the state said yes. He’d carried it up on the six o’clock bus, in the box from Singapore with other things round it so nobody would know, and put it on the counter of the empty unit, and not opened it. He’d thought about asking her out there first. With it. In the corridor, or by the balcony door with Malacca beyond it.

But she’d said not because there’s a door. And he’d thought about it for weeks, dipping at night, and understood what she’d meant. That out there was where they lived. Where the door was, and the key, and the corridor. And in here was where they’d met. On a green, with a stick and a goose.

He’d wanted to ask her where they’d met. Properly. First.

“Saturday,” said Cherning. “Out there. Open it then.”

“Saturday,” said Deston.

“And Deston.”

“Mm.”

“Don’t make a speech.”

He laughed. On the green, at Tallow Green, softly, with his face red. The way she’d only ever heard him laugh at her.

“I won’t,” he said.

“I know,” said Cherning. “You never do. When it matters.”

The two flames burned down together, all the way to the bottom, a little after the third dawn. Neither of them went out. They got lower, and closer, and at the end there was only a little pool of wax in her hands with two flames in it, side by side. And then they went, both at once, in the same breath of air off the pond.

Mother Dipp took the stub.

She took it out of Cherning’s hands, very gently, still warm. And went across the green to her own long low cottage, to the window by the door, where her candle always burned. And put it on the sill.

Beside the others.

Deston hadn’t noticed them, the first time, in November. He’d never looked at the sill. He looked now.

There were dozens. All along the windowsill, and the next one, and the one after. Small black stubs of candle, each with two burnt wicks sticking out of the top, very close. Some of them so old they’d gone grey, and cracked, and you could hardly tell what they’d been. Some of them new. A row of them, all the way along Mother Dipp’s windows, going back years. Hundreds of years. Every one of them a green at dusk, and a village with its candles, and somebody saying yes before it burned down.

Mother Dipp put theirs at the end of the row. She looked at it for a moment.

“Both,” she said.

And went in, and shut the door, quietly this time.

The letters came while they were still sitting on the green, at the third dawn, with the goose asleep.

Gabe’s was first, of course. It was eleven lines long and mostly capitals. Deston read it out and Cherning laughed until she couldn’t breathe. Then Rachael’s, which said finally. Then Kotori’s, which said thank you and nothing else, and which made Cherning cry again. Then Suzu’s, which said good.

And then, the next evening, in Mother Dipp’s long low room with the vats, one more. From Masmark. From the far side of the world, a day late, because the man who’d written it had spent the night at the top of a bell tower. From a man who’d stood in a corridor while he peeled a sticker off a door and said go on, then.

Deston read it three times.

Then he put his face in his hands, in Mother Dipp’s long low room, with Cherning’s hand on his back and the goose asleep on the green outside the window. And laughed so hard he couldn’t stop. Because it was the thing you said to somebody you were very glad to see, when you couldn’t say the thing itself. He’d said it to Dec, on the south steps, every time Dec came home. He’d said it to Cherning, on that green, the first time.

And now Dec had said it to him.

“What’s funny?” said Cherning.

He showed her.

She read it. She looked at it for a long time.

“He’s right,” she said. “You are slow.”

“I know.”

“It took you forty-three candles.”

“I know,” said Deston.

“It was worth it,” said Cherning, and took his hand.
