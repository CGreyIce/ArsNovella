Live from Elsewhere · Volume 2: The Long Night

Chapter 29

Profit Day

4,053 words · 18 min read

Deston came up on the last bus on Tuesday night with a duffel bag, a box of egg tarts and a face like Christmas.

“I want to meet the pods,” he said, before he’d said hello. Then he dropped the duffel on the pavement outside Melaka Sentral, at twenty to midnight, under the orange lights, and hugged Ice so hard Ice’s back cracked. “Dec. Dec.”

“You saw me last night.”

“I saw your shield. I saw your back. You were kneeling on a broken plank and I was counting at the back of your head.” Deston stood back and held him at arm’s length and looked at him. “You look tired.”

“I am tired.”

“Good,” said Deston. “Me too. Where do I sleep?”

He’d said in November that he’d come up the weekend after finals to meet the pods. Then there’d been a ford, and then a door, and he’d been in Singapore on the other end of a call through all of it. Singing in the shower. Crying on a bubble tea. Counting in the dark at the back of Ice’s head on Monday night while a man with a maul tried to put him through the boardwalk.

Now he was here, in the passenger seat of Ice’s car, with the window down and the egg tarts on his knees, looking at Malacca going past in the dark. The river, the old red buildings, a lit kopitiam with two uncles in it playing chess at midnight. He looked at all of it as if he was trying to remember it for later.

“Has Xav said anything to you?” he said, at the lights by the bridge.

Ice glanced at him. “About what?”

Deston looked out of the window.

“Nothing,” he said. “He’ll tell you. Let him tell you.”

Ice drove on and didn’t ask. He’d put it away on Saturday, by the heron-stones, when Xav sold everything he had and said I’ve got plans and looked at the stones. He put this beside it.

Deston slept on the sofa, because Suzu had the guest room and Deston said he’d slept on worse sofas in better houses. At seven in the morning Ice came out of his room and found his front door open and Deston standing out in the corridor in his socks with a mug of kopi, looking across it.

The unit opposite was the mirror of Ice’s, more or less. The same door, the same metal grille, the same little ledge for shoes. Its grille was padlocked. There was a dead plant in a pot by the door, and post piled up on the floor behind the grille, and a faded sticker on the door that said DIJUAL and a phone number.

“Who lives there?” said Deston.

“Nobody. Since June.”

“Hm,” said Deston.

He looked at it a while longer. Then he drank his kopi and came in and didn’t say anything else about it.

Vivian’s office was above a kopitiam on a street near Jonker Walk, up a narrow staircase with a brass plate at the bottom. Ong Secretarial Services · Est. 1989. At half past nine on a Wednesday the kopitiam was busy, all clattering cups and the radio, and the auntie behind the counter saw them come in off the street and put both hands flat on the counter.

“Vivi!” she shouted up the stairs, without taking her eyes off them. “The gardening boys! Three of them!”

“I know, Auntie,” said Vivian’s voice, faintly, from above.

The auntie looked at the three of them. Ice, whom she’d watched die in a river. Suzu, tall and pale and silent in black, whom she seemed to recognise and approve of. Deston, whom she didn’t know, holding a box of egg tarts.

She pointed at Ice.

“The three-finger uncle,” she said. “Sunday. He was on!”

“He was,” said Ice.

“He was very tired.”

“He was very tired,” Ice agreed. “Two hundred people wanted him to teach them.”

The auntie nodded slowly, satisfied, as if this confirmed something she’d suspected about the world for a long time. Then she looked at Deston’s box.

“Those are from Singapore,” she said.

“Yes, Auntie.”

“Hm,” said the auntie. Her tone said that Singapore had been warned. She went back to her cups.

Vivian had the papers out on the teak desk before they’d sat down.

The long cool room was the same as ever. The slats of light across everything. The orchid. The photograph of her father on the wall, in a white shirt in front of the same window, younger than she was now. There were three chairs on their side of the desk today instead of one. She’d borrowed the third from somewhere, and it didn’t match.

“Mr Declan,” she said. “Mr Suzu.” She looked at Deston over her glasses. “And you are Mr Deston.”

“Hi,” said Deston. “Sorry. Yes.”

“You’re the contractor.”

“I am?”

“You are.” She turned a page. “You haven’t invoiced the company for anything.”

“I haven’t really done anything.”

Vivian looked at him for a moment. Then at the photograph of her father, briefly, as though consulting him.

“Mr Deston,” she said, “I have watched four hours and fifty-one minutes of you singing in a drowned church. The auntie has watched it three times. You have done something.” She made a small mark on her page. “It’s untidy. Please invoice.”

Deston opened his mouth, and closed it, and looked at Ice. Ice looked at the orchid.

“Okay,” said Deston faintly.

“Thank you.” Vivian squared the papers. “Now.”

She turned them round and slid them across the desk, and put her finger at the top, and waited for him to read.

Management accounts · 16 November to 15 December 2026

Rent, Melaka Raya: RM 2,400.00

Contract labour (fenwort; raft security): RM 238.14
Total expenses: RM 6,309.32
Profit before tax: RM 34,786.30
Equipment (capital): two units, RM 20,991.60 ]

He read it.

He read it twice. It was easy to read. Vivian had made it easy to read, the way she made everything easy to read, by leaving nothing out and putting everything where it went.

“The deposit on the shoplot isn’t there,” he said.

“The deposit is not an expense, Mr Declan. You’ll have it back one day.” She took her glasses off. “Unless you set fire to the shoplot. Please don’t.”

“I won’t.”

“I’ll go through it,” said Vivian. “And then I’ll tell you what I think.”

She went through it.

She did it line by line, without hurrying, the way she’d taken him through the boring things the day after the river. Revenue first. Every ringgit that had come out of the Exchange wallet and into the company’s account at the bank, each one traced back to what it was for. The herbs and draughts of the first twenty-five days, which were small. The first ringgit was in there, fifty-two ringgit and ninety-two sen for a leaf, the first line in the books. The one-third share of the first-clear materials, sold on Saturday, to nine guilds whose names she’d written in the margin in small neat capitals. The draughts of the last four days.

“Contract labour,” she said, when she got to it. “Two hundred and thirty-eight ringgit and fourteen sen. Paid in their gold, converted at the day’s rate.” She looked up. “Who is Rafi?”

“He’s nineteen,” said Ice. “He’s from Surabaya. He picks fenwort, two copper a plant. And since Sunday he guards a widow’s garden.”

“In the game.”

“In the game.”

“And Joy, and Bram.”

“Joy’s from Cebu. Bram’s Dutch. They guard it with him. Shifts.”

Vivian looked at him for a moment.

“The widow,” she said.

“Sedge. She’s the herbwife. People stripped her raft gardens on Saturday night. All of it, right down to the root. Because of the video.” He stopped. “Because of me. So I’m paying three people twenty silver a day each to stand on her steps.”

Vivian didn’t say anything for a while. Then she picked up her pen and wrote something very small in the margin beside the line. He couldn’t read it upside down.

“Contract labour,” she said, and went on.

She went on to the expenses, which were boring, and which she made him understand anyway. The rent, the fees, the licence with its faint stamp, the electricity for three eggs running twelve hours a day. Then the profit, on its own line. Then the two units. She put her finger under each and moved it down the page, line by line, to the end. Then she put down her pen.

“Mr Suzu,” she said.

Suzu, who’d been sitting perfectly still with his hands in his lap looking at the photograph of her father, looked at her.

“Forty lights,” said Vivian. “From the drowned church. Which you took instead of money.”

“Eighty-one,” said Suzu.

Vivian’s eyebrows went up a millimetre.

“Eighty-one,” she said.

“I bought forty more. Yesterday. For fifty cents each.” The corner of his mouth moved. “From the red ones.”

“With your own money.”

“Yes.”

“Which belong to you. Not to the company.”

“Yes.”

“And which you’re not selling.”

“No.”

“Then they’re not income,” said Vivian, “and they’re not the company’s, and they’re not my problem.” She wrote something. “Good. Please keep it that way until your pass comes through. I don’t want to explain eighty-one lights to the Immigration Department.”

“Any news?” said Ice. “On the pass?”

“None. Still processing. Some weeks.” She looked at Suzu. “I’ll tell you the moment I hear. You first.”

Suzu nodded once.

“So,” said Ice.

Vivian sat back in her chair.

She didn’t say anything at first. She looked at the page in front of him, upside down from where she sat, and he had the feeling she wasn’t reading it. She’d read it already. She’d read it eleven times. She was looking at it the way Sedge looked at the fen.

“In its first month,” she said, “this company has made a profit of thirty-four thousand, seven hundred and eighty-six ringgit and thirty sen. That’s before tax. It has bought two units of equipment at twenty thousand, nine hundred and ninety-one ringgit and sixty sen, and paid for them out of its own earnings. And it has thirteen thousand, seven hundred and ninety-four ringgit and seventy sen left over.” She paused. “Your paid-up capital hasn’t been touched. Not one sen.”

Nobody said anything. Down in the kopitiam the radio was playing an old song and somebody was laughing.

“Most of it was in four days,” said Ice.

“Most of it was in four days.” Vivian nodded. “Eight hundred and twelve ringgit in the first twenty-five. Forty thousand two hundred and eighty-two in the last four. I don’t project from four days, Mr Declan. I’d be a very bad secretary if I projected from four days.” She took her glasses off and put them on the desk beside the orchid. “But I don’t only read your statements.”

She opened a drawer and took out a second, thinner set of papers and laid them beside the first.

“The Exchange publishes its own numbers,” she said. “Did you know? Every day. Volume traded. Number of accounts. Average price of a gold coin against the dollar. Nobody reads them. I read them.” She turned the top page so he could see it. A plain table, the studio’s own, which she’d printed out and underlined in blue. “Yesterday, the Exchange traded thirty-eight million US dollars. In one day. A month ago, on its first day, it traded four hundred thousand. There are three million, four hundred thousand accounts. Each of them belongs to someone who paid two and a half thousand dollars before they were allowed in.”

She tapped the underlined number.

“That’s not a phase,” she said. “I thought it was a phase. I told you so, in November, in this chair. That’s a market. A real one, with real money in it, growing faster than anything I’ve ever seen. And there are not many people in it yet who know what a market is.” She looked at him. “You do.”

He didn’t say anything. He didn’t trust what would come out.

“And there’s this,” said Vivian.

She didn’t take anything out of the drawer for that one.

“On Saturday,” she said, “the auntie made me watch your stream. Because the three-finger uncle might be on.” The corner of her mouth moved. “He wasn’t. But I watched you sell. Nine guilds. I wrote them down. I watched you tell eight thousand people what a corner is, which you did very badly, and I understood it anyway. I watched you sell for a hundred and ten something that someone else would have paid you a hundred and forty for. And for one-forty something they’d have paid three hundred for. On purpose. While eight thousand people typed at you to stop.”

“About three thousand dollars,” said Ice. “Xav worked it out.”

“Why?”

He’d explained it to nine thousand people. He’d explained it to Kotori and Xav and Deston by the heron-stones. He found he didn’t want to explain it again. He wanted to say it properly, once.

“Because if you sell everything to the one buyer,” he said, “the one buyer owns your price.”

Vivian looked at him for a long moment.

Then she looked up at the photograph of her father on the wall. The young man in the white shirt, in front of the same window, in the same slatted light.

“He used to say something very like that,” she said. Quietly, as if to herself. “A business that takes every ringgit it’s offered ends up belonging to whoever offers the most.”

She put her glasses back on.

“The sixteenth stands,” she said. “I said I’d look at the numbers. I’ve looked at them. They’re good, Mr Declan. They’re very good.” She squared the papers. “I said if they were good, I’d look at the egg.”

Before they went, she took a third folder out of the drawer and put it on top of the others. It was a new one, still stiff, with a label on the front in her small neat capitals.

[ MR GABE (surname to follow)
Singaporean · resident in Australia
Employment Pass, Category III · second unit ]

“You emailed me on Saturday night,” said Vivian. “At two in the morning. You said, there’s a second person, can we do it again, he’s my friend, he wants in.” She looked at him over her glasses. “I’ve started. Same as Mr Suzu. He comes on a visit pass while it’s processed. The contract starts the day the pass is approved, not before. Until then—“

“He’s a guest who plays video games,” said Ice.

“He’s a guest who plays video games.” She almost smiled. “Tell him to bring his CV. And to make it one page.”

She came to the shoplot at three o’clock.

She walked in through the glass front door in a grey blouse with her bag over her shoulder, the way she’d walked in on the first day. Past the front office with its desk and its pot plant, which she noted, and into the back room. Two white eggs side by side, humming, with the fan going round above them. The folding table, with a kettle and an empty egg-tart box on it. Three men standing in a row against the wall, trying to look as if they hadn’t been waiting.

She stood and looked at the eggs.

The last time she’d been in this room, she’d touched one of the shells, very lightly, with two fingers, the way you’d touch something in a museum. She hadn’t touched the glass. Permanently, she’d said, when Ice told her what the fingerprint did, and taken her hand back.

This time she put her palm flat on the glass of Ice’s egg. She left it there.

“Which one?” she said.

“Mine,” said Ice. “It’s got a guest thing. Show someone. Ten minutes. You only see the first room. It doesn’t take your fingerprint and it doesn’t keep anything. When it’s done, it’s like you were never in.”

“Like I was never in,” said Vivian.

“That’s what it says.”

She looked at the egg a moment longer. Then she took off her shoes and lined them up neatly beside the folding table. She folded her glasses and put them in their case and the case on the table. She climbed into the gel without any help and without any fuss, and lay back, and looked up at him.

“Ten minutes,” she said.

“Ten minutes.”

He pressed the guest preview. The lid came down.

They waited in the front office, because it felt wrong to stand over her.

Deston sat on the desk that nobody worked at and ate the last egg tart and pretended to read his phone. Suzu stood at the glass front door with his hands in his pockets, looking out at the car park. Ice sat in the chair. He watched the second hand on the clock above the door that Vivian had sent with the pot plant, because she’d said an office should have a clock.

At four minutes, Deston said, “What do you think she—“

“Don’t,” said Ice.

At seven minutes, Suzu said, without turning round, “She took her shoes off.”

“She did.”

“She lined them up.”

“She did.”

“Hm,” said Suzu.

At ten minutes, exactly, they heard the lid.

She was sitting up in the gel when they came in, with her hands folded in her lap, looking at the wall.

She didn’t say anything. She didn’t reach for her glasses. She sat there in the white egg, in the back room in Melaka Raya, with the fan going round over her head and the afternoon coming in through the high window. Ice didn’t say anything either. He knew where she’d been. A small wooden room, with rain on the window. A mirror with nobody in it yet. And a door.

“It was raining,” said Vivian, eventually.

“It’s always raining,” said Ice. “The first time.”

“There was a door.” She was still looking at the wall. “It asked me where I was.”

“What did you say?”

“I said Melaka.” She paused. “Out loud. To a door.” She turned her head and looked at him, and without her glasses her face was different. Younger. Somebody he hadn’t met. “I could feel the floor. Through my feet. It was wood. It was a bit cold. I could hear the rain on the glass, and it wasn’t a recording. It was rain. I went to the window and I put my hand on it, and it was cold.” She looked down at her hand in her lap, the one she’d put on his egg. “My hand. I could see my hand.”

Nobody said anything.

Vivian Ong reached for her glasses, and put them on, and was Vivian Ong again.

“Where do I buy one?” she said.

“The studio’s site. They’re—“ Ice stopped. “They ship in six to eight weeks. They’re sold out.”

“February, then.” She climbed out of the egg without any help and stood in her stockinged feet on the white tiles and put her shoes back on, one, two. “I’ll order it tonight. For myself. Not for the company. I’ll pay for it myself.”

“You don’t have to—“

“I know I don’t have to.” She picked up her bag. “That’s why I’m doing it.” She paused in the doorway of the back room and looked back at them. Three men standing by two eggs, with an empty egg-tart box. “What does a person like me do in there? I don’t want to hit things with a sword.”

“You don’t have to hit anything,” said Ice. “You’d be a trader.”

“I don’t know what that is.”

“You buy things where they’re cheap and sell them where they’re dear. You read the Exchange the way you read a balance sheet. You see the number nobody else is looking at.”

Vivian thought about this.

“I already do that,” she said.

“I know,” said Ice. “That’s why.”

She looked at him for a long moment over her glasses. Then she nodded, once, the way she’d nodded over every form she’d ever filed for him. As if a box had been ticked.

“Regards, Mr Declan,” she said, and went.

He went back in at seven, because the fen’s evening was coming round again, and because there was a party waiting for him in Fernwick.

Fernwick was still busy, but it was a busy he could walk through. Four thousand travellers, the town said. Lanterns on the boardwalks. The eel man with eels again, and a queue. Marta on her stool by the crate with her arms folded. Rafi at the top of Sedge’s steps with the black sword on his back, too big for him, grinning at everyone who went past, and especially at Ice.

He’d just reached the north walk when every window in Elsewhere opened at once.

He felt it. Everybody felt it. Four thousand people in Fernwick all stopped where they were on the boardwalks and looked at the air in front of their faces. So did the six thousand in Brackenford and the two hundred in Larkhollow and everyone in Saltwhistle and Heatherbrook, and three and a half million people in four thousand towns all over the world.

[ The year turns in five days.
On the longest night, the dark will not lift when it should.
Keep your lights. Keep your towns. ]

That was all. No timer. No reward. No ranking, yet. Just the words, hanging in the air for a moment over the evening fen, and then gone.
