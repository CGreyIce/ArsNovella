Live from Elsewhere · Volume 4: The Ninth Heron

Chapter 108

Where It Hits

2,677 words · 12 min read

He’d put pain on fifty on the first night, in a little wooden room with rain on the window, and told twenty-one people why. I’m going to stand at the front with a shield, and things are going to hit me, and I need to know exactly where they hit. Twenty was a sock. He didn’t want the sock.

He’d never once regretted it. He’d thought about it, on the plank, at three in the morning, with the cold up to his knees. He’d thought about it on the Long Night. He’d thought about it on Saturday, in a city with no name, with a girl with horns hitting him twice in the left arm as hard as she could. But he’d never regretted it.

On Wednesday, at the second dawn, at the bottom of a hill at the top of the world, Ice put his hand flat on a stone door with nine herons carved round it, and pushed.

It opened inward, without a sound.

Behind it was a gallery. Long and straight and narrow, cut into the hill, going north and very gently down, further than their lamps could reach. The walls were smooth grey stone. And all along both walls, on either side, one after another into the dark, were alcoves.

In every alcove, a figure.

They were stone. Life-sized, or a little more. Worn so smooth with age that they had no faces left, only the shapes where faces had been. Every one of them was standing the same way. Facing north, down the gallery, away from the door, the way the herons faced. Every one of them was holding up a lantern in its right hand. A real lantern, iron, with a little door of thin horn. And every lantern was dark.

A door for five. ]

Nobody said anything.

Then Suzu’s pack began to glow so brightly that it lit the first two alcoves on either side, blue. The stone figures stood in the blue light with their dark lanterns up, looking north, and didn’t move.

“Watchers,” said Kotori, very softly.

“Watchers,” said Ice.

He thought of Sedge’s mother’s story, in the ring, by the ninth stone. Someone had to watch.

He went in first. That was his place.

The first of the things came at the fifth alcove.

He heard it before he saw it. A sound like breath let out slowly, from the dark ahead, beyond their lamps. Then the lamp on Deston’s belt went out. Not blown out. Closed. The way a hand closes round a moth.

[ The Unwatched · Lv 31 ]

There were four of them. Thin and grey, with no faces, the shapes of the figures in the alcoves but loose, unfinished, drifting. Like somebody had made a watcher out of smoke and forgotten to finish it. They didn’t come at Ice. They came at the light. At Deston’s other lamp, at the one on Kotori’s staff, at the blue glow coming out of Suzu’s pack.

“Anchor,” said Ice, and set his feet.

[ Anchor ]

The shield came up. Steadfast hummed under his arm, the way it always did when he Anchored. A low steady note he felt in his teeth. While you Anchor, those behind you keep time. The first of them reached past him for Kotori’s lamp, and he turned it on the shield. Turned Block, the slope, the first thing he’d ever learned in here. Its long arm slid off the rim into the wall of the gallery, and it hit the stone and came apart a little at the edges, like breath on a window.

“One,” counted Deston, behind him. “Two.”

Suzu went wide on the left, into the space between the alcoves, with the scythe low. He took two of them across the middle in one long flat stroke, the wide row, and they came apart into grey, and drifted down, and were gone.

Sheng Yang was two paces from Kotori. He’d been two paces from Kotori since Gabe left. He’d promised. The fourth one went round Ice, low, for her lamp. Sheng Yang was already there, between them, with the long sword up. He let it come. On Deston’s three he brought the sword down through it from the shoulder, and the grey came apart all the way to the floor.

[ Ender’s Cut · 94% ]

“Light,” said Sheng Yang, breathing hard. “They want the light.”

“Everything does,” said Kotori, and relit Deston’s lamp.

[ The Unwatched ×4 · returned to the dark ]

They stood in the gallery among the faceless watchers. Their health was a little down. Not much. Kotori’s Mend came round all of them, warm, and put it back.

“Okay,” said Ice. “On.”

He felt it at the ninth alcove.

He didn’t think anything of it at first. A small cold in his left wrist, under the shield strap. Like a draught. Like cold water running very slowly under the skin, from the wrist toward the elbow.

Hyvred, he thought.

He very nearly laughed. Saturday. A city with no name, a girl with horns and a closed fist, that’s for the dollar, that’s for the heron. Two hits, both on the left arm, both in the same place, as hard as she could. A window had said no harm can be done here. He’d been on fifty. He’d felt it for an hour afterwards, at least, a dull ache he’d been rather fond of, and he’d felt it again on Sunday walking north, a ghost of it, whenever he lifted the shield.

So: Hyvred. A ghost of Hyvred. He put it down to that and kept walking.

The tenth alcove. The eleventh. Faceless watchers on both sides, dark lanterns up, looking north. The cold in his wrist didn’t go away. It didn’t get much worse either. It just stayed, the way a bruise stays. Very slowly, it crept a little further up the arm. Not pain, really. The place where pain would be, if there were any.

He’d been hit by worse.

At the fourteenth alcove he glanced left, the way he always did, to see where Suzu was.

Suzu was walking beside him in the blue light of his own pack, the scythe on his shoulder. And with his right thumb, absently, without looking, he was rubbing the inside of his left wrist.

Ice stopped walking.

Suzu hadn’t been hit. Not on Saturday. Not by Hyvred, not by anyone. Not in here, either. The Unwatched hadn’t touched him; he’d been wide, with the scythe, out of reach. He’d been nowhere near a fist or a claw or anything at all.

“Suzu,” said Ice.

Suzu looked at him. Then down at his own hands, as if he hadn’t known what they were doing.

“Your wrist,” said Ice.

“It’s cold,” said Suzu slowly.

“Left?”

“…Yes.”

“Since when?”

Suzu thought about it. He always thought before he answered. He never said a thing until he was sure it was true.

“The third alcove,” he said. “Maybe the fourth. I thought it was the bridge. Holding the Eye up. My arm got tired.”

“I thought it was Hyvred,” said Ice.

They looked at each other in the blue light.

They were both on fifty. They were the only two of the five who were. Suzu had put it on fifty in his own white room in November, a week and a half after Ice, and when somebody asked him why he’d said Ice is on fifty, and nothing else.

“Everybody stop,” said Ice.

He opened the party window. He didn’t need to. He knew already. Pain told a tank exactly where he’d been hit, even when nothing had hit him. He looked anyway, because the others needed to see it.

Kotori had healed all of them to full at the fifth alcove. Nobody had been hit since. Nothing had come out of the dark. There’d been no window. Ice opened every one he could think of, and there was nothing on any of them. No mark, no name, no little picture of a skull or a drop or a snowflake. Nothing ticking. Nothing to see.

And all five of them were going down.

Kotori made a sound like somebody had hit her.

“I didn’t—“ She was staring at the window. “Nobody got hit. I was watching. I was watching, Ice, I’d have seen it, I’d have— there’s nothing there, there’s no—“

“There’s nothing to see,” said Ice. “That’s the point.”

“Then how did you—“

“Me and Suzu are on fifty.” He held up his left hand. “It’s in our wrists. Cold. Like water. It’s been there for ten alcoves.” He looked at Deston, at Sheng Yang, at Kotori. “Can you feel anything? Anything at all. Anywhere.”

Deston closed his eyes. He stood very still in the middle of the gallery for a long moment, the way he did when he was counting something only he could hear.

“No,” he said. “Nothing.”

“No,” said Sheng Yang.

“No,” said Kotori, very small.

“You’re on twenty,” said Ice. “It’s under twenty. It’s so small you can’t feel it at twenty. It doesn’t show. It doesn’t make a sound. It just takes, a little at a time, for as long as you’re in here.” He looked down the gallery, into the dark, at the long line of faceless watchers. “We’d have walked till it was half. Then a quarter. Then something would’ve come out of the dark and found us at a quarter, and we’d never have known why.”

Nobody said anything.

“Pain tells you where you got hit,” said Suzu quietly. “Even when you didn’t.”

Sheng Yang looked at him for a long moment. Then at Ice.

“Twenty is a sock,” said Sheng Yang.

Ice almost laughed. He’d said that on the first night, to twenty-one people in a chat. He hadn’t known Sheng Yang had ever heard it.

“It’s the dark,” said Deston.

He was looking back down the gallery the way they’d come. Ice looked too. Behind them, all the way to the door, the watchers stood with their dark lanterns up. Ahead, the same, into the black.

“The Unwatched went for the lamps,” said Deston. Everything in here wants the light out.” He looked at the nearest watcher, at the dark lantern in its stone hand. “And all of these are out.”

In Suzu’s pack, the jar was pulling.

Ice could see it. The canvas was straining, as if something inside were trying to get out through one side of it. Toward the alcove on their right. Toward the watcher standing in it, faceless, with its dark lantern held up.

Suzu took the jar out.

He held it in both hands, in the middle of the gallery. Eighty-one small blue lights, the ones he’d kept in a sweet jar since December, the ones he couldn’t let go of. All of them were pressing against the side of the glass nearest the watcher. So hard the glass was ringing, very faintly, a note so high it was almost not there.

“They want to go,” said Kotori.

Suzu didn’t answer. He was looking at the lights. Ice watched his face. He knew all of Suzu’s stillnesses. He’d never seen this one.

Suzu put his thumb on the lid.

He didn’t open it. Not really. He just loosened it. A hair. Ice saw him do it, and saw him not mean to, and saw him mean to anyway.

One light came out.

It came up out of the gap at the edge of the lid like a spark off a fire. Small and blue and very bright. It hung in the air in front of Suzu’s face for a moment, as if it were looking at him. Then it went sideways, slowly, into the alcove. Up the stone arm of the watcher. Through the little horn door of the iron lantern in its hand.

The lantern lit.

Blue. Soft. Steady. The faceless watcher stood in its alcove with its lantern lit, looking north down the gallery. For the first time in nobody knew how long, there was a light in the Long Watch that somebody hadn’t carried in.

[ A watch is kept. ]

The cold went out of Ice’s wrist.

All at once, the way a tap turns off. He felt it go. Beside him, Suzu’s right hand stopped moving on his left wrist and stayed there, very still.

Ice looked at the party window.

The numbers had stopped going down.

Kotori was already moving. Her bell rang once, and her Mend went round all five of them, warm. The numbers went up, and up, and stayed up.

“In its light,” said Deston slowly. “In the light of one that’s lit. It doesn’t take.”

Suzu was looking at the jar.

He put the lid back on. Very carefully, all the way, with both hands. He looked at the lights inside. Eighty. Still pressing, all of them, against the glass toward the alcove.

Then he took the Heron’s Eye out of his pocket, and held it up, and looked north down the gallery through it. Past their lamps. Past the edge of the light. Into the dark where the watchers went on, one after another, two by two, out of sight.

He was very still for a long time.

“How many?” said Ice.

Suzu lowered the Eye.

“Eighty-one,” said Suzu.

Nobody said anything.

Eighty-one watchers. Eighty-one dark lanterns. Eighty-one small blue lights in a sweet jar, carried since December by a boy who couldn’t let them go. They’re going home, Sedge had said, in March, holding the jar up to the lamp on her steps. My mother said the lights that don’t finish go north. Where the herons look. That’s where they go to finish. You’re carrying them home.

One was home. Eighty to go.

Suzu’s face had gone white, all of it.

“We go back,” said Ice.

Nobody argued.

They walked back to the door, the five of them, past the watchers. They didn’t hurry. At the fourteenth alcove they passed the one that was lit, blue and steady in its stone hand. For the length of its light the cold didn’t come, and then it came again, faintly, in his wrist and Suzu’s, all the way to the door.

Outside, in the mist, at the foot of the grey hill, it was evening. The twenty-sixth heron stood a quarter of a mile below them, looking north at the door. Past it, the gorge, and the bridge nobody could see.

Suzu sat down on the grass with his back against the hillside, beside the door, and held the jar in his lap.

Eighty small blue lights. Pressing, all of them, against the side of the glass nearest the door.

Ice sat down next to him. Not close. Just there.

“It’s yours,” he said, after a long time. “The jar. All of it. Whatever you want.”

Suzu didn’t say anything. Ice hadn’t expected him to.

He sat with him in the mist until it was dark, and the lights in the jar were the only light on the hill. Then they logged out.
