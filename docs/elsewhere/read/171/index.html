Live from Elsewhere · Volume 7: Keep the Lamps

Chapter 171

Not Eleven Minutes

4,375 words · 19 min read

Ama Corrie opened the hatch for them herself.

She didn’t have to. The banner did it. Bee’s banner, on the two hooks under the stair, with the old gold star along its top edge that nobody had painted. When you came to the iron hatch and the banner knew you, the seven spoke-lines lit and the hatch sank into a stair. But Ama Corrie came out from behind her counter at a quarter to midnight anyway. She stood at the top of the stair with her lamp, the way she’d stand at the door of a room she was letting out.

“Downstairs,” she said.

“Yes,” said Xav.

“The two of you.”

“Yes.”

Ama Corrie looked at them both over her spectacles for a long time. At Xav, with his two blades. At Dec, beside him, with his shield on his back and his sword on his hip and nothing at all on his face.

“I’ve had worse in that hall,” she said at last. “My mother had a wedding down there. A man fell in the fourth tunnel and they didn’t find him till Tuesday.” She held the lamp out to Dec. “Mind the third step. It’s always been loose.”

Dec took the lamp. Xav went down first.

At the top, as the hatch began to close, he heard someone come into the Hall behind them. Light steps, a bow knocking on a doorframe. Kestrel’s voice, a little out of breath, saying is he—. And Ama Corrie’s, saying yes, and you’re not. Then the hatch closed and the voices went away, and it was very quiet.

The Seven Below was round.

He’d always liked it. It was a Hall for ten, and it was theirs. Constellation’s. It had been under the round town for three hundred years, waiting for a banner. A big round room of pale old stone, with seven arched mouths going off it into the dark, one under each road. The floor was cut with seven lines running in from the mouths to a ring in the middle, the same shape as the round market above. The lines were faintly lit, always, a pale silver, as if the stone remembered something.

Dec put the lamp down by the foot of the stair. He walked out to the middle of the room. He turned round.

He put his hand up to the little floating light at his shoulder, the Eye, the thing that let a hundred thousand people see out of him. And he touched it, once.

It went out.

Xav looked at it. The place in the air where it had been.

“Not for them,” said Dec.

“No,” said Xav.

That was all either of them said about it. Upstairs, somewhere, Tif’s counter would be ticking over. Four hundred and something. The chat would be going mad. It didn’t matter. It wasn’t theirs.

They didn’t say begin. They’d never said begin, not once in nine years, in any game. They’d stood across from each other in a hundred places. Practice rooms on servers that didn’t exist any more. A rooftop map in a shooter, and a field in something else, and a bridge. And neither of them had ever said begin.

“Whenever,” said Xav.

“Whenever,” said Dec.

Nobody moved.

They stood ten paces apart in the middle of the Seven Below with the silver lines under their feet. And nobody moved. Ten seconds. Twenty. Xav had his blades out, low. Dec had his shield up and his sword down, the way he always did, loose. Neither of them did anything.

Xav was trying very hard not to decide.

That was the only thing he’d taken from two weeks of first dawns. He’d taught it to Kestrel. He’d known it for years without having to say it. Then he’d said it a hundred times in two weeks, and saying it had made it clearer. Don’t decide where he can see it. Because the moment he decided, Dec would see it. Six frames, four, two, it didn’t matter. Dec would see it. And he’d go first, and Xav would spend the rest of the fight answering.

So he didn’t decide. He stood in the silver lines and held the whole room in his head at once, the way he’d told her. And waited.

At thirty-one seconds, Dec moved.

He moved first. Of course he did. He always did. But Xav saw it. For the first time, maybe, he saw it as it happened, not after. Dec’s weight went onto his front foot, a step began, and the back foot—

The back foot didn’t come.

It stayed. A breath. Exactly the breath Xav had drawn in the margin of the fourth page in the Hart. It stayed on the stone while the step was already happening. So the step could still go either way, left or right, when it landed. And Xav, who’d been waiting for the step to tell him which way, had nothing to read.

He went anyway.

He went behind the sword arm. That was his oldest thing, his very first. Hollis had shown him on the first day, at the fence, in a fen. Be somewhere before the other man knows you’ve moved. He was there before Dec’s step had finished. Low, on the right, behind the sword arm, both blades coming up, faster than he’d ever done it.

And Dec was there.

Not the sword. The shield. The back foot had come round at last, late, the right way, and taken Dec’s whole body round with it. The shield, which should have been on the left, was on the right, on the wrong side, square across the place Xav had arrived.

Xav hit it at full speed.

He came off it rolling, the way you came off a wall, and was on his feet two paces off before he’d stopped thinking about it. Dec hadn’t followed. Dec was standing where he’d been, sword down, shield up, looking at him.

Four years early, Xav thought. He was there four years early.

He went again, and again. He tried things he’d never shown anybody. He tried things he’d only thought of, on the ledge, in the mornings. He went in low and high and, once, astonishingly, through, between the shield and the sword arm, a gap of about a hand’s width that only existed for a quarter of a second. And every time, Dec was there first. Not by much, now. That was the thing. Not by much.

By a breath. By half a breath. By the length of a back foot.

Nobody else had ever got close enough to make it only half a breath.

Xav knew that. He knew it with his whole body, the way you know you’re running faster than you’ve ever run. Every other person who’d stood in front of Dec this year, Kestrel and Kaede and Brann and the rest, he’d beaten by miles. By a page, by four pages, by six frames. And Xav was only half a breath behind. He was so close he could almost see the back of Dec’s head.

At three minutes and forty seconds, he got there.

He didn’t know how. He’d never be able to say, afterwards. He went in on the left, where everybody went, the obvious place, because it was the last place Dec would expect him to go. Dec was there, of course, shield first. But Xav had gone in on the left without deciding to. And for one half-breath Dec had been waiting for a decision that never came.

The left blade went past the shield’s rim and opened a line across the back of Dec’s shield arm, above the wrist.

Xav stopped.

He stood there with the blade out and looked at the window. Then at Dec.

Dec was looking at his own arm. At the line on it. At fifty per cent pain, Xav thought. He always kept it at fifty, because pain told a tank where he’d been hit. Dec looked at it for a long moment, the way you’d look at a letter in handwriting you hadn’t seen in years.

Then he looked up at Xav.

And Xav, who had known him for nine years, saw something come into his face that he hadn’t seen in a very long time. Not since they were nineteen, maybe. Or twenty. In a dark room somewhere with the whole night ahead of them. It wasn’t surprise. Dec didn’t really get surprised. It was something better than that. Something that looked, quite simply, glad.

“There you are,” said Dec.

It went on.

It wasn’t like the seven. Xav had drawn the seven, every step. He knew what they’d looked like from outside. They’d looked like a man walking somebody to a door. This wasn’t that. This was two people, very fast, in a round room, in silver light, going round and round each other without stopping. And Dec still went first. Every time. He didn’t stop going first. But now every time he went first, Xav was already moving too, a half-breath behind. And sometimes, not often, less than a half.

Dec used everything.

Xav saw it all go past. He’d been at the edge of the ring every night with his sketchbook, and now he was inside it, and he saw it all from the other side. The shoulder, the way he’d hit the girl from Osaka. The beat, one, one, one, set on purpose so Xav had to fit himself into the gaps, the way he’d done to the healer. The walk-round, slow, the way he’d circled Brann, choosing which way Xav had to face. He did it for twenty seconds and then stopped, because Xav wouldn’t turn, and Dec saw that and gave it up without a flicker. And the given distance, the thing he’d done to Kestrel. Three paces back, here, have it. So Xav took it, because you always took it. And then Dec came and took it away again, to see what he’d done with it.

He’d learned all of it this year. Or got better at it. Xav could see the difference now. It was like looking at a drawing you did a long time ago, next to one you did yesterday. The same hand. Different.

And the back foot. Always the back foot. Waiting a breath, so the step could go either way.

He doesn’t hold back against me, Xav thought. He’s never held back against me. He’s using everything he’s got, every single thing. And it’s more.

He got Dec twice more in the next eight minutes. A touch on the right thigh, going past. A real one on the shoulder above the shield, where you weren’t supposed to be able to reach. Dec took them both without a sound. After each one, for a half-second, there was that look again. There you are. As if each one was a door opening somewhere and Dec had been waiting all year for somebody to come through it.

Xav took far more than he gave. He knew that. He wasn’t stupid. Every time he got through, he paid for it twice on the way back out. The shield’s rim. The flat of the sword, which Dec never turned to the edge, not once. The sword was the one thing he held back.

At fourteen minutes, Xav was at forty-one per cent and Dec was at seventy-three.

At nineteen, Xav was at thirty-four and Dec was at sixty-six.

And at nineteen minutes and some seconds, Xav saw the window.

He’d known it was coming. He’d known the whole time. Every blow Dec turned went under the surface, and Dec had turned hundreds of them. It had been filling for nineteen minutes like a cistern. Nearly three thousand, now. More than all seven of the others put together. If it landed, it would take him off his feet and across the room and most of what he had left.

So it couldn’t land.

Xav backed off. Not far. Just out of reach. Just far enough that Dec couldn’t get a strike in without coming to him. And he stayed there. He didn’t go in. He circled. He used every inch of the round room, the seven mouths, the silver lines, the ring in the middle. He stayed just out of the length of a sword, the length of a pull.

Dec came after him.

Not fast. At a walk. He walked at Xav the way he’d walked at all of them, steadily, every step first, taking the room away a piece at a time. And Xav gave the room back. Every time Dec took a piece, Xav was already somewhere else, half a breath ahead for once. Not answering. Leaving.

A minute.

Two.

Three.

It was the hardest thing Xav had ever done in any game. He was so tired he could feel it in his teeth. He knew he couldn’t do it for ever. He knew Dec could. Dec could walk at you all night, at that speed, with that face, and not get tired at all. Waiting was his favourite thing. But for three minutes, then four, Xav did it. He stayed out of reach of a man nobody had ever stayed out of reach of. And the cistern sat full under the surface, nearly three thousand, with nowhere to go.

At twenty-four minutes, Dec stopped.

He stopped in the middle of the room, in the ring where the silver lines met. He stood there with his sword down and his shield low, and he lowered the shield a little more. Just a little. Just enough that the left side, the side everybody went, was open. Wide open. Wider than it had been all fight.

It was the most obvious invitation Xav had ever seen in his life.

He knew it was an invitation. He knew it exactly. It was a door Dec had opened on purpose, the way he’d opened one for Kestrel with the graze, to see what she’d do. He knew that if he went through it, the cistern was waiting on the other side.

He also knew it was the only door there was going to be.

You never got an opening from Dec. Not ever. Not in nine years. You only got invitations. And if you wanted in, you had to take one, and go through, knowing.

Xav laughed. Out loud, in the round room, the sound going away down all seven tunnels at once.

Then he went through.

He went in on the left, through the open side, faster than he’d ever gone at anything. Both blades. Everything he had. And for one tiny moment, a quarter of a breath, he was there, inside, past the shield, with both blades coming in at Dec’s open side—

And Dec let it go.

It took him off his feet.

He’d watched it take Brann. He’d drawn it. A long pencil smear going forward off a square. It was different from inside. It was like being hit by a wave from the wrong direction. It didn’t push him; it pulled. Toward Dec, past him, off the ground. All his speed and his weight and his clever going-in turned round and taken away from him. He went across the floor of the Seven Below on his back, through the silver lines, and stopped with his head against the edge of the ring in the middle and the whole round ceiling going round above him.

[ Sketch · 7% ]

He lay there.

After a while, the ceiling stopped going round. And Dec’s face came into it from one side, looking down. The flat of his sword came and rested, very lightly, on the side of Xav’s neck.

“Yield?” said Dec.

Xav lay on his back on the cold stone and looked up at him.

“Eventually,” he said.

Dec laughed. A real one. Short, surprised, out loud, the way he hardly ever laughed in front of anybody now. It went away down the seven tunnels after Xav’s.

“Yield,” said Xav.

Dec took the sword away and sat down beside him on the floor, cross-legged, with the shield across his knees.

The window above them said what it said.

Twenty-six minutes and fourteen seconds.

They sat there for a long time.

Xav didn’t get up. He lay on his back with his head against the ring and looked at the ceiling. It was plain stone, no lines, nothing on it at all. He felt Dec sitting beside him, the way you feel somebody sitting beside you in the dark.

“You made me work,” said Dec, after a while.

“Not enough.”

“Sixty-one.”

“Not enough,” said Xav again.

Dec didn’t argue. He knew it was true. Xav could tell he knew, because he didn’t say anything kind. If it hadn’t been true, he’d have said something kind. He was terrible at kind things, but he always tried.

“You’ve got better,” said Xav.

Dec looked at him.

“Since the last time.” Xav was still looking at the ceiling. “Years ago. You were good then. You were the best I’d ever fought. And I thought I’d got closer. All this year, I thought I’d got closer.” He breathed out. “I haven’t. You’ve gone further. You’ve gone further than you were.”

“Have I?”

“Your back foot,” said Xav. “You wait on it now. A breath. So you can go either way.”

Dec was quiet for a moment.

“Oh,” he said. “That’s from the fen.”

Xav turned his head and looked at him.

“Fernwick,” said Dec. “The ring. The heron-stone.” He was turning his sword over on his knee, slowly, looking at the edge. “I used to sit with my back to it. Most nights, before the Long Night. Waiting for things to come up out of the water. You don’t know which side they’ll come up. So you wait on the back foot. So you can go either way.” He shrugged. “I didn’t notice I was doing it. I just did it, for a year.”

A year of sitting with your back to a stone in a fen, Xav thought. Waiting for things.

He looked back at the ceiling.

He thought about the last nine years, and the last ten months. About saying he’d win, eventually, on the oak floor at Elmcross, so easily, like a fact about the weather. He’d said it a lot this year. To Kestrel. To the eight. To himself. He’s being nice. He’d win. Eventually. He’d thought it was a comfortable thing to say. A thing you could say forever and it would always be true and it would never cost you anything.

He’d been wrong. It wasn’t eventually. It was further. Dec hadn’t been standing still waiting for him. He’d been sitting in a fen for a year getting further.

And Xav found, lying on the cold stone of the Seven Below with seven per cent and a headache, that he minded.

He minded very much.

He hadn’t minded anything in a game for a long time. That was the strange part. He’d minded for people. For the eight, for Riverbank, for Dec. He’d minded whether RX was all right, and whether Malcolm’s March ever came. He’d minded whether Val was having a good time and whether John’s mirror had got cracked. He’d minded so much for everyone else that he’d forgotten, somewhere along the way, what it felt like to mind for himself. To want something. Not for the eight. Not for the guild. Just for him.

He wanted to be half a breath closer.

It sat in his chest like something lit.

“Again,” said Xav.

“Now?”

“No. A month.” He sat up, slowly, because everything hurt at fifty per cent, which he’d turned it up to that morning without telling anybody. “I want to be ready.”

Dec looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded, as if Xav had said something perfectly ordinary, like same time next week.

“Okay,” said Dec.

“Don’t make it a thing.”

“I won’t.”

“You will,” said Xav. “You’ll go and sit in the tunnel and think about it for three hours.”

“Probably,” said Dec.

Kestrel was sitting on the bottom step of Ama Corrie’s stair when the hatch opened. She’d been there, Ama Corrie said afterward, for twenty-six minutes, with her bow across her knees, not moving. Listening to the iron through the floor.

She stood up when Xav came up into the Hall.

She looked at him. At the way he was standing, at seven per cent, with his headache. At Dec behind him, at sixty-one, with the lamp.

“Well?” she said.

“Twenty-six minutes,” said Xav.

Kestrel stood very still.

“Not eleven,” she said.

“Not eleven.”

She didn’t say anything else. She didn’t need to. She’d been on the oak at Elmcross. She knew exactly what eleven minutes meant, and twenty-six. And she looked at Xav the way he’d watched her look at the window above his head on the twelfth morning, when the line opened on his arm.

Then she nodded, once, and picked up Ama Corrie’s broom from where it was leaning against the counter, and went down the stair into the Seven Below to sweep. Nobody had asked her to.

Ama Corrie watched her go.

“Hm,” she said. “That’s four.”

Riverbank was waiting in the banner room. All seven, at the long end of the second table, pretending not to be.

“You went dark,” said Rui Xiang, before Xav had sat down. “Dec went dark. At third dawn. Tif’s counter’s at four hundred and thirteen. Chat thinks he found a door.” He leaned so far forward his chair went up on two legs. “Did you fight?”

“Yes,” said Xav.

The table went silent.

“Who won?” said John.

Xav sat down in his chair, the first of Riverbank’s. He looked down the banner room to the first of the nine. Dec was sitting down there with the lamp still in his hand, as if he’d forgotten he was holding it. Kotori was coming round the table toward him with a tray.

“He did,” said Xav. “Eventually.”

Malcolm put his stick down on the table.

“How long’s eventually?” he said.

“Twenty-six minutes.”

Malcolm looked at him for a long time. He was the one who’d started the group chat, in the first week of poly. The one who’d decided their class would be the most bonded in the school. He’d known Xav as long as Dec had.

“You’ve got a face,” said Malcolm.

“I know,” said Xav. “Everybody keeps telling me.”

“It’s not the letter face,” said Malcolm. “Or the other one. The one Higu said.” He picked up his stick again. “It’s the one you had at seventeen. When you’d lost at something and you were going to practise till you didn’t.”

Nobody at the table said anything. Jun Liang didn’t sing. Ryan didn’t say yeah.

“It’ll be March,” said Malcolm, “before you get there.”

“Then it’ll be March,” said Xav.

He logged out at eight, at the end of his twelve hours, and lay in his egg in his flat a long way from anywhere for a while with the lid up. It was morning there too. Out of the window the man with the loaf-shaped dog was going past again. He watched them go.

Then he got up and found a pencil and a real sketchbook, the paper kind. He sat at his small table by the window and drew a back foot, waiting a breath on a stone.

He drew it forty times. Then he drew his own, beside it, the way he did it now. Then he sat and looked at the two of them for a long time.

He set an alarm for first dawn. Then he set another one, ten minutes earlier, just in case.
