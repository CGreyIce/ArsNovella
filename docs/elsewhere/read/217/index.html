Gabe had kept a secret for twenty-two days, and it was killing him.

He’d never kept one before. Not a real one. He’d kept little ones, the kind everybody keeps: a birthday present, a surprise party, once, for Deston, which Deston had found out about because Gabe had told him. He’d kept the one about Dec and his phone in January for three weeks, and that had nearly finished him. He’d stood in the kitchen with the kopi saying absolutely nothing while Dec went pink at a screen, and it had been the hardest thing he’d ever done.

This was harder.

It had started on the nineteenth of May, a Friday, at eleven at night. He’d been in the spare room, out of the egg, lying on his bed watching a clip of a man walking round a goose for the ninetieth time. And his phone had buzzed on his chest.

Twenty-two days.

He’d nearly told Suzu on day two. Suzu had looked at him over breakfast and said what, and Gabe had said nothing, very loudly, and Suzu had looked at him for a long time and gone back to his toast.

He’d nearly told Deston on day five, on a call, when Deston said two weeks till the keys and Gabe said two weeks and one day, because she’d chosen the tenth, and Deston said what’s the one day for, and Gabe had hung up.

He’d nearly told Dec about forty times. Once in the back room at Melaka Raya, when his phone buzzed and his face went red and Dec looked at him and said who’s that. And Gabe had said nobody, which was the worst lie anyone had ever told. And Dec had looked at him for a long time. And then, very strangely, said okay, and not asked. Dec never not-asked things now. He’d learned to ask, this year. Gabe had watched him learn it. And he’d looked at Gabe’s red face and decided not to.

Gabe had thought about that for days.

The keys came at ten o’clock on Saturday, the way the agent had said they would three times on the phone.

He came up in the lift in the same batik shirt, with the same forty keys on his ring, and a man from the lawyer’s office behind him carrying a folder. And Deston, who’d come up on the six o’clock bus on Friday, of course. With a pink box of kueh for the auntie and nothing on his knee this time, because the cacti were already here.

They stood in the corridor. All of them. Deston, the agent, the man from the lawyer’s, Gabe. Suzu, leaning in Dec’s doorway at the far end with his arms folded, because he’d said he wasn’t coming and then come. And Dec, beside the two cacti, with his hands in his pockets.

The agent made a speech. It was quite a long speech. It was about how he’d been selling units for twenty-six years and he’d never once had a sticker stay on a grille for twenty-two months, and he’d never once had a buyer offer seventy thousand more than asking. He’d never once had a buyer cry on the phone when the state said yes, which Deston said he hadn’t. And this was, the agent said, the happiest day of his professional life. He said that last bit twice.

Then the man from the lawyer’s opened his folder and Deston signed a last thing on the top of it, against the wall. And the agent took one key off his ring of forty, and then another, and held them out.

Two keys, on a plain steel ring.

“Always two,” said the agent. “Spare. For family.”

Deston took them.

He stood there in the corridor under the strip light and looked at them in his hand for a long time. Gabe saw his face. He’d known Deston a long time, years of war zones side by side in an old game, and Deston had always had the networking face, the relaxed one that made people like him in ten seconds. It wasn’t that face. Gabe didn’t know what face it was. He’d never seen it.

Then Deston unlocked the padlock on the grille. And slid the grille back, with the sound grilles make. And put the first key in the door, the real door, and turned it.

It opened.

Nobody’s smell. White walls. A balcony door at the end, gone grey with dust, and Malacca beyond it, hot and bright.

“Oh,” said Deston.

They moved him in that afternoon. It took about forty minutes, because he had almost nothing.

A mattress, which Gabe and Dec carried up in the lift between them, Gabe at the front, talking. A kettle. Two cups. A box of things from Singapore, which Deston wouldn’t let anybody open. A folding chair. And a frame, wrapped in newspaper, which he unwrapped in the middle of the empty living room with everybody watching.

The sticker. DIJUAL. Faded pink, with a long rip down one corner where his thumbnail had gone under it in April. Behind glass, in a plain black frame, with a little brass plate at the bottom that said NINETEEN MONTHS. TWENTY-TWO.

He hung it on a nail that the last owner had left in the wall, opposite the door, where it would be the first thing you saw coming in.

“There,” said Deston.

Gabe was crying. He’d started at the frame and hadn’t really stopped.

“You’re ridiculous,” he said.

“I know,” said Deston.

“It’s a sticker, Dest.”

“It’s not a sticker,” said Deston. He was looking at it. “It’s the thing that was on the door for twenty-two months. Every time Dec walked past it. Every time you walked past it.” He was quiet. “It’s how long nobody lived here. I want to remember how long.”

Dec, by the balcony door, looked at the frame for a long time.

He didn’t say anything. But Gabe saw his hand, the one that wasn’t in his pocket, open and close once at his side. As if it were holding something.

The auntie came up at four.

She’d been told not to. Vivian had told Deston in January, do not tell the auntie, she will want to come and see the unit. And Deston hadn’t told her. Nobody had. But the auntie ran the kopitiam downstairs. She’d watched a sticker fade on a grille for twenty-two months, and three boys in the flat at the end who she called the gardening boys. She’d watched a fourth boy come up on the six o’clock bus every few weeks with Singapore kueh, too sweet. And she’d watched the agent come up in his batik shirt with forty keys and go down again forty minutes later with a face like a man walking out of church. She knew.

She came out of the lift in her apron with a plate of something under a cloth and walked down the long corridor to the open grille and stopped in the doorway.

She looked at the empty white room. At the mattress, and the kettle, and the folding chair, and the frame on the wall opposite the door. At Deston in the middle of it, red to the ears.

“Aiyo,” said the auntie. “Got people already ah?”

“Yes, auntie,” said Deston.

“Finally.” She looked round again, at the empty walls. “Nothing inside one. Like a hotel.” She held out the plate. “Eat. You’re too thin. You eat Singapore kueh, no wonder.” She looked at the frame for a long time. She read the brass plate, moving her lips. Nineteen months. Twenty-two. Then she looked at Deston. “You frame the sticker?”

“Yes, auntie.”

She looked at him for a very long time.

“Good,” said the auntie, at last, unexpectedly, and went away down the corridor to the lift without another word.

Under the cloth, it was kueh. Malaccan. Not too sweet. Deston ate three.

By seven, it was just the two of them, in the unit opposite, with the grille open and the door open a crack.

Suzu had gone back to the flat. Dec had gone back with him, to make tea. Tea’s on my side, he’d said, which was a thing he’d started saying in January and nobody had ever asked about. And Gabe and Deston were on the floor of the empty bedroom with a flat-pack bed in pieces round them and the instructions upside down.

“Part C,” said Gabe. “Into part F. With the—“ He turned the page round. “With the little wooden ones.”

“Dowels.”

“With the dowels.”

His phone buzzed in his pocket.

He didn’t look at it. He’d been not looking at it since half past two, when it had buzzed with on the bus. He’d looked at that one. He’d looked at all of them, every one, in the bathroom of the unit opposite with the tap running, because he couldn’t not.

That one had been at a quarter to seven. He’d had to sit down on the edge of the bath.

Now it buzzed again. He didn’t look. He held part C very tightly.

“Gabe,” said Deston.

“Mm.”

“Who’s that?”

“Nobody,” said Gabe.

Deston looked at him across the flat-pack bed.

Deston had known him a long time. Since a war zone in an old game, side by side, shouting. And Deston had known about Hyvred before anybody, before Bee, before the stupid swamp guy himself. He’d known since the first winter, from an Accord on a road that put you in time with somebody, and what it felt like when one of the people in it was looking at somebody else.

Gabe watched him work it out. It took about four seconds.

“Gabe,” said Deston.

“Don’t,” said Gabe. “Don’t say anything. I’ve kept it twenty-two days. I’ve never kept anything twenty-two minutes. Don’t you dare.”

Deston stared at him.

Then the lift went ding at the end of the corridor.

They both went very still.

Through the crack in the door, through the open grille, the long corridor. Strip lights. The window at the end, dark now, with Malacca lit up beyond it. The lift doors at the far end, opening.

Somebody came out of the lift.

Gabe couldn’t see much. He didn’t try. He didn’t want to. It wasn’t his to see. Somebody standing just outside the lift with a small bag on one shoulder, looking down the corridor. The whole corridor, both ends, all of it, the way Hyvred looked at a room in the game, edge to edge. Then walking. Down the long corridor, past the other doors, slowly, not hurrying. Looking at everything. The doors, the numbers, the window, the floor.

She stopped at the end. At Dec’s door. His side, the first of the two. The long home with two front doors.

She stood there for a long time.

Gabe couldn’t breathe. Beside him, on the floor of the empty bedroom, Deston had a dowel in one hand and wasn’t moving either.

She looked at the doorbell.

And didn’t ring it.

She knocked.

The door opened.

Gabe saw Dec. From across the corridor, through a crack, past a grille. In a t-shirt, with a cup of tea in his hand, the way he’d been about to come back across the corridor with tea for everybody. He’d opened the door the way he opened every door, left hand first.

And then he saw who it was.

Gabe had known him eleven years. He’d seen Dec at the front of everything. He’d seen him at nineteen per cent at the bottom of a lake, on a phone screen, and on the edge of a pod in the morning after. He’d seen him read a window that said VANTA and say good for them and mean it. He’d seen him go pink. He’d seen him laugh.

He’d never seen his face do this.

It didn’t do anything, for about two seconds. Nothing at all. The way it did when something hit him in the game. Two seconds of nothing.

And then it opened. All of it. Like a door.

“Hi,” said a voice in the corridor. A voice Gabe had heard on a thousand calls, through a game, on a stream, calling left. Small and level and shaking. “Hi.”

Dec put the cup down. On the floor, beside his door, without looking. It was the first time Gabe had ever seen him put a cup down without looking where it went.

“Hi, Hyv,” said Dec.

She hit him.

On the arm, not hard. Gabe heard it. It was the same sound it made in the game.

“That’s for— “ said Hyvred. Her voice went. “I don’t know what that’s for. I had something. On the bus. I had a whole thing.”

“That’s fair,” said Dec.

She hit him again.

“Don’t say that’s fair.”

“Okay.”

“I can see you,” said Hyvred.

Dec didn’t say anything.

“All of you,” said Hyvred. “At once. Without turning round.” She was crying; Gabe could hear it. “Your door. Your feet. The cup. The corridor. Both ends of the corridor. The plant pots. The window. Your face.” A breath. “I wanted to see. Out here. With the new ones. Everything at the edges and you in the middle. All of it at the same time. I’ve never— I didn’t know if I’d— I wanted to see you the way I see you in there.”

There was a long silence in the corridor.

“Okay,” said Dec. His voice was very odd. “Look.”

And he stood there. In his doorway, at the end of the long corridor, under the strip light. Perfectly still, the way he stood at the front of everything. Not to hold anything back this time. So she could look.

She looked for a long time.

Then she made a small sound, and he put his arms round her. Gabe saw that. His arms, coming round, slowly, the way they had at a gate in January, on a clip Gabe had watched more times than he’d ever admit. And his hand, at the back of her head, where in the game there’d have been horns.

There weren’t any horns. Just her head. And his hand on it.

“Mrp,” said Hyvred, into his t-shirt.

“I know,” said Dec. “It’s allowed.”

Gabe made a noise.

He didn’t mean to. It just came out. A noise like a kettle that had been on the boil for twenty-two days, very small, very high. And Deston, who’d been holding a dowel in one hand all this time, reached across the flat-pack bed and put the other hand over his mouth. Exactly the way Rachael did in the game.

But it was too late.

Across the corridor, Dec looked up.

Over Hyvred’s head, through the open grille of the unit opposite, through the crack in the door. Straight at Gabe, on the floor of an empty bedroom with Deston’s hand over his mouth and his face wet.

“Gabe,” said Dec.

Deston took his hand away.

“Hi,” said Gabe. It came out in pieces.

“You told her.”

“She asked,” said Gabe. “Properly. At eleven at night. She said please.” He wiped his face with his whole arm. “I didn’t tell anyone, Dec. Twenty-two days. Not Suzu. Not Dest. Not the auntie. I nearly died. I’ve never kept a secret in my life.”

Dec looked at him for a long time across the corridor.

“I know,” he said. “I noticed.”

Gabe stared at him.

“Your phone,” said Dec. “In the back room. You said nobody.” Something happened at the corner of his mouth. “You’re the worst liar I’ve ever met.”

“You knew?”

“I knew it was something,” said Dec. “I didn’t know what.” He looked down at the top of Hyvred’s head, against his chest. “I didn’t ask.”

“Why not?”

Dec thought about it, standing in his doorway at the end of the long corridor with his arms round somebody.

“Because you were trying so hard,” he said. “And I thought somebody should get to go first.”

Gabe shut the door then. Properly, not a crack. It was the second hardest thing he’d ever done.

He sat down on the floor of the empty living room of the unit opposite, under the framed sticker, with his back against the wall. Deston sat down beside him. Neither of them said anything for a long time.

Through the door, very faint, from the end of the corridor: voices. Hers and his. Not words. Just the sound of two people talking quietly in a corridor. And then, after a while, his laugh. Out loud. Gabe had heard it maybe twenty times in eleven years.

“Dest,” said Gabe.

“Mm.”

“I kept a secret.”

“You did.”

“Twenty-two days.”

“You did,” said Deston. He put his arm round Gabe’s shoulders, the long arm of a man who’d stood beside him in a hundred war zones. He’d never once asked permission to do that either. “I’m very proud of you.”

Gabe cried a bit more. It wasn’t sad. None of it ever was, any more.

His phone buzzed.

Gabe put the phone face down on the floor of the empty living room, under the frame. And put his face in his hands. And laughed.

In the morning, he saw them in the corridor.

He’d slept on Deston’s mattress, on the floor, because the bed was still in pieces. Deston had slept on the folding chair, upright, with his head against the wall, and said in the morning that it was the best night’s sleep he’d ever had. Gabe came out of the unit opposite at half past eight to go and get kopi. And they were at the end of the corridor, by the window. Dec and Hyvred. He’d walked her to her room by the river at midnight, Dec said, and walked back. And at eight she’d come back up in the lift on her own, to see the corridor in the daylight.

Dec saw him and nodded.

“We’re not going in today,” said Dec.

Gabe stopped.

Dec went into the game at nine on Sunday mornings. He always had. Every Sunday, for nineteen months. Into the first of the nine at the round town, or the room over the boatbuilder’s in Masmark. To watch Ama Corrie sweep, or to sweep himself. Gabe had never known him miss one. Not once.

“You always go in on Sunday,” said Gabe.

“Not this one,” said Dec.

Then they went to the lift together, and it came, and they got in. Gabe saw Hyvred look round the inside of it. All four walls, the ceiling, the buttons, the scratched metal, the little mirror. All at once, without turning her head. As if a lift were the most interesting room in the world.

The doors closed.

Gabe stood in the long corridor under the strip lights for a long time. Between the two doors. Dec’s, at the end. And the unit opposite, with its grille open, and its door open. And a frame on the wall inside that said nineteen months, twenty-two. And beside the door, two cacti. A very small one that hadn’t died, and a bigger one that had sat on a bus.

Nobody had chosen one yet.
