Live from Elsewhere · Volume 5: Ask Me Again

Chapter 133

One Banner

1,573 words · 7 min read

Kei woke up on Monday morning, read the notice on his phone in bed, and lay there with the phone on his chest for a long time.

A hall for ten answers only to a banner.

“Hm,” said Kei, to his ceiling.

Crimson Ledger went in at the first dawn.

Of course they did. They’d had a banner since January, the first in the world, and rooms in eighteen Halls. They had more travellers at level thirty-five than anyone, and they’d known this was coming. Everybody in the Creator Program had known something was coming, and couldn’t say. Kei suspected CL had known exactly what, and when. Three tens of them walked through three doors at the same minute on Monday, in three different places, with their red sashes on.

[ The Halls of Ten · Crimson Ledger: 3 tens · in ]

Kei watched it on the Tally in the Hall of Roads. The guilds’ Tally, the one that had said Guilds (1) for six weeks in January and now said Guilds (14). He sat at the bottom of the eye’s stair with his map rolled under his arm and didn’t say anything.

At nine, Kestrel messaged him.

He’d never had a message from Kestrel. Nobody he knew had, except Ice, and Hyvred once, and Tiger at a city with no name. He read it twice.

Kei laughed out loud, alone on the stair.

He looked at that for a while. Next door. She’d said it to Ice, at the bottom of a flight of steps in a white city. Now she was saying it to him.

The problem was ten.

Constellation had thirty-two names in Ama Corrie’s black book, and three thousand more on the south steps every evening, asking. It was big. It was, by Thursday of the week before, the guild everybody wanted. Kei hadn’t planned that. He’d planned twenty people who’d stand next to each other. He’d got the whole world asking to.

But big wasn’t the same as ten.

He sat at the bottom of the stair on Monday afternoon with the guild’s levels on a sheet of paper on his knee and went down it with a pencil. Higu thirty-one. Gigi thirty-one. Himself, thirty-one. Drew thirty-three; Drew had always been ahead. Tiger thirty-three. Hyvred thirty-two, when she was in, which she wasn’t this week, because she was learning to see the edges of her own room. Bee thirty. Salty thirty-two. Maigo, Ina, Hako, Wendy, thirty or close. Niko’s four, twenty-nine to thirty-one. Kite thirty-one. Tobi, Bram, Joy, Rafi, in the twenties.

[ The Halls of Ten · the first five · Lv 35 ]

Thirty-five.

You could go into a hall four levels under it. Everybody knew what happened when you didn’t respect a number. Twenty-five per cent of a level, plus ten for every level it’s got on you. A whole ten, wiped, four levels under, would lose most of a level each and a whole day locked out. In the middle of a race.

He went down the sheet again. He knew what he’d find. He’d known on Monday morning, in bed, before he’d finished reading the notice.

Nine people in a fen with a shield and a scythe and a bell. A company of nine. Thirty-three, thirty-four, the highest group of friends in the world after CL’s best. They’d cleared a door of the second rank first, in April, without racing.

Eight people on a riverbank, somewhere near Brackenford. Xav’s eight. Thirty-two, thirty-three. A founder who’d said, in an inn by a bridge in January, ask me when it’s big. I’ll be first through the door.

Two companies. Not banners. A hall for ten answered only to a banner.

He sat on the stair and looked at his map.

He’d drawn it in December, in a café in Australia, on the back of a menu. Dots for people, lines between them. Who knew who, who’d brought who, who’d stand next to who. He’d drawn it again in January, properly, on paper. Again in March, the night of the founding, at four in the morning, with twenty dots inked solid at the top. And again every few weeks since, as dots got inked, and lines went from dotted to solid.

There were a lot of solid lines now. Thirty-two dots. Hundreds of lines.

And at the edges, still dotted, a few. Thea’s six, a dotted line, not yet. He knew why. Higu had told him, carefully, the little she’d decided he should know. Riverbank, a dotted line from Xav back to the middle. Ask me when it’s big.

And Moonlight Harvest. Nine dots in a cluster on the right of the paper, in a fen, with every one of their lines to the middle still dotted. From the first night. Not yet. Make it big. Then ask me again.

And I’ll say yes before you finish.

Kei looked at the dotted lines for a long time.

He knew what Ice had said. He’d been there. Sunday, seven-thirty in the morning, January, on the phone, Ice just back from the gym. Make it big. Then ask me again. And I’ll say yes before you finish. And again in March, on the steps of the Hall, the night of the founding. Before you finish.

He believed it. He’d never once known Ice say a thing he didn’t mean.

But you didn’t ask somebody because you knew the answer. You asked because the answer was theirs to give, and they had a right to be asked properly, to their face. Not on a call. Not in a message. Not because a notice had gone up on a wall and made it convenient.

Ask him anyway, Drew had said, in January, on the first call, when Niko predicted Ice would say no. That’s manners.

He told the guild on Monday night. All thirty-two, on the call.

“I’m going to ask them,” said Kei. “Riverbank. And Moonlight Harvest. In person. I’m going to walk.”

Nobody said anything for a moment.

“It’s eight days to Fernwick,” said Higu. “Nine, if you go by Brackenford.”

“I’m going by Brackenford. Xav first. He said he’d be first through the door.” Kei looked at his map. “I’m not asking them because of the halls. I want that to be clear. If there weren’t any halls, I’d still be asking. It’s big. That’s what he said. Make it big, then ask me again. It’s big.”

“It’s huge,” said Maigo. “It’s the BIGGEST. I’ve got eleven names for how big it is.”

“Thank you, Maigo.”

“Somebody should go with you,” said Wendy. “In case it goes badly.”

“It’s not going to go badly,” said Gigi. “He’s going to say yes before you finish. It’s horrible. Go and get it over with.”

“I’ll walk with you,” said Drew.

Kei looked at the little square on the call with Drew in it.

“I walked to Tiger,” said Drew. “Through December and half of January, in the snow. I like walking to people.” He smiled, a bit. “And I’d like to see his face. When you ask. I’ve never seen him surprised.”

“He won’t be surprised,” said Kei. “He told me what he’d say. Twice.”

“He’ll be surprised,” said Drew. “Trust me. People always are, when it’s real.”

Ama Corrie stopped him at the north door on Wednesday at the first dawn, with his pack on, and Drew beside him.

“You’re going to ask the swamp one,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Hm.” She looked at him over her spectacles. Ama Corrie heard everything in the Hall. She said it was the dome; it carried. “Bring him back. And the others. The room’s been waiting since the founding.” She sniffed. “I sweep under the chairs on Sundays. All of them. Even the ones nobody sits in.”

Kei looked at her.

“Even the ones nobody sits in,” said Ama Corrie again. “That’s what keeping is.”

She opened the north door for them, the way she’d taught them, though it didn’t stick any more. Then she stood in it and watched them go down the north road in the early light. Two men with packs, toward the river road, and Brackenford, and a fen.

Kei didn’t look back. Drew did, once, and lifted a hand. Ama Corrie, in the doorway, lifted hers.
