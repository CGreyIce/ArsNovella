Live from Elsewhere · Volume 2: The Long Night

Chapter 52

Not Zero

3,800 words · 17 min read

Vivian rang him first.

She’d said she would. On profit day, in the long cool room above the kopitiam, with the photograph of her father on the wall: I’ll tell you the moment I hear. You first. Suzu hadn’t thought much about it at the time. People said things like that.

At fourteen minutes past nine on Monday morning, the twenty-eighth, his phone rang on the end of the bed in the guest room, where he was sitting with the fan going round, putting his socks on. The screen said Vivian Ong (Secretary). He looked at it for two rings before he picked it up.

“Mr Suzu,” said Vivian.

“Yes.”

“Your Employment Pass has been approved. Category III. Game Operations Executive. Two years.” A pause. Paper moving. “Your contract begins today.”

Suzu sat on the edge of the bed with one sock on.

“Okay,” he said.

“Congratulations.”

“Thank you.”

There was a small silence on the line. He could hear the kopitiam downstairs from her office, faintly, the clatter of cups and the radio.

“I’m going to ring Mr Declan now,” said Vivian. “About the other matter.” Another pause. “I wanted you to hear it first. From me. Not from him, in the car, being excited about it.”

Suzu looked at the fan going round.

“Thank you,” he said again, and meant it differently.

“Regards, Mr Suzu,” said Vivian, and rang off.

He sat there for a while with the phone in his hand.

Down the corridor, in the spare room, Gabe was asleep. Gabe slept like a man who’d decided sleep was a competition he intended to win. He’d been in the flat four days. He’d brought his own pillow, a grey one, and put it on top of the one Suzu had bought him. On Sunday night, when Ice drove the two of them to Melaka Sentral to collect Suzu off the nine o’clock bus, Gabe had got out of the car and hugged him before he’d said hello.

“You bought me a pillow,” Gabe had said.

“Everyone brings their own.”

“I know. I used yours.” Gabe had grinned at him in the orange light of the drop-off. “Mine’s for my head. Yours is for my arm. I sleep like this.” He’d demonstrated, in the car park, at twenty past midnight, with his arm round an invisible pillow. Ice had laughed so hard he’d had to sit down on the kerb.

Suzu liked him. He’d decided that in the car park. He didn’t decide things like that quickly, as a rule.

He put the other sock on and went to find Ice.

Ice was in the kitchen with the kettle on, in a T-shirt, with his phone in his hand and a face on. He was looking at the phone the way he looked at the stone in Brackenford when he didn’t want to look at it.

“Vivian rang you,” said Suzu.

“Vivian rang me.” Ice put the phone down. “She rang you first.”

“She said she would.”

Ice looked at him for a long moment across the kitchen. Then he came round the counter and hugged him, quickly and very hard, in the kitchen, at twenty past nine on a Monday. Suzu let him.

“It’s approved,” said Ice, into his shoulder.

“I know. She said.”

“Two years.”

“I know.”

“You’re here.” Ice let go and stood back and looked at him, and his eyes were doing the thing they did. “You’re actually here. Properly. It’s not a guest thing any more. It’s real.”

“It was always real,” said Suzu.

Ice laughed and wiped his face with the back of his hand.

“She wants us at ten,” he said. “For the other thing.” He picked up his car keys from the counter. “She says we have to agree a number in the car. She says she’s not doing it in front of the orchid.”

They agreed a number in the car.

It took about four minutes, between the flat and the lights by the bridge. Ice said a number. Suzu looked out of the window at Malacca going past: the old red buildings, the river, an uncle on a bicycle with a long wooden ladder over his shoulder. He didn’t say anything for a while. Ice didn’t say anything either. He just drove, and waited, the way he’d waited on the narrows. He’d learned that, somewhere. Waiting.

It wasn’t zero.

Suzu had known it wouldn’t be. He’d known since profit day, since he’d heard the words come out of his own mouth in Vivian’s office as if they belonged to somebody else. Not zero. He hadn’t meant to say them. He’d meant to say whatever he says, and stop there, the way he always stopped there. But they’d got out on their own. Ice had heard them, and laughed. And Suzu had sat in the borrowed chair with the corner of his mouth going up, feeling something in his chest that he didn’t have a name for. Something like the moment the scythe catches a light before it falls.

He’d thought about it every day since. On the bus to Singapore. At the kitchen table at home, reading his mum the post. In the egg, in the ring, by the moonbells. He hadn’t said anything about it to anyone. Not to Ice, not to his mum, not to Gabe or Deston or Kotori, not to chat. But he’d thought about it. In the back of his head, all the time, the way you think about a light in a jar you haven’t decided what to do with yet.

He wanted it.

Not the money. The salary was money. The salary was a smoke bomb, something solid for the Immigration Department to look at, and it was enough. More than enough. Enough for him and enough for home. The game was money too, more of it, and that was his and went back into the game. This was something else. This was the thing he’d tried to say to Ice in Vivian’s office and hadn’t quite managed.

What we’re making. Mine too.

The lights by the bridge changed.

“Okay,” said Suzu.

Ice didn’t say anything. He just reached across and put his hand on Suzu’s shoulder for a second, and took it away, and drove on. Neither of them said the number again. Not then, and not afterwards. It was on a piece of paper in Vivian’s office, and that was where it lived.

Vivian had the papers out before they sat down.

She had the orchid moved to the windowsill. Suzu noticed that. She’d moved it, he decided, so that she could say she hadn’t done it in front of the orchid.

“The number,” she said.

Ice told her.

She wrote it down without any expression at all. Then she looked at Suzu over her glasses.

“Mr Suzu?”

“Yes,” said Suzu.

“You agree.”

“I agree.”

“Good.” She turned the papers round. “Then here is what happens. I’ve written to the Expatriate Services Division this morning, to tell them before they find out. That’s the only way I know how to do anything. A transfer of shares, from Mr Declan to you. There’s stamp duty, which Mr Declan is paying, because he insisted, on the phone, three times.” She looked at Ice. Ice looked at the orchid on the windowsill. “I’ll lodge it with the Companies Commission. Two days. Then your name goes on the register of members of Moonlight Harvest Workshop Sendirian Berhad. Permanently, unless you sell them.”

“I won’t sell them.”

“I didn’t think you would,” said Vivian. She held out a pen. “Sign here. And here. And initial here.”

He signed. Here, and here, and initialled here. His signature was small and neat and very upright. It had been since school.

Vivian took the papers back and looked at them. She looked at his signature for a moment longer than she needed to.

“That’s a very good signature,” she said.

“Thank you.”

“The best-formatted CV I saw this year,” said Vivian, “and now the best signature. You’ll make me look bad.” She squared the papers. “Your first salary will be paid on Thursday, the last working day of the month. It’ll be small. Four days. Pro rata.” She looked at him. “I’ll make sure it’s right to the sen.”

“I know you will,” said Suzu.

Downstairs, as they left, the auntie behind the counter looked up from her cups and saw the two of them. Her face lit up.

“Gardening boys!” she said. “Two of you!”

“Good morning, Auntie,” said Ice.

The auntie looked at Suzu: tall, thin, pale, in black, with his hands in his pockets. She looked at him for a long time. Then she reached under the counter and took out a paper bag of something warm and put it into his hands.

“Kaya puffs,” she said. “You’re too thin. Vivi says you’re staying.” She patted his arm. “Eat properly.”

Suzu looked down at the bag in his hands.

“Everybody says that,” he said.

“Because it’s true,” said the auntie.

On Tuesday morning at seven, Ice went to a gym for the first time in his life.

It was Suzu’s fault, mostly. And the game’s.

They’d worked it out between them on Monday night, on the floor of the back room at Melaka Raya, after the papers were signed. They’d been comparing the Long Night: Ice’s twelve hours on the plank against Suzu’s twelve hours in the water. The game had always been honest about it. On the very first day, in Hollis’s yard, Ice had sat down against the fence and told twenty people that the stamina bar wasn’t just a number, that your arm really got tired. They’d all known it. They just hadn’t thought about what it meant.

“It’s yours,” Suzu had said. He’d been sitting cross-legged with Ice’s party log open on his phone and his own beside it. “The stamina. The egg doesn’t make it up. It reads you. Your heart, your breathing, how tired you actually are. That’s why your forms go to pieces after eight hours. It isn’t the game being tired. It’s you.”

“So?”

“So if you were less tired,” said Suzu, “you’d last longer.” He’d scrolled. “At four in the morning you were dropping turns on the beat. Not because you didn’t know how. Because your real body was tired of holding a real shield up, for eight hours, lying in a bath of gel. And the grades. Your back foot is better on days you’ve walked somewhere. I checked. Three per cent, about.” He’d looked up. “Your body learns it here, and then it already knows it in there.”

Ice had looked at the numbers on the phone for a long time.

“There’s a gym,” he’d said slowly. “In the building. I’ve never been in it.”

“I know,” Suzu had said. “I’ve been in it every morning since I got here.”

So on Tuesday at seven, the three of them went down in the lift to the gym. Ice and Suzu and Gabe, who’d heard about it at breakfast and invited himself, because Gabe invited himself to everything and was always right to.

It was a small room with a window onto the car park and a smell of rubber floor. A rack of weights. A bench. A bar fixed high on the wall for pull-ups. A mat. Nobody else was in it. Ice stood in the doorway and looked at it the way he’d once looked at the little wooden room with rain on the window.

“I don’t know how to do any of this,” he said.

“I know,” said Suzu. “That’s why we’re here.”

Gabe did the weights. Gabe knew weights, it turned out, the way Gabe knew everything he knew: without making a fuss about it and without stopping. He showed Ice how to hold the bar, and where his feet went, and how to breathe. “Out on the push, like your cut,” he said, and Ice stared at him, because it was exactly what Suzu had typed in chat once, about the Falling Cut. Gabe stood behind him with his hands ready, and said “good” and “again” and “slower” in his plain straightforward voice, and never once laughed at him.

Suzu did the rest. Bodyweight things. Push-ups, pull-ups, holding yourself still in odd shapes on the mat. Calisthenics, Suzu called it, and did it the way he did everything: quietly, precisely, with no wasted movement. He showed Ice once. Then he made Ice do it, and watched, and corrected one thing at a time, never two. And he counted, out loud, the way Deston counted on the Landing. One. And two.

Ice did four pull-ups on Tuesday and his arms shook so badly afterwards that he had to hold his kopi with both hands. On Wednesday he did four again, and Suzu said “good,” because the fourth one had been better. On Thursday, five.

“Every day,” said Suzu, on Thursday, on the mat. “Until you don’t need us. Then on your own.”

“Every day?”

“You held a ford for twelve hours,” said Suzu. “You can hold a bar for thirty seconds.”

Ice looked at him, lying on the mat, sweating, in a gym he’d lived in the same building as for years and never once walked into.

“You’ve been planning this,” he said.

“Since the plank,” said Suzu.

On Wednesday evening, in the ring, Ice asked Kotori.

The five of them were sitting on the stones with their feet in the warm water, the way they did now at the end of a night, with the moonbells open and ringing. Ice, Deston, Kotori, Sheng Yang, Suzu. Gabe was somewhere far off on the Grey Coast, hitting things with a hammer.

“Kotori,” said Ice. “Would you like to work for the company?”

Kotori nearly fell off her stone.

“Sorry, what?”

“Moonlight Harvest Workshop. The real one. In Malacca.” He’d thought about how to say it all day, and it was coming out exactly the way he hadn’t planned. “You’d be paid. Properly. Vivian would do it. You could be remote, from Singapore, around your lectures, if you’d rather. You don’t have to come here. Or you could come here, if you wanted. Sometimes. Or more than sometimes.” He stopped. “It’s three hours on the bus. Suzu does it every week.”

Kotori didn’t say anything for a long time. She sat on her stone with her staff across her knees and the bell on it very still, looking at him. Suzu watched her from the ninth heron. He saw her face do four different things in a row, the way it did when she was trying not to feel all of them at once.

“Would I—“ She stopped. “Sorry. Would I eat with you?”

“Eat with us?”

“You said. On stream. You all talk about the mamak. With the—“ She made a shape with her hands. “The roti. And Suzu gets chicken rice balls. And Gabe has the big one. I’ve watched you talk about it.” She went pink. “Sorry. That’s a weird thing to ask first. I just. I’d like that. Eating with people after. Like a—“ She didn’t finish.

“Like a family,” said Deston, gently, from his stone.

Kotori went pinker and looked at the moonbells.

“Sorry,” she said.

“You’d eat with us,” said Ice. “Every time you were here. Every single time.”

Kotori looked at him across the warm water. Then at Suzu, and Deston, and Sheng Yang. Then back at the moonbells. The bell on her staff rang once, very softly, because her hands had started to shake.

“I have to ask my parents,” she said. “Sorry. I have to. They’ll say—I don’t know what they’ll say. My dad will say what about your degree. My mom will ask if you’re nice.” She looked up. “I’ll tell her you’re nice.”

“Tell her the truth,” said Sheng Yang.

“That is the truth,” said Kotori, and then, realising what she’d said, put her hands over her face.

Nobody laughed. Suzu, against the ninth heron, felt the corner of his mouth go up about a millimetre.

She’d ask her parents. She’d say she was asking. But he’d seen her face when Ice said every single time, and he knew what it had done. It was the same thing his own face had done in Vivian’s office, when he’d heard himself say not zero.

His first salary came in on Thursday, the thirty-first of December, at four minutes past ten in the morning.

He was in the egg when it came. He was in the ring, in the heron-stones, at the end of a long night of levelling with the others in the old fen. His phone buzzed on the folding table in the back room, where he couldn’t see it, and he didn’t find out until he logged out at noon.

Four hundred and thirty-eight ringgit and seventy-one sen. Four days of five thousand a month, less the deductions. Mostly tax: anyone who’d been in Malaysia less than half a year was a non-resident to the Inland Revenue Board, and non-residents paid thirty per cent, flat. A little for the provident fund. Vivian had listed every one of them on a separate sheet, attached to the email, under the heading So that nobody is surprised. At the bottom she’d added, in a different font: This evens out once you’re resident. Please don’t worry about it.

He looked at it for a long time, sitting on the edge of his egg in the back room at Melaka Raya, with the fan going round. Beside him, Ice was asleep in his own egg with the lid up, his mouth slightly open, one hand trailing over the side.

It was the first money anyone had ever paid him for being himself.

He’d been paid before. Of course he had. For other things, for jobs, for hours. Never for this. Never for standing in a church with a scythe, and catching the lights, and being the person who did that. He looked at the number and thought about Wenna’s dog, and the long meadow in Larkhollow, and the barrow wisps’ warm blue light settling in his hand on the first night. Aldo’s scythe, always cutting a bit high. He’d done all of that for nothing. For a game. Because Ice had said there’s a scythe in a text, and he’d come.

And now it was four days of a job, with his name on a register in an office above a kopitiam. Not zero.

He sent it home. All of it, this month. It was small. It wasn’t the point. He did it on his phone, sitting on the edge of the egg, and then he video-called his mum.

She picked up on the second ring, the way she always did, with the phone held close to her face. The kitchen was behind her: the same kitchen, the same calendar on the wall, the same light. He told her it was there. He told her what it was, and where it had come from, and that next month it would be bigger. And then he told her about the register, and his name on it. The number on the piece of paper in Vivian’s office. Not what it was. What it meant.

She was quiet for a while on the call. She couldn’t see much of his face, he knew, even that close. She’d always been able to hear it, though.

“You sound happy,” she said.

He did. He could hear it too.

Deston came up on the six o’clock bus, for New Year’s, the way he’d said he would.

So there were four of them in the flat that night. Ice and Suzu and Gabe and Deston. The back row of a school choir, and one more. Deston brought egg tarts again, from the same shop, and Gabe ate four before anyone else had one. They ate at the long table on Ice’s side of the flat, with the opening in the wall to his parents’ side lit up and people moving about beyond it. At eleven o’clock, Xav messaged the party from Japan, an hour ahead.

At five to midnight, the four of them went out onto the balcony.

It was small and high and looked out over the town, toward the river and the old part of Malacca. The air was warm and thick and smelled of rain that hadn’t come yet. Somewhere down below, a long way off, people were counting.

Suzu stood at the end of the balcony with his arms on the rail and watched.

He thought about everything that had happened in six weeks. A bus with a pillow on it. A contract on four plain pages. A scythe that cut a bit high. A church full of singing lights, and nineteen of them going up past his blade, and one he’d kept. Eighty-one lights in a jar now. A long night, and a man on a plank who didn’t kneel. A tall friend from a winter guild coming down out of the alders. A man from Melbourne with his arm round a pillow in a car park. And a piece of paper in an office above a kopitiam, with his name on it, and a number.

He didn’t say any of it. He never did.

Down below, the counting reached one. Over the river, the first firework went up, and burst, gold, over the old red town. Then another, and another, until the whole sky over Malacca was full of them, gold and white and green, falling slowly. Like the lights in the church, going up the wrong way.

Beside him, Ice was laughing at something Gabe had said. Deston was singing under his breath, something in four verses. Gabe had his arm round both of them.

Suzu stood at the end of the balcony and watched the sky, and didn’t say anything at all. But the corner of his mouth went up about a millimetre, in the dark, under the fireworks, and stayed there for a long time.

Not zero.
