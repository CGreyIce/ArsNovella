Live from Elsewhere · Volume 3: Constellation

Chapter 79

Two Per Cent

1,956 words · 9 min read

Suzu read his mum her post every Friday at seven in the morning, and had done since he was small.

He did it from Malacca now, on video, sitting on the end of the bed in the guest room with the fan going round. His mum was in her kitchen in Singapore with the phone propped against the sugar tin. She held each letter up to the camera, close, the way he’d taught her. He read them out. Bills, mostly. The town council. A flyer for a supermarket. Sometimes a card from an aunt. It took ten minutes, fifteen if there was a form. Then she’d tell him about the neighbours, and he’d tell her about the swamp, and she’d say eat properly, and he’d say I am, and that was Friday.

This Friday there was a letter from the eye clinic.

He knew the envelope before she’d got it open. He’d been reading envelopes from that clinic for most of his life. The blue logo in the corner, the window with her name in it. Usually it was an appointment. Sometimes it was a change to an appointment. He’d read every one of them out in a steady voice, because she was listening.

“Read it,” said his mum. “It’s from the clinic. I can feel the logo.”

She couldn’t feel the logo. She knew the envelope by its weight. He didn’t say so.

He read it.

He read the first paragraph and stopped.

It was a standard paragraph. Dear Madam. Her name. Her patient number. We are writing to all patients with your condition to inform you of a new procedure. He’d read a hundred paragraphs like it. He read it out in his steady voice, the way he always did.

Then the second paragraph, and he stopped reading out loud.

He didn’t mean to. His voice just stopped. He sat on the end of the bed with the fan going round and read the second paragraph silently, and then again. Then a third time, slowly. A trial. A new procedure, developed with the company that made the Cradles. A small device behind the ear, and a camera on a pair of glasses. It did not use the eyes. It sent the seeing directly. Eleven patients so far. Results very encouraging. You may be eligible for assessment.

“Suzu?” said his mum.

He looked at the phone. At his mum in her kitchen, with the letter held up to the camera in both hands, waiting. Her face turned toward the screen, toward his voice, the way it always was. Her eyes, which hadn’t been able to see him properly since he was small, looking at the place where she knew he’d be.

“Suzu. What does it say?”

He read it to her.

All of it. In order, in his steady voice, the way he always did, every word. Because she was listening, and because he had been her eyes since he was small, and you didn’t stop being somebody’s eyes because the letter was hard. He read trial and device and camera and encouraging and eligible. He read the third paragraph, about the assessment, and the appointment they’d offered her in April.

And then he read the last paragraph, because it was there, and you read to the end.

Please note that this procedure is not currently covered by insurance or government subsidy. The estimated cost, if you are found to be eligible, is approximately ten thousand US dollars, not including assessment.

He stopped.

His mum didn’t say anything for a long time. In her kitchen in Singapore, she lowered the letter slowly to the table and put her hands flat on top of it.

“Ten thousand,” she said.

“US.”

“US,” said his mum. She was quiet for a moment. Then she said, in the voice she used for the town council and the supermarket flyers: “Then it’s for someone else.”

“Mum.”

“It’s for someone else, Suzu. Someone who has it.” She folded the letter, very carefully, along its creases. “You read me my post. That’s enough. That’s been enough for years.”

He didn’t say anything.

He thought about the sum. He’d done it on the bus, on the first day, coming up to Malacca with a duffel and a pillow: the salary, and what went home, and what was left. He’d done it again on the first pay day, when all four hundred and thirty-eight ringgit and seventy-one sen went home. And he’d done it since, the other money. The money from the game, which was more than the salary now, and which was his, and which he’d put back into the game. Every gold of it. Into the lights. Eighty-one of them, in a jar on a stone, glowing, pointing north.

He didn’t have ten thousand dollars. Not anywhere. Not close.

“I’ll ring you tonight,” he said.

“Eat properly,” said his mum.

“I am,” said Suzu, and ended the call, and sat on the end of the bed with the fan going round for a long time.

Ice was in the kitchen.

He’d been back from the gym since twenty past seven. Suzu had heard the door, and the lift, and then nothing. No kettle. No radio. Just Ice in the kitchen, being quiet. That was unusual. Ice was never quiet in a kitchen. He talked to the kettle.

Suzu went in.

Ice was sitting at the little table by the window with two mugs of kopi-o in front of him. The two that didn’t match. One of them was Suzu’s, already poured, going cold. Ice had his phone face down on the table beside him. He looked up when Suzu came in, and Suzu saw his face, and understood that Ice already knew.

He didn’t know how. He didn’t ask. He sat down.

“I read it at six,” said Ice. “On the news. Before the gym.” He turned his mug round on the table, slowly. “I knew it was Friday.”

Suzu didn’t say anything.

“Is it her?” said Ice. “The clinic. Did they write?”

“They wrote,” said Suzu.

“How much?”

“Ten thousand.” Suzu looked at his kopi-o. “US. Not on insurance. She’s eligible for assessment. April.” He picked up the mug and held it and didn’t drink it. “She said it’s for someone else.”

Ice was quiet for a while.

“I can lend you ten thousand,” he said.

Suzu went very still.

Not the ordinary still. Ice had seen the ordinary still a thousand times: the half-shut eyes, the back against the ninth heron. This was the other one. The one from the bus station on the first day, when he’d said you look like you.

“US,” said Ice. “Today, if you want. Vivian can make it tidy.”

Suzu put the mug down.

He thought about a great many things, sitting at the little table by the window. About his mum in her kitchen, with her hands flat on a folded letter. About being somebody’s eyes since he was small, and maybe not being needed for that. He didn’t know what that would feel like. He thought he’d find out. About the money in the game, all of it, gone into a jar of lights pointing north. About his own voice in Vivian’s office on profit day, saying not zero, and meaning it. About owing.

“Lend,” he said.

“Lend,” agreed Ice.

“Not give.”

“Lend. I know you. I wouldn’t try.”

“I pay it back from the game,” said Suzu. “Not the salary. The salary goes home.”

“From the game.”

“With interest.”

“No.”

“Then I’m not taking it.”

Ice looked at him across the table. Suzu looked back. The fan went round in the guest room down the hall. Somewhere a motorbike went past in the street below.

“One per cent,” said Ice.

“Two.”

“Suzu. You’re supposed to argue it down.”

“Two,” said Suzu.

Ice put his face in his hands. When he took them away he was laughing, the quiet laugh, the one he did when somebody in chat had caught him out.

“Fine,” he said. “Two.”

“Two per cent,” said Suzu.

He picked up the kopi-o and drank it, cold, all of it. Then he put the mug down on the table very carefully, the way his mum had folded the letter.

“Thank you,” he said.

He’d hardly ever said it to Ice. Not like this. Not in all the years of calls and games and the bus and the pillow and the job and the share. He’d said good, and okay, and not zero. He’d never said thank you like this, because there’d never been anything big enough to say it to without it being too small.

Ice didn’t say anything. He just nodded, once, the way Suzu nodded.

The kitchen door opened, and Gabe came in in his gym shirt, still wet from the shower, and stopped in the doorway. He looked at the two of them at the little table, with the two mugs and Ice’s red eyes and Suzu’s face, which was doing something Gabe had never seen it do.

“Is this a car conversation?” said Gabe. “Should I get the car?”

“Two per cent,” said Ice.

“Of what?”

“He argued it up,” said Ice.

Gabe looked at Suzu for a long moment.

“Of course he did,” said Gabe, and went to put the kettle on.

He rang his mum at nine that night, from the guest room, with the fan going round.

“Mum,” he said. “Put your name on the list.”

His mum didn’t say anything for a while.

“Who’s paying?” she said.

“Me,” said Suzu. “Slowly. From the game. Ice is lending it first. With interest.” He paused. “Two per cent.”

“Two per cent,” said his mum. She was quiet again. “Did he ask for two per cent?”

“I asked for two per cent. He wanted one.”

His mum laughed. It was a sound Suzu hadn’t heard in a long time. A real one, surprised out of her, in her kitchen in Singapore, at nine o’clock on a Friday night.

“You’re my son,” she said.

“I know.”

“Tell him thank you.”

“I did,” said Suzu. “I said it. Out loud.”

His mum was quiet for a moment.

“Then he knows,” she said.

In the game, they were a day out of Fernwick on the road south by then. The six of them, with two more walking behind: a silent Dutch spearman called Bram and a girl from Cebu called Joy, who’d held some steps once. All of them going toward a round hall where seven roads met, as friends and not members.

Suzu walked beside Ice at the front, with the scythe on his back and the jar in his pack, glowing on one side through the canvas, pointing north.

He didn’t say anything for most of the morning.

Then, at the top of a long hill, with the road going on ahead of them into heather and grey stone, he said:

“Two per cent.”

“Two per cent,” said Ice.

That was all. They walked on. And for the first time in his life that he could remember, Suzu let himself think about something he’d never let himself think. A kitchen in Singapore. A letter on a table. His mum lifting her face from it and looking, really looking, across the room at the place where he was standing. And seeing him.

Possibly.
