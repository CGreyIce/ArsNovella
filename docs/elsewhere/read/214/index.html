Live from Elsewhere · Volume 9: Properly

Chapter 214

Tempo

4,904 words · 21 min read

Val had thought Declan was weird since she was seventeen.

She had reasons. She’d never told anybody what they were, and she wasn’t going to start now. They were small things, mostly, the kind you notice in a classroom when you’re seventeen and quiet and you sit at the back so you can watch people. Nobody else had noticed them. Or if they had, they’d decided it was just Declan, the way you decide a door sticks and stop thinking about it.

Val hadn’t stopped thinking about it. That was the thing about her. She didn’t stop.

In March, on the north-west road into Masmark, she’d told Jun Liang he wasn’t weird any more. She’d meant it. She’d watched him queue an hour at the oldest counter in the Rim Hall and rent the smallest Hall in the city and sweep it. Then he’d held somebody’s hand up four hours of stair from the bottom of a lake. And she’d thought, oh. He’s just a person. He was always just a person. I was seventeen.

She was wrong about that. She found out in a cellar.

The netloft’s cellar was very wet. Xav had said so in his letter in April, and Xav didn’t usually exaggerate in letters. If anything he left things out.

Salty had found it in March, the week Riverbank took over the room. He’d been looking for somewhere to put his sword down that wasn’t on top of Azac. He’d moved a heap of old nets in the corner by the wavy window, and under them there’d been a trapdoor. Under the trapdoor, a ladder. And at the bottom of the ladder, a round stone room the size of the netloft above it, with a low ceiling full of old iron hooks and an iron grate in the harbour wall at one end. The harbour came in through the grate twice a day.

At low tide the floor was just wet. At high tide the water came up to your ankles, cold and green, and moved very slightly in and out with the harbour, as if the room were breathing.

Val loved it.

She’d taken to going down there in the evenings with a lamp and her book, when the netloft was too loud. The netloft was always too loud. Eight people in a Hall for ten, and RX counting things. She’d sit on the bottom rung of the ladder with her feet up out of the water and read lines out loud to the hooks.

Not game lines. Real ones. Auditions, the kind she practised at home in Singapore. She’d found out the cellar was the best room she’d ever read in. Stone all round, low and round, with water on the floor. Every sound she made went out and came back to her a quarter of a second later, softer, from every side at once. She could hear exactly what she’d done wrong.

Everything she’d ever learned about it came back to the same thing. A line has a tempo before it has a meaning. Get the tempo right and the meaning comes. Get it wrong and nothing you do with your voice will save it.

She’d learned the cellar’s tempo the way she learned a line. The drip from the third hook. The harbour breathing at the grate. Her own feet when she climbed down, splash, splash, and the echo.

So when Xav said downstairs, and Declan said okay, she knew she’d hear it better than anybody.

They came down at the third dawn on Wednesday, the tenth of May, with the tide halfway in.

Riverbank first, because it was their cellar. Xav went down the ladder without a lamp, because he knew the way, and stood in the middle of the floor in the dark with the water round his ankles. Then the rest of them, to sit on the stone steps along the curved wall where the old net-makers had sat to work. Malcolm with his stick. John with his mirror. Azac, much too big, eating bread. RX, already counting the hooks. Ryan, with his hands in his coat. Jun Liang, humming. And Val, at the end, near the ladder, with her book on her knees.

Then Hyvred.

She came down the ladder carefully, with her bow on her back, and stopped on the bottom rung with her boots just over the water. She looked round the cellar once, the whole of it, edge to edge, in the light of John’s lamp. Then she sat down on the bottom rung where Val usually sat, and pulled her tail up out of the wet, and folded her hands.

Xav had asked for her. In the letter. Bring hyv. She can call it. Val hadn’t understood what that meant until Xav explained it at the long table in the netloft on Monday night, with his sketchbook open.

“Call it for both of us,” he’d said to Hyvred. “Out loud. Whatever you see. Where I am. Where he is. What he’s about to do.” He’d looked at Declan. “If that’s okay.”

“That’s fair,” Declan had said.

“It’s not fair,” said Hyvred. “I’ll be calling for you. He’ll hear me calling where you are.”

“He’ll hear you calling where he is too,” said Xav. “That’s the point. I want to know what it looks like. From outside. All of it at once.” He’d tapped the sketchbook. “I’ve got two hundred pages of his feet. I’ve got nothing of the whole room.”

Hyvred had looked at Declan. He’d nodded, once.

“Fine,” she’d said. “Both of you. Out loud. And if either of you complains, I’m hitting you.”

“That’s fair,” Declan had said again.

The last one down was Kestrel.

She hadn’t been asked, exactly. She’d come to the netloft door on Tuesday night and asked Wendy if she could watch. Wendy had asked her who’d stand next to her, and Kestrel had said nobody, I’m just watching. Wendy had said that’s not an answer and sent her inside to ask Xav. Xav had looked at her for a long time and said the top of the ladder. Don’t come down. So she sat at the top of the ladder, in the trapdoor, with her legs hanging into the dark. Brann was somewhere behind her in the netloft, keeping out of the way.

And then Declan.

He came down the ladder with his shield on his back and the lamp in his hand. At the bottom, before he stepped off into the water, he put his hand up to the little floating light at his shoulder and touched it.

“Not for them,” said RX, under his breath.

“Not for them,” said Malcolm.

He stepped off the ladder. Splash. The water came up round his boots and the echo came back from every side, and Val wrote down in her book, without meaning to, one.

“Whenever,” said Xav.

“Whenever,” said Declan.

Nobody moved.

Val had heard about this part. Everybody had. The thirty-one seconds in October, the minute and forty in January. Two people standing ten paces apart, neither of them deciding. She’d thought it would be like a held breath. It wasn’t. It was like the silence before somebody speaks, in a recording booth, when the light’s gone red and you can hear your own blood.

The harbour breathed at the grate. The third hook dripped. Plink. A long time. Plink.

“Both still,” said Hyvred, from the ladder. Very quietly, very level, the way Val had heard her call the fells on Niko’s stream. “Xav’s back foot. Dec’s back foot. Dec’s weight’s going forward. Not yet.”

Plink.

“Now,” said Hyvred.

Declan went.

Splash-splash. Two steps, fast. Then nothing.

Val heard it before she saw it, the way she always heard things first. The two quick steps and the long rest. She’d watched the fight in January from the step of the Seven Below and written it down without understanding it. Now she understood it. It was in the water. You could hear exactly how he fought.

Two quick. A long rest. Two quick. A long rest.

That was Declan. That had been Declan for a year and a half, since a stone in a fen. The waiting. The long rest on the back foot, with the step already started, until somebody else decided. And then two quick, and it was over.

“Xav left,” said Hyvred. “Dec back. Xav’s not going. Xav’s waiting.”

Splash-splash. Rest.

“Xav in,” said Hyvred. “In. Under the rim—“

“One,” said RX.

It went on.

Val didn’t count. RX counted. She’d decided in January to let him do it, because he needed to and she didn’t. She listened instead. She wrote it down the way she’d mark up a script. Dashes for the rests, dots for the steps. The page filled up.

Declan. The rests changed length. That was the cleverness of it. You never knew how long the rest would be, so you never knew when the two quick would come. A whole fight of people waiting for the end of a silence.

But underneath it, now, there was something else. Something that hadn’t been there in January.

Xav.

Xav’s feet were in the rests.

Val heard it at about six minutes, and then she couldn’t stop hearing it. Every time Declan rested, Xav moved. Not much. A slide of the back foot, a half-step, a turn. Shff in the water, soft, almost nothing. And every time the two quick steps came, Xav was already somewhere else. As if he’d heard the silence the way she had, and known how long it would be.

“Oh,” said Malcolm, beside her. Very quietly.

“What?” said Jun Liang.

“He’s learned the rests,” said Malcolm. “He’s not reading the steps. He’s reading the gaps between them.” He watched. “Four months. Every first dawn. With a pigeon.”

“Two,” said RX. The thigh.

“Three.” The shoulder.

“Xav right,” said Hyvred. “Dec turning. Xav’s already— he’s already there, he’s there before—“

“Four.”

And Xav didn’t look at it.

That was the second new thing. In January, RX said, Xav had watched the number the whole fight. He’d paid for it every time he went in, without Declan having to spend a point. You were already paying for it. Xav had written that on the first page of a new sketchbook in January, in pencil, and underlined it.

Now he didn’t look. Val watched his face, in the lamplight, between his blades. Not once. Not a glance to the corner of his eye where the number was. He fought as if it wasn’t there.

“Five,” said RX, at sixteen minutes. “Six.” At seventeen. Then a long gap, and then three very fast, so fast RX’s voice went up into a squeak. “Seven, eight, nine—“

“Nine at eighteen minutes,” said RX. He sounded frightened. “In January it was nine at thirty-nine.”

Nobody said anything.

Val looked at her page. The dashes and dots. Declan’s rhythm, the same as ever. Two quick, a long rest. And Xav, in the rests, every time, before. He’d found it. Whatever he’d been looking for at first dawn with a chalk mark and a pigeon. He’d found the tempo, and now he was ahead of it.

At the top of the ladder, Kestrel had both hands round the edge of the trapdoor.

“Ten,” said RX, at twenty-two minutes.

“Eleven.” Twenty-four.

Declan was hurt. You could hear it. Not in his voice; he didn’t say anything. In the water. His two quick steps were still two quick steps, but there was something under them now, a drag. The left foot, the one Xav kept coming in on. Val had marked it on the page without meaning to, a little hook on every second dot.

“Dec’s slow on the left,” said Hyvred. Her voice had changed. It was still level, but it was very tight, the way it had been on the longest night when the lake stood up. She was calling for both of them. She’d said she would. “Xav knows. Xav’s going left.”

“Twelve.”

RX’s voice had gone very small. “Thirteen,” he said again, to himself, as if he didn’t believe it.

Val looked at Azac. Azac had stopped eating. He was holding the bread in both hands in his lap, not looking at it. She’d never seen Azac stop eating in a fight. Ryan had taken his hands out of his pockets. Jun Liang wasn’t humming.

At twenty-eight minutes, Xav went in again on the left, in the rest. And came out. And Declan’s window said fourteen.

“Fourteen,” said RX. And then he stopped. He didn’t say anything else. He didn’t need to. Everybody on the steps could do the sum. Fourteen in twenty-eight minutes, and Declan at eleven per cent, and Xav at sixty. One more. One more in the rest, on the left. And it would be over.

The cellar was very quiet.

The harbour breathed at the grate.

“Xav’s going to—“ said Hyvred.

She didn’t finish.

Afterward, on the steps, Val would try to explain what she heard, and fail. She’d fail for days. She’d try it on Jun Liang and on Malcolm and on Xav, and none of them would quite get it. Not because they were stupid. Because they’d been looking, and she’d been listening.

She heard the rest.

Declan’s two quick steps, the left foot dragging. And then the rest, the long one. The silence on the back foot that he’d been doing for a year and a half, that Xav had learned. And in the rest, very soft, shff, Xav’s back foot going, the way it had gone fourteen times. In, on the left, under the rim, before—

And the rest wasn’t there.

There wasn’t a silence. Where the silence should have been, where it had been for twenty-eight minutes and a year and a half, there was a step. And another. And another.

Splash-splash-splash-splash-splash.

Val’s pencil went through the page.

She’d never heard anything like it. Not in this game. Not anywhere.

He didn’t stop. That was the whole of it, and it was so simple she wanted to scream. He’d stopped for a year and a half. Waited, every time, on the back foot, until the other person decided. And now he didn’t. He came forward into the place where Xav had been going and kept coming. Sword, shield rim, sword, step, step, rim, sword, step. Every beat. No gaps. No rests. Not two quick and a long silence but a hundred quick, one after another, like rain on a tin roof. Like somebody who’d read a line slow and careful for a year and a half suddenly reading it so fast you couldn’t breathe, and getting every word right.

“Dec— left— Dec right, Dec in— Xav— Dec—“ Hyvred was trying to call it. Val could hear her trying. Her voice going faster and faster to keep up, and not keeping up. “Dec— I—“

She stopped.

“I can’t,” said Hyvred, from the ladder. Her voice cracked right across. “I can’t call it. There’s no gaps. There’s nowhere to put the words.”

Xav was answering.

Val heard that too. That was the worst of it, or the best. Xav, who’d spent four months learning not to answer, learning to wait and go with, was answering. He had to. You couldn’t go with something that decided a hundred times a minute. You couldn’t wait for a decision when there was a new one every beat. Every blade stroke that came at him, he had to meet, half a breath behind, the way Kestrel had met them on the oak floor at Elmcross. The way Xav himself had met them in October, a long time ago, before the chalk mark and the pigeon.

Shff-shff-shff. Xav’s feet in the water, going backward. For the first time in the whole fight, going backward.

The water was everywhere. It was coming up off the floor in sheets with every step, catching the lamplight. Xav was against the curved wall now, below the steps, below Val. Close enough that the spray came up onto her book. She didn’t move it.

The shield rim came round. Not hard. Just exactly where Xav’s left blade was going to be, because Xav was answering, and answering meant you were always where the question put you. The blade went down into the water with a sound like a fish jumping.

And the sword came round after it and stopped, very lightly, at the side of Xav’s neck.

Plink, went the third hook.

Fifty-one seconds. Val looked at the clock in the corner of her window afterward and worked it out. From the rest that wasn’t there to the sword at Xav’s neck. Fifty-one seconds.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

The water settled. It took ages. It went on slopping against the curved walls and the steps and the grate, smaller and smaller, until it was just the harbour breathing again, in and out. Declan stood in the middle of it with his sword at Xav’s neck and eleven per cent and his left foot dragging. He was breathing very hard. Val had never seen him breathe hard.

“Yield?” said Declan.

Xav was staring at him.

“What,” said Xav, “was that.”

Declan didn’t say anything.

“Nine years,” said Xav. His voice was very odd. “I’ve known you nine years. I’ve watched every— I’ve got two hundred pages. Two hundred. Of your feet. I’ve drawn every way you’ve ever stood.” He didn’t move his neck. “You’ve never done that.”

“No.”

“Not once.”

“No.”

“Not in October. Not in January. Not at Elmcross, not in the seven, not—“ Xav stopped. “Where’s that from?”

Declan thought about it, with the sword still resting on Xav’s neck.

“Before,” he said.

“Before what?”

“Before I learned to wait,” said Declan.

Xav looked at him for a long, long time, standing against the wall of the cellar in the green water with three per cent.

“You learned to wait,” he said slowly.

“In the fen,” said Declan. “With the stones. Before that I didn’t.” He paused. “Before that I just went. All the time. At everybody.”

At the top of the ladder, very quietly, so quietly Val only just heard it, Kestrel said, “Oh.”

“You never showed me,” said Xav.

“You never needed it,” said Declan. “You were learning the other one.” He looked at Xav, and something happened at the corner of his mouth. “You learned it. All of it. The rests. You were in every one. You’d have had me.” He paused. “You had me. One more.”

“One more,” said Xav.

“So I couldn’t use the one you’d learned,” said Declan. “I had to use one you hadn’t.”

Xav’s face did something. Val didn’t know what. She’d known him nine years too, and she’d never seen it. Something between being hit and being given a present.

“How many have you got?” said Xav.

Declan thought about it.

“I don’t know,” he said. “I’ve never needed a third.”

Xav put his head back against the wet stone wall of the cellar and laughed.

Not the laugh from October, or January. Bigger. It went round and round the low ceiling and came back off the water from every side at once, the way Val’s lines did. It went on and on. It set Azac off, and then Jun Liang, and then RX, high and helpless. Even Ryan, who never laughed at anything out loud, put his hand over his face. And Malcolm, beside Val, said very quietly, “March,” and shook his head.

“Yield,” said Xav, through it.

Declan took the sword away.

Hyvred didn’t get up off the ladder for a long time.

When she did, she stepped down into the water without lifting her tail out of it, which Val had never seen her do. She walked across the cellar to Declan, splash, splash, and stopped in front of him. She looked at him, the whole of him, at eleven per cent, with his left foot dragging.

“I couldn’t call it,” she said.

“I know.”

“I can always call it. I called the longest night. I called a crown.”

“I know,” said Declan. “That’s how I knew it would work.”

Hyvred stared at him.

“If you couldn’t see it,” said Declan, “he couldn’t.”

She hit him. On the arm, not hard, the way she did.

“That’s for fourteen,” she said.

“That’s fair.”

She hit him again.

“That’s for not telling me you could do that.”

“I didn’t know if I still could,” said Declan. “I hadn’t done it in a long time.”

Hyvred looked at him for a long moment. Then she put her forehead against his shoulder, there in the water, in front of all of them, and stayed there.

“Mrp,” she said, into his shoulder.

“I know,” said Declan.

At the top of the ladder, Kestrel had gone. Val heard her afterward, through the floor, walking very fast out of the netloft and down the lane toward the harbour. She’d come back the next day with a page covered in writing, and give it to Xav without a word, and go away again.

They sat in the netloft afterward, all of them, in the evening light from the wavy window. Declan on the old bench with a draught in one hand and Hyvred’s hand in the other, at twenty-six per cent and climbing. Xav at the long table with his sketchbook open in front of him to a clean page.

He’d been looking at the clean page for half an hour.

“I can’t draw it,” he said at last.

Nobody said anything.

“I can draw feet,” said Xav. “I can draw where you stand. I can draw a back foot, a step, a turn of the shield. Two hundred pages.” He put the pencil down. “I can’t draw that. There’s nothing to draw. It’s not where you are. It’s when.”

Val looked at her book.

She’d forgotten it was in her lap. It was damp at the edges from the cellar. And on the page, from top to bottom, in pencil, there was the whole fight. Twenty-eight minutes of dashes and dots, the long rests and the two quick steps, with Xav’s soft little marks in the gaps. And then, near the bottom, where her pencil had gone through the paper, a line of dots so close together they ran into each other. Fifty-one seconds, with no rests in it at all.

She got up.

She went across the netloft to the long table and put the book down in front of Xav, open at the page. She didn’t say anything. She wasn’t very good at saying things, out loud, to people’s faces. Lines were different. Lines were somebody else’s.

Xav looked at it.

He looked at it for a long time. He turned it toward the window to see it better. He put his finger on the dashes at the top and ran it down the page, slowly, and stopped where the pencil had gone through.

“What’s this?” he said. Though she could tell he knew.

“It’s the tempo,” said Val. “I heard it. In the water.” She made herself go on. “You were drawing where he was. For two hundred pages. And you got so good at it you were in every rest.” She pointed at the little marks. “That’s you. In the gaps. You learned the gaps. And then he took the gaps away.”

Xav stared at the page.

“You can’t draw it,” said Val. “But you can write it down. Like a line. Like a script.” She looked at the bottom of the page. “Every line has a tempo before it has a meaning. If you get the tempo, the meaning comes.”

Something happened in the corner of her eye, then. A window. She’d been doing Inscription for a year and a half, slowly, the way she did everything, mostly runes on the backs of shields for Azac. She’d never once had a window like it.

Xav read it over her shoulder. Of course he could see it; she’d made it on his table.

He didn’t say anything for a long time. Then he picked up the book, very carefully, with both hands, the way Suzu held his jar.

“Can I keep this?” he said. “Copy it. I’ll give it back.”

“Keep it,” said Val.

“It’s your book.”

“I’ve got another one,” said Val, and went back to her chair by the window, and sat down, and found that her face was very hot.

Jun Liang leaned over to her later, when the netloft had got loud again. RX counting the draughts Declan had drunk. Azac asleep on his shield. Hyvred asleep on Declan’s shoulder. Malcolm and Xav at the long table with their heads together over her page, pointing at the place where the pencil went through, saying there, there, look.

“You said he wasn’t weird any more,” said Jun Liang. “In March. On the road.”

Val looked across the netloft at Declan. On the bench by the wavy window, with Hyvred asleep against him and a cup in his hand, looking out at the harbour. Perfectly still. As if he’d never once in his life done anything fast.

“I was wrong,” she said.

“He’s weird again?”

Val thought about it. About seventeen, and a classroom, and the small things she’d never told anybody. About a stone in a fen, and a year and a half of waiting. And fifty-one seconds in a cellar with no rests in it at all.

“He was always weird,” she said. “I just thought I knew which way.”

Jun Liang laughed. It was the laugh that made people want to keep talking to him. And Val, who didn’t stop thinking about things, sat by the wavy window in the evening and didn’t stop thinking about it. She opened her other book on her knee and started, very quietly, to read a line out loud to herself. Slow. Then fast. To hear what it did.
