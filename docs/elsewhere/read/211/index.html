Live from Elsewhere · Volume 8: Somewhere Else

Chapter 211

What It's For

3,255 words · 14 min read

Suzu kept the jar on the long table in front of the old woman’s chair, because she liked to look at it.

He hadn’t meant to. On the first morning after Christmas Eve, he’d carried it in his pack, the way he’d carried it since Larkhollow. A plain clay jar the size of two hands cupped together, with a lid, glowing very faintly through the clay. Then on the second morning he’d seen Isa Corrie in her chair by the north door looking at his pack. Just looking. The way you’d look at a window with a light in it, from a long way off, on a dark road.

So he’d taken it out and put it on the table in front of her. And she’d looked at it all day.

He’d put it there every morning since. Three months. He’d take it off the table at the third dawn when he went down the north tunnel. He’d put it back when he came up. And she’d look at it.

“You don’t have to,” she’d said once, in January.

“I know,” said Suzu.

“It’s yours now.”

“It’s still yours,” said Suzu. “You carried it home. I’m just keeping it.” He’d put it down on the table in front of her. “You can look at it.”

She’d looked at him for a long time with her bright black eyes. And then said, very quietly, “Hm,” exactly like Ama Corrie. And looked at the jar.

On the Saturday after the race, the round town was quiet.

Half of Constellation was in Masmark. The ten, and Riverbank’s eight, and Xyro, and Ice. The twenty who’d gone into the Crown Below, and come out second. The other half was here. Moonlight Harvest, all eight of them without Ice. Thea’s six without Thea and Xola and Xyro. Hana’s five. Tiger and Maigo and Hako on the road of lights. Niko’s four. Kite at the gate.

The banner room was half full. Half the chairs empty, pushed in neatly, the way Ama Corrie liked them. Sunday was tomorrow. She’d sweep under all of them anyway.

Suzu sat in the second of the nine, beside the first, which was empty. He watched the stream on the window in the corner of his eye. Ice’s, from Masmark. The netloft. Ice sitting on the old bench by the wavy window, with Hyvred beside him, holding his hand. Not saying anything. The way they sat now.

The chat was very quiet. It had been quiet for days. Sixty, seventy thousand people, watching a man sit on a bench and hold somebody’s hand. Hardly anybody typing.

Suzu looked at it for a while.

He’d known Ice for a long time. Longer than the wind game, and the guild Ice had dragged him into one winter. Longer than most of the others. He’d moved into the guest room in Ice’s flat in Malacca on the eleventh day of this one, a long time ago. I need him, Ice had told Vivian. He’d never said it to Suzu. He didn’t need to.

He’d seen Ice tired, and focused. He’d seen him at the front of everything for fifteen months. He’d seen his hand shake on an oak floor. He’d seen him come out of the egg in the back room on the morning after the longest night, lie there with his hands on his chest, breathing. Suzu had knocked on the lid and said good.

He’d never seen him like this.

“Good,” said Suzu, to the stream. Very quietly.

Across the room, by the north door, Isa Corrie was looking at him.

“Who’s that?” she said.

Suzu looked at her.

“In the air,” said Isa. “In front of you. You’re watching something. You’ve been watching it for an hour. Your face did a thing.”

NPCs couldn’t see windows. Ama Corrie never had; she only knew a window was there because everyone went still at once. But Isa had been out in the dark for a long time, on her own, with a lamp. She’d learned to watch faces instead.

“Ice,” said Suzu. “In Masmark.”

“The one under the floor,” said Isa.

“Yes.”

“With the girl with the horns.”

Suzu blinked.

“I saw them,” said Isa. “In January. At the south gate. From my chair, through the banner room door, across the Hall. I was awake. I’m always awake at the first dawn.” She folded her hands on the blanket. “She said me. And he went still. And then he didn’t.”

Suzu looked at her for a long time.

“Yes,” he said.

“Good,” said Isa Corrie.

She said it exactly the way he did. He didn’t know if she’d heard him say it, or if it was just what you said, when you’d been waiting a long time for something and it came.

He made her tea that afternoon, with Kotori. They did it most afternoons. Kotori brewed it, because Kotori brewed everything, and Suzu carried it, because Isa liked him to. She said he walked carefully. Like somebody carrying a light.

He put the cup down on the table in front of her, beside the jar. She looked at both of them.

“Sit down,” said Isa.

He sat down. On the stool from the kitchen, beside her chair, where Ama Corrie usually sat.

“I want to tell you something,” said Isa, “about the hill.”

Suzu waited.

“There’s a ring on it,” said Isa. “In the dark. Past the edge. Past where the lamps go. A long way. A hill in the middle of the still water, with twelve stones round the top, and a flat stone in the middle with a crown on it. My grandmother’s grandmother’s mother’s people set it. When they went.” She was looking at the jar. “A kept place. Nothing comes inside. And in the middle, on the flat stone, one lamp. That one.” She nodded at the jar. “For three hundred years.”

“I know,” said Suzu. “You told the long table.”

“I told the long table it was kept,” said Isa. “I didn’t tell them what it was for.” She picked up her tea, very slowly, in both root hands. “Nobody asked.”

Suzu didn’t say anything. He thought about it. He thought about the Long Watch, in April a year ago. Eighty-one empty lanterns in eighty-one dark alcoves, and the Last Watcher in its great round hall at the end, turning round to face north again when the last light went in. Set one to watch, so the rest may sleep.

“What’s it for?” said Suzu.

Isa looked at him over her tea. Her bright black eyes.

“So it knows where the middle is,” she said.

Suzu waited.

“The dark,” said Isa. “Out there. It’s not a thing. Not really. The tall ones, the low ones, the ones that come up out of still water. They’re bits of it. But the dark itself isn’t anything. It’s just what’s there when there’s no light.” She drank her tea. “And it doesn’t know where it is. It just spreads. Out and out. Into every place that hasn’t got a light. And then one night a year, the longest one, it comes south all at once. Looking.”

“For what?”

“For the middle,” said Isa. “Everything wants a middle. Even the dark. It wants to know where it is.” She put the cup down. “So my people went out into the middle of it and lit one lamp. On a hill, in a ring. And kept it. So the dark would always know where its middle was. It would always have one thing to look at. One light it couldn’t put out. And it would stay round that. Mostly. It wouldn’t need to come looking.” She was quiet for a moment. “Except on the longest night. When it forgets. And comes south all at once. And that’s why the road. And the round town. And the lamps behind.”

Suzu sat very still on the stool.

“And now the lamp’s here,” he said.

“And now the lamp’s here,” said Isa. “In your jar. On a table. In the round town.” She looked at it. “And the hill’s dark. For the first time in three hundred years. And the middle’s gone.”

He didn’t say anything for a long time.

He thought about the longest night. The road of lights and the round town and the north door two shields wide. The lake under the floor, standing up. He thought about the dark coming south all at once, a hundred miles long, because it had forgotten where it was.

He thought about it coming again. Next year. On the longest night. Looking for its middle. And the middle wasn’t on a hill in the dark any more. It was here. On a table. In front of an old woman’s chair, by the north door, in the round town.

“It’ll come here,” said Suzu. “Next year. Won’t it. Looking.”

“It came here this year,” said Isa. “It always comes here. That’s what the round town’s for.” She looked at him. “But this year it’ll know why.”

Suzu looked at the jar.

It glowed through the clay, faintly. The way it had since Christmas Eve. It remembers keeping. Eighty-one, and then a hundred and sixteen wicks, and now one. A flame three hundred years old that had never gone out. That had been the middle of the dark for longer than anybody had been alive.

“Somebody has to take it back,” he said.

Isa didn’t answer.

“Not you,” said Suzu. “You walked home. You can’t walk back.” He looked at her. “Somebody has to take it back to the hill. And light the ring again. And keep it.” He paused. “Or the dark won’t know where it is.”

“Yes,” said Isa.

“When?”

“Before the longest night,” said Isa. “Some year. It’s been dark before. My grandmother said. Once, a long time ago, before my mother, the lamp nearly went out. And the hill was dark for a winter. And the dark came south that year all at once, and the round town nearly didn’t keep.” She was quiet. “But not this year. The hill can be dark for a bit. The dark won’t notice for a while. It’s very slow.” She looked at the jar. “And the lamp’s been in the dark for three hundred years. It can be home for a bit.”

It can be home for a bit. He’d said that. To her. On Christmas Eve, with the jar open between them.

“I’ll take it,” said Suzu.

Isa looked at him.

He hadn’t known he was going to say it. That was the thing he’d tell Ice later, on the edge of a pod in the back room in Malacca, at nine at night. He hadn’t decided. It had just come out. The way go on had come out in the Long Watch, at the eighty-first lantern, with his hands shaking.

“When it’s time,” said Suzu. “Not this year. Not this winter. But some year. Before the longest night. I’ll carry it back to the hill. And light the ring. And keep it.”

“On your own?”

“No,” said Suzu. “Nobody alone. Kei says. It’s on the board.” He looked at the jar. “But I’ll carry it. Somebody always carries it. I’ll be the one.”

Isa Corrie looked at him for a very long time with her bright black eyes.

Then she reached out, very slowly, with one root hand. She put it flat on the lid of the jar, beside where his always went. And left it there.

“Hm,” she said.

That was all. But Suzu had known Ama Corrie for a year, and he knew what hm meant from a Corrie. It meant kept.

He told Kotori that night in the Hart’s kitchen, at the second dusk, while she made tea for the morning.

“You’re going to take it north,” said Kotori. She didn’t turn round from the pot. “The light. To the hill.”

“Some year.”

“Not this year.”

“Not this year.”

Kotori was quiet for a while. She was very careful with the pot.

“I’ll come,” she said.

Suzu looked at her.

“Somebody’ll need tea,” said Kotori. “In the dark. On a hill. For however long.” She put the lid on. “I’m good at tea. I’m good in the dark.” She paused. “I heal on the beat. Whether they need it or not. I learned that in the Long Watch. With you. When you were lighting the lanterns and I was trying not to say sorry.” She turned round at last. “I didn’t say sorry once, that night. Do you remember? Not once. You said better.”

“I remember.”

“So I’ll come,” said Kotori. “When it’s time. And I won’t say sorry.”

Suzu looked at her for a long time.

“Okay,” he said.

The twenty came home on the first of April, by the south road, and Suzu was on the south steps when they came round the curve.

Not all twenty. Ten. Riverbank had stayed in Masmark, at the netloft, to keep the room. Xav said somebody should. And Wendy had stayed with Salty, because Wendy said Masmark needed a gate and Kite had this one. Ten came home. Fourteen days of road. Dusty, slow. Without the second banner; Bee had left it on the netloft’s hooks for Riverbank, with its star in the middle, so the room would still know whose it was.

At the front, beside Kei, Ice. With his shield on his back and a dent in it the shape of a crown. And beside him, holding his hand, Hyvred.

The whole round town was on the steps. It always was, for anybody coming home. Moonlight Harvest by the fountain, Gabe at the front with his shield and his enormous face. Hana’s five. Tiger and Maigo and Hako, down from the first mile. Niko’s four. Chernn on the steps beside Deston, with a stick, at Lv 19.

Kite stood in the gate with his hands at his sides.

They came to the bottom of the steps and stopped.

“Who’d stand next to you?” said Kite.

He went along them. Kei said Higu. Higu said Kei. Gigi said Gu, and whoever calls the Turn. Thea, behind her, said Xola, and the thorn on my left, and Gigi’s hands went still on her staff. Drew said the one who stays, and on the steps Tiger went so red that Maigo had to hold him up.

Ice stood at the bottom of the steps with Hyvred’s hand in his.

“Who’d stand next to you?” said Kite.

Ice looked at him. Then up the steps. At all of them. At the round town and the fountain and the Hall of Roads with its dome. At the open south door, and the banner room through it, and the north door at the far end. And the chair by it, set apart, facing north, with an old woman in it with a blanket round her shoulders, awake, looking back at him through two doors and a whole Hall.

At Suzu, on the steps, near the top. Out of the way.

“Everybody,” said Ice. “Today.” He looked at Hyvred beside him. “And her. Every day.”

Kite looked at him for a long time.

“Fair,” he said.

Suzu didn’t go down the steps. He didn’t need to. Ice came up them, the way he always did, slowly. Past Gabe, who hugged him without asking and talked the whole time. Past Deston, who put a hand on his shoulder and said you’re slow. Past Chernn, who held up a candle and said I’m nineteen. And past all of them, to the top, where Suzu was.

He stopped.

“Hi, Suzu,” said Ice.

“Hi.”

Ice looked at him. The way he’d looked at him for a long time, since a winter in a game with wind in its name.

“Isa told me,” he said. “On a letter. She asked Ama Corrie to write it. She said you’re going to carry it back. Some year.”

“Some year.”

“Not this year.”

“Not this year,” said Suzu.

Ice looked at him for a long time more.

“I’ll come,” he said.

“I know,” said Suzu. “Kotori’s coming too. She says somebody’ll need tea.”

“Somebody always needs tea,” said Ice.

Hyvred, beside him, holding his hand, was looking at Suzu with her eyes that saw everything. She didn’t say anything. She just looked at him, and then at Ice, and then at the chair by the north door through two doors and a Hall. And her tail, which had been hanging loose, curled very slightly at the end.

“Good,” said Suzu.

It was what he always said. It was the only word he’d ever needed. He said it now on the top of the south steps of the Hall of Roads, on the first of April, with the whole round town below him. And it meant everything it had ever meant, and something new.

Ice nodded. Once. The way he nodded when something was right.

And went in, with Hyvred, through the Hall, to the banner room, to the first of the nine. And sat down. And put his feet on the rung of the next chair, the way he always did. And held out his hand, without looking, for the cup Kotori was already bringing.

Suzu went in after them. He went to the long table, to the place in front of the old woman’s chair by the north door. He put the jar down.

Isa Corrie looked at it. Then at him.

“Home,” she said.

“For a bit,” said Suzu.
