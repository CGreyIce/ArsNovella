Live from Elsewhere · Volume 1: Moonlight Harvest

Chapter 26

Everyone Saw

4,537 words · 20 min read

His phone had four hundred and twelve notifications on it when he sat up in the egg.

He didn’t look at them. He sat in the gel with the lid lifting slowly over his head, in the back room in Melaka Raya, with the ceiling fan going round and the high window full of late gold afternoon. He was shaking. Not a lot. A fine steady tremor in both hands, like after too much coffee, that he couldn’t make stop.

Beside him, Suzu’s lid was lifting too.

Suzu sat up. He didn’t say anything. He looked at Ice across the little gap between the pods, his hair sticking up on one side, half-closed eyes, and for a long moment neither of them said anything at all.

“Hi,” said Ice.

“Hi,” said Suzu.

On the folding table, between the kettle and Suzu’s pillow, Ice’s phone buzzed again. Then again. Then it didn’t stop. It went on buzzing, on and on, a long low continuous rattle on the plastic, walking itself slowly toward the edge of the table.

Suzu reached out and caught it before it fell, and put it face down, and kept his hand on it.

“Later,” said Suzu.

They didn’t look at anything for an hour.

They sat on the floor of the back room with their backs against the pods, the way they had on the night of the ford. Suzu went to the mamak on the corner and came back with two teh tarik in plastic bags with straws, the way they sold them, and they drank them sitting on the tiles. Through the high window the light went from gold to orange to the soft grey-blue of a Malacca evening. Somewhere outside a dog barked at a motorbike.

The one light Suzu had kept was in the Reliquary Jar on the floor between them. What you keep in it does not fade. It sat there on the white tiles, glowing, its one faint note going on and on. It was a strange thing to have in a back room in Melaka Raya, a light from a drowned church in a game, singing. It wasn’t really there. It was in the game. But Suzu had looked at it on his phone as soon as he logged out, in his inventory, and then put the phone down beside him on the floor, screen up, so the little image of the jar glowed there between them. Neither of them looked away from it for very long.

“Deston cried,” said Suzu eventually.

“Deston always cries.”

“You cried.”

“I didn’t cry.”

“You cried at the ford. On Wednesday. In the egg. When I came round.” Suzu drank his teh tarik. “And today, when the lights went up. I saw.”

Ice didn’t say anything.

“It’s fine,” said Suzu. “I nearly did.”

At seven, Xav rang. The video call came up on the laptop on the folding table: Xav in his room in Japan, the size of a pod and a half, with the heater going in the corner and his hair wet.

“Have you looked?” said Xav.

“No.”

“Don’t.” Xav looked at something off screen. “It’s—don’t, Dec. It’s stupid. It’s everywhere. The window went to every player in the game. Three million people. Somebody’s already made a map of every beginner town that starts with F. The forum crashed twice. Somebody bought the name MoonlightHarvest without the space an hour ago and is trying to sell it for four hundred dollars.” He paused. “Kestrel’s posted.”

“What did she say?”

Xav read it out, flatly, the way he read everything that mattered.

“Congratulations, Moonlight Harvest. Genuinely. We’ve been down our steps four nights and you walked down yours and walked out. See you at the next one.” He looked up. “That’s a pro being gracious in public. It means she’s furious in private, and she means it about the next one.”

“Good,” said Suzu, from the floor.

Deston came on the call at quarter past, from his room in Singapore, with his eyes still red, holding a bubble tea he’d gone out for specifically, because he said a day like today needed a bubble tea. Then, at half past, a small square on the call with a pale blue background and nobody in it. Kotori, with her camera off, saying “Hi! Sorry! My camera doesn’t work. It does work. I just don’t—sorry. Hi.”

“Hi, Kotori.”

“Hi.” A pause. “My dad saw the window.”

“What did he say?”

“He said, that’s the game I got sick in.” Kotori’s voice did something. “And then he said, is that you, the Kotori one? And I said yes. And he was quiet for a long time. And then he said, well, I’m glad somebody’s using it.” A smaller pause. “He’s never said he was glad about a game before.”

They talked about the recording.

It was sitting in the Cradle’s storage, five hours and twelve minutes of it, from the moment Ice had pressed the setting at the end of the north walk to the moment he’d logged out on the end of Sedge’s porch with his boots off. Every second from inside his eyes: the tussocks, the ring, the ninth heron, the lurker, the door, the steps, the nave, the Precentor, the Choirmaster, the lights going up, the windows. Five hours and twelve minutes he’d promised chat they’d see.

“Cut it,” said Xav.

“I’m not cutting it.”

“Not edit it, Dec. Cut it. The beginning.” Xav leaned toward his camera. “If you put out five hours from the end of the north walk, three million people watch you walk to the heron-stones. They watch you walk into the ring. They see the moonbells.” He held Ice’s eyes through the screen. “And then there’s no ring by Monday. Sedge said.”

Ice thought about the ring. The warm water and the herons and the thirty silver lights coming up out of the dark on the first night, ringing like a glass harmonica in another room. And Sedge on her porch with her arms folded: If half your lot in their linen shirts go tramping out to the stones and pull them up by the fistful, there won’t be a moonbell in the fen in a month.

“Start at the steps,” said Ice.

“At the foot of the steps,” Xav agreed. “Inside. Nobody can tell where a staircase is from the inside of a staircase. Finish at the chest. Before you come out.” He sat back. “They’ll come to Fernwick anyway. They’ll come looking. Everybody knows the swamp guy’s from a swamp. But they won’t know which way to walk from the end of the north walk, and they won’t know about the ninth heron, and they won’t know about the ring.”

“And it starts in a church,” said Deston, from his bubble tea. “That’s a better start anyway. It starts on the bass line.”

“Everything else stays,” said Ice. “Every mistake. Every time I’m late. When I’m on my knee at eleven per cent. All of it. That’s the point. That’s always been the point. You see exactly when I make the mistake, because you’re looking where I’m looking.”

Nobody argued with that.

“Nine o’clock,” said Xav. “Malaysia time. That’s ten here, nine in Singapore, the middle of the night in Europe and the morning in America. Best you’ll get for everyone.” He looked at Ice through the screen. “Stream it. Watch it with them. Don’t just put it up and hide.”

“I wasn’t going to hide.”

“You were going to hide,” said Xav, Deston and Suzu, all at the same time.

He cut it in twenty minutes, on the laptop, at the folding table, with Suzu reading over his shoulder. It wasn’t really cutting. It was finding the second where his foot came off the last of the old green steps onto the drowned stone of the nave, and the second where the windows came up in front of the chest, and throwing away everything either side.

Four hours and fifty-one minutes.

He called it The Drowned Choir (POV). Nothing else. He sat and looked at the title for a long time. Then he uploaded it, and set it to go public at nine, and went upstairs to wash his face, because he still hadn’t stopped shaking.

At eight fifty-five, he went live.

He did it from the folding table, on the laptop, with his webcam on. His real face, his real hair flat on one side, in a T-shirt, in the back room, with a white egg on either side of him and Suzu sitting on his pillow on the floor at the edge of the frame. The title said the drowned choir. watching it with you. trust.

He’d thought he was ready for it.

At nine o’clock exactly, the video went public. He shared his screen, and turned the sound up, and pressed play.

It opened on the bass line.

On the drowned green stone at the foot of the steps, from inside his eyes, with the water at his knees and the pillars going away into the dim. Deston’s voice was behind him and to the left, low and a little flat, coming in under a choir that had been missing its basses for three hundred years. Somewhere far down the nave, the choir, verse one, going round. Kotori’s pale blue robe at the very edge of the frame, going up onto the first pillar like a heron.

Chat went quiet.

He watched them watch it. He watched the counter in the corner climb, three thousand, three and a half. He watched the chat slow down until whole seconds went past with nobody typing anything. For the first twenty minutes, nobody said much at all. People were just watching. Watching from inside his eyes as five people walked down the aisle of a drowned church, moving like a hand. The bell rang before the cold went in, and a long low shhhhk went round at knee height at the edge of everything.

He saw that one too. He was watching for it, without knowing he had been. Wendy, who never said anything in anyone’s chat and was always there. A colon and a bracket.

He had to stop the video for a minute. He told chat he needed water, which was true. He went out into the front room of the shoplot, where the roller shutter was down and the white tiles were dark, and stood there for a while with his hands on the back of his neck.

When he came back, Suzu had paused the video at the exact second he’d stopped it, and was sitting on his pillow, waiting.

The end of the video was the part he hadn’t watched.

He’d been there. He’d lived it. He’d been on one knee on the floor of the drowned church at eleven per cent with the cold going into him at fifty and a bone baton pressing on his shield, with Deston singing the fourth verse behind him as loud as he’d ever sung anything. He knew what happened. He didn’t know what it looked like.

It looked like this.

It looked like the shield filling the whole frame, the slope of it, and the white baton pressing on it. The edge of the picture going dark and grey at the corners, the way the game showed you were nearly gone. A long, low, continuous silver sound from the left, a bell rung so fast it had become one note. A man’s voice behind, singing, catching on a word, going on. A key change, up a whole step, everything getting brighter all at once. The frame not rising. Waiting, on the knee, under the baton, while a pair of daggers came out of nowhere behind the thing and a long dark blade went round at knee height. A little red number over the Choirmaster: four per cent. The choir going up for the home note.

And then, on the home note, the frame rising. Off the knee, up out of the Anchor, the whole world tilting a fraction as his weight went back and round, before the blade came down.

He watched the screen fill with it, from the top to the bottom, over and over, faster than he could read. It was the first thing he’d ever taught on stream, on a walkway in a swamp at dawn, to sixty people, because he didn’t know what else to do with them.

On the screen, the baton cracked, and the choir found the home note, and the lights went up.

Nobody typed anything while the lights went up.

The whole chat, eleven thousand, three hundred and something people, went completely still. In the recording, from inside his eyes, a whole drowned church full of small white lights rose slowly through the green water like snow falling the wrong way. Every one of them sang its one note of the chord. The chord got quieter, voice by voice, as they went up through the roof, until there was one, and then none. A long dark scythe at the edge of the frame didn’t lift. A man with long silver hair stood and watched them all the way up.

Then the windows. WORLD FIRST. Five names.

And then, on the screen, from his eyes, a man on the bottom step of an altar in a drowned church, sitting down in the water with his head in his hands, laughing and crying at the same time. The frame moved, and sat down beside him, and an arm came into the edge of the picture and went round his shoulders.

The video ended.

He stopped sharing his screen. He was back on the webcam. He sat there at the folding table in the back room in Melaka Raya, with his real face and his real hair flat on one side, and looked at the chat, and didn’t say anything.

He looked at the names.

That was what he did. He’d done it every stream for four years, even when there were only ten, and he did it now: just looked at who was there. There were too many to read now, mostly. But the ones he knew were still there, scattered all through the eleven thousand, and he found them.

Hanarisu. He’d been in her chat since she was nineteen, doing funny things to make her laugh. He’d raided her, and gifted her subs, and been there on Tuesdays and Thursdays and Saturdays for three years.

AltheaVT. xolalotl. He’d been in their chats for years. He’d gifted them subs and raided them and been the voice at three in the morning, before, for his own reasons, he’d gone quieter for a while.

Aleaka. 88EXP. Hyvred. KeiKatamori. itswinter__. Inaxho. ItzMaigo. tigeraxd. Every one of them, he’d sat in their chat. Every one of them, at some point, he’d been the funny guy in the corner typing something to make them laugh. He’d raided them all. He’d been in everyone’s chat.

And now they were in his.

He didn’t say it out loud. He didn’t need to. He just sat at the folding table and looked at the names for a long time, and Suzu on the floor looked at him.

“Thank you,” Ice said eventually, to the webcam, to eleven thousand people. His voice wasn’t working right. “Thank you for watching. I—“ He stopped. He started again. “Four years ago I had four viewers. Teh Peng was one of them. Frog was one of them. I don’t know who the other two were. Thank you.” He laughed, not very steadily. “That’s all. That’s all I’ve got. Thank you. Trust.”
