Live from Elsewhere · Volume 6: There's a Chair

Chapter 150

Later

4,264 words · 19 min read

The hatch opened at the second dusk on Monday, and Gigi was on the stair to see it.

She hadn’t meant to be. She’d meant to be in the cellar, at the Alchemy bench, doing something useful with her hands, the way Ama Corrie had said. But she’d got halfway down the cellar steps and turned round and come back up. Then up the main stair, to the second landing, where Hyvred sat in the evenings with her glasses pushed up in her hair. She’d sat down beside her without asking.

Hyvred hadn’t asked why either. Hyvred was watching the hatch.

It was under Ama Corrie’s stair, an iron square in the floor with seven lines cut round a ring and two hooks on the wall above it. When the banner hung on the hooks, the lines glowed and the iron sank away into a stair of light. When the banner came down, it shut. Bee always opened it. Bee had gone down to the hooks with the banner over her arm about ten minutes before, as she did every evening, because people were due up.

The lines glowed. The iron sank. And up the stair of light, one at a time, came Moonlight Harvest.

Ice first. He always came up first. He went down first, too, which was the same thing from a different end. He came up with his shield on his arm and mud to the knees and a long scratch down the side of his helm. Something with claws had nearly had his face. He was saying something over his shoulder to Suzu behind him, about a turn in the south-east road.

He didn’t see PenguChi.

PenguChi was standing at the top of the hatch stair with her long spear held upright like a staff. She’d been standing there for twenty minutes, very still, waiting. Gigi had watched her come out of the banner room and take up her place, and had thought, oh no.

Ice came up the last step of light and onto the floor of the Hall. And PenguChi, without any expression at all, kicked him in the shin.

It was a good kick. Gigi heard it from the second landing. Ice made a sound like hk and went up on one foot.

“I said I’d kick you,” said PenguChi.

“You said eventually,” said Ice, on one foot.

“It’s eventually,” said PenguChi.

Behind Ice, coming up the stair, Suzu stopped dead. Then Deston, behind Suzu, walked into him. And then Gabe walked into Deston, and the whole of Moonlight Harvest concertinaed on the stair of light. Calvin at the very back shouted what, what is it, what’s happening. And Ice, on one foot in the middle of the Hall of Roads, holding his shin, started laughing.

Properly. Out loud. With his head back.

“Hi, Chi,” he said.

“Hi,” said PenguChi.

“Hi, Penguin.”

She kicked him in the other shin.

Gigi laughed.

It came out of her before she could stop it. It was loud, in the quiet of the stair, and it went up the dome and came back down again, and she put her hand over her mouth. She couldn’t remember the last time she’d laughed out loud. Not in days. Not since the six chairs, maybe. Not since Higu had said tomorrow I’m going to ring her.

Beside her on the step, Hyvred was not laughing. Hyvred was watching Ice hop about the Hall floor with his shin in his hand. Her long thin tail, which had been lying quiet along the step, had curled itself up into a tight hook at the end. It was twitching.

Gigi looked at the tail. Then at Hyvred’s face. Then back at the tail.

She didn’t say anything. It wasn’t hers.

Down on the floor, Ice had put his foot down and stopped laughing, mostly. He looked across the Hall at the banner room door.

Thea was standing in it.

She’d come out at the noise. Xola was behind her, and Slep, and Tif with her hands over her mouth and her ears straight up. And Thea and Ice looked at each other across the floor of the Hall of Roads, and the whole Hall went a little bit quiet.

“Hi, Thea,” said Ice.

“Hi,” said Thea.

That was all. He didn’t go over. She didn’t come out. He just said hi the way he’d said it to everybody, and nodded. Then Xola came out of the banner room like a cork out of a bottle and hit him at full speed, shouting ICEY. She knocked him back two steps into Gabe. Gabe caught both of them and said hello, who’s this, oh, it’s the fish one.

And over Xola’s shoulder, Ice looked up. Up the stair, to the second landing, to Gigi.

He nodded at her. Just once.

Then he peeled Xola off, gently, and said something to her that made her hit him on the arm. He went across to the far end of the banner room, to the nine chairs Moonlight Harvest sat in, and sat down in the first one. He took his helm off, and put his feet up on the rung of the chair next to him. And he didn’t come back out.

Gigi sat on the stair and thought about it’s not mine.

He’d meant it. He’d come up out of the ground, and been kicked twice, and said hi, and then taken himself away to the far end of a room. So that whatever happened between the banner room door and the second landing of the stair wouldn’t happen round him.

She thought she might have to start revising a lot of things she’d thought about people.

Tuesday was the longest day of Gigi’s life. She knew that was a stupid thing to think about a day in a game, and she thought it anyway.

She went down to the cellar at the first dawn and did Alchemy. That was the plan. She’d do something with her hands and be useful, and not be on the stair, or in the banner room, or anywhere Thea might come round a corner and have to decide whether to stop. She’d stay out of the way. That was the thing. Let Thea have the town, and the Hall, and the chairs, and the ceiling, and not have to see Gigi in every one of them.

She ruined three potions before noon.

The third one went green when it should have gone blue and then brown when it should have gone anything at all. She stood at the bench looking at it. Then, out loud, to the empty cellar, she said, “My fault. I didn’t let it settle.”

“Hm,” said somebody behind her.

It was Kei. He was on the cellar steps with his map under his arm, looking at her. He’d come down for something, and stopped.

“Sorry,” said Gigi.

“For what?”

“I don’t know. The smell.”

Kei came down the last steps and looked at the brown potion.

“You said my fault,” he said.

“I say that now.”

“I know,” said Kei. “I’ve heard you.” He looked at her for a moment, in the slightly helpless way Kei had, when he wanted to say something kind and didn’t know which way round to say it. “I just like hearing it.”

Then he went back up the stairs without whatever he’d come down for.

She saw them, all day, without trying to.

You couldn’t not, in the round town. The Hall of Roads was the middle of everything, and everybody came through it. She saw Thea and Kei at the counter, Kei unrolling his map and showing her the six dotted lines. She saw Bee take Thea and Xola out onto the floor of the Hall, under the eye, to show them where she’d painted the guild. Every member, in gold and silver on the old stone, and six dotted lines at the edge, near a town of wheels. She saw Thea look up at the ceiling of the dome for a long time. In the day the ceiling was just grey stone. At moonrise, Higu said, the lines came out.

She saw PenguChi asleep in a chair in the banner room in the afternoon, with Patience asleep on her feet. She saw Tif find Kotori. Kotori had come up from the hatch the night before and been ambushed in the Hall by a cat-eared girl who said you’re Kotori and burst into tears. Today they were sitting on the steps outside the south door with a pot of something between them, talking bells. She saw Xyro fall into the fountain, which was apparently a tradition; Tiger had done it first. She saw Slep standing at the gate with Wendy and Drew, not saying anything, watching the road, as if he’d found the place in the round town that was most like him.

She saw Xola, once, across the market, carrying two baskets of fish from the river. Xola saw her too. They looked at each other for a second across the stalls, and Xola’s gills flared, and then Xola went on walking.

And she didn’t go near any of them. She did Alchemy, and ruined potions, and said my fault to the cellar, and waited.

Later, Thea had said. Can we talk? Later?

Gigi had said whenever you want. Or never.

She’d meant it. She’d meant never as much as she’d meant whenever. She’d sat on a roof with Higu for months learning what it was to say no hurry and mean it. But it turned out that meaning it and living through it were different things. Living through it was a whole day of brown potions.

At the second dusk Higu went down.

She did it on purpose. Gigi knew she did. Every evening for months Higu had gone up two hundred and twelve steps to the eye and stayed there until the moon was down. Tonight, at the second dusk, she put her cup down on the ledge and stood up.

“I’m going to the Hart,” said Higu.

“You never go to the Hart.”

“Hobb’s paying out the book,” said Higu. “Rafi won it. He had the twenty-sixth. He says he just knew.” She smiled. “He’s buying everyone a drink with turnip money. I want to see it.”

“Gu.”

“I’m the road, Chi,” said Higu gently. “Roads don’t sit in the middle of conversations.” She bent down and kissed the top of Gigi’s head, quickly. “I’ll be back at midnight. Or later. Keep my place.”

And she went down the stairs, and Gigi was alone on the ledge.

She sat there with two cups of tea, because she’d brought two up. She always brought two up. She’d brought two up every night for months, and tonight she’d brought them up without thinking, and now one of them was Higu’s and Higu had gone. She looked at it for a while.

The sky in the eye went from gold to blue to dark. Down in the Hall the lamps came on, one by one, and Ama Corrie went round the floor with her new broom. A pale silver-gold one, from a fen.

At about half past nine, Gigi heard feet on the stair.

She knew they weren’t Higu’s. She knew Higu’s feet on that stair better than her own. These were heavier, and slower, and they stopped at the first landing for a long time. Then they came on. They stopped again at the second, by the step where Hyvred sat. Then they came on again. Sixty-six more. Gigi counted them without meaning to.

Thea came out onto the ledge, out of breath.

“Two hundred and twelve,” said Thea.

“You counted,” said Gigi.

“Everybody counts,” said Thea. “Higu told me. Everybody counts the first time.”

She stood at the top of the stair for a moment, holding the wall, getting her breath. She wasn’t carrying the shield, Gigi noticed. Or the hammer. She’d come up with nothing at all.

Then she came and sat down on the ledge. Not close. In Higu’s place, on the other side of the lamp, which was where you sat if you were Higu, and where you sat if you didn’t want to sit next to someone. Gigi didn’t know which it was. She didn’t ask.

They sat there.

Neither of them said anything for a long time.

Gigi had practised. Of course she had. She’d practised on the ledge last night, and in the cellar all day between brown potions. She’d had dozens of first lines. She’d thrown every one of them away, because every one of them had been about her. I’ve been trying. I’m different now. I know you don’t believe me. All of them her, her, her.

And now she was here, and Thea was here, and she didn’t have any of them.

The moon came up.

It came up over the rim of the eye, enormous and white. As its light came down through the hole in the roof and touched the ceiling of the dome, the lines came out. Gigi had seen it a hundred times. It still did something to her chest. Silver lines on the old grey stone, one by one, all round them. Roads, and towns, and rivers. A map of the whole world, on the inside of a roof, every line running north. All of them toward one great gold star above the north door, off every map.

Thea made a small sound.

“Higu said,” said Thea. “She said they come out at moonrise. I didn’t—“ She stopped. “I didn’t think it would be like this.”

“Nobody does,” said Gigi. “The first time.”

They watched the lines come out. It took a few minutes. When the last one had come, Thea was still looking at the gold star, and Gigi was looking at her hands.

“I know what I was,” said Gigi.

She hadn’t planned to say it. It had been the first thing she’d thrown away last night, because it was too big. It came out anyway, on its own, into the moonlight.

Thea turned her head and looked at her.

“What were you?” said Thea.

Gigi hadn’t expected that. She’d expected I know, or nothing. She hadn’t expected to be asked. Thea had never asked her anything, before. Not like that. Not straight.

She made herself say it.

“Everything you did,” said Gigi, “I found something wrong in it. Every time. If you did it one way, it was wrong. If you did it the other way, it was wrong. If you did nothing, that was the most wrong of anything.” She looked at her hands, very hard. “There wasn’t anything wrong. Not most of the time. Not any of the time, maybe. I put it there. I went looking for it, and if I couldn’t find it, I put it there. And then I told myself you’d done it to me.”

Thea didn’t say anything.

“There was never a right answer,” said Gigi. “You kept looking for one. I watched you look. And I made sure there wasn’t one.” Her voice had gone very thin. “I made sure, Thea.”

The moonlight lay on the ledge between them. Down in the Hall, very faintly, Gigi could hear Ama Corrie’s new broom going hush, hush under the chairs.

“I’m not saying it so you’ll say anything back,” said Gigi. “I’m not asking for anything. I’m not saying it so you’ll say it’s okay. It’s not okay. I just—“ She stopped. “You came eight days. The least I could do was say it to your face. Instead of to Higu. Or Drew. Or a roof.”

Thea was quiet for a very long time.

“Good,” she said eventually. “Because I haven’t got anything yet. To say back.”

“That’s fair.”

“I thought I might. On the road. I practised. Chi said I’d stopped.” Thea almost smiled. “I had stopped. I didn’t have anything. I still don’t.”

“That’s fair,” said Gigi again.

“It took me a year,” said Thea. “To stop looking. For the right answer. After.” She was looking at the gold star. “I’d do something, anything, the smallest thing, and afterwards I’d stand there and think, was that right, was that the right way to do it. A year.”

Gigi shut her eyes.

“There wasn’t one,” she said.

“I know that now.”

“I made sure there wasn’t.”

“I know that too,” said Thea quietly. “I know it now. I didn’t then.”

They sat. The moon moved up the sky. The silver lines on the ceiling got brighter as it rose.

“Xola watched your streams,” said Thea.

Gigi opened her eyes.

“Eleven hours,” said Thea. “On Friday. Before we left. She went looking for—“ Thea paused, choosing. “For the thing she was sure would be there. Where you’d make me into a story about you. On stream. To your chat.”

Gigi’s hands had gone very still in her lap.

“She said it wasn’t there,” said Thea. “She said your chat says my name every week, and you never say it. Not once. She said you say not mine to say. And one night somebody asked if you missed me and you turned the stream off.”

Gigi didn’t say anything. She couldn’t, quite.

“Why?” said Thea.

“It wasn’t mine,” said Gigi. Her voice came out rough. “You weren’t mine. To—to use. For anything. I’d done enough of that. I wasn’t going to do it to six thousand people as well.”

Thea looked at her for a long moment in the moonlight.

“Thank you,” said Thea.

It was very quiet. It was the smallest thing. Gigi felt it go into her like the moonlight going into the stone, line by line.

She didn’t say anything back. She didn’t trust herself to. Instead, because her hands needed something to do, she picked up the second cup of tea from the ledge. The one she’d brought up without thinking, that had gone cold an hour ago.

“This is Gu’s,” she said. “She’s gone to the Hart. Rafi won the book.” She held it out across the lamp, not very far. Not far enough to make Thea reach. “It’s cold. You don’t have to.”

Thea looked at the cup.

Then she reached across the lamp and took it. Their fingers didn’t touch. She held it in both hands, the way you hold tea on a cold night even when it’s gone cold, and drank some.

“It’s terrible,” said Thea.

“It’s been up here an hour.”

“It’s still terrible.”

“I know,” said Gigi. And something happened in her face that she couldn’t do anything about. Thea saw it, and looked away, quickly, at the ceiling. Which was kind.

“I’m going to sign,” said Thea, after a while. “Tomorrow. All six of us. Ama Corrie says nobody signs on the day they come in, so tomorrow.”

“I know.”

“I’m going to be in this guild. In this Hall. In the same room as you. At the same table. Every day.” Thea was still looking at the ceiling. “I want you to know I’ve thought about that. I’m not doing it by accident.”

“I know,” said Gigi. “I didn’t think you were.”

“I don’t know what we are,” said Thea.

“Nor do I.”

“I don’t want to be what we were.”

“No,” said Gigi. “That wasn’t—no. Nor do I.”

Thea turned the cold cup round in her hands.

“Can we not be anything yet?” she said. “Can we just not be anything. For a while. And see.”

Gigi looked at her.

“Yeah,” she said. “Not yet.”

And Thea smiled. Not at her, exactly. At the cup. A small, tired, real smile, the first one Gigi had seen on her face in a very long time that wasn’t on a stream.

She left at about eleven. She stood up and put the cup down on the ledge, carefully, next to Gigi’s, and went to the top of the stair. Then she stopped, the way she’d stopped on the step above Gigi at the gate. With her back to her.

“I was scared,” said Thea. “On the road. That I’d get here and get small. The old size. That I’d start looking for the right answer in my head the second I saw you. Out of habit.”

Gigi held very still.

“Did you?” she said.

Thea thought about it.

“No,” she said.

“Good,” said Gigi.

She meant it. And she noticed something, saying it, that she’d go on noticing for the rest of the night and most of the next week. She’d sat on a ledge with Thea for an hour and a half. Thea had told her she had nothing to say back, and about a whole year of wondering whether the smallest things were right. She’d told her the tea was terrible. And Gigi hadn’t found the wrong in any of it. Not once. The old thing hadn’t come out of her mouth. It hadn’t even come up into her throat. She’d been so frightened of it for two days, and it had just not been there.

“The counter,” said Thea, at the top of the stair, still with her back to her. “At the founding. When you held it.”

“You said. Through Gu.”

“I’m saying it to your face,” said Thea. “Well. To the stair. It was brave.”

And she went down.

Gigi sat on the ledge and listened to her go. Sixty-six steps. A pause at the second landing. Forty steps. A pause at the first, longer. A hundred and six.

Then she leaned forward and looked over the edge of the eye, down into the Hall.

It was a long way down. Two hundred feet of moonlight and silver lines and dark. But at the bottom, at the foot of the stair, by the lamp, there was somebody waiting.

Xola.

Of course it was Xola. She was sitting on the bottom step with her knees up and her trident across them. She’d clearly been sitting there the whole hour and a half. Gigi watched Thea come off the last step, and Xola stand up, and the two of them stand there for a moment, not saying anything. Then Xola put her arms round Thea and held on.

By the south door, in the shadow, a tall shape with a glaive was leaning against the wall. Watching the other side.

Gigi sat back from the edge.

That was who held her up.

She’d known. Higu had told her, and she’d worked it out herself, on the north steps with Drew. But knowing a thing and watching it, two hundred feet below you in the moonlight, were different. It was a girl with gills sitting on a step for an hour and a half because her best friend had gone up a stair.

She didn’t feel the old thing about it. She checked. She felt about for the old sharp she tells Xola everything and it wasn’t there. What was there instead was something she didn’t have a name for. It was a bit like what she’d felt on the floor after the founding, with her arms shaking. That’s what it’s like. To hold something and not blame anybody for how heavy it is.

Higu came back at midnight, smelling of the Hart and a bit pleased with herself. She sat down in her place on the other side of the lamp and looked at the two cups on the ledge. One was full and cold. One was half empty and cold.

She didn’t say anything about them.

“It’ll fall down,” said Gigi, after a while.

“Probably,” said Higu.

“I’m going to build it anyway.”

“I know.”

Gigi pulled her knees up and put her chin on them.

“I didn’t do it,” she said. “The thing. The old thing. It didn’t come out. Not once. I was so scared it would, and it just—wasn’t there.”

“I know,” said Higu.

“How do you know? You were at the Hart.”

Higu put her hand out flat on the stone ledge, the way she always did, between them.

“Because you’d have rung me,” she said.

Gigi looked at the hand for a long moment. Then she put hers on top of it.

Down in the Hall, Ama Corrie’s new broom went hush, hush under the chairs, under all fifty-five of them, in the moonlight. Under the six by the window too. Tomorrow those would have people in them.
