Live from Elsewhere · Volume 4: The Ninth Heron

Chapter 109

On the Count

2,778 words · 12 min read

Kotori healed where things were going to lean. That was the whole of it. That was what she was for.

She’d worked it out in December, standing on an eel man’s mooring post in a fen. Healing what was already hurt was too late. By the time you saw the hurt, it had happened. So you watched. Everyone, all the time, from the highest thing you could stand on. You watched the way they stood, and the way the thing in front of them stood, and you saw where the weight was going before it went. And you put the Mend there first, so that when the hit came, it landed on something already mending.

It was the best thing she’d ever been good at. She’d never been good at anything before, not like that. She’d said sorry to the whole world her whole life, for being in it. Then she’d stood on a post in a fen and found she could see where people were going to get hurt before they did. And she could stop it.

And on Wednesday, in a gallery full of faceless stone watchers, all five of them had been bleeding for ten alcoves, and she hadn’t seen a thing.

She didn’t sleep much on Wednesday night.

She lay in her room in Singapore, in the dark, with the egg’s lid up, and thought about the party window. 66. 71. 63. Going down. Going down while she watched them, with her bell in her hand and her eyes on all of them, and nothing to see. No hit. No lean. Nothing.

There’s nothing to see, Ice had said. That’s the point.

She’d said sorry four times, at the door, after. She hadn’t meant to. It had just come out, the old way, the way it used to come out like breathing before they’d all told her, in the ring, that she didn’t have to. Mostly it means thank you, Ice had said, that night. Or I’m here. She’d tried to mean that. At the door, in the mist, she hadn’t. She’d just meant sorry.

Ice had looked at her and said, “Nobody could have.”

“You did.”

“I’m on fifty. That’s not seeing. That’s just hurting.” He’d shrugged. “You can’t watch something with no shape. Nobody can.”

She’d lain in the dark and thought about that until three in the morning.

No shape.

Then, at three in the morning, she’d sat up in the egg and said, out loud, to nobody, “Then don’t watch the shape.”

It came on Thursday afternoon, from wherever Suzu was. Just that. Nobody answered it. Nobody needed to. Kotori read it three times on the train and put her phone away and looked out of the window. She thought about a boy with a sweet jar on his lap, on a hillside in the mist, not saying anything until it was dark.

One at a time.

Then she got her phone out again, because something else had come in, and it was in every chat at once.

VANTA · room three. still going.

Kestrel. One line, the way she always did it. And under it a picture: a vaulted room of black stone, half flooded, five shapes in it, one small one in plain black at the front.

And then another, ten minutes later, from somebody else.

CL_Field_01: three companies. three doors. see you at the bottom.

Kotori sat on the train and looked at the two of them, one above the other.

The second doors were opening. Everywhere, all at once, the way the first ones had in December. And somewhere in the middle of them, a door in a hill at the top of the world, facing south, with nine herons round it. And a boy who’d said one at a time.

“Kestrel’s in the third room,” said Sheng Yang.

He said it at the second dawn, at the foot of the grey hill, in the mist, while they waited for Suzu. He said it quite calmly. Sheng Yang said everything calmly. But Kotori had had Gabe two paces from her for three months and Sheng Yang for one, and she’d learned to read the backs of tall people. His back was not calm.

“I saw,” said Ice.

“CL’s got three companies in three doors.”

“I saw.”

Sheng Yang looked at him. He’d been fifth on the Tally at Brackenford for a month, or sixth, or fifth again. He’d played on his own for two months before the trials, and told nobody, because he’d wanted to see how far he could get. Kotori knew that about him. He’d told her one night on a long road, quietly, when the others were ahead.

“We could be first,” said Sheng Yang. “If we went now. Tonight. Straight through. We could be first.”

“We could,” said Ice.

“Then—“

“Then Kestrel might be first,” said Ice. “Or CL. Or somebody we’ve never heard of.” He looked up at the door in the hill. “It’s his lights.”

Sheng Yang didn’t say anything for a long time.

Then he said, “Yes,” quite quietly. Kotori watched his back, and saw it change. Not give in. Settle. The way a long sword settles back into its sheath.

“Yes,” said Sheng Yang again. “It’s his lights.”

Suzu came up the hill out of the mist ten minutes later, with his pack glowing, and didn’t say anything. He didn’t have to. He’d said it already. One at a time.

Kotori went in third, behind Ice and Suzu, with Sheng Yang two paces from her. Deston came last, counting.

The first alcove. The second. The faceless watchers, dark lanterns up, looking north. At the fourteenth, the one that was lit, blue and steady in its stone hand. She’d never been so glad to see a light. She stood in it for a moment while the others walked on, and felt nothing, which was the point. Ice and Suzu felt something, and then didn’t, and she watched their shoulders come down as they stepped into it. Then up again, as they stepped out.

At the fifteenth, Suzu stopped.

The jar was pulling. She could see it pulling at his pack. Suzu stood in front of the fifteenth watcher and took the jar out and held it in both hands, the way he had on Wednesday. He looked at the lights for a long time.

Then he loosened the lid, one hair. One light came out, and went up the stone arm, and into the lantern. The lantern lit.

[ A watch is kept. ]

“Seventy-nine,” said Suzu, very quietly, and put the lid back on.

That was how it was going to be, Kotori understood. One at a time. One alcove, one light, one number. And in between them, the dark, and the cold that only Ice and Suzu could feel, taking from all five of them, a little at a time, with nothing to see.

So she didn’t watch the shape.

She’d worked it out at three in the morning, in the egg, and she’d had all day on the train to get it right.

You couldn’t heal where it was going to lean, because it didn’t lean. It didn’t hit, and it didn’t show, and it didn’t go anywhere. It just took. A little, all the time, from everybody, evenly, like water out of a cracked jar.

So you didn’t wait for it. You didn’t watch for it. You didn’t heal where it was going.

You healed where it was, which was everywhere, all the time. A little. On the beat. Whether anyone needed it or not.

“Dest,” she said, between the fifteenth alcove and the sixteenth. “Can you count for me? Not for the fighting. Just for me. Slowly. All the time.”

Deston looked at her. He didn’t ask why. He just opened the book.

“One,” said Deston. “Two. Three. Four.”

On every eighth beat, she rang the bell.

Not a Mend. Not a big one, the kind you put on somebody who’d been hit. Something smaller than she’d ever tried. Just a little warmth, out of the bell, to all five of them at once. Like a hand on a shoulder going past. I’m here. On the eighth beat, and the sixteenth, and the twenty-fourth. Over and over, down the dark gallery between the watchers, without stopping, whether anyone needed it or not.

She didn’t look at the party window. That was the hardest part. She’d looked at it every few seconds for five months. Now she made herself not look. She looked at the back of Ice’s head, and Suzu’s lit pack, and listened to Deston counting. And on the eighth beat she rang.

At the twentieth alcove, Ice stopped and turned round.

“Kotori.”

She stopped ringing. Her heart went into her throat. “Is it—“

“It’s gone,” said Ice.

She stared at him.

“In my wrist,” he said. “It’s gone. Not like in the light. It’s still there, under. I can feel it under. But it’s—“ He looked at Suzu.

“It doesn’t get any further,” said Suzu. He was looking at his own wrist. “It takes. And you put it back. Every eighth.”

Kotori looked at the party window then, finally.

And then a window she’d never seen in her life, coming up in front of her on its own. Gold at the edges. The kind of window that only came when the game had decided you’d done something it hadn’t taught you.

She read it twice. Then she read the last line again. You made this.

She didn’t say sorry. She didn’t say anything at all. She just stood in the dark gallery between two faceless watchers and held her bell. Deston, behind her, very gently, said, “One,” and kept counting.

They met the Unwatched at the twenty-sixth alcove.

A lot of them. More than on Wednesday. They came out of the dark past the last lit lantern, thin and grey and faceless, and they didn’t go for the lamps this time. They went for Suzu.

For the jar.

[ The Unwatched · Lv 32 ×9 ]

“Anchor,” said Ice, and the shield came up, and Steadfast hummed. The first three hit it and turned. But there were nine, and the gallery was narrow, and they went over him and round him and through the gaps between the watchers in the alcoves, all of them, for the blue light in Suzu’s pack.

Suzu didn’t use the scythe.

Kotori would remember that. He stood in the middle of the gallery with nine grey shapes coming at him, and he didn’t swing. He pulled the pack round in front of him and wrapped both arms round it, round the jar, and turned his back on them. He curled over it the way you’d curl over somebody you were keeping out of the rain.

The first one hit him across the back. His health dropped a fifth.

“Ward,” said Kotori.

She put it on him. The shimmer, the one she’d made in December on the mooring post. It came up round Suzu’s curled back like a bubble just as the second one hit, and the second one came off it like water off glass. And Sheng Yang was there, two paces from her, and then two paces from Suzu, because Suzu was in front of her. The long sword came down through the third.

“Five,” said Deston, steady. “Six. Seven.”

On the eighth, she rang. Vigil went round them all, warm, under everything.

Ice came back from the front, through the grey, with the shield. He stood over Suzu and the jar the way he’d stood over Ama Corrie and the Roll in the Hall of Roads, when the Rook came through the eye. Undertow. The pull went out from him in a ring, and every grey shape in the gallery came toward him at once, off Suzu, into the shield. Sheng Yang went through them from behind, and Deston counted, and Kotori rang on the eighth. And Suzu stayed curled over the jar in the middle of all of it with his eyes shut, and didn’t move.

Afterwards, Suzu straightened up slowly and looked down at the jar in his arms. Seventy-nine lights. Every one of them still there.

“They wanted these,” said Suzu.

“Everything in here wants them,” said Ice. “That’s why you’ve got to choose.”

Suzu didn’t say anything. He went to the twenty-sixth alcove and loosened the lid one hair, and one light went up into the dark lantern, and it lit.

“Seventy-eight,” said Suzu.

By the end of the evening they’d lit seven.

Seven blue lanterns, counting Wednesday’s, all down the gallery behind them, in stone hands, like a row of lamps left on in a house at night. Six numbers tonight, said very quietly. Seventy-nine. Seventy-eight. Seventy-seven. Kotori watched Suzu’s hands after each one. After the first, they were steady. After the third, they weren’t. After the last, seventy-four, he put the lid back on with both hands and held it for a moment as if it might come off on its own.

She went up to him while the others were looking ahead.

She didn’t say anything. She rang the bell, very softly, just once, just for him. It wasn’t Vigil. Just a Mend, a small one, on his hands, though there was nothing wrong with them.

Suzu looked down at his hands. Then at her.

“Thank you,” said Suzu.

“Sorry—“ said Kotori, and stopped. She took a breath. “No. You’re welcome.”

Suzu looked at her for a moment. Then something happened to his face that Kotori had seen perhaps twice in five months. Very nearly a smile.

“Better,” he said.

The gallery ended at the thirty-second alcove.

It didn’t end at a wall. It opened out. The walls went back and up and away, and the ceiling went up into the dark further than their lamps could reach. They came out of the narrow gallery into a round hall so big that Kotori’s bell, when she rang it on the eighth, came back to her twice.

All round the walls of the hall, in alcoves, there were more watchers, dozens of them, dark lanterns up. These faced inward, toward the middle.

In the middle of the hall, with its back to them, there was a watcher.

It was the same as the others. Faceless, stone, standing, looking north. Except that it was ten times the size of the others. It stood in the middle of the round hall like a tower. It held up, in a stone hand the size of a cart, a lantern big enough to stand inside.

The lantern was dark.

[ The Last Watcher · Lv 34 ]

Nobody said anything.

Then, slowly, so slowly that at first Kotori thought it was the lamplight moving, the Last Watcher began to turn round.

“Back,” said Ice. “Everybody back. Into the gallery. Now.”

They went. Kotori went with Sheng Yang two paces from her and Deston counting and Suzu in front with his arms round the jar. Ice came last, with the shield, walking backwards. Behind them, in the great dark hall, the huge stone figure finished turning, and stood facing south, toward the gallery. Toward them. Toward the light.

It had been looking the wrong way. Or it had been looking the right way for longer than anybody remembered, and now it wasn’t.

They stood in the gallery in the blue light of the thirty-second lantern and didn’t speak. It was a quarter to one in the morning, and nobody was going to sleep.

Then Ice put his shield back on his arm and said, “Let’s see what it wants.”
