Chapter 259

The Light in the North

3,463 words · 15 min read

The boy with the map and the girl with the lantern carried her up at the second dusk, because the two who usually did were on the first mile.

Isa didn’t mind. She’d been carried up two hundred and twelve steps every dusk for three weeks now. She’d learned the stair the way you learn a face. A hundred and six, and a landing. Forty, and a landing. Sixty-six, with the wind coming down through the eye. The third step loose. Ama Corrie at the bottom, every dusk, looking up from her ledger: mind the third step. And Isa every dusk saying I minded it before you were born.

Tonight Ama Corrie didn’t say it.

She came round from behind her counter as they went past with the chair. And stood at the bottom of the stair, and looked at Isa in it, with the grey blanket round her shoulders. For a long time. Over her spectacles.

Then she reached out and tucked the blanket in. At the side, where it had come loose. Very carefully.

“Look north,” said Ama Corrie.

“I always look north,” said Isa.

“Look anyway,” said Ama Corrie, and went back to her counter.

They set her chair down beside the bench, on the south side of the eye. And then, because she asked, turned it to face north. The bench faced south, the carpenter had built it that way. Toward the round market and the White Hills. She didn’t want south. She’d come from the north.

The boy with the map sat on the parapet on the north side, by his brass arm, with his sheet on his knee and a lamp. The girl with the lantern stood beside him with her hand up. She did that; she could put a light out from her hand and see what it saw. Isa had watched her do it every night for three weeks. A soft gold light going out north from the girl’s palm over the roofs and the moor, a long way. And the girl’s face going still while it went.

The boy wrote down what the girl saw. The girl said it, quietly. North road lit. Ninety-four. Standing.

And Isa looked north with her own eyes.

She could see the road. The ninety-four. She’d lit every one of them herself, a year ago, walking south behind the dark with her lamp on the longest night. Thirty-five hours of the game’s time, the boy said. She didn’t know about the game’s time. She knew it had been a long walk, and she’d been very old, and very tired, and she’d done every one.

They stood straight now. All the way up the moor in the dusk, little gold points on the backs of grey stones, facing the wrong way, standing straight. Not leaning. For the first time since the winter before last.

They’d stood up at dusk three weeks ago. The boy had told her. Tiger says they stood up. All ninety-four. At once. Like a field of grass when the wind stops.

She’d asked to be carried up to the roof that night. And every night since.

Past the ninety-fourth, past the top of the moor, she couldn’t see the road. It went on. To the old hall, and the twenty-two, and the edge. She’d stood at the edge every spring of her life after she was grown, the way all of them had, once. And looked south. And seen nothing. Not yet.

Past the edge, nothing.

She looked at it anyway. Every night. For three weeks.

She’d told the one under the floor, on the stair, in August. When he lights it, I’ll see it from here. He’d looked at her the way young people looked at old people who said things. Kindly. Not believing.

She hadn’t seen it.

Not for three weeks. Not since the night the lamps stood up. She’d sat on the roof every dusk till the first dawn and looked north past the edge at nothing. And seen nothing.

The boy with the jar had written to the boy with the map. Not tonight. The longest night. You’ll see it then.

It was the longest night.

She’d seen the twenty-two lit from the hill. A year and a quarter ago, in September. That was the thing nobody understood. She’d told them, at the long table, the night she came home. I saw you light them. One after another. Up the line toward me. Ten of you. And they’d looked at her the way the one under the floor had looked at her on the stair. Kindly.

It was true. From the hill, past the still water, past three hundred stones and the edge, she’d seen them. Twenty-two little lights, very far, very small, going on one by one.

You could only see them from the hill on the longest night, her mother had said. Or when they were lit for the first time. Or when somebody on the hill was looking for them so hard they’d have seen them anyway.

She’d been looking for three hundred years. Her mothers had.

She was looking now.

It came at dusk. Twenty past eight.

She’d been sitting with her root hands folded on the blanket, looking north. And she saw it.

Past the moor. Past where the road went over the top. Past the old hall, and the twenty-two, and the edge. Further. Much further. At the very bottom of the sky, where the dark met the world, so far away that it was hardly there.

A point. Gold.

Very small. Smaller than the smallest of the ninety-four. Smaller than a star. But not a star; it was too low, and too warm, and it didn’t twinkle. It stood still, at the bottom of the north, like the flame stood still over the jar. Straight up.

“Oh,” said Isa Corrie.

The girl with the lantern turned round.

“What?” she said. “What is it?”

Isa didn’t answer. She couldn’t. She sat in her chair on the roof of the round town, beside a bench made from a pole she’d put her hand on, and looked at a point of gold at the bottom of the north. She’d sat beside that point every night of her life until a year ago. Inside a ring of twelve stones. On a hill. And looked at it from a pace away.

She’d never once seen it from here.

Nobody had. Not in three hundred years. Not her mother, or her mother’s mother, or the one who’d walked here once on her own and bent a star in the smith’s fire and walked back. They’d all looked at it from the hill. Never from the round town. Never from the other end of the road.

“The hill,” said Isa.

The boy with the map stood up off the parapet so fast he nearly dropped his sheet.

“Where?”

“There,” said Isa. She didn’t point. She couldn’t; her hands were shaking. “North. The bottom. Past everything.”

The girl with the lantern looked. With her own eyes, and then with her light; Isa saw her hand go up and the soft gold go out from it north. Over the roofs, over the moor, up the road. Further than Isa had ever seen it go.

The girl’s face went still. Then it did something.

“Kei,” said the girl.

The boy with the map didn’t write it down. Isa watched him not. He stood at the parapet with his sheet in his hand and looked north for a long time. At a point of gold he could only just see, if he knew where to look, at the bottom of the sky.

Then he sat down on the parapet again and sent a call. To everybody. She couldn’t see it, but she saw his face as he did it, and she knew what he’d say.

He’d say Isa says she can see it.

He didn’t. She found out afterward. He said the hill’s lit. You can see it from the eye. And the whole round town, on the board, in threes, at every door and window and road, looked north. And most of them couldn’t see it.

But they looked.

She didn’t take her eyes off it for four hours.

She’d tell the boy with the jar, after, when he came home. She hadn’t looked at anything else. Not the round town below her, every window lit. Not the road. Not the boy with the map at the parapet beside her, sending calls. Not the girl with the lantern. Just the point of gold at the bottom of the north.

And the dark round it.

She could see that too. Not the dark itself; you couldn’t see dark. But the way the point of gold sat in it. At dusk, small and still and straight. And then, slowly, through the first hours, something changing round it. As if the dark round it were getting deeper. Thicker. Gathering. Coming from everywhere, from all the black of the north, toward that one small point. And standing round it.

It gathers, she’d written them. Before it goes.

“It’s at the hill,” she said, at about ten.

The boy with the map sent it.

“It’s stopped,” she said, at a quarter to twelve. “At the middle. Looking.”

He sent that too.

At about eleven, the point leaned.

She saw it. She’d seen it lean once before in her life. In the winter her mother was ill, when Isa was very small. The flame on the flat stone going down so low you could cover it with a thumb, and leaning. Toward everything at once. Her mother on her knees in front of it with her hands round it. Saying the words, over and over, very quietly. Here’s the middle. Not yet. All night.

It leaned now. The point of gold at the bottom of the north. Getting smaller. Going down.

Isa stopped breathing.

She couldn’t do anything. She was in a chair on a roof a very long way away, past an edge and three hundred stones. She was old, and lighter every morning, the boy with the jar said, though he never said it to her. She couldn’t walk back. She’d told them. You walked home. You can’t walk back.

She folded her root hands in her lap and said the words.

“Here’s the middle,” said Isa Corrie. To the point of gold, going down. Very quietly. The way her mother had, on her knees, the winter she was ill. “Not yet.”

The girl with the lantern, beside her, heard. She didn’t ask. She knelt down beside Isa’s chair and said it too.

“Here’s the middle. Not yet.”

And the boy with the map, at the parapet.

And at the bottom of the north, past everything, the point of gold stopped going down.

It stood still for a long moment. Small. Very small. Smaller than it had been at dusk.

Then it stood up.

Straight. Gold. As tall as it had been at dusk. Taller.

She didn’t know what had happened. She found out afterward, from a girl with gold horns who could see everything, who wrote it all down for her in a letter she had to have read to her three times. He turned round. He said everybody in. Nine of us round it, shoulder to shoulder. In the lee of everybody.

She didn’t know then. She just saw it stand up.

She put her hands over her face.

At four minutes past midnight, the dark round the point moved.

She saw it go. All of it. The deep, the gathered, the thick black round the hill that had been standing there since dusk. It stood up straight round the point of gold for a moment, the way the point stood. Looking.

Then it turned. South.

Toward the edge. Toward the road. Toward the round town.

“It’s coming,” said Isa. “Not looking. Just coming.”

The boy with the map sent it. His hand was shaking.

And then, because she’d watched them all night, the nine round the point, and she knew, she said the other thing.

“They held,” said Isa.

He sent that too. Very small. She says they held.

It came south all night. She watched it come.

Not the point. She watched the point, still. Standing straight up at the bottom of the north, past everything, after the dark had gone from round it. Gold, and small, and still. Kept.

But she watched the dark come, too. Down the moor. Up the road. The far lamps going out, the ninety-fourth and the ninety-third, north to south, covered. A black line coming down the road toward the round town under her. And the town, every light lit, waiting for it. Four hundred and thirty-one, the boy said.

She’d seen it come once before. From the road, behind it. Walking.

From the roof it was different.

From the roof she could see that it wasn’t reaching. Last year, walking behind it with her lamp, she’d felt it reaching. Every step. Looking for its middle. Leaning on every light it passed, hard, to see if that was it. This year it came down the road like weather. Like the tide. The way her grandmother said it used to come, before the winter the lamp nearly went out. Because it was the longest night, and it always came south, and the round town was where it came.

It wasn’t looking for anything.

It knew where it was.

She watched the north door hold.

She couldn’t see it; it was under her, at the north end of the Hall. But she could see the light on the steps. A small square of yellow from the little window over the door, where the girl who dipped candles had set one every morning for fifty days.

At about four the square went small.

Isa leaned forward in her chair.

Then it grew back. Steady. Tall. And stayed.

“Hm,” said Isa.

The girl with the lantern, beside her, laughed. She’d been crying on and off since dusk. She was laughing now.

“The big one,” said the girl. “Gabe. At the north door. Kei says he held.”

“I saw,” said Isa.

At half past six, the lamps came back on.

She watched them. From the far end. The ninety-fourth, at the top of the moor. Then the ninety-third. One after another, down the road, south, toward the round town, behind the dark as it sank. The way they’d come back on last year, behind her, as she walked.

Nobody walking this year.

They just came back on. On their own. Because the dark had gone by, and it knew where its middle was. And it didn’t need to take them with it.

And at the bottom of the north, past everything, the point of gold. Still there. Still standing straight up.

[ The Long Night has ended. ]

She couldn’t see the window. She saw the boy with the map’s face when it came. And the girl’s. And she knew.

At a quarter to eight, the boy with the map stood up.

He did it every morning now. He’d started it the morning after the nine went past the edge, at the long table. Standing up at the head of it at a quarter to eight. When the nine would be making their ring, a very long way north, past everything. Saying the first half. And sixty people saying the other.

He said it now. On the roof. At the eye. Looking north at a point of gold at the bottom of the sky that most of the round town couldn’t see.

“Set one to watch,” said the boy with the map.

Isa didn’t wait for anybody. She said it first. She’d said it every morning of her life. Her mothers had. Three hundred years.

“So the rest may sleep,” said Isa Corrie.

And the girl with the lantern. And from below, very faint, from the Hall and the steps and the doors and the windows and the roads, sixty-one people. And twenty of Meridian. And a woman at a crossroads with two blades across her knees, who’d been sitting at a milestone for seven months.

And very far away. Too far to hear. Past the edge, past the still water and three hundred stones. Round a flat stone on a hill, with a point of gold standing straight up over a jar. Nine.

She couldn’t hear them.

She knew.

They carried her down at eight, the boy and the girl, two hundred and twelve steps. She didn’t remember much of it. She was very tired. She’d been awake for a long time, and she was old, and lighter.

At the bottom, Ama Corrie was waiting. At the foot of the stair, not behind her counter. With her broom.

She looked at Isa in the chair for a long time. At her face.

“You saw it,” said Ama Corrie.

“I saw it,” said Isa.

“From here.”

“From here,” said Isa. “From the roof. Where my grandmother’s grandmother sat. Before she went.” She closed her eyes. “Three hundred years. Nobody ever saw it from here.”

Ama Corrie didn’t say anything for a long time.

Then she bent down. Very slowly; she was old too. And tucked the blanket in again, at the side, where it had come loose on the stair.

“Kept,” said Ama Corrie.

Not hm. The word.

Isa opened her eyes and looked at her. At her cousin, three hundred years removed. The one whose line had stayed, to keep the Roll and sweep the banner room and read the ceiling by the moon. While Isa’s line went north to carry the lights.

“Kept,” said Isa Corrie.

They carried her to her chair by the north door. She was asleep before they’d set it down. Under Ama Corrie’s grey blanket, round her shoulders, the way it had been every night since she came home.

And a very long way north, past the edge, past the still water and three hundred stones, on a hill, at the foot of a flat stone with a worn crown on it, the other one lay where she’d left it. Folded. Beside a bowl and an empty jar and a stick. The same grey.

Kept.
