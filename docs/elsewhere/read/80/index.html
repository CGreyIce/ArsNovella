Chapter 80

Not Today

3,138 words · 14 min read

Salty and Wendy had been eighteen inches apart in Singapore and eleven days apart in the game since December. On Saturday, the twenty-seventh of February, at a crossroads on the moor, the eleven days ran out.

Ice saw it happen. He’d walked nine days to see it.

The crossroads was a day out of Carrow Cross, where the road down from the black cliffs of Grimmet met the road out of the Greatwood from Ashcombe. A grey stone cross stood at the meeting, leaning, worn smooth. There was heather in every direction and a sky full of fast cloud. Moonlight Harvest had got there at noon. They’d been waiting three hours, on the low wall by the cross, with Bram and Joy. Nobody had said anything about why they’d come this way. They all knew. Kite had told Kei, and Kei had told Ice, in a message that said only: Ash likes crossroads. At dusk.

Salty came down the Grimmet road at three, with his greatsword on his back. Black dust from the mines was still in the creases of his coat.

Wendy came out of the forest road at three minutes past, with her bow, and her star-and-crescent necklace, and the grey ash of Ashcombe’s clamps on her hands.

They saw each other at about a hundred paces.

Neither of them ran. Ice had wondered if they would. They didn’t. They walked toward each other down the two roads, not fast, not slow, the way you’d walk toward something you’d been walking toward for two months and didn’t want to rush now that it was here. They met at the stone cross, and stopped, a foot apart, and looked at each other.

“Hi,” said Wendy.

“Hi,” said Salty.

“Eighteen inches,” said Wendy.

“Eleven days,” said Salty.

She put her hand flat on his chest, on the black dust, and left it there. He put his hand over hers. That was all. They stood at the cross in the heather with the cloud going over, and didn’t say anything else, because they didn’t need to. They’d been holding hands across eighteen inches in an old cinema in Singapore every night since December.

Ice looked away anyway. He looked at the sky, and the cloud, and the long grey line of the road south toward Carrow Cross. When he looked back, Salty was looking at him over Wendy’s head.

“You came,” said Salty.

“It’s on the way.”

“It is not on the way,” said Salty. “Fernwick’s in a swamp. This is a moor.”

“It’s on the way now,” said Ice. He’d heard Niko say it on the call, and liked it.

Dusk came at twenty past four.

They’d stayed at the cross on purpose. Ice had thought about it all the way from Fernwick, nine days of thinking. He’d decided that if Ash liked crossroads at dusk, he’d rather meet her at one he’d chosen, at the time he’d chosen, with ten people instead of two. We don’t start it. We don’t run. He’d said it on stream in December. He’d meant it then. He meant it more now.

“They’re coming,” said Suzu.

He was standing by the wall, with his scythe, looking at nothing. Then Ice saw it too. Out on the moor to the east, low in the heather, a shape that wasn’t a shape. Then another. Dark coats, with the grey of the evening on them, moving the way people moved when they’d done this a hundred times.

Seven. Eight.

“Positions,” said Ice quietly.

They knew where. They’d practised it at noon, the way Xav would have drawn it. Ice in front of the cross, where the four roads met. Gabe two paces from Kotori, and Kotori on the wall, the highest thing for a hundred yards. Sheng Yang behind whatever looked at Ice. Suzu wide, on the left, in the heather. Deston on the cross’s step with the book. Bram, square and silent, with his spear across the Grimmet road. Joy with her round shield beside him. Salty in front of Wendy. Wendy behind the wall with her bow, looking at the dusk.

The dark coats stopped at about forty paces.

One came forward. A woman, slight, with a calm narrow face, and two short curved blades at her hips that she didn’t draw. A little dark bird stitched on her collar.

“Swamp guy,” said Ash.

“Ash.”

“You’re a long way from your swamp.”

“It’s on the way,” said Ice.

Ash looked past him, at Salty and Wendy, at the wall and the cross and the ten of them. Then back at him. Her face did something very small.

“Somebody’s paying very well for this month,” she said. “Very, very well. For every name that doesn’t get to a Hall in time.” She tilted her head. “Two names, coming down two roads, alone, at dusk. They’d have been worth a lot. You’ve made them very expensive.”

“Red?” said Ice.

Ash smiled. She didn’t say anything. She didn’t need to.

“We’ll have your day another day, swamp guy,” she said. “I said that in December. I meant it.” She drew the blades, both at once, without any hurry. “Today’s another day.”

“Not today,” said Ice.

They came all at once. Nightjar always did.

The big man with the maul came at Ice, because the big man always came at whoever was in front. A spearwoman came round the left, at Suzu. A thin one with a staff stood back in the heather and started to cast. Three more went wide, round the right, at the wall, at Kotori and Wendy. And Ash went nowhere. She stood at forty paces with her blades out and watched, because that was what Ash did. She watched, and waited for somebody to be alone.

Nobody was alone.

The maul came down on Ice’s shield like a falling tree. He turned it. He felt it slide down the slope of Osric Fell’s old boss and go past him into the road, and the weight of it go down into the dark under his feet and stay there.

“Mend,” said Kotori, on the wall, without a single sorry. The light went into Gabe before the next blow had finished arriving.

The thin one with the staff finished casting. Something dark and fast came across the moor at Wendy, behind the wall.

She didn’t freeze.

Ice saw her not freeze. He’d heard about the longest night: a clamp in a dark wood, something huge coming out of the trees, and Wendy holding. Holding, and not letting go, until Salty said from eleven days away, so don’t wait. Let go. He saw her see the dark thing coming. He saw her draw. And he saw her let go.

Four arrows. Four. Before the dark thing had crossed half the distance. It came apart in the air over the wall like smoke in a wind. The fourth went on across the heather and into the thin one with the staff, who sat down very suddenly.

“I let go,” said Wendy, to nobody, breathing hard.

“I know,” said Salty, in front of her, with his greatsword up. “I heard.”

The maul came down a sixth time. A seventh. Ice turned it and Anchored it and held it, every blow, all of it, going down into the dark under the crossroads. The big man was breathing hard now. He couldn’t understand why the swamp guy wasn’t going down. Nobody ever understood. They hit you and hit you and you didn’t move, and they didn’t know that every blow was going somewhere.

“Now,” said Deston, from the step of the cross.

Ice let go.

Everything the maul had put into him came up out of the dark at once into the one cut. And the big man came toward him, off his feet, dragged through the road like a boat on a chain, into the space between Ice and the cross where Sheng Yang was standing.

The big man didn’t get up.

Then it was only Ash.

She’d watched all of it from forty paces, with her blades out, not moving. She’d watched her maul go, and her spear, and her staff, and two of her three on the right, and the third run. She stood alone in the heather in the last of the dusk, looking at the ten of them round the cross. Ice saw her do the sum. He’d watched Xav do sums for nine years. He knew what it looked like.

She didn’t run.

She came at him instead. Fast, faster than anything in the fen. She was the best player he’d faced since EVE, and he’d known it since December, and he’d been waiting for it ever since. Two blades, never in the same place twice, at his shield, past his shield, under it, round it. She didn’t hit the shield. She hit everywhere the shield wasn’t. That was the thing about Ash. She didn’t fight the line. She fought the gaps in it.

He turned what he could. He couldn’t turn all of it. A blade went across his forearm under the shield’s rim. Another across his ribs. He didn’t make a sound. He never did. Kotori’s light came into him before the cuts had finished arriving.

“You don’t flinch,” said Ash, between blows. Breathing hard. Almost admiring. “I watched you on the plank. Twelve hours. You never flinched once.”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because if the front flinches,” said Ice, “everybody flinches.”

He let go.

Not all of it. Just enough. The Undertow came up out of the road under his feet and into the cut, and Ash came toward him, off balance, one step, two. And this time the shield came round too, the flat of it, the old boss. Osric Fell’s, who’d held a ford for a day and a half with nineteen men and broken them at dawn. It took her in the chest, and she went down on her back in the heather at the foot of the stone cross with both blades gone out of her hands.

Ice stood over her with the sword.

He could have. He knew it, and she knew it, and all twenty-five thousand people in his chat knew it. We don’t start it. We don’t run. She’d started it. She’d come to a crossroads at dusk to take two people’s days, for money, for somebody in red.

He looked down at her. She looked up at him, flat on her back in the heather, with her calm narrow face, waiting.

“Tell whoever’s paying you,” said Ice, “that every name on that road has friends on it.”

He stepped back.

Ash lay there for a moment. Then she got up, slowly, and picked up her blades, and looked at him for a long time.

“You’re very annoying,” she said. “You know that?”

“I’ve been told.”

She looked at the cross, and the wall, and Wendy with her bow, and Salty, and the eight others standing round in the dusk with their weapons still up.

“Not today, then,” said Ash. And she turned and walked away into the heather, alone, toward the east, and didn’t look back.

They walked into Carrow Cross on Sunday at noon. Ten of them, down the moor road, through the north-west door of the Hall of Roads. Ice saw it for the first time: round, pale grey stone gone gold, seven doors.

Kei was on the steps.

He’d been on the steps every day for a month. Ice had heard about the steps on the phone, and in messages, and on the call when Kei had patched MH in for the escort. He’d pictured them a hundred times on the walk. He’d been wrong about them. They were just steps, worn in the middle. Kei was sitting on the third one with a notebook on his knee and the green tie in his hair, looking up at the ten of them coming across the round market.

They’d never met in the game. In years of calls, three months of ELSEWHERE, a phone call on a Sunday morning between the gym and the egg, they’d never once stood in the same place.

Kei stood up.

“Carrow Cross,” he said. “Here.”

“Not a member,” said Ice.

“I know,” said Kei. “You’re a friend.” He looked past Ice, at Salty and Wendy, standing together with her hand through his arm. Something went across his face, very quickly, like steam lifting off a spring. “Grimmet. Ashcombe.”

“Here,” said Salty.

“Here,” said Wendy.

Kei wrote it down. Then he looked at the two people standing a little behind Moonlight Harvest, not quite with them. A square silent Dutchman with a spear. A girl from Cebu with a round shield and a careful face.

“You said you knew people,” said Kei.

“I said I knew people who’d be good,” said Ice. “Not famous. Good.” He stepped aside. “Bram. Joy. They held some steps once, in a swamp, all night, on the longest night. Bram fell at four in the morning holding them. He came back the next day and held them again.” He paused. “They’re not mine. They’re not anyone’s. They walked.”

Kei looked at the two of them for a long time.

“At the gate,” he said. “Every time.”

“That’s all they want,” said Ice.

Ama Corrie looked at him over her spectacles in the Hall and said, “You’re the swamp one.” Ice said yes. She said, “Hm,” and went on sweeping. He understood that he’d been measured, and found adequate, and nothing more would be said about it.

He saw the ceiling. Not by moonlight. Just by lamplight, from the one place under the north lamp, the way Kei had seen it first. A few faint silver threads in the dark of the dome. He knew where the ring of nine was, from Chris’s message. He didn’t look for it. Not yet.

And outside, on the north road, at the milestone, he saw Kite.

He’d known he would. Kite was sitting where he’d sat for a week, with his bow across his knees, unstrung, and Tobi beside him, restringing a crossbow one-handed. Kite looked up as Ice came down the road toward the milestone. Ice saw him go still. The way people went still when the thing they’d been waiting for and dreading arrived.

Ice stopped in front of him.

“I shot at you,” said Kite. “On the north walk. In December.”

“I know.”

“You didn’t turn round.”

“No.”

“I wasn’t missing,” said Kite. “You just didn’t turn round.”

Ice looked at him. The narrow face, the dark patch on the collar where a bird had been, the loose threads. The bow, unstrung, the string tied off so that anybody could see from a long way away it couldn’t be used.

“You held the left side of a wall,” said Ice. “For Drew. All night.”

“Yeah.”

Ice nodded. Once. The way Suzu nodded.

He didn’t say anything else. He didn’t need to. He went back up the road to the round market, where the others were waiting on the steps of the Hall with Kei. Behind him, at the milestone, Kite sat for a long time without moving. Then he took the string off its tie, very slowly, and strung his bow.

There were twenty people in red sashes sitting round the fountain in the middle of the round market when Ice came back.

They’d arrived that morning, Kei said. Twenty, at once, down the plain road, in step. They hadn’t done anything. They’d sat down round the fountain, where Tiger had fallen in, and they’d stayed there. They were polite. They bought food from the stalls and paid properly. One of them, a neat young man with a ledger, had come to the steps and introduced himself to Kei as CL_Steward_03. He’d explained, very courteously, that the writ for the banner room ended at midnight on Thursday. If the room was free at one minute past, he and his colleagues would be happy to begin a writ of their own. They had the gold. They had the names. They had, he’d said, all the time in the world.

“Twelve signed,” said Kei, on the steps, quietly. “Four at the gate. Four on the road.”

“Who’s on the road?”

“Niko. Newish. Winter. Chris.” Kei looked at the notebook. “Four days out. Chris is fourteen. The writ ends Thursday at midnight.”

Ice looked at the twenty red sashes round the fountain, sitting in the sun, waiting.

“Then we go and get them,” he said.

“You’re not a member.”

“No,” said Ice. “We’re not joining.” He looked at Gabe, and Suzu, and Kotori and Sheng Yang and Deston, standing on the steps of a hall they weren’t in, under a ceiling that wasn’t theirs. “We’re walking.”
