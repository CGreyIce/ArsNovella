Live from Elsewhere · Volume 10: The Middle

Chapter 245

Hooded

3,624 words · 16 min read

“It can’t be seen,” said Kei. “That’s the whole thing. Not by us. By it.”

Ina looked at the jar on Joss’s anvil.

A plain clay jar the size of two hands cupped together, with a lid. Glowing, very faintly, through the clay. The way it had glowed on the long table in front of Isa’s chair for ten months, and in Suzu’s pack before that, and in the dark for three hundred years before that, on a flat stone with a crown cut into it.

She’d never been this close to it. She didn’t think anybody had, except Suzu and Isa. She could feel it from here, a pace off. Not heat. Something else. The way you feel somebody looking at you across a room.

“Isa says the dark’s looking for it,” said Kei. He was standing in the door of the smithy with his map case under his arm, the way he stood in every door. “The middle. It’s been gone ten months. The lamps are leaning. If it sees this go north, it’ll follow. All of it. The way it leans toward every light it can find.” He looked at the jar. “So it can’t see it go. Not till it’s on the stone.”

“So you want a box,” said Ina.

“I want something it can’t see through,” said Kei. “Isa says her mothers carried it in their hands. In the open. They didn’t need to hide it. It was always on the hill, and the dark always knew where it was.” He was quiet. “This time it’s been gone. And it’s looking.”

Ina looked at the jar for a long time.

“It doesn’t want to be in a box,” she said.

Kei looked at her.

“I can tell,” said Ina. “I don’t know how. I just can.”

She’d been a lure once. At Emberly, her town, a long way off now. In the first winter, when she’d come into the game because Maigo said she should, and she’d been so tired out there that she didn’t know how to be anything in here. They’d needed a light to draw the things off the wall, at Emberly, on the first longest night. She’d stood at the end of the wall with a Kindled brand in her hand and been the light. And everything had come at her. That was what a lure was for.

She’d hated it. She’d done it anyway. She’d been very good at it.

She knew what it was to be a light that something was looking for.

So she knew, looking at the jar on Joss’s anvil, that it didn’t want to be hidden. Lights didn’t. Lights wanted to be seen. That was the whole point of them. Isa’s mothers had kept it lit for three hundred years so that something could see it. So that the dark would always have one thing to look at that it couldn’t put out.

And now they had to carry it through the dark for three weeks with nothing able to see it at all.

“It’ll mind,” said Ina.

“I know,” said Kei. “Ask it to mind a bit less.”

Joss’s smithy was behind the Hart. It had been Joss’s grandfather’s, and his before. A low stone room with a forge at one end and an anvil in the middle, and a window at the back that looked out onto the yard where Jared had kept the middle pole. And on the windowsill, nineteen cracked test pieces of black iron in a row. Salty’s. From last autumn, when he was making the big sword, before Wendy held the lamp in the fourth tunnel and he got it right.

Joss was very old. Older than Ama Corrie, nearly. He sat in his chair at the back of the smithy now, mostly, and watched. And told people when they were doing it wrong, which was always, and cried sometimes, which he said was the smoke.

He looked at the jar on his anvil for a long time.

“Black iron,” he said.

Ina looked at him.

“It doesn’t shine,” said Joss. “My grandfather said. The dark’s own iron. It doesn’t like the light.” He looked at the nineteen test pieces on the windowsill. “The big one’s sword. The things in the dark can’t see it. Not on the longest night. It doesn’t give anything back.” He coughed. “If you want something the dark can’t see through, make it out of the dark.”

Ina looked at the test pieces. Then at the jar. Then at Joss.

“It’ll crack,” she said. “In the light. In the quench. Salty cracked nineteen.”

“Then do what he did,” said Joss. “Quench it where it came from.”

Salty went down the fourth tunnel for the iron on Tuesday. With Wendy, and the lamp, the way they went down most nights now. He came back up at the third dawn with a sack over his shoulder, very heavy, and put it down on Joss’s floor. And didn’t say anything, because he never did.

Black iron. Lumps of it, the size of fists, dull, so dark it seemed to drink the light from the forge. Ina picked one up. It was heavier than it looked. Everything about it was heavier than it looked.

“How much?” said Salty.

“Enough for a box,” said Ina. “With a lid. Round a jar.” She turned the lump in her hand. “And a star.”

Salty looked at her.

“On the top,” said Ina. “Isa’s lamp had a star on it. The cage it came home in. Five lines, bent, on the top.” She looked at the old cage hanging on its hook by the north door, through the smithy window, across the yard, through the Hall. Empty, with Carried. And carried home. on its rim in Curse’s blue. “Her great-grandmother’s mother bent one in this forge. Joss’s grandfather saw. An old woman from the north road, the last of somebody.”

At the back of the smithy, Joss made a noise and said it was the smoke.

“So there’ll be a star,” said Ina. “In black iron. On the top. So it knows whose it is.”

She’d made stars before. Ninety-four of them, last autumn, for the cages on the backs of the waystones on the north road. Five lines, bent in the fire, crossed, riveted. She’d done the twenty-fourth at seventy-four per cent, and Joss had cried. Then she’d done seventy more, and got faster, and stopped thinking about it.

She’d never made one in black iron.

It didn’t bend like iron. That was the first thing. It didn’t want to. She put the first bar in the forge on Wednesday night and it took twice as long to come up to heat as ordinary iron. And when it did, it didn’t glow red the way iron did. It glowed a very dark red, nearly brown, like a coal that was about to go out. And it didn’t shine. Even hot, even in the forge, it didn’t give anything back.

She took it out with the tongs and put it on the anvil and hit it.

It didn’t move.

She hit it again. Harder. Joss’s hammer, the big one, the one that had been his grandfather’s.

It moved a hair.

“It doesn’t want to,” said Ina.

“Nor did the sword,” said Salty, from the door. “Four hundred and eighty folds. It took me a month.”

“I haven’t got a month.”

“Then ask it,” said Salty.

Ina looked at him.

“That’s what Ice says,” said Salty. “About everything now. Since the cellar. Ask it.” He shrugged. “He asked me how to swing it. He asked Hyv if they were together. He asked the jar, at Christmas, if it wanted to go home for a bit.” He looked at the bar on the anvil, dark, not shining. “Ask it.”

She didn’t know how to ask a bar of iron anything.

She stood at the anvil on Wednesday night with the hammer in her hand and the bar going cold. And thought about it. She thought about being a lure at Emberly, with a brand in her hand, and everything coming at her. About standing at the end of a wall being the light that something was looking for. About how much she’d hated it, and done it anyway, and been good at it.

She thought about how she’d stopped, out there. How she’d been so tired, the first winter, that she’d stopped doing everything she used to do. And come in here, and found her hands again. At an anvil, at Joss’s, with a star.

She put the bar back in the forge. She waited for it to come up to its dark red. She took it out.

She didn’t hit it.

She held it on the anvil in the tongs and looked at it. The dark’s own iron, that didn’t like the light, and didn’t give anything back.

“You’re going to carry a light,” she said to it. Out loud, in the smithy, at the third dawn. Feeling stupid. “Not shine. Carry. Through the dark. So nothing can see it till it gets home.” She swallowed. “You don’t have to like the light. You just have to hold it.”

She hit it.

It bent.

Not much. A hand’s width. But it bent, the way iron bent, on the anvil, under the hammer, the first time.

At the back of the smithy, Joss said oh very quietly and didn’t say it was the smoke.

It took her nine nights.

Not a month. Nine nights, at the forge, at the third dawn, with Salty bringing more iron up from the fourth tunnel and Wendy holding the lamp. And Joss in his chair at the back, telling her when she was doing it wrong. Drew came most nights and did the bars, the long thin ones for the frame, the way he’d done them for the waystone cages. Gabe came on the fourth night and did the rivets without being asked. Slep came on the sixth, with his glaive on his back, and stood at the second anvil and didn’t say anything and made the hinge. Slep was going north. He wanted something of his on it.

She made a box.

Not square. Round, like the jar, a little bigger. Black iron, in bars, close together, with plates between them so nothing could get through. A lid that fitted down over the top like a hood. A latch on the lid that only opened one way, from the top, from above, so it couldn’t come open by accident on a rope in the dark. And holes underneath, small, angled down, so it could breathe.

Because it had to breathe. She’d known that on the first night. It didn’t want to be in a box. But if it was going to be in one, it had to be able to breathe.

And on top of the lid, a star.

Five lines. Bent in the fire, crossed, riveted. Black iron. She did it on the last night, the ninth, at the third dawn, with Salty and Wendy and Drew and Slep and Gabe all in the smithy and nobody saying anything. And Joss in his chair at the back with his hands on his knees.

She bent the five lines the way she’d bent ninety-four. The way she’d copied the old cage, last autumn, from the star Isa’s great-grandmother’s mother had bent in this forge with her hands. She crossed them. She riveted them. She put it on the lid.

Ina read it twice.

Then she put the hammer down on the anvil, and sat down on the floor of Joss’s smithy with her back against the anvil’s block. And didn’t get up for a while.

“Try it,” said Kei.

He’d come at the second dawn, the next day, with Suzu and the jar. And Hyvred, because Kei said if anybody could see through it, she could.

Suzu held the jar in both hands, the way he always held it. Carefully. Like somebody carrying a light. He looked at the black case on Joss’s anvil. Open, the hood back, the inside dark and empty.

He didn’t put the jar in.

“It doesn’t want to,” said Suzu.

Ina looked at him.

“I know,” she said. “I asked the iron. The iron didn’t want to either.” She stood up off the floor. “Ask it.”

Suzu looked at her for a long time.

Then he looked down at the jar in his hands. Glowing faintly through the clay. Three hundred years, and ten months on a long table, and a winter in the dark before that.

“It’s not for long,” said Suzu. To the jar. Very quietly. “Three weeks. In the dark. So it doesn’t see you coming.” He was quiet. “And then the stone. And the ring. And home. Properly.” He swallowed. “It can be dark for a bit. You’ve been dark for three hundred years. It’s just three weeks more.”

He put the jar in.

Ina closed the hood over it. The latch went home with a small sound, from the top. And the star sat on the lid, black, crossed, riveted.

The smithy was dark.

Not dark. Joss’s forge was going, and Wendy had the lamp. But the faint glow that had been on Joss’s anvil, that Ina had been feeling for nine nights, was gone. Completely. There was a black iron case on the anvil with a black iron star on it, and nothing coming out of it at all.

“Hyv,” said Kei.

Hyvred looked at it.

All of it. Edge to edge, the way she looked at everything in here. Ina watched her face. She looked at the case for a long time. Then she put the lamp out herself, Wendy’s lamp, without asking, and the smithy went dark except for the forge. And she looked at the case again in the dark.

“Nothing,” said Hyvred. “I can’t see anything. Not through it. Not round it.” She was quiet. “It’s not there.”

“It’s there,” said Suzu. He had his hand on the lid. “I can feel it.”

“It’s there,” said Ina. “It’s just not showing anybody.”

Isa held it, at the end. She asked to.

They carried it to her, Suzu and Ina, through the Hall to the banner room. To the chair by the north door, where she was awake with her blanket. She looked at the black case for a long time. At the star on the lid.

Then she held out her root hands, and Suzu put it in them.

She held it in her lap. She didn’t open it. She ran one finger along the five black lines of the star, very slowly, the way you’d run your finger along a line of writing you’d known all your life.

“My great-grandmother’s mother bent one of these,” she said. “In that forge. For the cage.”

“I copied it,” said Ina. “From the old cage. The one by the door.”

Isa looked at her.

“It’s the same,” she said. “The bend. The way the third line goes under.” She touched it. “She always did the third line under. Everybody else does it over.” She looked at Ina for a long time with her bright black eyes. “Who taught you to do the third line under?”

“Nobody,” said Ina. “It was on the old cage. I just did what it did.”

“Hm,” said Isa.

She held the black case in her lap for a long time, with her hand on the star.

“My mothers would have liked it,” she said at last. “They’d have been too proud to use it. They carried it in their hands. Every one of them.” She looked down at it. “But they were always walking to the hill. Never away. The dark always knew where it was.” She was quiet. “This is the first time it’s ever been carried home into the dark. Instead of out of it.”

She held it out to Suzu.

“It needed a hood,” said Isa. “For that.”

Milli made the frame. He’d been at the long table when Ina came back from the banner room with soot on her hands and her face doing something. He’d looked at the black case under her arm and said it needs something to go on, and gone to his corner.

He made it in two nights. Leather, brown, good hide, with straps that went over the shoulders and round the chest, and a cradle at the back that the black case sat in, snug. So it rode high, between the shoulder blades, where you could feel it. And on the left strap, where your hand would fall if you reached up to check it was there, a cat’s-paw, stamped.

Suzu put it on at the long table on Sunday, with everybody watching.

He stood up in it. The black case on his back, between his shoulders, with the star on top. Nothing coming out of it at all. He reached up with his left hand to check it was there, the way Milli had meant him to. His hand found the cat’s-paw on the strap.

“It’s heavy,” said Suzu.

“Everything about black iron is heavier than it looks,” said Ina.

Suzu looked at her across the long table.

“Thank you,” he said.

It was, Deston would say afterward, about the fifth time. He was keeping count now. Everybody was.

She went back to the smithy that night, on her own, after. Nobody alone, Kei said, but that was for the longest night, and she’d been in the smithy alone a hundred times. Joss was asleep in his chair at the back. The forge was banked.

She sat on the floor with her back against the anvil’s block and looked at the windowsill. Nineteen cracked test pieces of black iron in a row, Salty’s, from last autumn.

There was a twentieth now. She’d put it there herself, at the end of the nine nights. The first bar, from the Wednesday, that she’d hit twice and it hadn’t moved, before she asked it. It had a crack in it too, a fine one, down the middle. From the first quench, in the light, before she’d learned to take it down the fourth tunnel to the dark stream with Wendy’s lamp out.

She looked at it for a long time.

You’re going to carry a light, she’d said, to a bar of iron, in the smithy, feeling stupid.

She’d been a light once. At the end of a wall, at Emberly, with everything coming at her. She’d hated it. She’d stopped being anything at all, out there, for a long time, because she was so tired of being looked at.

And she’d come in here, and found her hands. And made a thing that carried a light without showing anybody. That held it, in the dark, so it wouldn’t have to be looked at. Until it got home.

She thought maybe that was the thing she’d been trying to make for a long time.

At the back of the smithy, in his chair, Joss said something in his sleep. It might have been the third line under. It might have been the smoke.
