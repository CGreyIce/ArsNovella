Suzu came over the chalk road into Brackenford on Saturday morning with Aldo’s scythe over his shoulder and rain in his hair, and the first thing he said was:

“It’s bigger than you said.”

“I said it was big.”

“You said big.” Suzu stopped at the end of the west bridge and looked at the town, the three rivers coming together, the mills and the bridges and the black needle in the square. He looked at it for a long time, slowly, the way he looked at everything. “This is bigger than big.”

They’d been waiting at the end of the bridge since dawn, the four of them. Deston had been sitting on the parapet with his legs swinging for an hour. Xav had been standing with his hands in his pockets, pretending he wasn’t waiting. Kotori had been holding her staff like the strap on a bus, and every time somebody with long hair came over the rise on the chalk road the bell rang, because she’d started forward and then stopped.

Ice had been sitting next to Suzu’s pod in the back room in Melaka Raya three days ago, watching him on a laptop. It wasn’t the same as this. Suzu was here, on the same wet stone, in the same grey morning light, with the same river going under them both. His silver hair was dark with rain and his half-closed eyes were taking in the town, and the long dark scythe that had been straightened once for a war and bent back once for a meadow was resting on his shoulder.

“Hi,” said Ice.

“Hi,” said Suzu. The corner of his mouth went up about a millimetre. “You’re fifteenth.”

“I know.”

“You were looking at it. I saw. On my way in.”

“I wasn’t looking at it.”

“He was looking at it,” said Xav, and held out his fist. Suzu bumped it with his.

Deston didn’t do fists. He came down off the parapet and hugged Suzu, scythe and all, so that the blade swung round and Xav had to step back sharply. Suzu stood very still in the hug with his arms at his sides, the way he always had for hugs, and then, at the very end, patted Deston twice on the back.

“Hi, Dest.”

“Suzu. You’re here.”

“I walked.”

“You walked. For two days. With a scythe.” Deston let go of him and held him at arm’s length. “Your hair’s so long.”

“It’s always been this long.”

“Not when you’re standing in front of me.”

And then Suzu looked past Deston, at the small girl in the pale blue robe with the white staff and the bell, who was standing a little way back with an expression of enormous worry.

“You’re Kotori,” said Suzu.

“Hi!” said Kotori. “Sorry! I’m the healer. Sorry. I don’t know why I’m saying sorry. Hi.”

Suzu looked at her for a long moment, with his half-closed eyes. He looked at the bell, and at the way she was standing, slightly to the side of all of them, where she could see all four.

“Good,” he said.

Kotori went bright red and put her face behind her staff, and the bell rang.

He’d thought they’d go straight back to the ford. He’d thought about nothing else for two days. He’d lain awake in the house in Malacca, with Suzu asleep at the other end of the landing and the fan going round, and played it in his head. Every strike. The second stone with the water over it. The small one, standing up behind him in the dark.

Xav made them wait until noon.

“Daylight,” said Xav. “Not morning. Noon. Middle of the day, sun on the water, nothing awake that doesn’t have to be.” He’d drawn the ford on a scrap of paper on the bench outside the Adventurers’ Hall: the stepping stones, the deep water below, the upstream water where the second one had been waiting. “And we do it properly this time.”

He’d thought about it. Ice could see that he’d thought about it for two days too.

Kotori on her rock, the flat one below the ford where the six silverleaf had washed up. Xav pointed it out with the pencil: from there, she could see the whole crossing, both banks, and both bits of deep water, and nothing in the river could reach her without crossing the current. Suzu on the near bank, upstream, facing the deep water where the small one lived, with his scythe, alone. “It’s smaller,” said Xav. “And it’s fourteen. And you cut a wide row. You hold it there. You don’t let it get to the stones.” Deston on the bank behind Ice, close enough to sing to all of them, with Share the Weight on Ice the whole time. “The whole time,” said Xav, looking at Deston, and Deston nodded. Xav himself was round the back of the big one, in the water. Ice on the second stone, not the third.

“Not the third,” said Ice.

“Not the third. The second’s got water over it at night. Not at noon.” Xav tapped the paper. “And you don’t step back. Ever. If something goes wrong, you Anchor, and you wait, and Kotori fixes it.”

Ice looked at the drawing for a while.

“You’ve done this before,” said Suzu, watching Xav.

“Every raid I’ve ever planned,” said Xav, “has started with Dec doing something stupid on his own the week before.”

“That’s fair,” said Ice.

“I know it’s fair. I wrote it down.”

At noon, with the sun high and the river glittering and Brackenford’s bridges full of people stopping to watch, they went down to the old ford.

Word had got round, the way it did. The swamp guy who’d died at the ford was going back with his party. By the time they reached the bank there were fifty people on the nearest bridge, leaning on the parapet, and three camera orbs hanging overhead. Ice had his own stream up, and it was busier than it had been since the Tally.

Kotori was on her rock. She’d got there without getting her feet wet, as she always did. Ice still didn’t know how. She was standing in the middle of it with her feet together and her staff held out, and the bell was very still.

Suzu was on the upstream bank, alone, with Aldo’s scythe held low and his long silver hair loose, looking at the deep water where the small one lived. He wasn’t moving at all.

Deston was behind Ice, on the bank, with the book open on his palm and his other hand on the spine.

Xav was gone. Ice didn’t know where. That was the point.

He stepped onto the first stone. The second. He stopped on the second, with the water cold and fast round his ankles and the sun on it, and set his feet, and sloped the shield.

Two little black eyes opened in the water below the third stone.

“Let the MANY move as ONE,” sang Deston, behind him, and then, without a breath between, low, “What one BEARS, two can CARRY. Ice.”

The Warden stood up.

It was the same thing. It was the same great grey shape coming up out of the river all at once, streaming, river stones on its shoulders like barnacles, the size of a falling tree. It was the same arm coming down from above. He’d dreamed about that arm for two nights.

But it wasn’t the same fight, because he wasn’t alone in it.

He turned the first strike, the way he had before, and some of it got through into his arm, white and ringing, the way it had before. And a third of it went somewhere else. He felt it go: a third of the weight leaving him, back along a thread to the bank, where Deston grunted and kept singing. And before the rest had finished arriving, there was a bell.

Warmth went into his arm from the side, from the river, from a small girl on a flat rock, and the red bar dipped and stopped and came back.

He Anchored the second strike. It went back the way it came, with the sound of a door slammed in a storm. And out of the water behind it, exactly one second later, because Deston was counting him in, came Xav.

Upstream, the second pair of eyes opened.

Ice didn’t look. He’d promised. He’d promised Xav and himself on the bench outside the Adventurers’ Hall: you don’t look, you don’t step back, you hold the stone. He heard it, though. He heard the second Warden stand up out of the upstream water, the great rush of it. Then he heard a sound he’d only heard on stream, from a long way off, on a hill in the rain.

Shhhhk.

Long and low, a scythe going round from the right at the height of a man’s knees. He heard the small Warden roar, high and surprised. Shhhhk, again. It hadn’t moved toward the stones. It couldn’t. Every time it tried, something long and dark went round at knee height and it had to stop.

The big one brought its right arm down, and then its left, and the left was half a second late, the way it had been on Wednesday night. The stones on that shoulder were heavier. Ice cut into the half-second, and breathed out, and the whole of him arrived at once.

It took nine minutes. He’d fought it alone for twenty, and lost. With five, it took nine.

The big one went first. It came at him one last time, both arms at once, and he Anchored, and it went back. Xav came out of the water behind it, and Deston held the last note of the line for exactly a second, and they both landed together. The great grey shape stood very still in the sun with the water running off it. Then it broke into grey dust that drifted away down the river in the light.

And out of the dust, out of the very middle of it, came a light. Bigger than the wisps on Suzu’s hill, the size of a fist, pale blue, humming so low that Ice felt it in his teeth.

On the upstream bank, Suzu’s head came round.

He’d been holding the small one in the deep water for nine minutes, with nothing but a knee-high wall of scythe. And when the big one’s light came out of the dust he turned, one step, and swung the long dark blade up and round, not at the small Warden, at the air above the river. The light caught on the blade, the way the wisps had on the ridge. It clung there for a second, blue and bright along the edge, and Suzu had the jar out of his belt and the stopper in his teeth before it could fade.

The small one saw the gap and came for the stones.

It didn’t reach them. Ice was already there. He’d stepped off the second stone toward it, not back, toward, the way he’d promised himself he wouldn’t. But this was different. This was the line, and he was the Holdfast, and where a Holdfast stands, a line exists. He Anchored in the shallows between the small Warden and his friends. It hit the slope and went back, and Xav was already behind it, and Suzu was already round, and Kotori’s bell rang twice, and Deston was singing.

There was a noise from the bridge he wasn’t expecting.

Fifty people, more by now, leaning on the parapet. They’d been watching the whole nine minutes. And when the second Warden broke into dust and drifted away down the bright river, somebody started clapping, and then all of them were. Somebody whistled. Somebody shouted something in Portuguese that came to him in English a heartbeat late: “THE SWAMP GUY!”

He stood in the shallows below the old ford with his shield hanging from his arm and his sword hanging from his hand, and his chest going up and down, and didn’t know what to do with his face.

Kotori was on her rock with her staff held against her chest, and she was crying, a little, and laughing about it, and saying “Sorry! Sorry!” to nobody. Deston had sat down on the bank with the book in his lap and his head back, laughing at the sky. Suzu was standing on the upstream bank looking into his jar at the light he’d caught, completely absorbed, as if there were nobody else in the world.

And Xav came up out of the water beside him, soaked to the chest, and stood there, and didn’t say anything for a while.

“Not the third stone,” said Xav eventually.

“Not the third stone.”

“Good.” He looked at the river. “That was good, Dec.”

They levelled for four days after that, and it was the best four days Ice had ever had in any game.

It wasn’t the levels, though the levels came. It was the shape of it. On the fourth day, standing in the middle of the footbridge at the weir with a pack of six mudwolves coming at him, he realised he no longer knew where any of the others were. He didn’t have to. He’d stopped watching for them. He set his feet and sloped the shield and banged the sword on the rim. Somewhere behind him Deston was singing. Somewhere to the right was a long low shhhhk at knee height. Somewhere he couldn’t see was Xav, a second from something’s back. Somewhere a bell rang before the teeth went in.

Five people who’d never all been in the same room. Two from the same school choir. One from a poly class. One off a bus from Singapore with his own pillow. And one who’d come to a bench outside the Adventurers’ Hall because a notice said kind people only.

They’d only been five for three days, and they moved like a hand.

He brewed every night, at the fire in the inn by the east bridge that the landlady let him use for a copper an hour. He’d come to Brackenford with a pack full of herbs from the ring and Sedge’s fenwort, and a little stoppered bottle of moonbell water he’d drawn off before they left. He made Good draughts with all of it, forty-one of them. He didn’t sell a single one. They were for the door.

Kotori sat with him on the third night and watched him grind, round and even and don’t stop, until the leaves gave up their green. She asked if she could try. She was terrible at it. She went up and down instead of round, and the first draught came out so cabbagey that the landlady opened a window. She apologised to the draught.

“Sorry,” she said, to the bottle. “That was my fault.”

“It’s a draught. It doesn’t mind.”

“It might,” said Kotori seriously, and corked it very gently.

On the second day, Chris found out about VANTA.

He told chat first, and chat told Ice, and Ice read it at the edge of his eye in the middle of a mudwolf.

He finished the mudwolf. Then he stood very still on the bridge.

Herons. Five hands. Five words.

His door.

No. Not his door. A door. Somewhere on the other side of the world, in a salt marsh he’d never heard of, there was another arch with herons cut wing to wing round it. Another slab, the same five words, the same choir singing underneath. The Drowned Choir had more than one way in. Every party that found a door would go down its own steps into its own drowned church, alone. But it was the same church and the same choir and the same song. And whichever five people finished it first, anywhere in the world, would be first in the whole world.

He’d been so sure nobody knew where the door was. He’d been right. Nobody knew where his door was. That had never been the question.

“Dec,” said Xav, beside him.

“I saw.”

“They wiped. That’s good. That’s a day.”

“They’ll go again tomorrow night.”

“And wipe again, probably. First tries always wipe.” Xav looked at him. “How far are we?”

Ice didn’t need to look. He’d been doing the sum for two days.

“Me and you, Tuesday. Dest and Suzu, Wednesday. Kotori—“ He looked across at her, on a rock in the middle of the weir pool, humming Deston’s line a beat behind. “Kotori’s thirteen. Two more.”

“Wednesday night, then,” said Xav.

“If we push.”

“Then Thursday,” said Xav. “We walk. And we do it once.”

VANTA wiped again on Monday night, all five, at the second room. Chris reported it with the precision of a war correspondent.

Ice reached fifteen on Tuesday afternoon, at the weir, with the bell ringing. A gold window. He stood and looked at it for a while. Fifteen. The number cut over a slab in the fen, five words in a plain old hand. Xav was fifteen an hour later. Deston and Suzu on Wednesday, within ten minutes of each other, Deston singing so loudly at his that two NPCs on the bridge came to see what was wrong.

Kotori was the last.

She was fourteen and ninety-one per cent at nine o’clock on Wednesday night. She’d had to log out for dinner and come back. She’d said sorry about it four times. They fought three more packs at the weir in the dark, with Ice pulling them to the bridge and holding them there longer than he needed to, so that she’d have more to heal. On the last mudwolf of the last pack, a bell rang, and a window opened over a small girl standing on a stone in the middle of a black pool, and she stared at it.

“Oh,” said Kotori. “Oh! Oh, it’s fifteen. Sorry! It’s fifteen!”

Deston cheered so loudly the mudwolves in the next pool came up to look.

They stood together that night at the foot of the needle in the square, the five of them, in the lamplight, and looked up.

Ice was ninth again. He looked at it once, and then he looked at the other four, and found he didn’t care very much where he was on the stone.

“Thursday,” said Xav.

“Thursday,” said Ice. “We walk at dawn. Sedge wants cress. Fresh.”

“I’ll pick the cress,” said Kotori at once. “I know where it grows. Sorry. I mean—I saw it. By the mill. I can pick it.”

“Twist and lift,” said Ice. “Root whole. One in three.”

“One in three,” said Kotori, very seriously, as if he’d given her a sacred instruction, which, he supposed, he had.

Suzu was looking at his jar again, the one with the big Warden’s light in it. It was still glowing through the glass after four days, pale blue, and still humming so low you felt it in your teeth.

“What are you going to do with it?” said Deston.

“I don’t know yet,” said Suzu. He held it up to the lamp. “I want to know where it goes.”
