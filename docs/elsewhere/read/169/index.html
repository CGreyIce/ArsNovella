Live from Elsewhere · Volume 7: Keep the Lamps

Chapter 169

Ask Me Again

3,591 words · 16 min read

Xav had drawn the seven fights, and he hadn’t drawn Dec once.

He’d drawn feet. That was all. Seven pages, one for each night, and on every page two sets of feet. Little marks, like the footprints in a dance diagram, going round the ring. The other person’s in pencil. Dec’s in ink. And between them, everywhere, dots. A dot wherever the other person had been going to be. A dot, then a line, then Dec’s ink mark already standing on the dot when the pencil mark arrived.

He sat in the Hart on Monday night with all seven pages spread out on the table and looked at them.

They were beautiful. He could say that about his own work. He didn’t, often. But they were. Seven nights of somebody being exactly in the right place before anybody else knew there was a place. Looked at all together, they looked like something a river does.

He was looking at the fourth page, the Crimson Ledger one, when he saw it.

He didn’t know what it was at first. A wrongness, the way you see a wrong line in a drawing before you can say which. He looked at it for a long time. Dec’s feet, going in under the greatsword. The step that had put the shield’s rim into the man’s hands. He’d drawn it exactly. He’d watched it from four paces off.

He took out his pencil and drew it again in the margin, smaller. The way Dec would have done it before.

It was different.

Not much. A quarter of a foot. An angle. The back foot came round later than it used to. As if Dec had learned to wait on it for a breath longer, so the step could go either way. Xav sat back in the Hart’s corner and looked at the two drawings side by side, the new one and the one he’d drawn from memory.

That’s new, he thought.

He hadn’t seen anything new in the way Dec moved for a long time.

Kestrel’s message had come on Monday morning, his time, while he was still in his flat with the window open and the kettle on. It came through the game’s letters, which arrived on your phone if you’d let them. He had. Mostly so that Rui Xiang’s forty-one messages a day had somewhere to go that wasn’t his actual phone.

[ Letter · from Kestrel (VANTA)
Sketch.
I’d like to come and ask you something. In person.
Not this week. Next week, after Brann’s fought. He asked me first if he could; I’m asking you first if I can.
— K. ]

He read it twice. He put the phone down on the counter and made his tea and drank it standing at the window. He looked at the street below, which was very quiet at that hour, a man walking a small dog the shape of a loaf. Then he picked the phone up and wrote back.

[ Letter · to Kestrel (VANTA)
Ask me then. ]

He didn’t say yes. He didn’t say no. He thought she’d understand that, because she’d once offered him a seat in VANTA. That had been in February, at a crossing in the rain, a long time ago. He’d said no, and she’d said ask me again when you’re bored of eight, and he’d said I won’t be. He hadn’t been.

Riverbank had the long end of the second table in the banner room, by the west window. Eight chairs, and they sat in them the way they’d sat in their seats in the poly classroom at nineteen. Which was to say, wrongly, in each other’s, with their feet on the rungs.

“Kestrel’s coming to see you,” said Rui Xiang, on Wednesday, before Xav had said anything.

Xav looked at him.

“How do you know that?”

“You’ve got a face,” said Rui Xiang. “You’ve got your letter face. You’ve had it since Monday. You only get a letter face for VANTA.” He counted on his fingers, for no reason. “Also Kei told me.”

“Kei didn’t know.”

“Kei knows everything. Kei knew we were called Riverbank before you did.” RX leaned back on two legs of his chair. “Are you going to say yes?”

“To what?”

“To whatever it is.”

“I don’t know what it is yet.”

“Yes you do,” said Malcolm, from the far end. He was cleaning his stick with a cloth, the way he did every evening, very slowly, as if it might be the last time. “She wants you to teach her. She lost to Dec. She wants to know how.” He held the stick up to the lamp and squinted down it. “It’ll be March before she gets it, mind.”

“It’s always March with you.”

“It’s always March,” agreed Malcolm.

“I’d teach her,” said John. He was turning his little round mirror over and over on its cord, the way he did when he was thinking, so the lamplight went round the ceiling. “She’s good. She just needs to stop deciding things where people can see.” He caught the light on the mirror and put it on Xav’s nose. “You could teach her that in a week.”

“Two,” said Xav.

“So you are going to,” said RX.

Xav didn’t answer. Down the table, Jun Liang started singing something under his breath about a woman with a bow. Val kicked him under the table without looking up from her book. Ryan, beside her, said “yeah,” to nothing in particular, which was the most Ryan said most nights.

Azac had been quiet. He was sitting at the end with his huge shield propped against the wall behind him, eating bread. When the singing had stopped he said, with his mouth full:

“You’ve been weird since Tuesday.”

“I’ve been weird since we were seventeen.”

“Since Tuesday particularly.” Azac swallowed. “You drew something. At the Crimson Ledger one. You stopped drawing for about a minute and stared at Dec’s feet like they owed you money.”

Xav looked at him.

Azac was a wall. He’d always been a wall; he’d said so on the first day, in the booth in Singapore. Dec’s tank. I’m a wall. But a wall stood at the front of things, and a thing at the front saw everything coming. People forgot that about Azac.

“He’s doing something new,” said Xav.

The table went quiet.

“Dec is,” said Xav. “With his back foot. He’s waiting on it longer. A breath. So the step can go either way.” He shrugged. “I haven’t seen him do anything new in years. Not since… a long time.”

“Is that bad?” said John.

Xav thought about it.

“I don’t know yet,” he said.

VANTA came in on Friday by the east road for Brann’s fight. Xav didn’t go to meet them. He was already in his place at the edge of the ring, between the fifth and sixth stones, with his sketchbook. He watched them walk in under their black banner with its one white line, and the market make room.

Kestrel looked at him once, across the whole round market, on her way to the Hall steps. Just once. Then she sat down with Ottie and didn’t look again.

He drew the seventh fight. Brann’s feet, planted, never moving, a solid pencil square on the paving. Dec’s ink, round and round it, in a circle, for two minutes. He drew it as fast as he could and still missed things. He drew the lean, and the step back, the first time anyone had seen Dec go back. He drew the three hits taken square. And then the pull, Brann’s square coming off the paving forward in a long smear of pencil, onto its face.

When it was over, Dec stood on the grey stone outside the ring with his hand in his pocket. Xav had seen his hand before he’d put it there. He’d seen it shake on the oak at Elmcross, too, after.

He shut the sketchbook.

Dec looked at him, from ten paces off. He wasn’t asking anything. He was just looking, the way he looked at the end of a fight. Making sure everyone was where they’d been.

Xav nodded. I’m here.

Dec nodded back, and went to the Hart door where Kotori was waiting.

Kestrel found him on Saturday morning, at the first dawn, on the roof of the dome.

He hadn’t told anyone he’d be there. He went up most mornings, the whole two hundred and twelve, because the light was good and Higu wasn’t up yet and nobody bothered you. He was sitting on the ledge in Higu’s place with his sketchbook, drawing the seven roads going out of the round market in the dawn, when he heard someone on the last flight. Sixty-six steps. They were counting under their breath, the way everybody did the first time.

She came out onto the ledge, slightly out of breath, with her bow on her back.

“You weren’t in the Hall,” she said.

“No.”

“The woman at the counter told me where you’d be.”

“Ama Corrie,” said Xav. “She knows where everyone is.”

“She told me to sweep on the way down.” Kestrel looked out at the view for a moment, at the roads and the roofs and the moor going north. Then she sat down on the ledge, a long way off. Not close. A respectful distance, the way she’d sat on the Hall steps. “Do people actually do that?”

“Under, not round,” said Xav. “Yes.”

She didn’t say anything for a while. She looked at the view. He went on drawing. He wasn’t going to make it easier for her; it wasn’t his to make easier. You asked or you didn’t.

“I should have done more research,” said Kestrel.

Xav’s pencil stopped.

She’d said it to the view, very quietly. But she’d said it on purpose. He heard the shape of it. It was what Dec had said to CL_07 on a road in November, in the first fortnight, when nobody was watching yet. Then you should’ve done more research. Everybody knew it now. Somebody had made it into a sticker.

“Did you watch it?” said Xav. “The Saltwell VODs. His. Before the duel.”

“I watched everything. Every VOD since December. I know how he holds a shield. I know where he stands in a room.” She was still looking at the view. “I didn’t watch EVE. I didn’t know there was an EVE. I’d never heard of it. I didn’t know there was a before.” She laughed, short, not happy. “Brann said a tank can’t catch a bow. I agreed with him. I’d done all the research on the wrong man.”

Xav said nothing.

“I’ve watched it a hundred times since,” said Kestrel. “The duel. Mine. I know exactly what happened, step by step. I can see him go first. I can see him go first before I’ve decided. I can see that he reads my shoulder.” She turned and looked at him at last. “And I still can’t see it. When I’m in it. I can see it afterwards. I can’t see it in it. I don’t know how.”

“No,” said Xav.

“You can.”

He looked at her.

“At Elmcross,” said Kestrel. “You sat at the edge of the ring and drew. And at the Long Watch, on his stream, in the corridor. You were behind the sword arm before anything knew you’d moved. And on Wednesday night, the girl from Osaka. She stopped at thirty-eight and looked at you. Not at him. At you.” She took a breath. “You’re the one he said. When I asked him if there was anyone. He didn’t even think. Sketch. Riverbank. Xav.”

“He was being nice.”

“He wasn’t. You said he was. He wasn’t.” She looked at him steadily. “I want you to teach me.”

There it was.

He looked at her for a long time. At the bow on her back and the way she sat, very straight, the way people sat who’d been the best at something for six years. At her hands in her lap. She was holding one of them in the other, tightly, so it wouldn’t move.

“Teach you what?” said Xav.

“How to fight something that always goes first.”

“You can’t,” said Xav. “Not him. Nobody can.”

“Then how to make it work,” said Kestrel. “You said that. On the floor at Elmcross. I’d make him work. I want that. I don’t want to beat him.” She stopped, and corrected herself, the way an honest person does. “I do want to beat him. I want to beat him more than I’ve wanted anything in a long time. But I know I can’t. Not this year. So I want to make him work. I want to be the second person who can.” She looked at him. “That’s all.”

Xav closed his sketchbook.

“Ask me again,” he said.

Kestrel looked at him for a long moment. He thought she might get up and go. She’d been the best in the world at something for six years. She’d walked two hundred and twelve steps, which were a lot of steps, to ask a man on a roof for something. And he’d said ask me again.

She didn’t get up.

“Tomorrow?” she said.

“Tomorrow.”

She nodded, once. Then she got up, and went to the top of the stair, and stopped there with one hand on the stone.

“You said that to him,” she said. “In January. Ask me when it’s big. I’ll be first through the door.” She didn’t look round. “It’s on his stream. Everything’s on his stream.”

“I know.”

“Was he?” said Kestrel. “First through the door. When you asked.”

Xav thought about the bank at Brackenford in February. Eight idiots coming over the bridge one at a time, and Dec standing with Moonlight Harvest on the near side. Not in the row. On the bank, watching. The first one there.

“Yes,” he said.

“Then I’ll ask you again,” said Kestrel, and went down the stairs. On the first landing he heard her stop, and come back up two steps, and say in a different voice, “Does she really expect me to sweep?”

“Yes,” said Xav.

A sigh, a long one, all the way from the landing. Then her steps went on down.

He didn’t tell Riverbank. He didn’t need to; RX told them.

“She asked,” said RX, at the long end of the second table on Saturday night. He said it as if he’d been there. He hadn’t been there. “On the roof. At dawn. She asked.”

“How do you know it was the roof?”

“Ama Corrie told me she’d sent a VANTA woman up with a broom.” RX looked enormously pleased. “And you said ask me again. You always say ask me again.”

“I said it to Dec once.”

“You said it to Dec eleven times,” said Malcolm. “I counted. In poly. About everything.”

“Did he ask again?” said John.

Xav didn’t answer.

“He always asked again,” said Azac, with his mouth full. “That’s Dec. You say ask me again and he does. Every time. Same voice. As if it’s the first time.” He swallowed. “It’s very annoying.”

“It’s very Dec,” said Val, without looking up from her book.

“Yeah,” said Ryan.

Jun Liang started singing something about a roof. Val kicked him.

Xav sat in his chair, the first of Riverbank’s, and looked down the long table at the banner room. At sixty chairs and the lamplight and Ama Corrie doing the Sunday chairs early because she’d felt like it. At the chair by the north door, by itself, facing north. At the first of the nine, Moonlight Harvest’s end, where Dec was asleep sitting up with his feet on the rung of the next chair and a cup of something on the table in front of him that Kotori had left.

His feet. Xav looked at his feet. Up on the rung, crossed at the ankle, perfectly still.

That’s new, he thought again, and didn’t know why it made him feel the way it did.

On Sunday she asked again.

Same place. Same time. The first dawn, on the ledge. He’d been there twenty minutes before she came. He hadn’t been drawing. He’d been sitting with the sketchbook shut, watching the north road go out of the round market and over the moor, waiting. He hadn’t told himself he was waiting. He was.

She came up the last flight without counting this time. She’d remembered how many.

“Teach me,” said Kestrel. She didn’t sit down. She stood at the top of the stair with her bow on her back and said it straight out, the way you’d say it at a gate. “Please. Properly. I’m asking again.”

Xav looked at her.

“No streaming,” he said.

She blinked.

“When I teach you. You don’t stream it. Not your stream, not his, not anyone’s.” He held up a finger. “You can stream everything else. Your guild. Your doors. Whatever. But the lessons are ours. If I see a clip, it’s over.”

“Why?”

“Because you’re going to lose to me a lot,” said Xav. “Hundreds of times. And you’re the best bow in the world, and a hundred thousand people watch you. And I’m not going to make you do that in front of them.” He held up a second finger. “First dawn, every day, for two weeks. The ring in the market. It’s empty at first dawn. Nobody’s up but Ama Corrie.”

“And?”

“And you sweep it after,” said Xav. “Under, not round.”

Kestrel stared at him.

Then something happened to her face that he hadn’t seen it do before. Not on her stream, not on the oak at Elmcross, not at the crossing in the rain in February. She laughed. A real one, surprised out of her, with her head back. Like someone who’d been holding something heavy for a long time and had just been told she could put it down for a bit.

“Yes,” she said. “Yes. All right. Yes.”

“Tomorrow,” said Xav. “First dawn.”

“Tomorrow.” She turned to go. Then she turned back. “Sketch.”

“Mm.”

“Why yes?”

He thought about it. He thought about saying because you asked properly, three times. That was true. He thought about saying because Dec would want me to, which was also true, and which he wasn’t going to say to anyone, ever. And then he thought about seven pages of feet on a table in the Hart. Ink and pencil and dots. And a back foot that came round a breath later than it used to. And the way his own chest had felt on Monday night, looking at it, that he didn’t have a word for yet.

“Because I want to see what you see,” said Xav. “When you’re in it.”

Kestrel looked at him for a long moment. She didn’t understand, he thought. That was all right. He didn’t either, quite.

“Okay,” she said, and went down.

He sat on the ledge for a long time after she’d gone. The sun came up properly over the east road. Higu came up the last flight at the second dawn with her lamp and two cups, and found him there, and sat down beside him, and handed him one without a word.

“You’ve got a face,” said Higu, eventually.

“Everyone keeps saying that.”

“It’s not your letter face,” said Higu. She drank her tea. “It’s a different one. I don’t think I’ve seen it before.”

Xav opened his sketchbook. He turned to the fourth page, the Crimson Ledger one. Two drawings side by side in the margin, the new step and the old one.

He looked at them for a long time.

“Neither have I,” he said.
