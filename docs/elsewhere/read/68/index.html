Chapter 68

The Banner

2,429 words · 11 min read

It was Bee’s idea. Bee would have said it wasn’t an idea, just a thing that needed doing that nobody had noticed yet.

“It’ll need a banner,” she said on Monday night, on the dyers’ bridge, without looking up from her book.

“What will?”

“Kei’s thing. The writ says a guild has a banner.” Bee turned a page. “Somebody has to make it. Nobody’s thought of it. They’re all thinking about levels and roads and money.” She sniffed. “I’m not thinking about levels. I’m a person with a pencil.”

“It hasn’t got a name,” said Hyvred.

“It doesn’t need a name to have a picture,” said Bee. “Names are words. I don’t do words.” She looked up for the first time. “Ask Kei for the map.”

Kei sent it at two in the morning his time, which meant he’d got up to do it. A photograph of a sheet of paper on a desk in Australia, taken from above under a lamp, a little blurred at one corner where his hand had shaken.

Hyvred opened it in the air above the bridge, in the game, where it was as big as she liked, and she and Bee looked at it together.

Dots. Lines between them. Short thick ones and long thin ones. Solid ones and dotted ones. Little clusters, scattered all over the paper, joined to each other by threads, and the threads joined to other threads. Some of the dots had tiny names beside them in Kei’s careful, upright hand. Maigo. Salty. Wendy. Two dots on the left, joined by a short strong line: Hyv. Bee. A long dotted line across most of the page from a mountain to a canal, with a little cross on it where an inn was. A cluster of eight round a riverbank. Near the bottom right, in a fen, a dot with a very thick line to another dot. Suzu. Ice.

And almost every line went, by one route or another, through the same few dots.

“He drew himself small,” said Bee.

Hyvred looked. She was right. Kei’s own dot, somewhere near the middle, was the smallest on the page. You could hardly see it under all the lines that ran through it.

“That’s Kei,” said Hyvred.

“That’s wrong,” said Bee, and closed the photograph, and went to find Wynn.

Wynn dyed the cloth.

Bee asked him on Tuesday afternoon, standing in the doorway of the dye-works with her book under her arm. He looked at her for a long time with his blue hands folded. He’d been dyeing cloth on that river for forty years, he said, and nobody had ever asked him for a banner. Then he asked what colour.

“Night,” said Bee.

So he dyed it night. A long piece of heavy cloth, two arms wide and longer than Hyvred was tall. He dipped it in the woad vat eleven times, Bee said afterwards, and hung it to dry between dips. Each time it came out a little darker, until on the eleventh it was the blue of the sky over the fen on the longest night, after the dawn didn’t come, before the stars. Wynn held it up against the evening and looked at it and said, “There.”

And then Bee painted it.

She did it on the dyers’ bridge, on Tuesday night and Wednesday night, with the cloth spread along the cobbles from one lantern to the next and stones on the corners. She had two little pots. One was gold, from Ambry the sign-painter back in Heatherbrook, who’d sent it by a carrier when Bee asked. The other was a silver-white that Wynn made from something he wouldn’t name. She painted on her knees with a brush the size of an eyelash, with her antennae forward and her wings perfectly still. She didn’t talk. She didn’t stream. She didn’t let anyone look until she was done.

Hyvred sat on the mill roof and didn’t look. It was the hardest thing she’d done in the game. She could see everything from up there, the whole town edge to edge. And the one thing in it she wasn’t allowed to see was on the bridge directly underneath her, being painted by her best friend.

So she looked at everything else, and that was when she noticed the boy again.

He’d been at the south end of the bridge for four days.

She’d seen him on Saturday, on the parapet, eating something. And on Sunday, when Kestrel came, sitting in the same place, not watching, very deliberately not watching. And on Monday, and Tuesday. A young man, younger than her, with very short hair and a crossbow across his knees. A heavy one, with a winding crank, the kind you had to brace against the ground to load. He’d sit there for an hour or two at dusk, and then he’d get up and go back into town, and the next day he’d be there again.

She’d half recognised him on Saturday. On Wednesday, with Bee painting underneath her and nothing else to look at, she finally placed him.

The longest night. The dyers’ bridge. Twelve strangers had come running toward their three lanterns out of the dark, because three lanterns were better than none. A boy from Lagos with a crossbow had taken the south end of the bridge. She’d called his targets for him from the roof, all night, one after another. Left, forty paces, low. Right, under the arch. Behind you, in the dye-water. He’d never once asked how she knew. He’d just turned and shot where she said, and the crank went round, and he shot again. At dawn, when the dark finally lifted, he’d stood up and waved at her on the roof. And then he’d gone, like the others, back into the town.

She slid down off the roof and walked along the bank to the south end of the bridge.

He saw her coming and stood up very quickly, so quickly he nearly dropped the crossbow.

“Good evening,” he said. Formally, the way you’d greet somebody’s mother. “Sorry. Good evening. You’re—“

“Hyvred.”

“I know. I know who you are.” He looked embarrassed. “I’ve been watching your stream. Since the long night. Since you called my shots. Left, forty paces, low.” He said it in her voice, nearly. “I heard it in my sleep for a week.”

“You’ve been sitting here for four days.”

“Yes.” He looked at the crossbow. “I was trying to work out how to ask.”

His name was Tobi. He was nineteen. He lived in Lagos, with his mother and two sisters and a grandmother who thought the egg was a freezer. He’d saved for it for a year, from a job he didn’t describe. He’d come into the game on day nine, in a fishing town on the far side of the river country, and walked to Brackenford alone. He’d been alone the whole time, he said, until the longest night, when he’d run toward three lanterns on a bridge because they were the only lights he could see.

“Everybody else was in the big places,” he said. “The island. The big bridges with the red ones. They didn’t want me. The red ones said do you have level eighteen, and I said no, and they said go away then.” He shrugged. “So I came to your bridge. And you called my shots all night, from a roof, and you never once asked what level I was.”

Hyvred didn’t say anything.

“On Sunday you said on your stream that you and Bee were in something,” said Tobi. “That it hadn’t got a name. That it was your friends.” He looked at the crossbow again. “And I thought, I want to be in a thing with somebody who calls my shots and doesn’t ask what level I am.” He stopped. “That’s all. Sorry. That’s what I was trying to work out how to say.”

Hyvred looked at him for a long time.

She thought about Kei, on a call at two in the morning, saying every time. She thought about a boy with the bird cut off his collar, in Sevenlocks, whom Kei had told her about on Monday. He’d walked a long way to stand somewhere, and they’d told him not tonight. She thought about Kestrel on the parapet, saying the seat stays.

“It’s not mine to say yes,” she said.

Tobi’s face fell, just a little.

“It’s not anybody’s,” said Hyvred. “That’s the rule. There’s a gate. In a round hall, where seven roads meet. And when you get there, the people who’d have to stand next to you decide. All of them. Every time.” She paused. “It might be no.”

“But I could walk there.”

“You could walk there. Same as anybody.”

Tobi thought about that. Then his whole face changed, slowly. Hyvred had seen it change exactly like that once before, on the longest night, when the dawn came up and he waved at her on the roof.

“Then I’ll walk to the gate,” he said.

Bee finished at eleven o’clock on Wednesday night.

She stood up off her knees on the cobbles of the dyers’ bridge, and put down her eyelash brush, and wiped her hands on her apron. Then she looked up at the mill roof, where Hyvred had gone back to sit, and said one word.

“Look.”

Hyvred looked.

She saw it all at once. That was the thing. She’d never be able to explain it to anyone who’d only ever seen the world whole. She didn’t look at it a piece at a time. She didn’t slide a circle across it. It was just there. All of it, the whole long banner on the cobbles from one lantern to the next, edge to edge, in one look.

Night-blue. Wynn’s eleven dips of woad, the dark after the dawn didn’t come.

And on it, dots. Gold ones. Dozens of them, scattered right across the cloth the way Kei had scattered them across his paper. Some in little clusters, some on their own. Between them, silver lines, very fine, painted with the eyelash brush, joining one dot to another. Short thick ones and long thin ones. And some of the lines weren’t solid. Some were dotted, a silver line that broke and broke and broke.

She found herself. Two gold dots on the left, close together, joined by a short strong line. Hyv. Bee.

She found the six, in a cluster. The four in the corner. Tiger and Drew with their long line between them, and a tiny gold cross on it where the inn was. Higu and Gigi on a lake and in a hedge, a long way apart, with a solid line between them anyway.

And in the fen, at the bottom right, six dots close together with thick lines between them. From that cluster ran all the lines Kei had drawn, out across the cloth to Maigo and Salty and Kei and Hako and Wendy and Ina. Every one of them was there. Every one of them was dotted. Breaking and breaking and breaking. Not yet.

The cluster of eight on a riverbank had its long thin line too, back toward the fen. Dotted. Ask me when it’s big.

And at the very edge of the cloth, right on the hem, a single small gold dot on its own, with a dotted line going in from it toward the middle. Bee must have painted it on Monday, after Kei had told them about Sevenlocks. Not tonight.

And then, beside it, still wet, painted in the last five minutes, another one. A small gold dot at the edge, near the south end of where a bridge might be. A dotted line going in.

“Tobi,” said Bee, from the cobbles. “He told me while you were on the roof. He’s walking. So he gets a dot.”

Hyvred looked at the middle of the banner.

Kei’s dot wasn’t small. On the paper in Australia he’d drawn himself so small you could hardly see him under all the lines. On Wynn’s night-blue cloth, Bee had painted him the same size as everybody else. A gold dot, like all the others, with the lines running through it.

“You drew him the right size,” said Hyvred.

“I drew him the right size,” said Bee, and sat down on the parapet, suddenly very tired, and looked at her own work in the lantern-light. Her wings buzzed once, for no reason, because they were for show.

Later, after midnight, Hyvred came out of the egg in her room in Singapore and lay there for a while in the dark.

Then she got her phone and opened the photograph Bee had sent her of the finished banner, and held it up close to her face.

She couldn’t see it.

Not all of it. Not on Earth. She saw one gold dot, in her small clear circle, and the silver line coming off it. She moved the circle along the line, slowly, the way she moved it along a platform. Another dot. Another line. A cluster. A long dotted thread, breaking and breaking. She went across the whole banner like that, a piece at a time, for ten minutes. She found every one of them, one by one, in the dark, with the phone an inch from her nose.

It didn’t matter. She knew what it looked like. She’d seen it all at once. She’d always know what it looked like now.

She sent it to Kei.

He didn’t answer for a long time. Long enough that she thought he’d gone to sleep, at three in the morning in Australia. Then:

Oh.

And then, a long time after that:

It looks like

He didn’t finish it. She waited. The three little dots came and went, came and went, and then stopped.

Sixteen, he wrote instead. And two walking.
