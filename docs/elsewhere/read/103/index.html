Chapter 103

Not Talking About It

1,639 words · 7 min read

Suzu came back from Singapore on Monday night and stood in the kitchen doorway with his bag still on his shoulder.

“Eligible,” he said. “Mum. Maybe next week.”

Ice put the kettle on. He got out the two mugs that didn’t match. He didn’t say anything, because there wasn’t anything that needed saying that Suzu’s face wasn’t already saying. He made two kopi-o and gave Suzu the one with the chip, which was Suzu’s now.

Suzu took it. He stood there holding it.

“Hyvred was there,” he said.

Ice looked at him.

That was all Suzu said. He didn’t say on her own, though she must have been. He didn’t say she’s paying for it herself, or she’s short, or you should. Suzu never told you what to do. He told you one true thing, and then he went and sat down. He did that now. He drank his kopi at the table and looked at nothing, and after a while he went to bed.

Ice stood in the kitchen for a long time.

Then he got his phone out and opened a stream he hadn’t watched in a month.

It took him two days. That was a long time, for him.

He knew why it took two days. Because he knew her a bit. Not well. A joke about her eyes once, and an apology that went on for ten minutes, and a text on her first night in the game. no flashlight now. He knew she didn’t want help. He knew she’d said so on stream, laughing, about the bar in the corner. Subs and arrows only. Don’t. He knew that if he asked, she’d say no, and mean it. Then she’d keep selling forty arrows at a time for thirty-nine dollars fifty, a piece at a time, for months, while her name sat on a list.

So he didn’t ask.

On Wednesday night, after his stream, at the kitchen table, he opened her stream again and looked at the bar. US$6,179 / 10,000. He did the sum. He typed it in, to the dollar. He typed a message under it and deleted it. He typed another one and deleted that. Then he ticked the little box that said anonymous and sent it with nothing at all.

Gabe came in for a glass of water at two in the morning and found him sitting there.

“Car conversation?” said Gabe.

“No.”

“Okay,” said Gabe, and drank his water, and went back to bed. Gabe was the best person Ice knew at not asking.

It came in on Thursday evening, at the top of the heron road, at the Heron’s Reach.

MH was on the lip of the gorge, the five of them, the way they’d been every evening since Gabe went south. Above the broken bridge, killing what came up out of it, a level at a time. Ice was twenty-nine. Leveti was thirty. The others were close. On the far side of the gorge, past the twenty-sixth heron, something waited that none of them had seen yet. They could all feel it, the way you could feel a door in a dark room.

The message came up in the corner of his eye. He read it, and didn’t answer, and went back to holding the gorge.

Deston read that one over his shoulder, because Deston had stopped pretending he wasn’t reading them on Thursday. He laughed so hard he had to sit down on the lip of the gorge.

“She’s going to kill you,” said Deston.

“She can’t,” said Ice. “That’s the point.”

On Saturday morning, early, before anyone in MH was in, she found another way.

He didn’t say anything on stream. He was walking along the lip of the gorge in the early light, alone for once. He read her line at the edge of his eye, and smiled, and kept walking. Twenty thousand people asked him what he’d done, and he didn’t tell them. He’d never told them anything he didn’t want to, and they’d stopped expecting him to around December.

Then he answered her. Not in chat. In the messages, where it was theirs.

Monday they turned south.

Gabe’s lot were ten. Deston had come out of a call with Gabe on Sunday night with a face Ice knew from thirteen years of choir and friendship and said they’ll be in Brackenford on Thursday. Ice had said then so will we. So on Monday, at the first dawn, MH left the gorge and walked south down the heron road. Back toward Fernwick, and Brackenford, and a clerk with a pen behind each ear, and three people none of them had seen in the game yet.

Ice looked back once, from the twentieth heron, at the gorge. Then he didn’t look back again.

She messaged on the road. Mid-morning, in the middle of the old fen, with Kotori humming and Suzu walking beside him with his scythe and his pack glowing faintly on one side.

He walked for a long time. Through the old fen, past the herons, one every mile, all facing north behind him now. Kotori stopped humming at some point and he didn’t notice when. Suzu walked beside him and didn’t say anything, and Ice was grateful for it, the way he was always grateful for it.

At noon, by the eleventh heron, the corner of his eye lit up.

He put it away.

Beside him, Suzu said, “She’ll hit you.”

“I know.”

“Hard.”

“…I know.”

“Good,” said Suzu.

Deston, behind them, made a noise like a man trying very hard not to laugh in front of a stone bird. Leveti, at the back, said, “You deserve it, Declan,” in his calm flat voice, which was the most Leveti had said all day. And Kotori, at the very front, turned round walking backwards. She didn’t say sorry for anything; she hadn’t in months. She just said, “I’m glad,” and turned back round.

Ice walked south down the heron road with his friends. Somewhere at the top of a dome in a round town, a girl who could see seven roads at once was writing his name at the top of a list. He thought she’d probably underline it.

He hoped she would.
