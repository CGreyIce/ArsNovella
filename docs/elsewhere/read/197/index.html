Live from Elsewhere · Volume 8: Somewhere Else

Chapter 197

A Roof of Lanterns

3,637 words · 16 min read

Alice said six hundred lanterns, and Jared said y’all, and that was how it started.

“Six hundred,” said Alice. “Red ones. Over the whole market. From the Hall to the fountain to the Hart. A roof. A roof of lanterns.” She was standing on the south steps on the Saturday before Chinese New Year with a list in her hand and her face doing the thing it did when she’d decided. “It’s Chinese New Year on Wednesday. It’s the first one we’ve ever had in here. All of us. Together.” She looked at the round market, empty and grey in the morning. “I want a roof.”

“Six hundred,” said Jared.

“Six hundred.”

“Lanterns.”

“Red ones.”

“Over the whole market.”

“A roof,” said Alice.

Jared looked at the round market. At the fountain in the middle, and the ring of nine dark stones, and the Hall of Roads with its dome. At the Hart on the far side, and the chestnut stall, and the seven roads going out. It was a big market. He’d never thought about how big it was until somebody asked him to put a roof on it.

“Y’all,” he said.

“That’s not a no,” said Alice.

“It’s not a no,” said Jared. “It’s a y’all.”

She hugged him. She did that. She’d done it for years, on calls, across eleven thousand miles, with the camera going sideways. And now in here, in person, on the south steps, with her list crumpled between them. He’d known her a long time. He’d called her Alice for all of it. He’d held a lot of roofs up for her.

He’d never held one up made of lanterns.

He did it the way he did roofs. He went and looked at it first.

He walked round the whole round market on Saturday afternoon with a ball of string and a stick of chalk, the way he’d walked round the quarry at Quarrymouth in March before he put a single beam in. He measured. He marked the paving. He went up on the Hall steps and looked down, and up on the Hart’s roof and looked across. He went up the two hundred and twelve to the eye, with Higu laughing at him, and looked straight down at the whole round town from above. The round market like the hub of a wheel. The seven roads going out like spokes.

“You can’t hang six hundred lanterns from nothing,” he said, at the long table that night. “They’ve got to hang from something. And the something’s got to hold.”

“So what’s the something?” said Alice.

“Seven poles,” said Jared. He’d drawn it on the back of her list. “One at the end of each road, where it comes into the market. Tall ones. Taller than the Hart. And one in the middle.” He tapped the fountain. “There. Behind the fountain. The tallest one. Then rope. From the middle pole out to each of the seven, like the spokes. And then rope round, between them, in rings.” He drew rings. “Three rings. And the lanterns hang off the rope.”

Kei leaned over and looked at the drawing for a long time.

“That’s the ceiling,” said Kei.

Jared looked at him.

“The Hall’s ceiling. Under the dome.” Kei pointed up, at the banner room’s roof, toward where the dome was. “When the moon comes through the eye and the lines come out. The round town in the middle, and the seven roads going out, and the rings. You’ve drawn the ceiling.”

Jared looked at his drawing. At the hub and the spokes and the rings.

“Huh,” he said. “I guess I have.”

“Can you hold it up?” said Alice.

Jared thought about it. Seven poles taller than the Hart. One taller than all of them. Rope. Six hundred lanterns. And a market full of people underneath, on Chinese New Year, looking up.

“Nothing comes down that I’m holding,” he said.

The poles came from Azac.

That was the surprising part, to everybody except Jared. Azac was Woodcutting and Carpentry too, and nobody ever remembered, because he carried the biggest shield in the guild and stood at the front of things and said I’m a wall. But he’d been cutting trees in the deep wood east of the round town since March. On his days off, he said. For fun. He had a pile of seasoned timber behind the Hart that nobody had ever asked about.

“Eight,” said Azac, on Sunday, standing beside the pile with his mouth full of bread. “Seven tall. One taller. I’ve been saving them.”

“For what?”

“I didn’t know,” said Azac. “A wall, maybe.” He swallowed. “Turns out it’s a roof.”

They put them up on Monday. All of them. Jared and Azac and Salty, who could lift one end of a pole on his own, which nobody else could. Ina, who said a word at the base of each one to dry the wet in the hole. And Gabe, who’d tanked the Thornway on Monday night for the first time in his life and was still so happy about it he couldn’t stand still, and kept telling the poles about SimpleMMO. Seven poles at the seven road ends, where they came into the market. And the eighth behind the fountain, the tallest. When it stood up out of its hole, people stopped in the market and looked at it.

Jared stood at the bottom of the middle pole with his beam over his shoulder and looked up.

It was very tall. Taller than the Hart, nearly as high as the first landing of the dome. It swayed a little at the top in the wind off the moor. He watched it sway. He knew exactly how much it would sway with six hundred lanterns on it, and how much was too much, and where it would want to go if it went.

It’ll hold, he thought. If somebody’s holding it.

The lanterns came from everywhere.

Alice had made a list. Of course she had. She’d gone round every shop and stall and house in the round town on Saturday asking for red lanterns, and the round town had looked at her blankly, because nobody in the round town had ever heard of Chinese New Year. So she’d changed the list. Lanterns. Any colour. And red paper.

They came in by the cartload. Hobb’s cart, with turnips under them. Tobi’s paper lanterns, from somewhere on the north-east road. Leaka, who did Tailoring, and Bee, who could make anything out of paper, sat at the long table on Sunday and Monday with a mountain of red paper. They made lantern covers, hundreds of them, red, to go over the ones that weren’t. Chernn came with a sack from Tallow Green. Mother Dipp had sent it after a letter. Two hundred candles, small ones, for inside, for the people who can’t see in the dark, and for the ones who can, the note said.

By Tuesday night they had six hundred and twelve.

Alice counted them twice. Then she sat down on the south steps with her list and cried, and Milli sat beside her and held her hand, and Jared stood behind both of them with his beam and pretended not to notice.

“It’s just a festival,” said Alice, wiping her eyes. “It’s just a festival. I’m being stupid.”

“You’re not being stupid,” said Milli.

“It’s the first one,” said Alice. “We’ve never— it’s always been on a call. Every year. Milli in the Netherlands and me in Singapore and Jared in America and Leaka in England and Curse in Australia. Five squares on a screen at different times of day.” She looked at the round market, the seven poles and the tall one behind the fountain, the rope going up. “And this year we’re all in the same town. All of us. Sixty people. In one market. And I just wanted—“

She stopped.

“A roof,” said Jared.

“A roof,” said Alice.

They hung them on Wednesday, Chinese New Year’s Day, at the first dawn.

Everybody. Every one of the sixty who was in. Gigi up a ladder on the north-east pole with Thea holding it, and Xola holding Thea. Tiger and Drew on the rope, swinging out along it like a pair of idiots, hanging lanterns at arm’s length. Riverbank on the west side in a line, passing lanterns along hand to hand. Kotori and Tif with tea. Kei with his map, telling everybody where. Ama Corrie in the doorway of the Hall with her broom, saying hm every few minutes in a way that might have been approval.

And in the doorway beside her, on her stick, Isa Corrie.

She’d come out of the chair. It was the first time she’d come out of it since the longest night. She’d got up at the first dawn and walked, very slowly, leaning on her stick, through the banner room and across the Hall. She stood in the south door beside Ama Corrie and watched them hang six hundred lanterns over the round market.

Jared saw her from the bottom of the middle pole. He didn’t say anything. He watched her watch.

At noon, they lit them.

It wasn’t dark. It was noon, the first dusk coming, the light going gold down the east road. But Alice said you lit them at noon on the first day, that was how it went, or near enough. And the light didn’t matter. They went up on the ladders and lit them one at a time, six hundred and twelve, with Chernn’s candles. Red, in the gold light. All the way round, in three rings, from the seven poles to the tall one behind the fountain.

A roof.

A roof of red lanterns over the whole round market, glowing. When the first dusk came and the sky went blue behind them, they glowed brighter. The round market under them went red and gold. The fountain red. The faces red. Six hundred little lights, swaying very slightly in the wind off the moor, all together, the way a roof doesn’t sway.

Alice stood in the middle of the round market under it and didn’t say anything at all.

The middle pole started to go at the second dusk.

Jared felt it before he saw it. He’d been standing at the bottom of it all afternoon, because somebody had to. Through the lighting, through Maigo’s reunion dinner at the long trestle tables they’d carried out of the banner room into the market. Rice and chicken and fish and long noodles for long life, and no pork in any of it, because Maigo said there’d never be pork in anything he made. Through Leaka and Curse’s lion.

The lion had been Leaka’s idea. Of course it had. She’d made it on Monday night out of red cloth and gold paper and an old basket for the head, with eyes Bee painted. It went over two people. Leaka at the front, with the head, and Curse at the back, bent double, holding on to Leaka’s belt and not able to see anything. It came out of the Hart at the first dusk with Newish beating her drum and went round the market in a dance that wasn’t really a dance. Leaka screaming CGI SHOULD BE HERE from inside the head. Curse saying, very calmly, from inside the back end, I can’t see, Leaka. Leaka. Left. Leaka, the fountain. And then they went into the fountain.

The whole round market laughed so hard that the lanterns shook.

That was when Jared felt the pole.

He felt it through his hands, where he’d had them on the wood all afternoon. A shiver. The wind had changed. It was coming harder off the moor now, from the north, cold. The middle pole had six hundred lanterns hanging off it by seven ropes, and the wind was pushing all six hundred, all at once, south. And the pole was leaning.

He looked up. He saw the top of it move. Not much. A hand’s width. Then back. Then a hand’s width and a bit.

Then two.

“Azac,” said Jared. Not loudly.

Azac was at the trestle table with a plate of noodles. He looked up at Jared’s voice and put the noodles down. He came across the market without asking anything, because Azac was a wall and walls knew when something was about to come down.

“It’s going,” said Jared.

Azac looked up.

“Which way?”

“South.” Jared pointed with his chin. “Onto the Hart. Or the tables.”

The tables. The long trestle tables in the market, with sixty people round them and the old woman from the north in a chair at the end. And the fountain, and the lion, still in it, with Leaka laughing too hard to get out.

Azac looked at Jared.

“Can you hold it?”

“I can hold it,” said Jared. “If somebody’s holding me.”

He put his beam against the pole.

His beam. The one from Quarrymouth, the one he’d brought all the way from the sea quarry, the one he’d stood under the roof of a tunnel with while Alice uncovered three hundred and forty-two little stone men holding lights. He’d carried it on every road since. He’d held the well lid down with it on the longest night. He wedged one end of it against the middle pole, high, as high as he could reach. And the other end into the paving at his feet, in a crack between two flagstones. And he put his shoulder under it, and his whole weight, and held.

The pole stopped leaning.

He felt it stop. All six hundred lanterns, the wind pushing south, and the pole trying to go with it. And the beam, and him under the beam, not letting it.

Azac put his back against Jared’s back.

He didn’t say anything. He just stood there with his enormous shield on his arm and his back flat against Jared’s, so that when the wind pushed the pole, and the pole pushed the beam, and the beam pushed Jared, there was a wall behind him.

“Nothing comes down that I’m holding,” said Jared, through his teeth.

“I know,” said Azac. “I’m holding you.”

The wind came harder.

Salty came. He didn’t ask either. He came across from the trestle tables and put his shoulder under the beam beside Jared’s, and pushed. Then Gabe, still talking about the Thornway, still crying a bit about it. Then Drew, who’d been watching their feet from across the market and had seen exactly what was happening before anyone else. Then Milli, who wasn’t strong, but put his hands on the beam beside Jared’s anyway, calm, and held. Then Hako. Then Rafi with his big sword strapped to his back, grinning. Then Tiger, who’d fallen off a rope twice that morning and wasn’t going to miss this.

And then, before Jared could say anything, Alice.

She came across the market from the middle, from under the roof she’d wanted. She put her hands on the beam between Jared’s and Milli’s. She didn’t say anything either. She just held it.

Nine people under one beam, under a pole, under six hundred lanterns, in the wind.

The pole didn’t move.

It was Isa who saw it first.

She was in her chair at the end of the long trestle table, where somebody had carried it out for her so she could watch the lanterns. She’d been watching them all afternoon. Not the people. The lanterns. Six hundred red lights in three rings over the round market, swaying in the wind.

She got up.

Nobody noticed, at first. Everybody was looking at the pole. She came across the market on her stick, slowly, the way she walked. Past the fountain, where Leaka and Curse had climbed out at last and were standing dripping in their wet lion. To the bottom of the middle pole, where nine people were holding up a beam.

She stood there and looked up at it. At the pole. At the seven ropes going out from the top of it to the seven poles at the road ends. At the three rings of rope round, between them. At the lanterns.

“Oh,” said Isa Corrie.

Jared looked at her, sideways, from under the beam.

“You’ve made the ceiling,” she said. She was looking up. “The Hall’s. The round town in the middle and the roads going out and the rings. Only in lanterns.” She was quiet for a moment. “We had this. When I was a girl. In the north. In the kept place. On the turn of the year, we’d hang every lamp we had on poles round the hill, in rings, like this. Like the ceiling. So that anyone out in the dark, anywhere, could look in and see where the middle was.”

She looked at the beam. At the nine people under it.

“It always fell down,” said Isa. “Every year. The wind. We never had enough people to hold it.” She smiled her small walnut smile. “My mother said that was the point. That you put it up anyway, every year, knowing it would fall down. And then one year there’d be enough people to hold it.”

Nobody under the beam said anything.

Isa reached up. She was very small and very old and she could only just reach. She put her root hand flat on the beam, beside Alice’s, the way you’d put your hand on the arm of a chair.

“There,” she said. “Ten.”

And the wind dropped.

Jared would never know if it was the wind, or the game, or something else. He was a practical man and he didn’t think about things like that much. But the wind off the moor dropped, all at once, the way it does sometimes at dusk. The pole stopped pushing. Above them, six hundred lanterns stopped swaying and hung still, in three rings, red and gold over the round market. The whole roof of them, steady.

He felt the weight go off the beam.

He didn’t let go. Not yet. He stood there with his shoulder under it, and Azac’s back against his, and eight other people’s hands, and Isa’s. He looked up at the roof he’d held.

They stood under it until the third dawn. Everybody did. The sixty and Chernn and Isa, and Ama Corrie in the south door with her broom. They ate the rest of Maigo’s noodles, standing up, under the lanterns. Leaka and Curse dried out by the trestle tables in their lion, which had shrunk.

At midnight a letter came for Alice, from the road. She read it out, under the lanterns, with Milli reading over her shoulder. Ice had been watching Kei’s stream in the corner of his window while he walked.

[ Letter · from Ice
it’s very big
tell jared it’s a good roof ]

Alice laughed. She’d been crying on and off since noon. She was crying again now.

Jared stood under the middle pole with his beam still against it, though he didn’t need it now. He didn’t say anything. He was a calm man; he didn’t say much when things were good. He just stood there under six hundred lanterns with his hand on the wood. He looked across the market at Hyvred, on the south steps, by herself, with a letter open in her window. She was reading it in the red light with her tail very still and her face doing something he’d only ever seen on Alice’s.

He looked at the lanterns again.

A good roof, he thought. Yeah.

He kept his hand on it until the dawn.
