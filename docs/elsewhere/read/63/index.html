Chapter 63

The Top Lock

2,418 words · 11 min read

Drew came over the last hill at a quarter to eleven on Wednesday night, his time, and there it was.

He’d seen it on Tiger’s stream for six weeks. He’d seen it at noon and at midnight and on the longest night of the year, every lamp lit and the river running. He’d seen it from the roofs and from the bottom of the canal, more than once, through Tiger’s eyes, upside down and full of water. He knew every gate.

It was still different, standing above it.

The hill fell away below him in one long slope to the river, and down the slope, one under the other like a staircase, went the locks. Seven of them. Seven pairs of great black timber gates and seven long stone chambers full of still water, each one a little lower than the last, all the way down to the town. The town was yellow stone, gone gold in the last of the light. There were boats moored along the bottom basin, and lamps coming on in the windows one at a time, and on every gate, on both sides, a lamp on a hook.

“I’m going,” said Drew. He didn’t move.

Forty days.

Kei’s sums had said twenty-four. Kei’s sums had been right about the road. They hadn’t known about the inn.

He’d reached the Waymeet on the ninth day, the night before the longest night, and he’d stood in its gate all night with his knuckles up. When the real dawn came, a day and a half late, it found him sitting on the low wall beside Nell with his back against the stone. The dog Harrow was asleep across his boots. He was too tired to stand, and so happy he couldn’t stop smiling. He’d meant to set off again that afternoon.

He’d stayed two weeks.

He hadn’t decided to. It had just kept being the right thing, one day at a time. On the first morning, the three travellers from the taken town had wanted to go home, or what was left of it. So Drew and Tobias and Paz, the Argentinian with the spear, had walked them back down the west road to see. It was a little beginner town in a hollow, with a square and a well. Every lamp in it was out, and nothing moved, and the well in the middle of the square was black to the brim. They’d relit it. Lamp by lamp, over three nights, standing in the square with their backs to each other while the three from the town went from door to door with a taper. On the third night, when the last lamp was lit, the window had changed.

One of the three had cried. Drew had looked at the window for a long time.

Then they’d walked back to the Waymeet, and the wall needed mending. Then the gate needed new hinges, and Tobias had a forge out the back, and Drew had Blacksmithing. And Nell had said you’re no use to anybody with your hands like that about his knuckles, and fed him, and he’d stayed. Every night on the call, Tiger had said when are you coming. Every night Drew had said soon. And Tiger had said okay, every night, the way he always said okay to Drew. As if soon were a real day and he could wait for it.

On the fourteenth day after the dawn, Nell had stood in the gate with her arms folded and said, “Go on, then.”

“The hinges—“

“The hinges are fine. The wall’s fine. The town’s kept.” She’d looked at him. “You’ve got somewhere to be. You’ve had somewhere to be the whole time. I’ve watched you look down that road every morning.”

So he’d gone. Fifteen more days on the road, south and east, through a forest and across a ferry and over a lot of hills. And two nights ago, Kei on the call, with thirteen people on it, telling them about a piece of paper on a wall.

And now the top lock.

He thought about Kite, the way he did sometimes. The boy with the bird on his collar had taken the left side of the wall all night on the longest night, a gap you couldn’t see from the gate. He’d held it without a word. When the dawn came he’d stood up and slung his empty quiver and walked out of the gate and away down the north road. He hadn’t looked back once. No thanks. Drew had told him not to, and he hadn’t.

Drew didn’t know why he thought about him. He just did.

He went down.

The towpath ran beside the locks, cobbled and worn, going down in steps beside each chamber. He took it slowly. He had nowhere else to be now. He’d been walking for forty days to get to this hill, and he found he wanted to take the last of it slowly.

Top Lock. The great black gates, taller than a house, with the water standing still and dark in the chamber behind them, and the lamps lit on both sides. He’d watched Tiger jump the gap in these gates while they were opening, on the twenty-fifth day, with a letter in his hand. Twelve feet of air over a drop into churning water, and the whole town on the roofs. He looked at the gap now, closed, the two great beams meeting in the middle, and he thought, you idiot, very fondly.

The sixth lock. The fifth.

And at the fifth lock, he saw him.

Far down the hill, at the bottom, where the basin was and the town began. A small figure on the towpath, running.

Not running. Skimming. That was the only word for it, the game’s word, and it was the right one. It wasn’t the way anybody else ran. Feet barely touching the cobbles, the way you’d kiss something, not marry it. A short sword on each hip, and brown ears flat back on his head with the speed. Up past the first lock, the second, coming up the staircase of the hill toward him three steps at a time, not stopping, not landing, just leaving every place he touched.

Drew stopped on the towpath beside the fifth lock and waited.

He couldn’t have done anything else. His legs had decided. He stood there with his iron knuckles in his pocket and the 88EXP plate on his belt, the plate he’d brought out of the mirror on the first day. Behind him was the long dark road he’d walked for forty days, and in front of him the friend he’d walked it to. And he waited, the way he always waited, the way he’d waited for nine years.

Tiger came up past the third lock. Past the fourth.

Twenty feet away, he looked up and saw Drew standing there.

And his face did the thing it did on stream when something was very, very good. The grin, the whole toothy grin, so wide it went right up into his eyes. The XX mark under the left one crinkled.

“DREW!”

He came off the towpath.

He shouldn’t have. There was no reason to. The towpath went straight up beside the lock to where Drew was standing. But Tiger had been running across the tops of the lock gates for six weeks. He’d been taught by a sixteen-year-old runner to go where the water wasn’t. Seeing Drew, after forty days, he did what he always did when he was happy, which was take the fast way. He jumped for the top beam of the fourth gate to cross over to Drew’s side.

He missed.

He didn’t miss by much. His front foot landed on the beam and his back foot didn’t. For one long second he was in the air above the fourth chamber, arms out, ears straight up, eyes very wide. He looked at Drew. Drew looked at him.

Then he went into the canal with a splash you could have heard at the Waymeet.

Drew walked to the edge of the lock and looked down.

Twelve feet below, in the dark still water of the chamber, Tiger came up. He came up the way he always came up, spluttering and coughing, hair plastered flat over his ears. He was already laughing. He trod water, looking up at Drew standing on the edge of the lock against the evening sky, and he laughed so hard he went under again. He came up again, still laughing.

“That’s two,” he shouted up. “Drew! That’s two!”

“That’s two,” said Drew.

There was an iron ladder in the side of the chamber. There always was. Drew had seen Tiger climb it on stream more times than he could count. He went down three rungs of it and held out his hand.

Tiger swam over and grabbed it. Drew pulled. For a second they were both on the ladder, Drew above and Tiger below, both holding on with one hand. Drew was dry and Tiger was dripping, nose to nose, and neither of them said anything at all.

Then Tiger hugged him. On a ladder. Soaking wet, one arm round his neck and the other on the ladder, so that the canal went all down Drew’s front and into his boots.

“You took forty days,” said Tiger into his shoulder.

“I know.”

“Kei said twenty-four.”

“There was an inn.”

“I know there was an inn. I was on the call.” Tiger let go and looked at him, wet ears and all, grinning. “I’ve been standing at the bottom lock since nine. Pell said I looked like a dog waiting for its owner. I said I’m not a dog, I’m a tiger. She said that’s worse.” He started to climb. “Gaspard’s going to be so happy. He had a bet.”

“On what?”

“On whether I’d fall in when you got here.” Tiger hauled himself up onto the edge of the lock and sat there on the stones, streaming water, beaming. “He said yes. The lock-keeper said no. Gaspard’s been saving his silver for this for a week.”

Down the hill, in the doorway of a chandler’s shop by the bottom basin, a very old man was dancing.

They sat on the top beam of the fourth gate, where Tiger had meant to land. Drew sat carefully, the way you’d sit on a wall. Tiger sat with his legs dangling over the drop, as though it were a bench. The lamps were lit on both sides of the lock. The town was gold below them. Somewhere down in it, Gaspard was still laughing.

“Hi,” said Tiger.

“Hi.”

“You’re actually here.”

“I’m actually here.”

Tiger looked at him for a long moment, sideways, with his ears still dripping.

“You look like you,” he said. “On stream you look like you. But you look like you.”

“So do you,” said Drew. “But wetter.”

Tiger laughed. Then he stopped, and looked down at his hands, and Drew saw his ears go flat, the way they did when he was about to say something he meant.

“I thought about running to you,” Tiger said. “On the longest night. I said I would. If it gets bad, I’ll run. And it got bad, and I was halfway up the hill with a lamp, and I thought, I could go now. Fifteen days. I’m fast now.” He looked out at the dark. “But Pell had the Top. And there were lamps. And I couldn’t.”

“I know.”

“You stayed at your inn. I stayed at my locks.” Tiger kicked his heels against the gate. “We both stayed.”

“Yeah.”

“That’s okay, isn’t it?”

“That’s the whole thing,” said Drew. “That’s what it’s for.”

They sat there for a while without saying anything. The lamps burned. Down at the bottom basin, a boat came in very slowly, and the lock-keeper went out with his long pole to meet it.

Drew saw it come in at the edge of his vision, in the ghost panel. He laughed out loud, on the gate, so suddenly that Tiger nearly fell in again.

“What?”

“Ice,” said Drew. “Ice just raided me.”

“How many?”

Drew looked at the number. Thirty-one thousand people had just arrived in his chat from a swamp on the other side of the world, all at once, to watch two people sit on a lock gate.

He’d raided Ice once. At three in the morning his time, on the night of the world first, with four hundred and twelve people, into a chat of nine thousand, because a man in a swamp had walked down some green steps and Drew had thought he should have more people for this. Yo ice. Brought some people. Huh. Cold.

“Enough,” said Drew.

“Say something,” said Tiger. “Say something to them. They’re all here.”

Drew thought about it. He looked down the hill at the town, gold in the lamps. At Gaspard in his doorway and the lock-keeper with his pole, and at the boy beside him on the gate, soaking wet and grinning, whom he’d walked forty days to sit next to.

“Hi,” he said, to thirty-five thousand people. “This is Tiger. He fell in.”
