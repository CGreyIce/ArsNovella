Live from Elsewhere · Volume 12: Even

Chapter 293

On Time for Something Slow

3,419 words · 15 min read

Lucan fell over for the four hundred and thirteenth time on the first Wednesday in June, and Ves wrote it down.

“Four hundred and thirteen,” said Ves.

She was sitting on the parapet of the bridge over the third stream with her knees up and a slate on them. Tall and pale, in Sable Court’s black with silver at the cuff, very patient. She’d been sitting on that parapet every first dawn since March, counting. She could do the slow one now. Badly, she said, but she could do it. Lucan still couldn’t.

He lay on his back on the planks of the bridge in the grey light and looked at the sky over Masmark.

“That was a good one,” he said.

“You fell over.”

“I fell over later,” said Lucan. “I got all the way to the end of the stroke before I fell over. That’s never happened.”

Ves looked at her slate.

“Four hundred and twelve, you fell over at the start,” she said. “Four hundred and thirteen, you fell over at the end.” She wrote something. “That’s progress.”

“That’s what I said.”

“I’m agreeing with you,” said Ves. “Get up.”

He’d been early his whole life. In every game he’d ever played. You moved, all the time, so nobody could say that’s where he is. And when they decided where you’d be, you were already somewhere else. He’d climbed the wind game’s board that way, from four hundred to four. He’d opened the first city of the fiftieth level in this game that way, before anybody else in the world. Early for everything. You’re slow, he said to everybody, because it was the truest thing he knew.

Then a tank on this bridge last July had stood still.

Not the way rocks stood still. Lucan had been going round rocks his whole life. The tank had stood still the way something very heavy stands still before it moves. Planted. Sword back, low and long, held. Lucan had been in the air mid-turn into a beat that never came. And the sword had come up off the floor of the bridge, slow. So slow you could see it the whole way. So slow there was nothing you could do about seeing it. And taken him across the bridge into the parapet. BOOM.

He’s not fighting me, he’d thought, going across the planks. He’s playing me.

The tank had learned it off the big quiet one with the black sword, Salty, in a cellar under the netloft. Lucan had heard. Off somebody who taught me, the tank had said. That’s the good kind.

So Lucan had been trying to learn it for eleven months. The slow one. It is slow because it is heavy. It is heavy because it is slow. Ves had found that written on a window when she got it to seventy per cent in March. She’d read it out to him, and he’d laughed. Then he’d stopped laughing, because he understood it, and still couldn’t do it.

He couldn’t be slow. He’d never once been slow. Everything in him was built to be early.

And the slow one didn’t care if you were early.

He got up. He set his feet on the planks of the bridge. He brought the sword back, low and long, behind him, the way he’d seen the tank do it. He held it.

The stream went under the bridge. The gulls went over. The first light came up gold over the east rim of the crater and lay across Masmark’s lake, a long way down, the size of a country. He held the sword back and didn’t move. Everything in him wanted to move. To be somewhere else before anybody decided where he was.

There was nobody on the bridge to decide anything. Just Ves, on the parapet, with her slate.

He started the stroke.

Not swung. Started. That was the thing Salty had taught him, in the cellar. You started it, and it ended where it ended. You didn’t swing it. You didn’t decide where it would land. You started it and then you went with it, slowly, the whole of your weight, all the way.

He went with it.

About halfway, everything in him said you’re late. Everybody else in the world would have moved by now. They’d have gone round, gone under, got there first. You’re late. You’re LATE. Move.

He moved.

His feet went. He was halfway through the turn into the beat he’d decided was coming, and the sword was still going the slow way. The two halves of him went in different directions.

He fell over.

“Four hundred and fourteen,” said Ves.

He watched the clip that night, out there.

He didn’t watch the tank’s streams, usually. He’d watched the bridge, once, the July one, about forty times, and then made himself stop. You couldn’t learn anything off a clip that you could only learn on a bridge. But somebody in Sable Court had sent it to him, and then somebody else had, and then Ves. Watch this. Not him. The other two.

A round pale room under a round town. Seven silver lines in the floor.

Two people he knew a bit, and one he didn’t. The one with two blades was Sketch. Riverbank’s. The best Lucan had ever seen anybody chase the tank, who’d drawn with him in January. The other one he’d never once seen in a fight. Long silver hair. A scythe. He’d been at the back of the tank’s guild in Masmark last year, at the netloft. Lucan had thought he was a healer. He’d been asleep on a bench most of the time.

He watched the first bout. He watched the man with the scythe sit down. He watched him lie down, and roll, and cut Sketch at rib height from the floor. Lucan had never in his life seen anybody do that. Not where a fight expects you, somebody wrote under the clip. Least effort, wrote somebody else.

He watched the second bout twice.

The first time, he didn’t understand it. Sketch sat down too. And then he stopped being early. Lucan saw it happen. He’d watched Sketch for a year and a half on clips, chasing the tank, always a breath ahead, always the river. Lucan’s river, which the tank had borrowed off him from a terrace, and Sketch had borrowed off the tank. He knew it better than anybody alive. He could see it in Sketch’s shoulders before Sketch moved.

In the second bout, it was gone. Sketch didn’t look a breath ahead. He looked at where the scythe was. And put a blade there. Tock.

Not early. Not late.

On time.

The second time Lucan watched it, he sat very still in front of the screen, out there, for a long time afterwards.

“You can’t be early for the slow one,” said Lucan, on the bridge, at the next first dawn.

Ves looked up from her slate.

“It doesn’t care if you’re early,” said Lucan. “That’s what I said last summer. On this bridge. I need to learn how to be early for something that doesn’t care if I’m early.” He was standing in the middle of the bridge with the sword in his hand. “I had it wrong. You can’t be early for it. Nobody can. If you’re early for it you fall over. Four hundred and fourteen times.”

“So?”

“So you have to be on time,” said Lucan.

Ves put the slate down.

“I’ve never been on time for anything in my life,” said Lucan. “Not once. Not in any game. I didn’t know you could be.” He looked down at the planks. “Sketch did it. In the second bout. Against the one with the scythe. He stopped being early, and he got him.”

“Sketch learned it off a man who sat down,” said Ves.

“Yes.”

“In a fortnight.”

“He’d been getting hit in the ear for a week first,” said Lucan. “Somebody said. On the clip.”

Ves looked at him for a long time.

“Do you want me to hit you in the ear?” she said.

Lucan thought about it.

“Yes,” he said.

She hit him in the ear for a week.

Not with a sword. With the slate, which she said was heavier than it looked. She stood on the bridge in front of him and moved, any way she liked. He didn’t look where she’d be. He looked where she was. Every time he caught himself looking a breath ahead, at the place she was going to step to, she wasn’t there. Thock. The slate across his ear.

It was the most humiliating week of his life. He loved it.

People came to watch, which he hadn’t expected. Sable Court, mostly, on the north terrace, where the tank had stopped every night last July on his way back from the Undercliffs. Then other people. The fish man, from the quay below, with his nets. Two of the Ninefold, who’d come up from the south coast for July and were sitting on the terrace rail looking at him the way people look at a strange bird. A boy from the netloft door, very thin, with a big black sword on his back.

“Boss says,” shouted the boy, from the terrace, on the Thursday, “don’t look where she’s going. Look where she is.”

Lucan stopped. Ves’s slate went past his ear and didn’t hit it.

“Who’s Boss?” said Lucan.

“The tank,” shouted the boy. “He wrote. He says tell Lucan: on time.” He grinned. “He says he heard you were falling over.”

Lucan stood on the bridge for a long time.

“Tell him I fall over less,” he shouted back.

“He knows,” shouted the boy. “I already told him.”

A letter came from the west on the Friday. Not from the tank. From Drew.

Lucan read it on the bridge, sitting on the parapet beside Ves, with the slate between them. Drew wrote the way he fought: short, watching your feet, never where you’d expect.

Lucan read it twice. Then he looked at Ves, on the parapet, with her slate on her knees and her pale patient face turned toward the lake.

He’d known her six years. Two games. She’d been his second in the wind game, when he was fourth on the board. She’d come into this one a week after him and found him on the first day and said I’ll count. She’d been counting ever since. Gates, levels, falls. Four hundred and fourteen. She’d sat on this parapet every first dawn since March in the cold and never once said you can’t.

He’d never told her anything. He’d been early for everyone, and you didn’t have to tell people things if you were always already somewhere else.

“Ves,” said Lucan.

She looked up.

“Who’d stand next to me,” said Lucan. “Somebody asked. On the shingle, a year ago. And at the netloft door. And now Drew.” He looked at the letter. “I never answered.”

Ves waited. She was very good at it.

“You,” said Lucan. “You count.”

Ves looked at him for a long time. Then she looked back at the lake, a long way down, going gold.

“I know,” she said. “I’ve always known.” She picked up her slate. “But thank you for being on time with it.”

Kestrel came on the Saturday.

He saw her on the north terrace at the first dawn, alone, with her bow on her back. Small and still, in VANTA’s plain black, at the rail. Not with the Sable Court watchers. A little apart. Watching.

He’d known Kestrel for six years, the way you knew the other people near the top of things. He’d never once spoken to her for more than a minute. She’d raced him for every great door in Masmark. She’d been first in the Crown Below and third in the Bell Above, and first again, in March, in the drowned city under Saltreach. With a boy calling slack water one breath early.

She came down the terrace steps when Ves stopped hitting him. She stood at the end of the bridge.

“I wrote to him,” said Kestrel. “The tank. In April.”

“I heard.”

“I said I’d be here in July. On the terraces.” She looked at him. “Not to watch him. To watch you.”

Lucan stared at her.

“Why?” he said.

“Because I’ve seen him,” said Kestrel. “Everybody’s seen him. Three hundred thousand people see him every night.” She looked at the bridge, at the planks, at the place in the middle where he’d fallen over four hundred and fourteen times. “I’ve never once seen you learn something.”

Lucan didn’t say anything.

“Nobody has,” said Kestrel. “You were always early. You’d already learned it before anybody saw you try.” She was quiet. “You’ve been falling over on a bridge every morning for eleven months. In front of people. Letting Ves hit you in the ear with a slate.” She looked at him. “I want to see what that looks like. At the end. When it’s done.”

“It might not be done.”

“I know,” said Kestrel. “That’s why I want to watch.”

She had somebody with her. Lucan hadn’t seen her, at first. A girl, small, younger than Fenn, standing on the bottom terrace step with a short bow, looking up. Not at Lucan. At the gulls. At the sky over the lake, where they were turning.

“That’s Linnet,” said Kestrel. “She looks up.”

The girl heard her name. She looked down from the gulls at Lucan on the bridge. She looked at him the way you’d look at a strange bird.

“You fall over a lot,” said Linnet.

“Four hundred and fourteen times,” said Lucan.

“Does it hurt?”

He thought about it. He’d never once been asked. In six years, nobody had asked him if anything hurt.

“Yes,” he said.

“Why do you keep doing it?”

Lucan looked at her. Then at Kestrel, behind her, who’d become a teacher without meaning to, the way the tank said everybody did. Then at Ves, on the parapet, with her slate. And at the planks, where he’d fallen over four hundred and fourteen times trying to be slow.

“Because somebody’s coming in July,” he said. “Who taught me something on this bridge. By beating me.” He was quiet. “And I want to have learned it. When he gets here. So he can see.”

Linnet thought about that for a long time, the way the young ones did.

“That’s a good reason,” she said.

And went back to looking at the gulls.

He stood on the bridge alone that night, after everybody had gone.

Not to practise. Just to stand there. In the middle of the bridge over the third stream, at the second dusk, with the sword in his hand and the stream going under and the lake going gold a long way down.

He stood still.

Not the way he’d stood still last July, when the tank smiled and came. Then he’d stopped because he’d finally understood he’d never once been early for the tank, only answering him. Not that.

Just still. On time. For nothing.

The gulls went over. The water went under. Nothing came.

He didn’t move.

A minute. Two.

Everything in him said move, you’re late, somebody’s going to decide where you are. And he let it say it, and didn’t. Because there was nobody on the bridge to be early for, and nothing coming, and he was exactly on time for that.

At three minutes he brought the sword back, low and long. He held it. Then, without deciding where it would end, he started it.

He went with it. All the way. The slow way. The whole of his weight.

Halfway, everything in him said you’re late.

He wasn’t. He was on time. He knew he was, because he was looking at where the sword was, and nowhere else.

It came round. All the way. Across the empty air in the middle of the bridge where nobody stood, slow, so slow you could see it the whole way. And at the end, where it ended, it stopped. Because it was heavy. And he was still standing.

Lucan stood on the bridge in the last of the dusk and looked at the window for a long time.

The third line hadn’t been there when Ves got hers in March. He knew. She’d read it out to him. Two lines.

Somebody had learned something new, in a round pale room in the west, sitting on a floor. And the game had written it down.

“Four hundred and fourteen,” said Ves, from the end of the bridge.

He hadn’t known she’d stayed. She was standing at the end of the planks in the dark with her slate under her arm.

“I didn’t fall over,” said Lucan.

“I know,” said Ves. “I’m not counting that one. I’m keeping the number.” She looked at him. “Four hundred and fourteen. And then one you didn’t.”

He wrote to the tank that night. Out there, he’d have sent a message. In here he wrote it on Sable Court’s paper, folded small, and gave it to the boy at the netloft door, who said Boss’ll get it.
