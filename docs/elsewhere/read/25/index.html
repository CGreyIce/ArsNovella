“Good,” said Suzu. “I hate waiting.”

They lay down at ten to eight, side by side, in the two eggs in the back room in Melaka Raya. Ice heard Suzu’s pod hum up beside his, and his own, and on the folding table his phone buzzed twice more and went still. He didn’t look at it. He pressed his finger to the glass.

Rain. Then the ring, at dawn, warm water to the ankle, and the moonbells shut tight in the middle like a handful of white fists. And the other four, sitting up out of nothing beside him on the flat stones, one after another.

Deston, with the book already open on his knee, because he’d been singing the four verses in the shower in Singapore for half an hour and couldn’t stop.

Xav, with the scrap of paper with the plan on it, folded small, which he didn’t need and had brought anyway.

Suzu, silver hair loose, Aldo’s scythe across his knees, nine small jars on his belt, singing faintly, one note each.

Kotori, last, sitting up on the stone with her staff held in both hands and her round eyes very wide, who said, before anybody else could say anything:

“Sorry. I’m really nervous. Is that okay?”

“Yeah,” said Ice. “Me too.”

Kotori looked at him in astonishment. Then she laughed, a beat late, and the bell rang, and she seemed a little less nervous.

The door was open when they got there. It had stayed open. The arch with the herons cut wing to wing, the slab sunk down out of sight in the black water, and the old green steps going down into the mound. From somewhere far below, the singing. Verse one, from the beginning, for the three hundredth year.

“Once,” said Xav, at the top of the steps.

“Once,” said Ice.

They went down.

The first half of the nave took forty minutes, and it was the cleanest forty minutes of fighting Ice had ever done.

They’d done it once. They knew it now. Deston on the bass line from the foot of the steps, low and steady and a little flat. Every group that came at them on the downbeat came apart into one and one and one. Ice turned them one at a time, Anchoring the ones he couldn’t turn. Xav was a second behind each. Suzu’s row went round at knee height, and the lights came off the blade and clung. Kotori went from pillar to pillar along the left side of the church without getting her feet wet, and the bell rang before the cold went in.

Nobody spoke. They didn’t need to. Five people in a drowned church, moving like a hand.

Suzu caught every light. Ice watched him do it. He caught them and stoppered them and hung them on his belt with the others, until by the step up to the screen there were twenty jars on him and they were all singing. Faintly, one note each, a soft high chord that went with them down the aisle like a second, smaller choir.

At the step, the Precentor saw them, and shouted the downbeat, and brought its staff down on the stone, and the whole back half of the choir turned round.

“Now,” said Xav.

This was Xav’s part. He’d drawn it on the paper: the Precentor shouts, the choir behind turns, and everything comes up the aisles at once. Last time that had been the worst minute of the day. So we don’t let it shout twice.

Suzu turned his back on the Precentor completely. He walked back down the aisle, alone, toward the whole of the rest of the choir coming at them on the beat, and stopped in the middle of the nave with the water to his knees and the long dark scythe held low.

He held them. The whole back half of a drowned choir, with one blade at knee height, going round and round. Every chorister that came within reach of the long dark edge had to stop. Every light that came off one clung to the blade for a second, and Suzu caught them without looking, by feel, and the chord on his belt got louder.

And at the step, Ice put himself between the Precentor and everyone else, and Anchored.

The staff came down. It met the slope, and it didn’t slide, and it didn’t bounce. It stayed, pressing, freezing, the Precentor leaning its whole weight on it. The cold went into Ice’s arm from the shield, slowly, steadily, worse than anything in the nave. A third of it went back along a thread to Deston, who grunted and kept singing. The bell rang. Ice held it.

He held it for exactly as long as it took Xav to get round.

Then again. And again. Every time the staff lifted for the next downbeat, Ice stepped into it and Anchored, and it came down on the slope and stayed, and behind it, in the second of nobody looking, there was Xav.

The Precentor went at the ninth.

It went with a sound Ice wasn’t ready for. It wasn’t a roar. It was a note. A long, low, unfinished note, cut off halfway, as it broke into pale mist and the staff dropped into the water with a splash. A light came up out of it, bigger than the others, white, singing a note so deep that Ice felt it in his chest.

Suzu didn’t catch that one. He was busy. It went up through the green water toward the roof, and was gone.

And the whole choir stopped.

The silence was the strangest thing Ice had ever heard.

Three hundred years of the first verse, and now, all at once, nothing. Every chorister in the nave stood exactly where it was, in the water, between the pews, with its face lifted and its mouth open and no sound coming out. Suzu stood in the middle of them with his scythe still raised, and they didn’t come at him. They didn’t come at anything. They were waiting.

Through the arch in the stone screen, up three wide steps, on what must once have been the altar, something moved.

It had been standing there the whole time. Ice had seen the shape of it yesterday, through the arch, in the green gloom. He’d thought it was a statue. It was tall, twice as tall as the Precentor, thin and stooped, in a long dark cope that hung to the floor and spread out on the water like ink. It had no face that he could see. It had hands, long and pale. In the right one it held a baton, short and white. Bone.

It raised the baton.

Every chorister in the nave turned toward it.

“Oh,” said Kotori, from her pillar, very small.

“Deston,” said Ice, without turning round.

“I know,” said Deston, behind him, and his voice shook. “I know. I know. Verse one. Start of verse one.” He drew a breath, a big one, the kind you draw in the back row before the hard bit. “I’ve got it. Go.”

The baton came down.

Verse one.

The whole choir sang it at once. Every voice in the nave came in on the same breath, so loud after the silence that Ice felt it in his teeth, and they came. All of them, from every aisle, on every downbeat. And at the top of the altar steps the Choirmaster beat time with the bone baton. Every time it came down, the cold came with it. Not from the choristers. From the baton itself. A long slow freezing pulse that went out across the whole church through the water and into all five of them at once.

Deston sang under them. He sang their bass line, the way he had yesterday, as loudly as Ice had ever heard him sing anything. It broke the nearest ones, the way it had yesterday, into one and one and one. But there were so many, and the Choirmaster held the time over all of them with the baton, and Deston was one voice.

Ice held the step.

That was all he could do in verse one, and he did it. He stood on the top step of the three, in front of the arch, with Deston behind him and the whole choir coming. He Anchored, and turned, and Anchored, and the cold from the baton went into him at three a second and the cold from the choristers at more. The bell rang so often it was almost one long note. Xav came and went behind things, a second at a time. Suzu’s row went round at the bottom of the steps and kept the aisles from reaching them all at once.

It went on for a long time, verse one. The longest verse Ice had ever heard. And at the end of it, he heard Deston breathe in.

The last phrase of the verse went up. It went up toward the home note, the way Deston had said it should, where every hymn ends a verse. Ice heard the whole choir reach for it, three hundred years of reaching for it, and he heard the Choirmaster lift the baton, ready to bring it down on the downbeat and send them all back to the start. The way it had been doing since before Sedge’s mother’s grandmother.

And Deston held the note.

He didn’t come down. He held the last note of verse one, the one just before the resolution, long and low, the way he’d held a note for Xav on the second day in the fen to stop him going early. He held it under the whole choir with his hand flat on the spine of the book and his eyes shut. Then, before the baton could come down, he went on. Down onto the home note, and straight through it, and into the first line of the second verse.

Let the many move as one.

For a second, nothing happened.

Then a single chorister, at the front, in the first row of the pews, close enough to touch, turned its pale face from the altar toward Deston, and sang the second verse with him.

The Choirmaster brought the baton down.

It came down a second too late, onto a downbeat half the choir didn’t take. Ice heard the church split in two. Half of the voices went back to the start of verse one, because the baton told them to. Half of them didn’t. Half of them went on, into the second verse, after a man with a book singing a little flat on the low notes, because somebody had finally kept time with them.

The ones who went with Deston stopped attacking.

They just stopped. They stood in the water and sang. And the ones who went back to verse one kept coming, on the beat, and the Choirmaster on the altar turned its faceless head toward the man with the book.

It came down the steps.

Ice had a second to understand it. A Choirmaster whose choir was leaving it would go for the one who was taking them.

He was already moving. Not back. Across. He stepped off the top step and down, one, two, into the water, between the Choirmaster and Deston. He set his feet and sank his weight, all the way down into the heels and the stone under the water, put the shield’s lower edge to the floor of a drowned church, sloped, and breathed in.

Where a Holdfast stands, a line exists.

The bone baton came down on him.

It was the worst thing that had ever hit him, in any game, in his life. It didn’t hit hard. It hit long. It came down on the slope of the Anchor and stayed there, pressing, and the cold came out of it into the shield and the shield’s arm and the shoulder and the chest and down into his legs. It was the cold of a church that had been at the bottom of a fen for three hundred years, and at fifty per cent it was everywhere. His health went down in a long steady slide that didn’t stop.

He didn’t make a sound.

Behind him, Deston’s voice caught. Just once, a hitch in the middle of the second verse, as a third of it went back along the thread and landed in him at twenty per cent.

“Keep singing,” said Ice, through his teeth.

Deston kept singing.

And the bell. The bell, the bell, the bell, from a pillar at the left side of the church, from a small girl standing on a stone base the size of a dinner plate with her feet together. She’d stopped saying sorry an hour ago. She was ringing it so fast it had become one long silver sound, and the warmth was coming into him as fast as the cold, almost. Almost. His bar sat at forty and didn’t go down, and didn’t go up.

The Choirmaster lifted the baton for the next downbeat.

Xav was behind it.

Verse three went on for a long time.

Ice would never be able to remember most of it. He remembered pieces, after, when he watched the recording, the way you remember pieces of a dream.

He remembered the Choirmaster lifting the baton, and him stepping into it, and Anchoring, over and over, and the cold. He remembered Xav coming out of nowhere, a second at a time, and the window under each cut, and the numbers getting bigger as Xav got angrier. He remembered Suzu, who’d been holding the bottom of the steps alone with his scythe. As more and more of the choir went over to Deston and stopped attacking, Suzu had less and less to hold, until he simply turned round and came up the steps behind the Choirmaster, and the long dark blade came round at knee height into the back of the ink-black cope.

He remembered his health at thirty-one per cent, and drinking a Good draught with his sword hand without taking his eyes off the baton. Moonbell water, from the ring above their heads, under the moon. Then another, at twenty-eight.

And he remembered the choir.

By the end of the third verse, nearly all of them were singing with Deston. Rows of pale shapes in the water between the pews, faces lifted, singing the third verse of something nobody had heard the third verse of in three hundred years. And as they sang it, they changed. They got brighter. The mist they were made of got thinner and whiter and more like light. It was as though every bar they got past was something they’d been carrying, and they were putting it down.

On the left side of the church, on her pillar, Kotori was humming. He didn’t know when she’d started. She was humming the melody a beat behind everyone else, the way she laughed, and somehow that made it better. It sounded like the start of a round.

The fourth verse changed key.

Deston had said it would. He’d said it on the mound yesterday: the fourth time it changes key at the end and resolves. He’d sung it under his breath in the ring until Xav asked him to stop and then asked him to start again. But hearing it was different.

It went up. The whole choir together, in the drowned church, a whole step up. Everything got brighter, the voices and the light and the green water. It was the moment in a hymn when the organ opens all its stops, and the hair stands up on the back of your neck, and you don’t know why. And the Choirmaster, on the steps, with nobody left keeping time with it, made the first sound it had made: a long, high, wordless howl, like wind in a chimney. It lifted the baton with both hands, high over its head, and brought it down on Ice with everything it had.

He Anchored.

It hit the slope, and it was everything, all three hundred years of it. It drove him down onto one knee in the water. The cold went into him so hard that for a second he couldn’t see. His bar went down, and down, and the bell rang and rang, and it went down further. Nineteen. Fourteen. And he was on his knee on the floor of a drowned church with a bone baton pressing on his shield, and somewhere behind him a man he’d known since he was thirteen was singing the fourth verse of a hymn as loud as he’d ever sung anything. A third of the cold was going back along a thread into him. He wasn’t stopping.

Eleven.

He didn’t make a sound.

Then he set his back foot. Inside the movement, not before it, the way he’d taught a hundred and sixty people on a walkway in a swamp. And he stood up out of the Anchor into the Falling Cut, the whole of him arriving at once, and breathed out.

And the bell rang.

And the choir reached the end of the fourth verse, went up for the last time toward the home note, and found it. The resolution, the one they’d been reaching for since before anybody could remember, after three hundred years of being pulled back to the start. The whole church rang with it, one great held chord. The Choirmaster stood in the middle of it with its baton raised and nobody left to conduct.

The baton cracked.

A small dry sound in the middle of all that music, like a stick breaking underfoot. A line ran down the white bone from end to end. The Choirmaster looked at it, if a thing with no face could look. Then it came apart: cope and hands and baton and all, into pale mist that went up through the green water like smoke from a snuffed candle.

The choir didn’t stop singing.

They held the last chord. Every voice in the drowned church held it, long and low and bright, and as they held it, they began, one by one, to go.

The one in the front row went first. The first one to have kept time with Deston. It came apart into light, a single soft white flame, still singing its one note of the chord. Then it rose, slowly, through the green water, up past the tops of the pillars toward the dark roof, and through it.

Then the next. Then a whole row. Then all of them, rising, a whole church full of small white lights going up through the water like snow falling the wrong way, every one of them singing. The chord got quieter as they went, voice by voice, until there were only a few left, and then one, and then none.

Ice stood at the foot of the altar steps with his shield hanging from his arm and watched them go, and didn’t say anything at all. He couldn’t.

He looked across at Suzu.

Suzu was standing at the top of the steps with Aldo’s scythe lowered, and twenty small glass jars on his belt, all singing. The lights were going up all around him, past him, a few feet from the long dark blade. All he’d have had to do was lift it. He’d caught every one he could reach all day. They were worth something, Ice knew, now. He was the only player in the world who could catch them, and they wanted to know where they went.

Suzu didn’t lift the blade.

He stood and watched them go up, his half-closed eyes following them all the way to the roof. Then, when the last one had gone, he unhooked the jars from his belt one at a time and took out the stoppers.

Nineteen lights came out and went up after the others, singing.

He kept one. The first, from the first room, the very first night. He held it up and looked at it in the green light, the note inside going on, faint and clear.

“I want to know where it goes,” said Suzu, to nobody, and stoppered it, and hung it back on his belt.

Then the windows came.

Not one. All of them, all at once, so many and so bright that for a moment Ice couldn’t see the church.

Nobody touched anything.

They stood round the chest in the drowned church, five people, wet to the waist, with their health bars at eleven and thirty-one and fifty-four and sixty-two and seventy. Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then Deston sat down.

Right where he was, in the water, on the bottom step of the altar, with the book in his lap. He put his head in his hands and laughed and cried at the same time, so hard his shoulders shook. Thirteen years ago, in the back row of a school choir, two boys mouthing the high bits.

Ice sat down beside him and put an arm round him, and didn’t say anything, because there wasn’t anything.

It was Kotori who found the numbers.

She’d sat down on the edge of the chest with the Choir’s Bell in her hands. It was silver, and a little bigger than her old brass one, and it rang very softly when she breathed on it. And she’d opened her Exchange window, the way she had in the ring last night with the one bit of silver, and Ice saw her face change.

“Sorry,” she said, very quietly. “Sorry. I just—I looked.”

“What did you look at?”

“The silver.” She swallowed. “There’s twenty-four. One was sixty-two dollars, yesterday. But it’s more now. It went up. It went up this minute. “How much?”

Kotori did the sum. He watched her do it, lips moving, the way he’d watched Milli do sums on stream.

“Just the silver,” she said. “Just the twenty-four bits. Two thousand one hundred dollars. US.” She looked at the chest. At the bell-metal and the hymnal pages and the pew-oak and the forty faint lights. At the five of them. “And that’s just the silver.”

She didn’t say anything else for a while. She turned the silver bell over in her small hands.

“My dad paid two thousand four hundred and ninety-nine dollars for the pod,” she said eventually, so quietly that Ice almost didn’t hear it over the water. “He used it twice.”

They came up the old green steps into the afternoon, out of the arch, onto the mound, into grey light and mist and a frog somewhere a long way off. They stood there in a row, blinking.

The fen was quiet.

It had always been quiet. But underneath it, for the whole of the twenty-six days he’d known it, there had been the singing. Faint and slow, through the stone, under everything, verse one, round and round, so constant he’d stopped hearing it. It wasn’t there now. It had stopped. He heard the gap where it had been, the way you hear a fridge stop humming in the night.

They went back through the heron-stones, and across the tussocks, and down the north walk.

Sedge was on her porch.

She was standing at the top of her steps with one hand on the post, with her pale sharp eyes on the old fen. She didn’t look at them as they came along the boardwalk. She was listening. Ice didn’t know how long she’d been standing there. Since the window, maybe. Since every traveller in Elsewhere had seen it at once, and the woman at the end of the north walk who wasn’t a traveller at all had heard the fen go quiet.

They stopped at the foot of her steps.

“They finished,” said Ice.

Sedge didn’t say anything for a long time. The bundles of drying herbs rustled over her head. Somewhere behind the house a heron called.

“My mother would have liked to hear that,” she said eventually.

She went inside. She didn’t start grinding. Through the open door, Ice could see her sit down at her table, in the dim green light of her house, with her hands flat on the wood, and just sit there.
