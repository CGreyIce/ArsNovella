Live from Elsewhere · Volume 10: The Middle

Chapter 238

On Foot

3,440 words · 15 min read

Aurelie had not walked anywhere far in six years, and by the fourth day her feet knew it.

She’d had barges. Three of them, enormous, blue, with ten rowers each. She’d had Halls, sixty-one of them, across half the world, so that wherever she needed to be there was a room with a long table and a chair that was too big for her. She’d had Harlan, who carried things, and two hundred officers, who carried everything else. And she’d had Vane, who made sure it was all fine before she knew it needed to be.

She hadn’t had feet. Not really. Not for walking on.

“Fourteen days,” she said, on the fourth day, on the long rise out of the crater with the lake gone behind them. “He walked this. In January. On his own.”

“Twice,” said Vane. “There and back.”

“Three times.”

“Three,” agreed Vane. “And the ten walked it twice. And Riverbank.” He looked at the road. “And a goose.”

“The goose didn’t walk it.”

“No,” said Vane. “The goose stayed where it was. That’s the point of the goose.”

She looked at him sideways. He’d been saying things like that since the harbour wall. Small things. Things he’d never have said before, in six years, in two games. He’d sat on a wall at the third dawn and said I was next to you to a man he’d known in a war, and since then something had come loose in him. Not much. A bit. He said one more sentence than he needed to, about once a day.

She liked it more than she was going to tell him.

They met Riverbank’s three at the ferry on the sixth day.

Not all of Riverbank. Three of them, on the far bank, waiting for the ferry to come back across. The one with two blades and a sketchbook. The one who counted things. The one with the stick, who’d looked at her across the shingle at the bottom of the island’s steps in March as if he’d been told about her and wanted to check.

“Meridian,” said the one with two blades, when the ferry put them down on his side.

“Riverbank,” said Aurelie. “Sketch.”

“Xav,” said Xav. “Please. Nobody says Sketch but the board.” He looked at her, and then at Vane, and then at their feet. “You’re walking.”

“I said I would.”

“I know. He read it out. At the netloft, before we left.” Xav’s face did something. “He read it twice.”

The ferryman, an old man with a pole, looked at the five of them on his landing. Then he raised two fingers to the side of his eyebrow.

“o7,” said the ferryman.

Aurelie stared at him.

“He does that to everybody now,” said the one who counted. “Since January. Since the GM went through. Somebody did it to the GM at this ferry, and the ferryman saw, and now he does it to everybody.” He paused. “I’ve counted. Four hundred and twelve people. He keeps a tally on the post.”

He pointed. On the ferry post, cut into the wood, a lot of small marks in rows of five.

“o7,” said Aurelie, to the ferryman, slowly, because it seemed rude not to.

The ferryman looked enormously pleased and cut another mark.

They walked together after that. Five of them. Nobody alone; Xav said it was on a board in the round town and they’d hear about it if they didn’t. Xav at the front, because he knew the road. The one who counted, RX, counting. The one with the stick, Malcolm, saying very little and watching Aurelie as if she were a sentence he was still reading. And Vane beside her.

They talked on the road. Not much. Enough.

“Why are you walking?” said Xav, on the eighth day. “You’ve got barges. And Halls. You could’ve sent Harlan. He came to the round town before, in October. He was very loud.”

“He’s still very loud,” said Vane.

“I said I’d ask at his door,” said Aurelie. “Kei’s. The way you’re supposed to.”

“You could’ve written.”

“He asked me in a letter,” said Aurelie. “Would you stand next to us? At the mouth of the Undercut. I said yes in eleven minutes.” She looked at the road going away north-west in front of them. “I’d like to ask him back. Properly. Not in a letter.” She was quiet for a moment. “And I’d like to see it.”

“See what?”

“Where he sits,” said Aurelie. “The GM. He told me in February, in the netloft, on the floor, when I asked why he wasn’t running it. I want to stand where Kei puts me.” She looked at Xav. “I want to see where Kei puts him.”

Xav didn’t say anything for a long time.

“The first of nine,” he said at last. “At the near end. By the window. With his feet on the rung of the next chair.” He almost smiled. “You’ll see. It’s the least impressive place in the whole room.”

“Good,” said Aurelie.

They walked round the goose on the eleventh day.

It was where everybody said it would be. The long straight stretch past the second ferry, between two empty fields. Standing in the middle of the road, looking at the five of them with deep suspicion.

Xav stopped. RX stopped. Malcolm stopped.

“Don’t,” said Xav.

“Don’t what?” said Aurelie.

“It’s on Kei’s map. In pencil. The goose, and don’t.” Xav was looking at the goose very carefully. “The GM walked round it in January. The ten walked round it. Everybody walks round it. In August he walked round it with Hyv, beside him, and it sat down.” He looked at her. “Nobody knows what happens if you don’t.”

Aurelie looked at the goose.

She’d run ten thousand people for six years. She’d stood on the rail of a barge at the mouth of the Undercut and said it’s theirs to the leader of the biggest guild in the world. She’d been second, for the first time in her life, by six seconds, to a man who stood in front of a bell. She was not afraid of a goose.

She walked round it.

Off the road, into the field, a wide slow curve, not too close. Vane walked round it beside her, the same curve. The goose turned its head to watch them both go.

And then, very slowly, with enormous dignity, it sat down.

“It sat down,” said RX, in a voice of awe. “It sat down for them.”

“It’s seen two before,” said Malcolm.

“It’s seen two Constellation,” said RX. “These are Meridian.”

The goose sat in the road and looked at them.

Aurelie looked back at it from the far side, in the field. She found that she was smiling. She didn’t do it much; she had a face that didn’t need to, most days. Ten thousand people read it, and it was simpler if there wasn’t much on it.

“Hm,” she said.

Vane, beside her, looked at her very sharply.

They came round the curve into the round town on Sunday at the second dusk. The tenth of September.

She’d seen it on streams. Everybody had. The round market with the fountain in the middle and the ring of nine dark stones. The Hart on the far side. The Hall of Roads with its dome and the eye at the top. Seven roads going out from it like spokes. She’d seen the longest night on it, a year ago, on a dozen streams at once, sitting in her too-big chair in the castle on the west rim with Vane beside her. Four hundred and fourteen lights.

She hadn’t seen it like this.

Small. That was the first thing. It was so small. Masmark was a ring of a city round a lake the size of a country. The round town was a market and a Hall and some houses and an inn. You could have put the whole of it in Meridian’s castle and had room for the barges.

And full. Every step of the Hall of Roads had somebody on it. The fountain had people sitting on its rim. The windows of the Hart had faces in them. All of them watching the south road, where it came round the curve. Where five people were walking, slowly, with their feet hurting.

“They knew,” she said.

“He read your letter out,” said Xav. “At the long table. Kei says he read it twice there too.”

At the bottom of the south road, where it came into the market, there was a milestone. Old, with CARROW CROSS on it and seven roads cut into the top. And on the base of it, with her back against the stone, a woman with two short curved blades across her knees.

Aurelie saw her before she saw anything else. You learned to, running ten thousand people. You saw the one who wasn’t on the steps.

The woman looked up at her.

Calm, narrow face. A dark coat. And on the collar, where something had been stitched once, a patch of darker cloth and some loose threads.

“Meridian,” said the woman.

“Yes.”

The woman looked at her for a long time. At her plain blue with no gold on it anywhere. At her feet.

“You walked,” she said.

“I walked.”

“Fourteen days. To ask him something.” The woman looked up the steps at the gate, where a narrow young man was standing in it with his hands at his sides. “Ten thousand people. And you walked.”

“Yes.”

The woman didn’t say anything else. But she watched Aurelie go past, all the way up the market to the bottom of the steps. Aurelie could feel her watching. The way you’d watch somebody do a thing you were trying to learn.

The young man in the gate was the one Xav had told her about on the road. Kite. The one who used to be a bird.

He stood in the south gate of the Hall of Roads with his hands at his sides, where she could see them. And all the round town on the steps behind and below him, silent. He looked down at her, at the bottom of the steps, with Vane beside her and Riverbank’s three behind.

He asked Xav first. Because Xav was in front.

“Who’d stand next to you?”

“Dec,” said Xav. “Always.”

“Fair.”

RX: “Xav. And I’m counting.” Malcolm: “Whoever’s chasing.” “Fair.” “Fair.”

Then Kite looked at her.

She’d thought about it for fourteen days. She’d thought about it on the long rise and at both ferries and round the goose. She’d thought about it in the nights, in the inns, lying in narrow beds with her feet aching. She’d known since the harbour, since February, since she first heard the question asked at a netloft door. That one day she’d have to stand at the bottom of these steps and answer it.

She’d known the answer the GM would expect. Ten thousand people. And Kite would say that’s not a person, the way the girl with two different eyes had said it at the netloft door, and the way Kei had, and the GM.

“Who’d stand next to you?” said Kite.

“Vane,” said Aurelie.

The round town didn’t make a sound.

“Vane,” she said again. “For six years. Two games. Every room. I’ve never once turned round and he wasn’t there.” She didn’t look at Vane. She couldn’t. “And—“ She stopped. She made herself go on. “And whoever will. That’s what I walked here to ask.”

Kite looked at her for a long time.

“That’s two answers,” he said.

“Yes.”

Something happened to his narrow face. Aurelie didn’t know him. But she’d been told, on the road, by RX, who counted things: that the GM had stood at the bottom of these steps in August and given Kite three.

“Fair,” said Kite.

Then he asked Vane.

Aurelie hadn’t thought he would. Vane was behind her, a little to the left, where he always was. People didn’t ask Vane things. He didn’t say things. He made sure they were fine.

“Who’d stand next to you?” said Kite.

Vane looked at him. Then at the steps, at all of them. Then, very briefly, at Aurelie.

“Her,” said Vane.

Aurelie went completely still.

She’d known him six years. He’d never once said it. He’d been next to her in every room in two games, and he’d never once said it out loud. He’d written we’re fine.

“Fair,” said Kite. And then, because it was the bit people forgot, he came down out of the gate and sat down on the top step. And moved over. Leaving room.

Aurelie looked at the space on the step.

She’d sat in a great many chairs in six years. All of them too big. She’d sat at the head of long tables with two thousand chairs in a hall the size of a valley. She’d sat on barges and on rocks and on the floor of a netloft that smelled of fish, once, because there hadn’t been a chair.

Nobody had ever moved over for her on a step.

She went up the steps, past the round town, which watched her and didn’t say anything. And she sat down beside Kite in the space he’d left. Vane sat on her other side. And Xav and RX and Malcolm sat on the step below.

Nobody said anything for about a quarter of an hour. Then somebody at the top of the steps, inside the gate, said:

“You walked.”

He was standing in the gate behind them. The GM. With a cup in his hand, the way he always had a cup in his hand. Looking down at her on the step.

“Fourteen days,” said Aurelie, without turning round. “My feet hurt.”

“Good,” said the GM.

She turned round and looked up at him.

“Don’t,” she said.

Something happened at the corner of his mouth. She’d watched him for a week in February and never once seen it, and now she saw it all the time. On streams, on the island, at the top of the steps at the bottom of a bell tower with his mouth shut. She’d seen it on a clip called LOOK with two million views, which she’d watched four hundred times and would deny.

“You walked round the goose,” he said.

“It sat down.”

“I heard,” said the GM. “Xav wrote. From the field.” He looked at her for a long moment. “It doesn’t sit down for everybody.”

“It sat down for you.”

“It sat down for two,” said the GM. “I’ve never seen it sit down for anybody on their own.” He looked at Vane, beside her on the step. Then back at her. “Come in. There’s a long table.”

The banner room was the least impressive room she’d ever seen.

She’d been told. She’d been told by Xav on the road and by Harlan for a year and by a hundred streams. A long table, very long, with sixty-one chairs. A banner on hooks under a stair. A window at the near end. An old woman at the far end by the north door, asleep in a chair, with a jar on the table in front of her glowing very faintly through the clay. Another old woman on a window seat with a broom across her knees.

That was all.

No gold. No banners on every wall. No two thousand chairs. A room for sixty, with sixty-one in it, and a stool, and an old woman in a chair who’d come home.

She stood in the door and looked at it.

And the GM went past her, down the near end of the long table, to the first chair. The first of nine, Xav had said. At the near end, by the window. And sat down in it. And put his feet up on the rung of the next chair. And held out his hand, without looking, for a cup, which a small woman in white was already bringing.

The woman with gold horns came in behind him and sat in the chair beside his.

Aurelie looked at it for a long time.

I want to stand where Kei puts me.

The least impressive place in the room. At the end, by the window, with his feet on a rung. Not the head of the table; the head of the table was a quiet man with maps and a cup of tea going cold. The first of nine, at the end. Out of the way.

He looked, she thought, happier than she’d ever seen anyone look at the head of anything.

There wasn’t a chair for her.

She noticed that too. Sixty-one chairs, every one somebody’s. She stood in the door of the banner room with Vane behind her and the long table full in front of her, and there was nowhere for her to sit. She was used to that. In other people’s rooms, she was used to being given the best chair, and it being too big.

The old woman on the window seat got up.

She came down the long table slowly, with her broom, and stopped in front of Aurelie. She looked at her over her spectacles for a long time. At her plain blue, and her feet, and how small she was.

“You’re the one with the castle,” said the old woman.

“Yes.”

“Ten thousand people.”

“Yes.”

“Hm,” said the old woman. She looked at the long table, at all sixty-one chairs, full. Then she went past Aurelie, out of the banner room, into the Hall. Aurelie heard her go behind her counter, and open a cupboard, and close it.

She came back with a chair.

Not a big one. Small. Old wood, dark with years, with a low back and short legs and a rush seat that somebody had mended. A chair for somebody small. She put it down at the near end of the long table, beside Moonlight Harvest’s end, across from the first of the nine.

“Sit,” said the old woman.

Aurelie looked at it.

“It was my mother’s,” said the old woman. “She was small. She swept this room forty years and never had a chair that fit her either.” She sniffed. “So she made one.”

Aurelie sat down.

Her feet touched the floor.

She sat at the long table in the banner room of the round town, in an old small chair with a mended seat, with her feet flat on the floor for the first time at any table in six years. And she found she couldn’t say anything at all.

Across the table, in the first of the nine, the GM was looking at her. With his face doing the thing.

“It’s the right size,” he said.
