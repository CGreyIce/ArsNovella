Live from Elsewhere · Volume 7: Keep the Lamps

Chapter 182

Two Spaces

3,885 words · 17 min read

John was up at the first dawn on Monday because he was always up at the first dawn, and because he wanted to see the list before anybody else did.

That was the scout in him. He’d been a scout in the game since the first week, since a mountain tower with a signal mirror on top of it, in a town at the end of a pass. An old woman there had taught him to catch the sun in a round bit of glass and throw it. Glint, the game called it. You held up the mirror, you said the word, and a little bright point of light went where you sent it. As far as you could throw it. You saw what it saw. He’d thrown it across valleys and down wells and once, in March, into the mouth of a cave where something had looked back.

He liked being first to see things. It was the most useful thing he could do. If you saw it first, you could tell people, and if you told people, they could decide. It was what scouts were for.

So he was on the south steps of the Hall of Roads at eight in the morning, Singapore time. Grey light down the east road, the market empty, the pigeon asleep on its dark stone in the ring. Kei came out of the south door with a sheet of paper and four nails.

Kei looked at him.

“You’re up,” said Kei.

“I’m always up.”

“I know,” said Kei. “That’s why I’m doing it now.” He went to the board by the south door, where the Hall put its notices, where the LAMP WATCH list had been since September and the ROAD OF LIGHTS list since October. He pinned the new sheet over both of them, one nail in each corner. Then he stood back and looked at it for a moment. He didn’t look like a man who’d finished something. He looked like a man who’d done the part he could do.

“Read it if you want,” said Kei. “Everyone will.” And he went back in.

THE LONGEST NIGHT. Tue 21 Dec. Here.

Nobody alone. Threes at least.

And under it, twenty lines.

John read them all, slowly, the way he’d have read a map from the top of the tower. Kei had done it in his small square hand, three names to a line, and next to each line a place. Where they’d stand. What they’d keep.

Kei · Higu · Mango — the eye.

He stopped there. Read it again. Mango. Him. On the roof with Kei and Higu, two hundred and twelve steps up, where you could see everything. Higu’s Lantern and his Glint and Kei’s map, all three in one place. Kei had put the three people who saw furthest at the top of the town, so that everybody below could be told.

He felt something go warm in his chest and didn’t look at it too closely. He kept reading.

Thea · Gigi · Xola — the north door, left.

He looked at that one for a long time. He’d been at the south-west road in July, when the two of them had held a gap together with thorn and Turn. Good thorn. Good turn. He’d seen them pass tea down a table through two other people’s hands. Kei had put them together, at the most important door in the town, on the longest night. With Xola on the other side, so there’d be somebody who’d stood next to both of them.

It was either very brave or very stupid. John thought it was probably the first. Kei wasn’t stupid.

Salty · itswendy · Kite — the north door.

Pingu · theidiot · Resent — the north door, right.

Azac’s wall. RX’s two knives. Ryan, who said yeah. The three of them, poly, with the biggest shield in the guild between the other two. On the right of the north door, where the road of lights came in. John knew Azac would have asked for that. He’d have stood up at the long table on Saturday and said I’m a wall, the way he’d said it in the booth in Singapore on the first of January, and nobody would have argued.

Baer · minksminko · cal.calvin — the north steps.

Kai · Chernn · tomatosoup30 — the windows.

Chernn was a name John didn’t know. Then he remembered Deston at the long table on Thursday, red in the face, telling everybody about a candle village. And Tif, laughing, saying Deston’s girlfriend’s called Chernn with two n’s because Chern was taken. And everybody else laughing too, kindly, and Deston going redder.

Suzuyunn · Kotori · Leveti — the banner room.

Sketch · Serenity · Sakami — the market, north side.

Xav, Malcolm, Val. His three, from Riverbank, without him, because Kei had put him on the roof. He felt a small pang about that. Then he saw that Kei had put them on the north side of the market, right under the eye. So the first thing John told anybody would go straight down to them, and he stopped minding.

Hanarisu · Millianna · YoJareddo — the market, the fountain.

Aleaka · Cursiffe · Nikorasu — the gallery.

Inaxho · Drew · Historia — the forge. Keep the forge.

Tiger · Maigo · HakoP — the first mile.

He read that one twice. Outside the north door. On the road itself. The first mile of the road of lights, the first waystone. Somebody had to keep it, the first lamp on the road. Because when the dark came down the north road it would see that lamp last, and everything behind it would already be out. And the three who’d lit the road were keeping its last lamp. That was right. It was horrible and it was right.

PenguChi · xyrolizea · Tif2 — the south steps. The back of the line.

sleptzzy · Rafi · Bram_V — the north-east road.

Joy · Tobi · somebodynewish — the north-west road.

Winter · dumbo · …

He went down the rest. The twelve who weren’t the thirty-six and weren’t the first names, the ones who’d come through Wendy’s gate since spring and signed the Roll and been there ever since. Kei had put every one of them in a three, next to somebody they’d said at the gate. That was very Kei. He’d remembered. Every one of them.

Nineteen lines, one of them a four. Fifty-eight names. Fifty-seven of the sixty, and Chernn.

And the twentieth line.

Ice · ______ · ______ — the Seven Below.

John stood on the south steps and looked at it.

Two spaces.

Kei had drawn them in ink, two lines, very straight. The rest of the sheet was full, every line, three names to a line, crammed in. And that one line at the bottom had a name and two blanks.

The Seven Below.

Under the town. Where the seven tunnels came in, one under each road. Where, at the end of the north one, the old stone met the new and it was cold. Where Dec went every morning, alone, and sat with his back to the wall.

John thought about it for a long time. He was a scout. It was his job to see things first and work out what they meant.

He understood two things at once.

The first was that Kei hadn’t put anybody in Dec’s three because everybody wanted to be in Dec’s three. If he’d put anybody, the others would have minded. And the one he put would have known they’d been put, not asked.

The second was that Kei had put Dec under the town. Not at the north door, where the road came in, where everybody would think the fight was. Under it. At the bottom. Because somebody on the roof, or at the forge, or in a tunnel with a scout’s eyes, had noticed the Delvers moving. Something was going to come up from underneath, as well as down the road.

And Kei had put Dec there, where nobody would see him. With two spaces.

John looked at the blanks.

Then he heard someone on the steps behind him, and moved without thinking. That was the scout in him too. Down the side of the steps, round the corner of the fountain, into the shadow of the chestnut stall with its famous shutter. Where he could see the board and the board couldn’t see him.

He didn’t know why he did it. He was going to feel terrible about it later. But he did it.

It was Hyvred.

She came out of the Hall’s south door with her bow on her back and a pen in her hand. She stopped at the top of the steps. She looked round the market, all of it, edge to edge, the way she looked at everything. John held very still in the shadow of the chestnut stall. Her eyes went over him and didn’t stop. He was a scout. He was good at being somewhere nobody looked.

She went to the board.

She read the list. He could see her reading it, from the stall: the top line, the second, the third, her head going down the sheet. She stopped at the north door, left. Thea, Gigi, Xola. Her tail went round her ankle, once, tight. Then she kept going, all the way down. To the bottom. To the twentieth line.

She stopped.

She stood there for a long time. Long enough that John’s knees started to hurt from crouching. The pigeon woke up on its dark stone in the ring, looked at her, and went back to sleep. The fountain ran.

She held the pen up to the paper.

She didn’t write anything. She stood with the pen a finger’s width from the first blank. Her tail went straight out behind her, stiff as a broom handle, the white tip trembling. John had seen it do that once before, in the banner room, the night Xola had said Hyv, you like him and the whole room had heard. She stood there. She took the pen away. She put it back.

Come on, thought John. He didn’t know he was thinking it until he was. Come on, Hyv.

She wrote.

Small. Very small, much smaller than everybody else’s names on the list. Her handwriting was careful and upright, the way it was on the backs of the drawings Bee did of her. Six letters in the first blank, after Dec’s name.

Hyvred.

And then she stood back from the board as if it had bitten her. She stared at what she’d done. Her face was red all the way to the gold of her horns. Her tail had gone round her ankle three times. John could see her think, quite clearly, about crossing it out.

She didn’t cross it out.

There was a sound from the door. Someone else coming out.

Hyvred spun round so fast she nearly dropped the pen.

It was Bee.

She came out of the south door with her wings folded down her back and her sketchbook under her arm and a piece of toast in her mouth, which you weren’t supposed to have outside the Hart. She saw Hyvred. She saw the board. She saw Hyvred’s face, and the pen in Hyvred’s hand.

She took the toast out of her mouth.

She didn’t say anything. John would remember that afterward, more than anything. Bee didn’t say anything at all. She walked over, took the pen out of Hyvred’s hand, gently, and looked at the twentieth line. Ice · Hyvred · ______. She leaned in and wrote her own name in the second blank, in her big loopy hand, with a tiny bee drawn after it, wings and all.

Bee.

Then she gave the pen back. She put the toast back in her mouth. She hooked her arm through Hyvred’s, and walked her down the south steps and across the market toward the Hart. Past the fountain, past the ring, past the chestnut stall, two feet from where John was crouching. Neither of them looked at him. Hyvred was walking like someone in a dream. Bee was chewing.

As they went past, John heard Hyvred say, very faintly, “Bee.”

And Bee said, round the toast, “I know. Shut up. You did it.”

They went into the Hart.

John stayed in the shadow of the chestnut stall for a long time.

He didn’t tell anyone.

He thought about it. He was a scout; it was his job to see things first and tell people. He’d have told Kei about a door. He’d have told Xav about a boss. He’d once spent forty minutes on a call telling RX in great detail about a cave he’d thrown a Glint into, and RX had fallen asleep.

He didn’t tell anyone this. It wasn’t his to tell. It was theirs. He’d just happened to be first.

He went up the two hundred and twelve to the roof instead, because the roof was his now, and because he wanted to look at something big.

Higu was there with her lamp. She looked at him when he came out onto the ledge and didn’t ask anything. She moved over. He sat.

“You’ve got a face,” said Higu, after a while.

“Everyone keeps saying that to everyone.”

“It’s going round,” said Higu. “Like a cold.” She drank her tea. “Did you see the list?”

“I saw the list.”

“We’re the eye.”

“I know,” said John. “I saw.” He took his mirror out from under his collar on its cord, the little round one. He turned it over in his hands. “Higu. Can you see north from here? With the Lantern?”

“As far as I can send it.”

“Will you send it? With mine?”

She looked at him. Then she set her cup down on the ledge and held up her hand, palm out, the way she did. She said her word, ahead. A light went out from her hand, gold and soft, and drifted out over the rim of the eye and north. John held his mirror up to it, caught it in the glass, and said his own word. The glint went with it, bright and quick, out over the roofs and the north door and the moor.

They went together. Her soft one and his sharp one. Up the north road, over the heather, past the first waystone with Tiger’s three’s lamp in it, past the second, the third. He saw what his glint saw, the way you did. He saw the backs of the waystones go by below, every mile, every one with a little black cage in it and a little flame. Lit. All of them lit. Ninety-four, from the north side, a road of tiny lights going away up the moor.

Past the rings. Past the old hall, half a dome on a hill with a birch growing through the floor. Past the broken wall. Up the line of twenty-two stones, every fifty paces, each one lit.

To the edge.

And past the edge, out in the black, a mile out, perhaps less, the lamp.

It was still. Set down. Gold. Steady. The same as Hako had seen it, three weeks ago. Somebody’s lamp, on the ground, in the dark, not moving. As if whoever it was had sat down beside it to wait.

“Still there,” said Higu softly. She could see it too. She saw from her light, the way he saw from his glint.

“Still there,” said John.

He was going to bring the glint back. Then he saw the other thing.

Behind the lamp. Past it. Further out into the black, where neither of them had ever sent anything, because there was nothing out there to see.

The black was moving.

Not the Unlit, not like the eleven Hako had seen at the third ring, tall and swaying and separate. He couldn’t see any shapes at all. It was just the dark, out past the lamp, the whole of it, as far as his glint could see. And it was moving. Slowly. All of it at once. The way the sea moves when the tide’s on the turn, before it’s begun to come in. A sort of heaviness, a leaning, a thickness gathering out there in the black past the edge of the world, all of it facing one way.

South.

Toward the lamp. Toward the edge, and the twenty-two, and the road of lights, every lamp of which faced north.

Toward them.

He pulled the glint back. It came home into his mirror with a little snap, and he sat on the ledge on the roof of the dome and found that his hands were cold.

Higu had brought hers back too. She was sitting very still with her palm on her knee.

“It’s not a line,” said John. His voice came out steady. He’d been told once that he could convince anybody of anything if he had good points, and he’d always been proud of that. He didn’t want to convince anyone of this. “It’s not going to come down the road in a line. Like Hako’s eleven. It’s going to come all at once. All of it. Like the tide.”

“It comes south all at once,” said Higu. “On the longest night.”

“Yes.”

“And it comes here.”

“Yes,” said John.

They sat there for a long time. The sun came up properly over the east road. Below them, through the eye, the floor of the Hall filled up slowly with people coming in for the day. Some of them stopped at the board by the south door and read the list. He could see them from up here, tiny, reading. Somebody pointing. Somebody putting a hand over their mouth.

He’d tell Kei about the tide. That was his. He’d go down in a minute and tell him, because that was what scouts were for. And Kei would write it on the map in very small letters, and then they’d all know, and they’d decide.

He looked down through the eye for a moment longer first.

Dec read the list at the second dusk, when he came in from the tunnel.

John saw him do it. He’d come down off the roof by then and told Kei about the tide. Kei had written it on the map and gone very quiet, and then gone to talk to Azac. And John was on the south steps again, sitting near the top, when Dec came round the curve of the north road and across the market. He came in through the south gate, past Wendy (“Who’d stand next to you?” “Everybody, today”), and up the steps to the board.

He stood and read it.

All of it. Every line, top to bottom. John watched him read the north door, left, and stop for a moment, and go on. Watched him read the first mile, and stop for longer. Then down to the bottom. To the twentieth line.

Ice · Hyvred · Bee (and a tiny bee, wings and all) — the Seven Below.

Dec stood there for a long time.

His face didn’t do anything. It never did. John had known him since orientation camp, since they were seventeen. He’d been on Dec’s stream every night in the facecam days, years ago, when you could see his face on camera. It hadn’t done much then either. Everybody said Dec’s face did nothing.

John had always thought that wasn’t quite true. It did one thing. Very small. You had to be looking.

He was looking.

Dec read the twentieth line, and then, after a long moment, he nodded. Once. Very slightly. The way he nodded when something was right.

Then he went into the Hall and through to the banner room and sat down in the first of the nine, the same as always. He held the cup Kotori brought him the same as always. Down the long table, beside Bee, Hyvred was very carefully not looking at him. Bee was drawing. Her wing was out a little, the way it had been for weeks now, so there’d be room behind it if anybody needed it.

He didn’t look at her either. He held the cup.

John went and sat at Riverbank’s end of the second table. Xav looked up from his paper sketchbook, at John’s face, and then down the room to the first of the nine. And then back at John.

“You saw something,” said Xav.

“I saw the tide,” said John. “I told Kei.”

“Not that,” said Xav.

John looked at him. Xav had been on the bank at Brackenford in February, waiting for eight idiots. He’d known John since the same orientation camp. He looked at things and put them in order.

“I saw first,” said John. “That’s all. It’s not mine.”

Xav looked at him for a while longer. Then he nodded, the same way Dec had. Once, very slightly. And went back to drawing feet.
