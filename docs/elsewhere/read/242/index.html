Live from Elsewhere · Volume 10: The Middle

Chapter 242

The Other Way

3,686 words · 16 min read

He took the six o’clock bus, the morning one, the way Deston always took the evening one. The other way.

Melaka Sentral at a quarter to six on a Saturday was mostly dark and mostly empty. A man selling coffee from a cart. A family with too many bags. Two students asleep on their rucksacks. The bus came in out of the dark with its lights on and its door hissing. And he got on with a small bag and nothing in his hands and sat by the window, on the left, the way Hyv had told him to.

Sit on the left, she’d written, on Thursday. You’ll get the trees first.

He hadn’t asked what she meant. He’d find out.

Gabe had come down to the bus station with him. Of course he had. He’d stood on the platform in the dark in a t-shirt, with his enormous face. He hadn’t said anything for the whole of the ten minutes they’d waited. Which was the longest Gabe had ever been quiet in eleven years.

When the bus pulled out, Gabe raised two fingers to the side of his eyebrow.

Ice did it back, through the window.

He’d lived on the other end of this road for twenty-four years.

He’d been down it and up it more times than he could count, before Malacca. On buses, in cars, half asleep. He knew it the way you know a corridor you’ve walked every day. He couldn’t have told you one thing about it.

He’d never once looked out of the window.

He looked now.

The town going away behind, the lights going off one by one as the sky came up grey. Then the highway, and the dark green on either side. Not the middle of the window, where the road was. The edges. The left edge of the glass, where the trees were coming past, close, one after another. Tall ones, thin ones, palms and something with pale bark, and under them more green, and under that more. All the way back from the road into a grey morning he couldn’t see the end of.

Nobody told me there were this many trees at the edges.

She’d written that. In June. From a bus, on this road, the other way, on her way to his door.

He sat with his forehead nearly on the glass and looked at the trees. He didn’t think about anything. He just looked. The way he’d looked at the lake from the notch above Masmark in January, a lake the size of a country, and not been able to think of anything to say.

The bus went over a bump.

He felt it in the seat, in his back. Just after the toll. Exactly where Deston said it would be.

There’s one after the toll. Deston had told him on Thursday night, in the corridor, very seriously. And a big one at the turn-off. You’ll feel it in your teeth.

He laughed. Out loud, on a bus, at half past six in the morning, on his own, with his forehead on the window. The man in the seat across the aisle looked at him. Ice didn’t mind.

The turn-off. The big one. He felt it in his teeth.

Johor, and the light coming up properly now, gold, over the green. He watched the edges. He watched both sides; you could, if you tried. If you didn’t look at the road in the middle of the window, where your eyes went by themselves. If you made them go to the corners, the way she’d told him her eyes went now, out here, since the surgery, a year and a quarter ago. All the way to the frame, and past it, to the other window across the aisle.

Both sides of the road at once.

He couldn’t really. Not the way she could, or the way she could in the game. But he could nearly. If he held still and didn’t decide where to look. If he let it all come, edge to edge, the way Kestrel had learned to shoot with a pigeon watching.

He sat like that for an hour. The bus going south. Both sides of the road going past at once, nearly. Trees, and towns, and more trees. A river, once, brown, wide, with a boat on it. A man on a motorbike on the left, keeping pace with the bus for a whole minute with his shirt flapping. Ice saw him without turning his head.

There’s a man on a motorbike on the LEFT and i can see him without turning my head.

She’d written that too.

He got his phone out.

The Causeway, and the checkpoints. Off the bus, through, back on. Off again, through again, back on. He’d done it a hundred times and never noticed it either. He noticed it now. The long hall, the queues, the woman at the desk who looked at his passport and then at him and then waved him on without a word. Like the fish man at the bottom of the north-west road, who’d seen every kind of person and every kind of hurry.

Then the other side. And the city.

He’d known it twenty-four years. He’d been gone a while. It looked the same and it didn’t. He looked at the edges of it, out of the window, the way he’d looked at the trees. Not the buildings. The sky round the buildings. The bits of green between them. The birds on the wires.

Bee had drawn her a picture, once, after the surgery. A year and a quarter ago, on a video call from Australia. Hyv had told him about it in the game, on a harbour wall, with fish. Nothing in the middle. Everything at the edges. Hands, cats, corners, feet. Birds. Sky round buildings. Hyv had said I hate it, I love it.

He looked at the sky round the buildings.

The bus stopped.

She was there.

He saw her before the bus had stopped, through the window, at the edge of the glass. He saw her the way she’d told him she’d seen him, in June, at the end of a long corridor. All at once. Not her face first. All of her, and the pavement, and the people round her, and the shop behind her, and the sky round the shop. And her, in the middle of it, standing very still with her hands holding each other very tightly. Looking at the bus door.

He got off.

He didn’t hurry. He never hurried. He came down the bus steps with his small bag, minding the bottom one, out of habit, and stepped onto the pavement. And stopped. Ten paces from her. The way he’d stopped ten paces from Xav in the Seven Below, and from Lucan on a bridge.

She didn’t move either.

People went past between them. A man with a trolley. Two girls laughing. Somebody’s child. The bus pulled away behind him with its door hissing.

“Hi,” said Hyvred.

It was the voice. The one from a thousand calls, from a game, from a gate. Left. Now. Behind, low. Small and level and shaking.

“Hi, Hyv,” said Ice.

She came the ten paces.

She didn’t run. She came the way she’d come down the long table in April to ask Ama Corrie for the broom. Fast at the start and then slower, as if she’d remembered halfway that she didn’t know how. She stopped in front of him.

She hit him. On the arm, not hard.

“You sat on the left,” she said.

“You said.”

“Did you get the trees?”

“All of them,” said Ice. “Nearly.”

She hit him again.

“Nearly counts,” she said. And then her face did something. He didn’t look at it the way you’d look at a face. He looked at all of her, at once, standing on a pavement in the morning. And at the people going past, and the shop, and the sky round it. And he let her be in the middle of it, the way she’d let him be in the middle of a corridor.

She put her forehead against his shoulder.

“Mrp,” she said, into it.

“I know,” said Ice. “It’s allowed.”

They walked.

He didn’t know where. She did. She’d planned it, she said, for three weeks; she’d made a list and thrown it away and made another. And then on Thursday night thrown that one away too, and decided to just walk, because that was what he did in the game. Walk, and look, and let things come.

So they walked. Through the city in the morning, in the heat, which was the same heat as Malacca and somehow not. Along streets he’d known for twenty-four years. And she showed him the edges.

Not the buildings. The things at the edges of them. She’d been learning them for a year and a quarter, she said, since the edges came. Nobody else ever looked. A cat asleep in a drain, curled tight, one ear twitching. A bird on the ledge above a shop sign that had built a nest in the second O. The sky in the gap between two towers, very blue, with a plane going across it so high it didn’t make a sound. A woman’s hands at a stall, folding something, very fast, the same fold over and over.

“I never saw any of this,” she said, “until last year. I lived here my whole life and I never saw any of it. I saw the middle. One thing at a time, down a tube.” She stopped on a corner and looked up at the gap between the towers. “And then the edges came. And it turned out the edges were where everything was.”

He looked at the gap. At the plane, going across it, very high.

“I lived here twenty-four years,” he said. “I never saw it either.”

She looked at him.

“You could see the edges,” she said.

“I didn’t look,” said Ice. “I was looking at the front.”

They found a bench in the afternoon. Under a tree, in a park. A big tree, very old, with roots that came up out of the grass round it like the backs of things in water. The bench was in its shade, and nobody was on it.

They sat down.

Close. The way the bench on the roof at the eye was built for two, close, with Somewhere on the back. This one didn’t have anything on it. Somebody had scratched initials into the arm, a long time ago, and the paint had gone over them.

She didn’t say anything for a long time. Nor did he. They sat on the bench under the tree and looked at the park. A man walking a dog that didn’t want to be walked. Two kids on bicycles. The light coming down through the leaves in pieces.

“Hyv,” said Ice.

“Mm.”

He’d thought about this on the bus. He’d thought about it in the corridor, on Thursday, with Deston telling him about the bumps. He’d thought about it on the bench on the roof in August, when he’d said can I come and see you and she’d said not some time and say a day. He’d said a day.

He’d never said the other thing.

Nobody had. Not him, not her. Since the gate. I like you. I like you too, Hyv. And then eight months of holding her hand, and her asleep on his shoulder, and his hand between her horns, and a door at the end of a corridor. And everybody in the round town knowing, and Kestrel on a bridge saying he’s happy, and the auntie downstairs saying the girl with the bag.

And nobody had ever said the word.

“Are we together?” said Ice.

She went completely still.

On the bench, under the tree. Not her face; all of her. The way she’d gone still on the roof when he said can I come and see you.

Then she turned and looked at him.

“Are we—“ she said. Her voice went up. “Are we together?”

“Yes.”

“Are you asking me if we’re together?”

“Yes,” said Ice. “I’ve never asked. Nobody’s said it. I thought—“ He stopped. He made himself go on, the way he’d learned to, on a bench, in August. “I thought I should ask. Properly. Out here. Not in there. Out here, where it’s—“ He didn’t know the end of the sentence. “Where it’s real.”

Hyvred stared at him for a very long time.

Then she hit him. Harder than usual. Not hard.

“Since the gate,” she said. “We’ve been together since the gate. Since January. You said I like you too, Hyv and put your hand on my head in front of the whole round town and went pink on a clip that everybody’s seen. You held my hand up a four-hour stair. I knocked on your door. I got on a bus and knocked on your door and your auntie calls me the girl with the bag—“ She stopped. She was crying. She was laughing. Both. “Yes. We’re together. We’ve been together. You just never said.”

“I’m saying,” said Ice.

“Say it properly.”

He looked at her. On the bench, under the tree, in the light coming down through the leaves in pieces. All of her, at once, edge to edge, the way she’d taught him.

“We’re together,” said Ice.

She didn’t say anything. She didn’t need to. Her face was doing all of it.

“Yes,” said Hyvred. Very quietly.

He leaned in.

He’d never done it with her. He’d thought about it, once or twice, in the last eight months, in the game, at the bottom of a lake with her forehead against his. On a harbour wall, with fish. He’d thought about it and not done it. Because it was a thing you went first for, and he’d always let her go first, on purpose, ever since the gate. Don’t stop waiting. Not with me.

He’d been waiting eight months. On the back foot. A breath.

He leaned in.

And she put both hands flat on his chest and pushed.

Not hard. But enough. He went back. Down. Against the back of the bench, all the way, his shoulders against the old wood. Surprised, for once in his life, by something he hadn’t seen coming. With his head tipped back and the leaves above him, and the light in pieces.

And she leaned over him, with her hands still flat on his chest, and kissed him.

It wasn’t long. It didn’t need to be.

When she sat back, he didn’t move. He sat against the back of the bench where she’d pushed him, with his head tipped back, and looked up at the leaves. He didn’t say anything. He didn’t think he could have.

Hyvred sat beside him with her hands in her lap, very straight, and her face so red he could see it at the edge of his eye without turning his head.

“I go first,” she said.

He turned his head and looked at her.

“At the gate,” said Hyvred. “I said me. First. Before you’d said anything.” She was looking at the park, not at him. Her voice was shaking. “And on a bus in June. I knocked first. I came down your corridor first.” She swallowed. “You wait. That’s what you do. On the back foot, a breath. You let people go first. You told me on a harbour wall. The first thing I’d ever done that let somebody else go first.” She looked at him at last. “So I go first. That’s how it works. With us. You wait, and I go.”

He looked at her for a long time.

“You pushed me down,” he said.

“You were leaning,” said Hyvred. “You’d have got there first. I could see it. I could see the whole thing coming.” She put her hands over her face. “I wasn’t going to let you go first. Not at that. Not after eight months of me going first at everything.”

He started to laugh.

He couldn’t help it. Against the back of the bench, under the tree, in a park in the city he’d lived in for twenty-four years without once looking at the edges of it. He laughed the way he’d laughed on the netloft step at I pick fenwort. Out loud, all of it, with his head back.

She hit him through her hands, blind, on the arm.

“Don’t laugh—“

“I’m not laughing at you,” said Ice.

“Then what—“

“I didn’t see it coming,” said Ice. “You. I didn’t see it coming.” He wiped his eyes. “Nobody’s ever done that. Not Xav. Not Lucan. Nobody’s ever gone first before I could.” He looked at her. “You’re the only one.”

She took her hands off her face and looked at him.

“Mrp,” she said, very small.

“I know,” said Ice. “It’s allowed.”

They sat on the bench till the evening.

Not saying much. They didn’t need to. A man came and fed the birds at the other end of the park. A woman jogged past three times, each time slower. The light went from pieces to gold to orange through the leaves. Hyvred put her head on his shoulder at some point, the way she did in the game, in the first of the nine, and didn’t fall asleep this time. She just sat there with it there.

At six he had to go. The evening bus, the one Deston always took, the other way.

She walked him to the stop. She stood on the pavement while it came in with its lights on, its door hissing.

“You’ll see both sides of the road,” she said. “On the way back. In the dark. The lights at the edges.”

“I’ll look.”

“And you’ll tell me.”

“I’ll tell you,” said Ice.

She hit him on the arm. Then she didn’t let go of it.

“Tomorrow,” she said. “In the game. The first of the nine. I’ll be in a minute before you.”

“So you can see me come out of the dark.”

“Left side first,” said Hyvred. “Like you’re feeling for a wall.”

He got on the bus. He sat on the left, out of habit, though going north it would have been the right side for the trees. He looked out of the window at her, on the pavement, at the edge of the glass, getting smaller. All of her, at once.

He raised his hand. Open, where she could see it.

She raised hers.

On the way back, in the dark, over the bump after the toll, he got his phone out.

He put the phone face down on his knee. The bus went north through Johor in the dark, and the lights went past at the edges, both sides, nearly. And he sat with his forehead on the glass and didn’t think about anything at all. Except a bench, under a tree, and somebody’s hands flat on his chest, and the leaves above him.

He hadn’t seen it coming.

He’d never been happier in his life.
