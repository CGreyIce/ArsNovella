Live from Elsewhere · Volume 1: Moonlight Harvest

Chapter 15

Two Thousand Four Hundred and Ninety-Nine

2,636 words · 11 min read

Cursiffe landed on Saturday night, and they met him at Lau Pa Sat on Sunday, because it was on the list.

Hana had been worried about it, a little. Not about Cursiffe. She’d known Cursiffe for years, on calls and in chats and in a group voice channel at strange hours, and she knew exactly what he was like: steady and dry, and the person who noticed the thing nobody else had. She’d never seen him in person. He’d never seen her. He’d flown up from Australia for four days because he’d found a cheap flight, and because, as he’d put it in the group chat, if Milli’s going to be in the same city as Hana for once in his life, someone should be there to witness it.

What she’d been worried about was the first minute. You knew someone’s voice for years, and then they were standing in front of you in the actual air, and you had to work out what to do with your face.

She needn’t have worried. They were standing by the old cast-iron clock tower in the middle of the market, in the evening heat and the smell of charcoal, when somebody said behind her, in a voice she knew as well as her own:

“You’re shorter than I thought.”

She turned round, and he was there. He was exactly himself and not at all what she’d pictured, which she was learning was how it always went. He was holding a small paper bag.

“Hi,” said Cursiffe. “This is for you. And this is for you.” He gave Hana the paper bag and held out a hand to Milli.

Milli shook it. They’d known each other for years too, on calls. They looked at each other, two men who’d been talking at three in the morning across two oceans since before either of them could remember. Then Milli said “Hi,” in the voice he used for the very small number of people he’d decided he liked, and pulled him into a short, hard, one-armed hug that clearly took Cursiffe by surprise.

Hana opened the paper bag. Inside was a fountain pen, dark blue, and a little glass bottle of ink to go with it.

“You write everything in that notebook,” said Cursiffe. “In pencil. It’s a crime.”

She laughed so hard she had to sit down on the edge of a planter.

Lau Pa Sat at night was loud and hot and wonderful. The street outside had been closed to traffic, and the satay stalls had come out onto it in a long line, smoke rolling off their grills in great clouds into the orange streetlight. Uncles in vests fanned the coals with bits of cardboard. Somebody shouted numbers. There were plastic tables on the road and plastic stools round them, and every one was full of people eating with their hands. Under the old Victorian roof of the market itself, behind them, a hundred more stalls were selling everything else.

They got a table on the road, which was a small miracle, and she ordered for all of them, because it was her city and it was on the list. Fifty sticks of satay, chicken and mutton, with the peanut sauce and the onion and cucumber and the little squares of ketupat. Sugarcane juice. Carrot cake, black, from a stall inside she’d been going to since she first lived here. Milli looked at the satay when it came, the great heaped plate of it, and said, “That’s too much,” and then ate nineteen sticks.

“The heat,” he said, between sticks. “How do you live.”

“You get used to it.”

“You don’t,” said Cursiffe. He’d taken his jacket off and rolled his sleeves to the elbow. He looked like a man who’d been told it was the nice time of year, and didn’t believe it. “I’ve been here a day. Nobody’s used to it. Everybody’s lying.”

“Everybody’s lying,” Milli agreed, and they clinked their sugarcane juice.

It was Cursiffe who said it, in the end.

They’d been talking about everything else for an hour. About Cursiffe’s flight, and the man in the next seat who’d watched a film about sharks. About the new towels. About Jared, who couldn’t come, because Jared was in America and had work, and had worked every weekend for a month, and said on every call that he’d stream again “when things calm down”. Things hadn’t calmed down for a long time. They talked about Aleaka, who’d sent eleven messages that day, all of them about what Milli was eating.

And then Cursiffe wiped his fingers on a paper napkin and said, quite casually, “So. Ice moved Suzu to Malaysia.”

Hana had been waiting all evening for someone else to bring it up.

“He hired him,” she said. “Ice has a company now. Moonlight Harvest Workshop. With a secretary. Chris looked it up on the government website live on stream and Ice told him off.”

“I know,” said Cursiffe. “Maigo sent me the clip.” He picked up another stick of satay and looked at it thoughtfully. “Ice made fifty-two ringgit from a leaf.”

“Fifty-two ringgit and ninety-two sen,” said Hana. “His chat won’t let him forget the ninety-two sen.”

“And fifty-four cents from potions on day two,” said Milli unexpectedly. Both of them turned to look at him. He shrugged. “I watched some. On the plane. Hana’s been sending me clips for two weeks. I had thirteen hours.”

“You watched it?”

“I watched the bit with the money.” He ate a piece of ketupat. “It’s an interesting economy. The coins go up in hundreds. A hundred copper is a silver, a hundred silver is a gold, and a gold is about ten US dollars on the company’s exchange right now. But the company takes a cut, and the price moves, because there’s a company buying everything it can.” He frowned. “Crimson something.”

“Crimson Ledger,” said Hana and Cursiffe together.

“They tried to buy all his good potions,” said Hana. “At two dollars each.”

“And he said no,” said Milli. “I saw. That was correct. If you sell everything to the one buyer, the one buyer owns your price.” He thought about it. “Ice is smarter than his stream makes him look.”

“Everybody’s smarter than his stream makes him look,” said Cursiffe. “That’s the point of the stream.”

“Is it worth it, though?” said Cursiffe, after a while. “Honestly. Two and a half thousand.”

He said it to the table, but he was looking at Hana.

She knew why. She was the one who streamed. Or had streamed, for three years, three times a week, until five weeks ago. She was the one with a model and a chat and a channel that would come back to life the moment she switched it on. Everybody knew what ELSEWHERE meant for people like her. They’d been talking about it on every VTuber forum since the first trade show. A body that moved exactly the way your model looked. Not just a face on a screen and a pair of hands at a desk. All of you, walking, breathing, climbing, fighting, in Follow, with the camera hanging behind you. She’d seen Suzu in it, on Ice’s raid, standing in that little wooden room with his long silver hair, looking down at his hands, and something in her chest had gone very tight.

She’d wanted it so badly she hadn’t let herself think about it.

“For streaming?” she said. “Yes. Obviously. It’s what we’ve all been waiting for.” She turned her glass round on the table. “Suzu’s chat was screaming. You could see his ears, Curse. His model’s ears. Moving.”

“So?”

“So I’m on hiatus.” She smiled at him, and it wasn’t quite a smile. “Because he’s here.” She bumped Milli’s shoulder with hers. “I’m not going to spend two months that he’s here in an egg.”

“No,” said Cursiffe. “That’d be stupid.”

“That’d be stupid,” said Milli.

They were quiet for a bit. A man at the next table was singing something to his daughter, very badly, and she was laughing at him.

“And then,” said Cursiffe, gently, “he goes home.”

Hana didn’t say anything.

That was the thing about Cursiffe. He noticed the thing. It was why she’d always loved him on calls. He’d let a conversation go on for an hour, and then put his finger on exactly the place it had been going round.

Then he goes home.

Two months from now, she’d stand at Changi again, at the other end, at the gate. Then the long flight, and then she’d be in her flat with the new towels and the list on the fridge with everything crossed off. He’d be in his, seven hours behind, putting his headset on. Then the calls at strange hours again, and the counting of time zones, and the waiting. And somewhere a long way in front of both of them, one day, which nobody had ever been able to tell her the length of.

She looked down at her phone, face down on the table by the satay. She didn’t turn it over.

“It’s two and a half thousand dollars,” said Milli, slowly. He wasn’t looking at her. He was looking at the satay with the face he made when he was doing a sum.

“I know.”

“Each.”

“I know.”

“Each,” said Milli again, and then went quiet, and the sum went on behind his face for a long time.

“What?” said Hana.

“A return flight,” said Milli. “Amsterdam to Singapore. At Christmas. I looked, when I booked this one. It was more than an egg.” He looked up at her. “One return flight. And that’s one visit. Ten days, if we’re lucky. Two weeks.” He put his hands flat on the table on either side of the satay. “An egg is every night.”

Nobody said anything.

“Every night,” said Milli, “for as long as the egg lasts. Same place. Same town. Same room. I could—“ He stopped. He was not, as a rule, a man who said the thing first. He’d always left that to her. “I could finish my day and lie down and you’d be there. Not on a screen. There. I’d feel the air. You’d be standing in it.”

Hana couldn’t say anything at all.

“That’s not a game,” said Cursiffe quietly, after a while. “That’s a door.”

They called Jared from the table, because it was Sunday morning in America and he’d want to know. He picked up on the fourth ring, from somewhere that looked like a kitchen, in a hoodie, holding a mug, blinking.

“Alice,” he said. “Y’all are at a hawker centre. Without me.”

“We’re at Lau Pa Sat,” said Hana. “We got fifty sticks.”

“Fifty.” Jared looked deeply wounded. “Milli, my man. You made it.”

“I made it,” said Milli. “It’s like breathing soup.”

“I told you. I told you.” He took a sip of whatever was in the mug. “What’re we talking about? You all got the face.”

“The egg,” said Cursiffe.

“Oh, the egg.” Jared leaned back. “Yeah. I been watching Ice. Everybody’s been watching Ice. That swamp is the most peaceful thing I’ve seen all month, and I work in—“ He stopped. “Doesn’t matter. Work’s crazy. I can’t even stream right now, I’m not gonna lie in an egg.” He looked at them, the three faces round the little phone. “Y’all thinking about it, huh.”

“After,” said Hana. “After the two months.”

Jared was quiet for a second. Then he nodded slowly, the way he did when he’d understood something without needing it explained.

“After,” he said. “Yeah. Okay. Makes sense.” And then, because he was Jared, “Y’all better not go without me.”

“We won’t go without you.”

“And what about Leaka?” said Jared. “She’s in Ice’s chat every single day. Every day. At like three in the morning her time. She wants it so bad, man. She said it once, when everybody was talking about the price. Just once. Then she changed the subject.” He drank from his mug. “You know she can’t.”

They knew. Aleaka had said it once, in the group chat, the day the price came out: haha well that’s not happening this year, with a crying-laughing face. Then she’d changed the subject. She’d been in Ice’s chat every single day since, at all hours, typing in capitals about moonbells, and she’d never said it again.

“If we’re doing it,” said Hana slowly, “we’re not doing it without her.”

“Nope,” said Jared at once. “Nope. If we’re doing it, Leaka’s doing it. I’ll go halves.”

“Halves,” said Hana.

“Halves,” said Jared. He raised his mug at the camera. “Don’t tell her yet. She’ll say no. She’ll say no about nine times.”

“She’ll say no about eleven times,” said Cursiffe, who knew her too.

“Then we’ll ask twelve,” said Jared, and grinned, and the call cut out for a second as somebody walked between Hana’s phone and the Wi-Fi of the whole of Lau Pa Sat. When it came back he was saying, “—gotta go, I’m late. Eat a stick for me. Love y’all.” And he was gone.

Cursiffe flew home on Wednesday.

They took him to the Botanic Gardens on Monday, early, before it got hot, which was a lie because it was hot at seven. They took him to East Coast on Tuesday, and he ate chilli crab and got it on his shirt and didn’t care. On Wednesday morning they took him to Changi. Hana didn’t jump on anybody’s back. She hugged him at the barrier, properly, and he hugged her back. He said, “Write in pen,” and she said, “I will,” and meant it.

Then he went through, and turned at the last moment, and lifted one hand, and was gone.

Milli stood beside her and watched the place where he’d been.

“He’ll get one,” said Milli.

“A pod?”

“He’s been watching too. I saw his face when you said the ears.” Milli put his hands in his pockets. “He’ll wait to see if we do. Then he’ll get one.”

On the way out they passed the screen again, the size of the side of a bus. It had gone black between the perfume and the banks. Rain, a wooden room, a door opening onto somewhere bright.

Where are you?

They stopped and watched it all the way through. When it had finished, Milli said, very quietly, “Somewhere else, for two months.”

Hana took his hand.

She did one thing that night, after he’d fallen asleep on the sofa with the fan on him and the television showing a cooking programme in Mandarin he didn’t understand a word of. She opened the ELSEWHERE website on her phone, and found the page with the pod on it, white and smooth with its smoked-glass lid.

US$2,499. Ships in: 6–8 weeks (high demand).

Six to eight weeks. She did the sum. She was not as fast at sums as Milli, but she was methodical, and she always got there. Six to eight weeks from tonight was the end of January. The end of January was the end of two months.

She didn’t buy it.

She bookmarked the page. Then she took the fountain pen Cursiffe had given her and her notebook out of her bag, and opened the notebook to the page that said TWO MONTHS. At the bottom, under Talk about one day, in dark blue ink this time, she wrote:

After: the egg. Both of us. And Leaka.

She looked at it for a long time. It was the first thing she’d ever written in that notebook in pen.
