Chapter 302

The Loudest Thing in Masmark

3,327 words · 14 min read

Hyv had said she’d be the loudest thing in Masmark, and she was.

She’d been screaming for fourteen minutes. She hadn’t stopped. She’d screamed when the swamp guy came out onto the east end of the bridge. She’d screamed at the first tock, when Lucan’s sword met his in the middle and stopped it. She’d screamed THAT’S ONE when Lucan put the pommel into his shield arm. She’d never in her life imagined anybody could do that to him. She’d screamed at the four minutes of nothing, which was the hardest. Because you couldn’t scream at nothing, and she had anyway, on and off, short, at the stream and the gulls and the sky. Half of Masmark had been holding its breath, and she hadn’t been able to.

Nobody had told her to stop. Rafi, beside her, at the front of the front, had looked at her once and grinned and started screaming too.

She held the rail with both hands and screamed.

She’d never once been loud about anything she loved, for most of her life. And now she was standing at the front of a terrace in front of half of Masmark, screaming at the man she loved. She was screaming at him to get hit less, and to hit back, and at Lucan to get up, and at both of them to keep going.

It was the best she’d ever felt.

Then the swamp guy went first again, and it was the rain.

She knew it the moment it started. She’d seen it a hundred times. In the Seven Below, in the cellar under the netloft, on the stream, in the north tunnel against Delvers in the dark. It was what he’d brought out of the war: three cuts that weren’t meant, from three sides, and then the fourth, meant, from wherever you weren’t looking. It came down on whoever was in front of him like weather.

She saw Lucan see it.

That was the thing she did. She saw everything. She saw the swamp guy’s shoulder start the first cut. And at the same moment, three paces off, she saw Lucan’s eyes. They didn’t go to the shoulder. They didn’t go to where the first cut would land, or the second, or the third. They didn’t go a breath ahead to the fourth, the way Sketch read the rain, the one after, off Val’s page.

They stayed on the swamp guy. On where he was.

The first cut came. Lucan didn’t move for it. It wasn’t meant. It went past his ear. The second, past his ribs. The third, low, past his knee. He didn’t move for any of them. He stood in the middle of the bridge on time, looking at where the swamp guy was. And the swamp guy was in the middle of the fourth, the meant one, coming from Lucan’s left, from wherever he wasn’t looking.

He was looking. At where the swamp guy was.

Tock.

Hyv screamed so loudly that Kestrel, at the end of the rail, a little apart, turned her head.

“He’s not reading it,” said Kestrel.

She’d come along the rail. She hadn’t meant to, Hyv thought; she’d just come, the way people did when they needed to say something to somebody. She stood a pace off from Hyv with her bow on her back. She was looking down at the bridge with her calm, unreadable face, which wasn’t unreadable now.

“He’s not reading the rain,” said Kestrel. “Sketch reads the one after. Val’s page. Lucan’s not reading anything.” She was quiet. “He’s just looking at where the tank is. And the tank’s always where the fourth one comes from. So he’s always there.”

“He learned that,” said Hyv. “From Sketch. From the clip. From Suzu sitting down.”

“I know,” said Kestrel. “Ves told me.” She watched Lucan meet the fourth cut again. Tock. “Everybody’s got everybody’s.”

“GET HIM,” screamed Hyv. At the bridge. At both of them. She didn’t know which.

Kestrel looked at her sideways.

“Which one?” she said.

“BOTH,” screamed Hyv.

And Kestrel of VANTA, who’d stopped deciding things a year and a half ago and never once been loud about anything in six years, put both hands on the rail. And screamed.

“GET HIM,” screamed Kestrel.

Linnet, on the other side of her, stopped looking at the gulls.

It went on. Twenty minutes. Thirty.

Hyv saw all of it. She’d always see all of it; that was what she was. But she’d never once seen anything like this.

The swamp guy chose everything. Every distance, every exchange. The river and the rain and the river again, far and close, first every time. And Lucan let him. He didn’t try to get there first. He didn’t try to get round. He stood in the middle of the bridge and looked at where the swamp guy was, and put his sword there. About one in two, now, instead of one in three. Hyv could see him getting better at it as she watched. A man learning to be on time in front of half of Masmark, faster than anybody should be able to learn anything.

And the ones that didn’t meet, came through. Both ways.

She screamed at every one. Every hit, both ways, the way she’d said she would. When the swamp guy’s sword went across Lucan’s shoulder she screamed YES. And when Lucan’s pommel came in short under the shield and took the swamp guy in the ribs, she screamed NO and then GOOD and then GET HIM BACK. Then, because she’d seen Lucan’s face as he did it, GOOD, LUCAN.

She didn’t know why. She liked him. She hadn’t thought she would.

On the north rail beside her, Rafi had stopped screaming and was just watching, with his mouth open. Gabe, behind them, had his hands on his head. Drew, further along between Ash and Kite, was watching their feet.

“His feet,” said Drew. Hyv heard him. He wasn’t loud; he never was. But she heard everything. “Lucan’s feet. They’re not moving. They’re pointing at him.”

“So?” said Ash.

“They’ve never done that,” said Drew. “In a fight. Ever. Not once.”

At thirty-four minutes, Lucan did the river.

Hyv didn’t see it coming. That was the first thing. She saw everything, and she didn’t see it, and it frightened her so much she stopped screaming for a breath.

He’d been standing still for thirty-four minutes. On time. Letting the swamp guy choose everything. And then, with no warning she could see, he went. A breath ahead. His own river, the one he’d been his whole life. The one the swamp guy had learned off him from a terrace in a month, which Sketch had learned off the swamp guy, which everybody had now.

Except it wasn’t the old way. He wasn’t early. Hyv could see it, once it was happening. He wasn’t going to where the swamp guy would be. He was going to where the swamp guy was, and being there a breath after. On time. A river that arrived exactly when it got there.

The swamp guy had made room for it. Hyv saw that afterwards, going back over it: half a step, a breath before, a gap he’d left on purpose. The way he did when he wanted to see what somebody would do with an opening. He just hadn’t known what Lucan would do with it. Nobody had ever done this with it.

Twice. Across the shield arm and across the back of the leg, one after the other, from a man who’d been standing still a breath before.

Hyv screamed. Not words. She didn’t have any.

Behind her, Gabe roared DEC, the way he did when something happened to Ice that Gabe couldn’t stop. Rae put her hand on his back, at no paces, without looking.

The swamp guy went back. Not far. Two paces, along the bridge. He stood there and looked at Lucan.

Hyv saw his face.

She’d been frightened, for a breath. She stopped being frightened. His face was doing the small sudden thing, and then not the small one. All of it. The face of a man who’d just been given the one thing he’d never once been given in two and a half years, by anybody. He’d been surprised.

“Again,” said the swamp guy. Out loud. On the bridge. Hyv heard it over the stream. Not to anybody. To Lucan. As if asking for a song.

Lucan didn’t smile. He didn’t look up. But Hyv saw his feet, pointing at the swamp guy, flat on the planks. And she knew, the way Drew would have known, that if he’d been anybody else he’d have been laughing.

At forty-one minutes, the swamp guy brought the weight.

Hyv saw him decide. She always did. A tiny thing, in his shoulders, before he moved, that nobody else in the world could see. Not even him, she thought. The river stopped. The rain stopped. He planted his feet on the planks in the middle of the bridge, three paces from Lucan. He brought the shield in close and the sword back, low and long, behind him.

And held it.

The Weighted Cut. The thing he’d learned off Salty in a cellar, off a man who’d always swung things like they were too small. You start it. It ends where you end. The thing that had taken Lucan across this bridge into the parapet last July. BOOM. And again. Seventeen per cent.

The terraces went silent. Everybody had seen the clip.

Hyv stopped screaming. She couldn’t help it. She held the rail and looked at Lucan, three paces off, at fifty-one per cent, looking at the swamp guy holding the weight.

Last July, Lucan had gone into it. Early. Mid-turn into a beat that never came.

He didn’t go into it now.

He planted his feet too.

On the planks, three paces off. Flat, pointing at the swamp guy, the way Drew had said they did. He brought his sword back. Low and long, behind him. And held it.

The slow one.

Four hundred and fourteen falls. And then one he didn’t. And then thirty-two days.

The two of them stood in the middle of the bridge over the third stream, three paces apart. Both planted. Both with their swords back, low and long, held. Like two people about to swing the same door from opposite sides.

Nobody on the terraces breathed. Hyv couldn’t have screamed if she’d wanted to. Kestrel, beside her, had gone completely still. On the parapet at the east end, Ves had put down her slate.

The swamp guy smiled. All of it.

Hyv saw it. Of course she did. Three paces from a man holding the slow one at him, the first man in two games to hold the swamp guy’s own weight back at him. And he smiled. The way he’d smiled at Xav against a wall. It’s the best part.

Then they both started.

Not swung. Started. She saw both of them do it, at the same moment. The swamp guy’s sword coming up off the floor of the bridge, slow, so slow you could see it the whole way. And Lucan’s, coming the same way, from the other side. Slow. Heavy. On time.

They met in the middle.

BOOM.

It went round the gorge like a bell. Off both walls, off every terrace, off the water. Hyv felt it in the rail under her hands and in her teeth. She saw both of them go. The swamp guy back, along the bridge, off his feet, across the planks into the east rail. Lucan back, the other way, into the west. Both of them. At once. Like a door slammed from both sides.

For one breath, nothing.

Then half of Masmark made a noise Hyv had never heard a crowd make. Not a cheer. Not a scream. A sort of roar that went up out of the gorge into the sky and kept going.

She was screaming. She found out afterwards. She didn’t remember starting. She was screaming his name. Not the swamp guy, not Ice. His other name, the one only Gabe and the poly lot used, that she’d never once said out loud in here. In front of half of Masmark.

She didn’t care.

He got up first.

Of course he did. He always would. He came up off the east rail onto one knee on the planks and stopped there, at forty-nine per cent. And didn’t get up any further.

Hyv saw why. She saw everything.

He was waiting. On one knee, in the middle of the east end of the bridge, with his shield on his arm and his sword on the planks. Looking along the bridge at the west rail, where Lucan was lying on his back with his sword beside him, not moving.

He was waiting for Lucan to get up.

“LUCAN,” screamed Hyv.

She didn’t know she was going to. It came out of her. At the man who’d just put the swamp guy across a bridge with his own weight.

“LUCAN. GET UP. HE’S WAITING FOR YOU.”

On the west rail, Lucan moved.

He got up. Slowly. One hand on the rail. On to one knee, then on to his feet, at twenty-nine per cent, with his sword. He stood at the west end of the bridge and looked along it at the swamp guy on one knee at the east end.

He didn’t look up at the terrace. He didn’t look at Hyv, who’d screamed at him to get up. He looked at where the swamp guy was.

The swamp guy stood up.

Not before. After. Once Lucan was up.

The two of them walked back to the middle of the bridge. Slowly, both of them, the whole width of the gorge watching. Three paces off. And stopped.

On the parapet at the east end, Ves held her slate up, so the terraces could see it, for the first time. In chalk, in her neat hand:

Hits: tank 23 · Lucan 17 Minutes: 58 Looked up: no

Then she put it back on her knee.

Kestrel was crying. Hyv saw it. Very quietly, at the rail, a pace off, without moving her face. She didn’t wipe it.

“He held it,” said Kestrel. “The slow one. Against the tank’s own.” She was quiet. “Nobody’s ever done that.”

“Nobody’s ever been on time,” said Hyv.

She was looking at the swamp guy. Three paces from Lucan in the middle of the bridge, at forty-nine per cent, with his shield up. He was looking at Lucan. And she could see his face from the north rail, the way she could see everything.

He was happier than she’d ever seen him.

Happier than at the long table on the last night of April, when he stood up and said she’s very loud and I love it. Happier than on the bench in Singapore, out there, when she’d pushed him back against it and gone first. He was standing on a bridge in front of half of Masmark at forty-nine per cent. Against somebody who’d learned to hold his own weight back at him. And he was so happy that she could see it from the terrace, in the dusk, in the whole of his face.

He’d found somebody who could make him use all of it.

She’d watched him look for that for two and a half years. In everybody. Kestrel, Xav, the seven class fights, a thousand people at the gate. Never quite. Xav had come closest. He’d drawn with him.

This was the first time she’d seen him look at somebody and not know what they’d do.

“He doesn’t know,” said Hyv. Out loud. Not screaming. To Kestrel. To nobody. “He doesn’t know what Lucan’s going to do.”

Kestrel looked at her.

“That’s never happened,” said Hyv. “Not once. In two and a half years. Not even Suzu. He knows where Suzu won’t be.” She was holding the rail very hard. “He doesn’t know anything.”

And then she saw it.

In the middle of the bridge, three paces off, the swamp guy moved his sword. Not into anything. He just turned it in his hand, a little. And tapped the flat of it, very lightly, against the rim of his shield.

Tik.

Small. Nobody on the terraces would have heard it over the stream. Hyv heard it. She heard everything. She’d heard that sound on the rim of a fountain at New Year, calling a lion’s steps a breath ahead. And on a hill in the still water, the boom on a shield so nine people would pull at once. And in the round market, twenty on the ropes.

Tik.

Tempo.

She saw what he was going to do. All of it, the whole shape of it. She went cold and then hot right up to her horns.

She didn’t say anything.

She didn’t call it. She didn’t scream. She held the rail in both hands and looked at the bridge and kept it. Because it wasn’t hers to call. Because he’d go first, and she’d watch, and Lucan would see it when it came. And because, for the first time in two and a half years, she wanted to find out with everybody else what happened next.

Tik.
