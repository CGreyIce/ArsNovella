Live from Elsewhere · Volume 4: The Ninth Heron

Chapter 113

Second

1,959 words · 9 min read

Kestrel didn’t waste things. Not time, not words, not arrows, not the half-second at the top of a draw when everybody else let their breath go. She’d been like that for as long as she could remember. It was why she was good.

It was also, she sometimes thought, why she had five people she trusted completely and nobody she’d call at three in the morning.

At five past one on Friday night, by the clock in the corner of her eye, she was standing in four inches of seawater at the bottom of a drowned well under a salt marsh. She had a short bow at full draw and seventy-four thousand people watching. In front of her, the thing they’d been killing for two days was at nine per cent.

[ The Brine Warden · Lv 34 · 9% ]

“Brann, left,” said Kestrel. “It’s going to swing left. Ottie, he’ll need it in two. Mags, under. Ren, on my shot.”

They did it. They always did it. That was what she paid them for, and what she’d spent six years teaching them. Brann put his tower shield where she’d said, and the great barnacled arm came round exactly where she’d said, and hit it. Ottie’s heal landed on Brann a second before the arm did. Mags went under the arm with her spear. Ren’s fire came down on Kestrel’s shot.

Eight per cent. Seven.

And then the window came.

It didn’t come from the fight. It came from everywhere. It came up in front of all five of them at once, white and gold, over the Brine Warden and the black water and the barnacled arm. It came up in front of seventy-four thousand people on her stream and, she knew without needing to be told, in front of every single person in the world who was standing in an egg.

[ WORLD FIRST
The Long Watch has been cleared for the first time in Elsewhere.
It is the first door of the second rank to be cleared in this world.
Cleared by: Moonlight Harvest
Ice · Kai · Suzuyunn · Kotori · Leveti ]

[ This message has been shown to every traveller in Elsewhere. ]

Brann looked up.

Brann never looked up. He was the best shield she’d ever had, in this game or the last three. He looked up now, for one second, at the window. In that one second the Brine Warden’s other arm came round low, under the shield, and took his legs out from under him.

[ Brann · 22% ]

“Ottie,” said Kestrel.

“I’ve got him—“

“Mags, under. Ren, now.” She loosed. Her arrow went into the Warden’s eye, the soft dark one in the barnacles that it only showed when it swung, and it reared back. “Read it later. Read it later. It’s still at seven.”

They read it later.

It took them six more minutes. Six minutes, four inches of seawater, one more heal from Ottie that Kestrel would remember for a long time. Then Kestrel’s last arrow, at full draw, at the half-second at the top where everybody else breathed out. The Brine Warden went down into the black water of the well, slowly, all of it, and didn’t come up.

[ The Saltwell has been cleared. ]

That was all. No gold. No first. No shown to every traveller. Just a line of plain white text, the kind you’d get for clearing a cellar of rats, hanging in the air over five people standing in seawater at the bottom of a well.

Nobody said anything.

Kestrel stood in the water and read her chat. She read part two twice. She didn’t laugh. It was close.

“Huh,” she said.

She didn’t watch it until four in the morning.

She streamed for another hour, because that was the job. She said the things you said. Good clear, proud of the team, Ottie’s heal, Brann’s legs, congratulations to Moonlight Harvest, properly. She meant all of it. Then she turned the stream off and lay in the egg in her room with the lid up and opened his VOD.

She’d watched every one of his VODs since December. She’d never told anyone. She’d told her team she watched the big ones, for the fights, which was true. She hadn’t told them she watched the rest. The plank, the fen, the twelve hours of nothing on a narrow walk in the dark. The walk home. The ring, which was always dark. A man who never once flinched, and never once explained why, until a woman with two curved knives asked him on a moor in February, and he told her. Because if the front flinches, everybody flinches. She’d thought about that a great deal.

She watched the Long Watch from the beginning.

She watched a boy with long silver hair stand in front of stone figures in a long dark gallery and take the lid off a jar. She watched him say numbers out loud. She watched him do it for an hour, one after another, while things came out of the dark for the jar, and he didn’t shake, and he didn’t stop.

She watched the round hall. She watched Ice walk out into the middle of it, alone, and stand in front of something ten times his size. He stood in front of it every time it moved, for forty minutes, while the boy went round the walls. He took every blow on the shield on pain fifty, she knew he was on fifty, everybody knew. Down to fifty-one. Down to nineteen. Up again. Down again. Never once going down. Never once making a sound.

She watched the head mod count it. Lights home: 61 of 81. She watched his chat say he knows exactly where every single one of those landed.

She watched the thing kneel.

She watched the boy take the lid off and say go on.

She watched the light go gold.

Then she rewound it. Not to the end. To the start. To the bit before the door. Because there was something she’d heard, and hadn’t understood, and needed to hear again.

We’re going in, Ice had said, at the foot of a grey hill, in the mist, with his stream just on. It’s Suzu’s tonight.

And his chat, underneath, the night before, and the night before that. She went back through the VODs. Wednesday: they’d gone in, found something, and come out after an hour. Thursday: in again, and out again, the same. Nobody on his stream had known why. Nobody had asked. They’d come out, and sat on the grass by the door, and not said anything. And Kestrel had watched it on Thursday night between her own pulls at the Brine Warden and thought, they’re slow. They’re stuck. We’ve got them this time.

They hadn’t been stuck.

She went back to the round hall on Thursday night, the first time. To a tall quiet man at six per cent on the floor of a gallery, saying something to the ceiling she couldn’t hear. And then, from somewhere off the edge of the picture, the same quiet voice, clearer:

It’s not a race. Not if it’s his lights.

Kestrel lay in the egg in the dark for a long time.

She’d spent six years teaching people to be good. She paid them to be good, every month, properly. She’d offered the archer from the mill roof a seat for the same reason: because she was good. Nobody’s ever told me my eyes were good, the archer had said, on a bridge, in January. I think I want to be wanted like that. More than I want to be good.

Kestrel had kept that sentence, too, the way she kept arrows. She took it out sometimes and looked at it.

She thought about her five. Brann, who’d looked up. Ottie. Mags, Ren. She trusted every one of them with her life. If one of them had needed to go and sit by somebody’s bed for two days in the middle of a race, she’d have said go, and meant it. And she’d have brought in somebody else, somebody good, to take their place for the two days. Because that was the sensible thing. Because the race didn’t wait.

He hadn’t. He’d sat on a pier with his legs over a gorge and waited.

And he’d still been first.

“Huh,” said Kestrel, to the dark.

She posted at five, the way she always did. One line.

Second. Again. Congratulations, Moonlight Harvest. You weren’t even racing. That’s the annoying part.

It went round the world in about four minutes. She didn’t watch it go. She opened her messages instead, and found his name, and looked at it for a while.

She’d met him once. In a city with no name, a week ago, at the bottom of a flight of steps. A girl with horns had just hit him twice in the arm. She’d said I’ve wanted to do that since December, and he’d said you can, and she’d decided not to.

She typed two words.

[ Messages · Ice

]

It was five in the morning where she was. She didn’t know what time it was where he was. Late, or early, or both.

He answered in under a minute.

[ Messages · Ice

Ice: your arrow in its eye at 7%. that was very good
]

Kestrel looked at that for a long time.

He’d watched. Of course he’d watched. While a boy sat on the floor of a blue hall with an empty jar in his lap, and his own arm was a single long ache, and forty-one thousand people were typing world first. Somewhere after all of that, before he slept, he’d gone and found her stream, and watched six minutes of seawater, and seen the arrow.

Good hands, a girl with wings had said to her once, giving her a drawing on a bridge.

She reached inside her coat, where she kept it. A drawing of her standing on an island in a river at dusk, with her hand lifted. It’s not a making one. It’s just a drawing. She looked at it in the dark of the egg by the light of her messages.

[ Messages · Ice
Kestrel: thank you
]

She didn’t say anything else. Neither did he. She lay in the egg with the drawing on her chest and the message open, until the light came up in her window. Somewhere in the world, a man who never flinched was probably asleep. On a hill at the top of it, a lantern was lit and facing north, and somebody else had got there first.

She found, to her surprise, that she didn’t mind as much as she’d expected to.

She minded a bit. She was Kestrel. She’d mind for a week.

Next door, she thought, and went to sleep.
