Live from Elsewhere · Volume 1: Moonlight Harvest

Chapter 7

Sdn. Bhd.

3,347 words · 15 min read

The first real money he ever made in ELSEWHERE arrived at twelve minutes past three in the morning, while he was asleep.

He found it at half past six, lying in bed with his phone held over his face, the way you check your phone when you’ve only half decided to wake up. The companion app had a little gold dot on its icon. He’d never seen it before. He opened it, and then he sat up.

[ ELSEWHERE · Exchange
Sold: Silverleaf (Rare · Quality: Good) ×1
Price: US$14.00 · Exchange fee (10%): US$1.40
Paid to your Exchange wallet: US$12.60 (RM 52.92)
Buyer: CL_Procurement_07 ]

He’d listed it last night, just before he fell asleep, almost as a joke. One silverleaf from the bottom of his pack, the best one, the one that had come up out of the warm sand with its whole root and a window that said Good. He hadn’t listed a moonbell, never a moonbell, Xav was right about that. But silverleaf grew in other places, he’d checked. There was a forum post from somebody in a beginner town in the mountains, and a stream clip from a woman in a forest town who’d found two. It couldn’t be traced back to one ring of stones in one fen.

He’d put it up at fourteen dollars because that was the number in his head when he typed it, and gone to sleep. Somebody had bought it at three in the morning.

Fifty-two ringgit and ninety-two sen.

He looked at it for a long time.

It wasn’t a lot of money. It was a nice lunch, or half a tank of petrol, or two months of a streaming subscription. He’d made far more than that, years ago, in a single afternoon, from a market in a game about guns. But that had been luck, and he’d always known it was luck. This wasn’t. This was a plant he’d picked with his own hands, twist and lift, in a game he’d been playing for two days. Somebody, somewhere, had wanted it badly enough to pay real money for it at three in the morning.

CL_Procurement_07. He didn’t know who that was. The 07 bothered him. It meant there were at least six others.

Under the number there was a button.

[ Withdraw to bank ]

He didn’t press it.

He lay back down and looked at the ceiling fan going round, slowly, in the grey early light. He thought about the button, and about fifty-two ringgit, and about a line of text in a chat window on Monday morning: cant afford it lol. ill watch. He’d fallen asleep thinking about how, and somewhere in the night, between the silverleaf selling and the dawn, his brain had gone on thinking about it without him.

If this was going to be money, it was going to be done properly.

Not in his personal account, not in a drawer. It was going to be a company, with books and a secretary and tax and all of it. And a company could do things a man in a spare room couldn’t. A company could employ people.

He sat up again.

He found her on the internet at a quarter to eight, in the time it took to drink a coffee.

He’d typed company secretary Malacca and got a long list of websites with stock photos of handshakes. Then, halfway down, a single plain listing with no website and no photo, just an address on a street off Jonker Walk and a phone number and forty reviews. Thirty-nine had five stars. One had one star, from somebody who’d written three angry paragraphs about being made to resubmit a form.

Under it was a reply from the business.

Dear Sir, the form was resubmitted because the date on it was in 2031. We wish you every success in the future, but would prefer not to file from it. Regards, V. Ong.

He laughed out loud in his kitchen and rang the number.

She picked up on the second ring. She had a low, level voice, the voice of somebody who had already been at her desk for an hour. He said he wanted to incorporate a private limited company, and she asked what the business was, and he said it was a bit unusual, and there was a short pause.

“Come at ten,” she said. “Bring your IC.”

The old town at ten on a Wednesday morning was hot and nearly empty.

He parked by the river, where the boats went up and down with tourists in them on weekends and nobody in them today, and walked in. Jonker Walk was quiet on a weekday, the stalls shut, the shophouses with their tall wooden shutters half open and their red lanterns hanging still in the heat. A man was sweeping the five-foot way outside a shop that sold old coins. Somewhere, somebody was frying garlic.

He’d lived in Malacca nearly two years and still came into the old town like a tourist. He liked it. He liked the way the buildings leaned on each other. They’d stood here so long they’d stopped bothering to be straight.

The address was a shophouse two streets back, narrow and deep, painted pale green a long time ago. The ground floor was a kopitiam. Marble-topped tables, wooden chairs, a big slow fan, three old men reading newspapers in three different languages, and an auntie behind the counter pulling kopi from a great height into a glass. She looked up at him as he came in and said, without being asked, “Upstairs, ah? For Vivi?”

“Yes. Ms Ong.”

“Upstairs. Door at the top.” She looked him up and down, kindly, the way aunties do. “You want kopi? I bring up.”

“Kopi O, kurang manis, please. Thank you, auntie.”

She nodded as if he’d passed something.

The stairs were steep and narrow, dark wood worn pale in the middle of each step. At the top there was a door with a small brass plate screwed into it, polished so often the letters had gone soft.

ONG SECRETARIAL SERVICES EST. 1989

He knocked.

“Come in.”

The room was long and cool and full of paper.

Old wooden filing cabinets ran down one wall, the kind with brass handles and little card labels in brass frames. A tall window at the far end looked out over the street through half-closed shutters, and the light came through in slats across a big teak desk that had obviously been there longer than the woman sitting behind it. On the wall behind her was a framed photograph of a man in his fifties, with her eyes and a different nose, smiling at someone off camera. On the desk, a laptop, a phone, an orchid in a blue and white pot, and three perfectly squared stacks of documents.

“Mr Declan,” she said, and stood up, and held out her hand.

She was about his age, give or take, small and very neat, in a plain white blouse, with her hair tied back and reading glasses pushed up on her head. She had the kind of face that didn’t give anything away unless it decided to. Right now it hadn’t decided.

“Vivian Ong. Please. Sit.”

He sat. She sat. She put her glasses on and looked at him over them anyway.

“You said on the phone the business is a bit unusual,” she said. “Tell me what it is, and I’ll tell you whether it’s unusual.”

He told her.

He tried to do it in the right order. A game came out on Monday. You lie down in a pod and it puts you somewhere else. It’s very real. In the game there’s money, gold, and things you can find or make, and the company that runs the game has a market, the Exchange, where you can sell those things to other players for real money. Real dollars. The company takes a cut.

“Like a casino,” said Vivian.

“No. Nothing’s random. You go and find something, or make it, and somebody who wants it pays you for it. Like a market.”

“For things that don’t exist.”

“They exist in the game.”

“The game doesn’t exist.”

“About a million people would disagree with you this morning,” said Ice. “And one of them paid me fourteen US dollars for a leaf at three a.m.”

Vivian looked at him.

He turned his phone round and slid it across the teak and showed her. Silverleaf. Rare. Good. US$14.00. Fee US$1.40. US$12.60. RM 52.92. She read it carefully, all of it, including the buyer’s name, and then she read it again.

“Fifty-two ringgit,” she said, “and ninety-two sen.”

“Yes.”

“Since?”

“Since three o’clock this morning.”

There was a knock at the door. The auntie came in with his kopi O in a glass on a saucer and a glass of something cold and pale for Vivian, and put them down, and looked at the two of them, and left without a word. Vivian didn’t take her eyes off him the whole time.

“And you would like,” she said, when the door had closed, “to incorporate a company to do this.”

“Yes.”

“A private limited company. Sendirian Berhad.”

“Yes.”

She pulled the laptop toward her. “Name?”

“Moonlight Harvest Workshop.”

She typed it without comment. Her fingers were very fast. “Directors?”

“Me.”

“Only you?”

“Only me. I live here. I’m the resident director.” He’d read that at two in the morning: a Sdn Bhd had to have at least one director whose principal place of residence was in Malaysia. “Shareholder, also me. One hundred per cent.”

“Malaysian-owned.” She typed. “Paid-up capital?”

“Two hundred and fifty thousand.”

The typing stopped.

She took her glasses off. She folded them. She put them down on the desk next to the orchid, very precisely, and looked at him across the slats of light.

“Mr Declan,” she said. “You can incorporate a Sendirian Berhad with one ringgit of paid-up capital. Most people your age do. A company that has, at this moment, fifty-two ringgit and ninety-two sen of revenue, which it doesn’t technically have yet because it doesn’t exist yet, does not need two hundred and fifty thousand ringgit of paid-up capital.”

“It does,” said Ice, “if it wants to employ somebody from Singapore.”

He watched her face. For the first time since he’d walked in, it decided something.

“The Expatriate Services Division,” she said slowly.

“For a company that’s a hundred per cent Malaysian-owned, the band’s two hundred and fifty thousand. Before they’ll even register you to apply for an Employment Pass.” He shrugged. “I read a lot last night.”

“So I see.” She looked at him a bit longer. “And the company would need premises. A real office. With a licence from the council. Not your living room.”

“I’ll find one this week.”

“And a bank account in the company’s name, which will take a week, perhaps two, because the bank will want to know what the company does, and you’ll have to explain it to them the way you’ve just explained it to me, and they will look at you the way I am looking at you now.”

“I’m getting used to that look.”

“And an Employment Pass,” she said, “since the first of June, under the new rules. Category Three at the lowest. That’s a basic salary of at least five thousand ringgit a month, taxable here, for your friend from Singapore. Every month. Not counting allowances.”

“Yes.”

“From fifty-two ringgit.”

“From fifty-two ringgit and ninety-two sen,” said Ice. “Today.”

She looked at him for a long moment more. Then she picked up her glasses and unfolded them and put them back on.

“Is your friend a shareholder?” she said, typing again. “If he is, you’re not a hundred per cent Malaysian-owned, and the band goes up.”

“No. He’s an employee. If he says yes.” He hesitated. “And there’s another friend, also Singaporean, who’ll work with us from Singapore for now. He’ll be paid as a contractor. Not a shareholder. For the same reason.”

“Good,” she said, which was the first time she’d said it. “That’s the right way round.” She typed. “Your friend’s full name, for the file?”

“I’ll send it to you.”

She looked up. “You don’t know your friend’s name?”

“I know his name. He doesn’t know he’s being employed yet.”

The typing stopped again.

“Mr Declan,” said Vivian Ong. “You are setting up a company, with two hundred and fifty thousand ringgit of paid-up capital and fifty-two ringgit of revenue, to employ a man in another country, who does not know about it.”

“Yes.”

“May I ask when you intend to tell him?”

“Soon,” said Ice.

She did it all in forty minutes.

He watched her do it, and he understood after the first five why she had thirty-nine five-star reviews and one very angry one. She didn’t waste a single movement. The name search on MyCoID: Moonlight Harvest Workshop Sdn. Bhd., available, reserved, fifty ringgit, while he sat there. The incorporation lodged after it, with the thousand-ringgit fee and his details, his IC scanned on a little machine on the corner of her desk. She told him the certificate would come in a day or two “if SSM is in a good mood”, and in a week if it wasn’t, and that it was usually in a good mood on a Wednesday.

“And you’ll need a company secretary,” she said. “Within thirty days of incorporation. It’s the law.”

“Will you be it?”

“I’m already typing my name in the box, Mr Declan. The law gives you thirty days. I don’t need them.”

She explained the rest while she typed: the bank, the premises, the paid-up capital, which he’d put in once the account was open. The ESD registration after that, with the bank statements and the tenancy and the licence. Then the pass for his friend. She wrote it all down for him too, on a single sheet of paper in small square handwriting, numbered, with dates against each line, and slid it across the desk.

He folded it and put it in his pocket. It was the first piece of paper anyone had given him in two years that he actually wanted to keep.

At the door, he turned round.

“Can I ask you something?”

“You can ask.”

It was what Sedge said. He almost laughed.

“Do you think it’s stupid?”

Vivian Ong considered the question. She took her time. She looked at the photograph on the wall behind her, of the man with her eyes, for just a second, and then back at him.

“I think,” she said, “that it’s a company built on a video game, and I think it’s a phase. Most things are a phase. My father set this office up in 1989 and people told him a secretarial firm in the old town was a phase.” She picked up the next document on her stack and squared it with the others. “But I think if it’s a phase, Mr Declan, it’s going to be the best-filed phase in Malacca.”

It wasn’t quite a smile. But it was close.

He went downstairs and finished his kopi at one of the marble tables. It had gone cold, so he ordered another, and sat in the slow air of the big fan and looked out at the street.

The auntie came over with the second glass and put it in front of him and stood there with her hands on her hips.

“Vivi help you?”

“She helped me a lot.”

“She very clever, that girl,” said the auntie, with great pride. “Since small. Her papa also clever.” She leaned closer. “What business?”

He thought about how to explain it to an auntie in a kopitiam in the old town at eleven on a Wednesday morning.

“Gardening,” he said.

The auntie looked deeply unconvinced, nodded anyway, and went back to her counter.

He looked at shoplots that afternoon. Online, at least, on his phone at the kopitiam table, scrolling slowly. There were a lot of them, all over Malacca, little two-storey units in rows in the newer parts of town, ground floor and upstairs, for rent at prices that seemed fine. He booked three viewings for Friday. One was in Melaka Raya, a ten-minute drive from home. He looked at the photos of it for a long time: an empty ground floor with white tiles and a roller shutter, and a back room with a high window, and an upstairs with two more rooms and a little bathroom.

The back room would take two pods.

He was in the game by two, which in Fernwick was the middle of the fen’s third night.

Deston and Xav were waiting for him inside the ring. They’d been fighting at the edge of the old fen all morning without him, and they were both nearly level four, and very pleased with themselves. Deston had worked out that he could keep Accord going by humming between lines. It cost him stamina, but it held. Xav had worked out that leeches were slowest at the moment they came out of the water, before they’d decided which way to go. He’d killed six without being bitten once and wanted Ice to know about it in some detail.

“How did the admin go?” said Deston.

“There’s a company.”

“Already?”

“There’s going to be. By Friday, she says. If SSM’s in a good mood.”

“Who’s she?”

“My secretary,” said Ice, and found that he liked saying it a great deal.

They fought until the window told him to rest. He got to level five about an hour before it did. That was a long, ugly fight at the edge of the deep water: four leeches at once, pulled to a narrow place in the reeds, with Deston’s voice steady behind him and Xav’s blades going in where the bodies bent. The Outnumbered line was on every kill.

That night, before bed, he opened the Exchange wallet again.

US$12.60. Fifty-two ringgit and ninety-two sen.

[ Withdraw to bank ]

He still didn’t press it.

There would be a bank account in a week or two, with a company’s name on it. The first fifty-two ringgit that company ever earned would go into it, and he wanted it to be this. One silverleaf, picked in warm water under the moon. He wanted it on the first line of the first page of the books Vivian Ong was going to keep so beautifully.

He closed the app and put the phone face down on the pillow.

Then he picked it up again, and opened his messages, and found the thread that said Suzu, and looked at the last message in it for a while. It was his own. soon.

Not yet, he thought. Not until there was an office. Not until he could say there’s a pod and a room and a contract and not there’s a plan.

He put the phone down. This time he left it there.
