Live from Elsewhere · Volume 10: The Middle

Chapter 253

Set One to Watch

3,594 words · 16 min read

Suzu sat by the flat stone all day, in the game’s day, waiting for dusk.

Not the real day. Out there it was night in Malacca, Friday, the first of December. He’d gone in at eight in the evening like always and come out of the dark on the hill, inside the ring. Twelve stones. The watch still lit in the middle of the ring of nine burned-down candles, exactly as tall as he’d left it. And the game’s day, here, past the edge, was the same as its night. Nothing.

But the game kept its clock. Dawns and dusks, three a day, the way it always had. Whether there was a sun or not. And the second dusk was at twenty past eight.

Isa had said dusk.

So he sat by the flat stone, with the black case on his back. The others round him in the ring, quiet, not doing anything, and he waited for twenty past eight.

He’d known Isa ten months, nearly.

He’d carried her chair onto the north steps every morning since April. Put the jar in her lap, and her root hands round it. Sat on the step below. He’d made her tea with Kotori. Listened to her tell him what the hill was for. So it knows where the middle is. He’d said I’ll take it, on a stool, without deciding. And then, in August, decided. This winter.

He’d never asked her what it would be like. The lighting. What you did.

He’d asked on the last night, on the north steps, at dusk, with the box of candles and the ninety-four lamps leaning up the road.

“What do I do?” he’d said. “When I’m there. At the stone.”

She’d looked at him for a long time with her bright black eyes.

“Put it down,” she’d said.

He’d waited.

“That’s all,” said Isa. “Put it down on the stone. Open it. Say the words. And then sit with it.” She’d folded her hands. “That’s all any of us ever did. Every dusk. For three hundred years. You put it down, and you say here’s the middle, and you sit with it.” She’d looked up the road. “It’s not hard. It’s just every day.”

At ten past eight, Kotori made tea.

She did it on the little fire Slep had made, inside the ring, out of nothing much. She’d been making tea on it every night since they came up the hill. She used the water from the still water, which Isa said tasted of nothing. She boiled it very slowly, on the little fire, in the pot she’d carried for three weeks. And put in the dark leaves from the waystones. One of the eleven tins, the fourth.

She poured nine cups.

Then, after a moment, a tenth. She put it on the edge of the flat stone. Beside where the jar would go.

Suzu looked at it.

“For her mothers,” said Kotori. Very quietly. “There’s never been tea on the hill. Isa said. In three hundred years.” She sat down. “Somebody should bring some.”

Nobody said anything for a long time.

“My mother would have hated it,” said Suzu. Isa’s line, from the north steps.

Kotori laughed. It came out wet.

Twenty past eight.

He knew without looking at the clock in the corner of his eye. He felt it. The way you feel the end of a song coming, or a step in the dark you know is there. Dusk. Somewhere, out past the nothing, past the still water and the three hundred stones and the edge, past the road, a sun was going down over a round town.

He stood up.

He reached up behind his head with both hands to the latch on the top of the black case. The one that only opened from above, so it couldn’t come open by accident on a rope in the dark. Ina had made it. Nine nights at the forge, asking a bar of black iron to hold a light without liking it.

It will hold it until somebody opens it on the right stone.

He opened it.

The hood came back. And nothing came out. Not yet. The jar was still in there, in the black, glowing through its clay where nobody could see. He reached in with both hands, carefully, over his shoulder, and took it out. And brought it round in front of him.

The jar.

Plain clay, the size of two hands cupped together. With a lid. Tamsin’s. Larkhollow had made it, for the Long Watch, a long time ago. A jar from Larkhollow remembers keeping. And on Christmas Eve, a year ago, in the banner room, he’d opened it in front of an old woman in a chair and said it can be home for a bit. And she’d said go on. And three hundred years had gone in.

It was glowing.

Not much. Through the clay, very faint. The way it had glowed on the long table in front of Isa’s chair. In his pack before that. And in the black case for three weeks, where nobody could see.

In the nothing, past the edge, it was the brightest thing in the world.

He heard them all breathe in.

All eight. Round the ring. Because for three weeks there’d been nothing to see but candles, and now there was this. A faint gold glow, through clay, in his hands. And it lit things. Not much. A little. More than a candle. It lit his hands, and his arms. And the flat stone in front of him, grey, with the worn crown on its top. And the tenth cup of tea on the edge of it, steaming.

He put it down.

On the flat stone. In the middle of the crown. Where the lamp had been for three hundred years and hadn’t been for ten months.

It sat there, glowing through the clay.

He took the lid off.

He’d thought about what would happen. For three weeks, on a rope. For ten months, on a stool. He’d thought there’d be a window, the way there always was. A sound, maybe. Something.

There wasn’t.

The light came out of the jar.

Not fast. Not all at once. It came up out of the open neck of the jar the way smoke comes up out of a chimney on a still day. Slow, straight, gold. And it didn’t stop at the jar’s mouth. It went on, up, into the air above the flat stone, a little way. And stood there. A flame without a wick, without oil, without anything under it, standing straight up in the air over a clay jar on a stone with a crown cut in it.

Three hundred years. Ten months in a jar. Three weeks in the dark.

It stood there.

And everything round it came out of the nothing.

The flat stone first. Grey, round, the worn crown on its top. Then the ground round it. Grit, and moss, and nine burned-down candles in a ring, and Slep’s little fire. Then the twelve stones. Grey and tall and rough, all the way round, leaning in. Then the people.

He saw them. All eight. For the first time in three weeks. Not lit from underneath, faint, by their own candles. Seen. All of them, at once, in the gold light from a flame standing over a jar.

Kotori by the fire with a pot in her hands, her face wet. Hako, standing, with his spear, his mouth open. Xola and Slep. Thea and Gigi, close. Hyv.

Hyv was looking at everything.

He’d never seen her face like that. Not on any stream. She was turning, slowly, where she stood, in the middle of the ring. Looking. At the stones, and the ground, and the people, and the sky above them. The sky, which wasn’t nothing any more. It was dark. Just dark. Ordinary, black, night-dark, the kind with depth in it. The kind with edges, because the light had made edges.

She turned all the way round, once. Then she stopped, and looked at Ice.

Edge to edge.

“I can see,” said Hyv.

Suzu didn’t look at the others for long. He looked back at the flame.

It was standing over the jar. Straight. Gold. Not leaning. Not toward anything. For ten months, on the long table and on the north road and in the dark, everything had leaned toward wherever it was. Lamps, and stones, and the dark itself, all leaning, looking for the middle.

It didn’t lean. It was the middle. It stood straight up, and everything leaned toward it.

He felt it happen.

Outside the ring. In the still water. In the nothing round the hill, that had been nothing for three weeks. And past it, all the way out, as far as anything went. The dark, which had been leaning for ten months. Looking. Getting heavier, gathering, coming up out of the still water at a hundred and fifty stones, leaning on a ring of twelve.

It stopped looking.

He felt it stop. The way you feel a room go quiet when somebody who’s been searching for something sits down, finally, because they’ve found it. A long breath, out, everywhere at once. Like the lake at the bottom of Masmark settling when the crown went out.

The dark knew where its middle was.

He’d forgotten the words.

He realised it, standing at the flat stone. He’d been saying them every dusk on the north steps for a month, and every morning on the rope, and on the hill every night. Here’s the middle. Not yet. He’d forgotten them. Standing there with the flame over the jar, and everything come out of the nothing round him, he couldn’t remember them at all.

“Suzu,” said Kotori. Very gently, from the fire.

He looked at her.

She didn’t say them for him. She just looked at him. The way she’d looked at him in the Long Watch at the eighty-first lantern, with his hands shaking.

He looked back at the flame. And they came.

“Here’s the middle,” said Suzu. Out loud. To the dark, all round, outside the ring. To the still water, and the three hundred stones, and the nothing that was just dark now. To three hundred years of women on this hill. “Not yet.”

And then, because Isa had said it was the other half:

“Set one to watch.”

And eight people, round the ring, in the light, said:

“So the rest may sleep.”

That was all the window said. Two words.

It was enough.

He sat down.

On the ground, beside the flat stone, the south side, out of the wind. Where the bowl and the blanket and the empty black jar and the stick were. Where Isa’s mother had sat. And her mother. And Isa, on her own, for a long time.

He didn’t decide to. His legs just went. He sat with his back against the flat stone and his hands in his lap. And the flame standing over the jar above his head, gold, and the light coming down round him.

Sit with it, Isa had said. That’s all any of us ever did.

He sat with it.

He didn’t know how long. A long time. The others didn’t come near him. They stayed round the ring, in the light, looking at each other. Hyv was looking at everything. Thea and Gigi were sitting against the third stone with their shoulders touching. Hako had sat down where he was, on the moss, and put his face in his hands. Xola was crying into Slep’s coat, and Slep had his arm round her, looking at the flame over her head, not saying anything.

Ice didn’t come near him either.

Ice was standing at the north side of the ring. Facing out. Of course. With his shield on his arm and his back to the flame. Looking north, at the dark, which was just dark now, with edges.

He didn’t need to look at the flame. He knew it was there.

Kotori came, after a while. With the tenth cup.

She’d taken it off the edge of the flat stone. It was still warm. It remembered being near a light. She knelt down beside him on the moss and put it in his hands.

“It’s for her mothers,” said Suzu.

“I know,” said Kotori. “They’d want you to have it.” She sat down beside him. “You’re one of theirs now. Isa said.”

He held the cup. He didn’t drink it.

“I put it down,” he said.

“You put it down.”

“That’s all,” said Suzu. “That’s all she said to do. Put it down. Open it. Say the words. Sit with it.” He looked at the cup. “I thought it would be harder.”

“It was,” said Kotori. “It was three weeks on a rope. And three hundred stones. And a shape in the water at a hundred and fifty.” She looked up at the flame. “That was the hard part. This is the part after.”

He drank the tea.

It was horrible. It was the horrible one. It went down him like something lit.

“Thank you,” said Suzu.

“You’re welcome,” said Kotori, and didn’t say sorry. And leaned her shoulder against his, by the flat stone, on the hill, in the light. And didn’t move.

He didn’t cry. He’d thought he might. He didn’t. His face did something, Kotori told him later. She said it was the thing it did, very small, the way the light used to do through the clay. Except the light wasn’t in the clay any more. It was out, standing over the jar, for everybody to see.

She said his face was like that now too.

Ice came at ten. Round the flat stone, from the north side, slowly. He sat down on the moss on Suzu’s other side, the side Kotori wasn’t, with his back against the stone. With his shield on his knees.

He didn’t say anything for a long time. Nor did Suzu. They sat there, the three of them, against the flat stone on the hill, with the flame standing over their heads.

“I told Vivian,” said Ice, at last.

Suzu looked at him.

“On the eleventh day,” said Ice. “When she asked why I wanted a foreigner on the payroll. Somebody who’d need a pass. I said I need him.” He was looking at the shield on his knees. “She wrote it on a card. I’ve never told you.”

“I know,” said Suzu. “Vivian told me. On profit day. She said he said I need him and then she looked at me over her glasses and didn’t say anything else.”

Ice was quiet.

“I’m telling you,” he said.

Suzu didn’t say anything.

“I need you,” said Ice. “I’ve needed you since the wind game. Since the guild I dragged you into and you never asked why. I needed you in the fen and at the bottom of the lake and in a guest room in Malacca. And I never said.” He looked up at the flame. “I’m saying. You carried it. Three weeks. Three hundred stones. You went first. You said no to me on the two hundred and sixteenth.” Something happened at the corner of his mouth. “Nobody says no to me. You did it right.”

Suzu looked at him for a very long time.

He thought about a bus station on the eleventh day, and a contract signed the same afternoon. A guest room. Not zero. Eighty-one lanterns. A jar from Larkhollow. A stool beside an old woman’s chair.

“Good,” said Suzu.

It was the only word he’d ever needed. It meant everything it had ever meant. And it meant I know. And me too. And you’re slow.

Ice laughed. Quietly, against the flat stone, under the flame.

“You’re slow too,” he said.

“I know,” said Suzu.

The letters came at about eleven. They hadn’t come for three weeks. Nothing had, past the edge. Letters were letters, but Ice said maybe the nothing had been too much nothing for them to find their way through.

They came now. All at once. A great many of them, in the corner of everybody’s eye, one after another.

Suzu didn’t read his for a long time. Then he did.

He read the last one several times.

Then he read Kei’s again. Then she asked to be carried up to the roof.

He looked up. Past the flame standing over the jar. South. Across the ring, past the twelfth stone, past the still water and three hundred stones he couldn’t see. Past the edge and the road and ninety-four lamps standing straight. To a round town, and a roof, and a bench by the eye with Somewhere on it.

She couldn’t see it. Not from there. It was too far. Isa had said, on the stair, on the way up to the roof, in August, when he lights it, I’ll see it from here. And he’d thought she was being an old woman.

He didn’t think so now.

“Not tonight,” he said. Out loud. To the flame. To her. “The longest night. You’ll see it then.”

The flame stood straight up over the jar and didn’t lean toward anything at all.

At a quarter to eight in the morning, they didn’t make the ring.

They didn’t need to. There was a ring. Twelve stones, standing. And in the middle, the watch. Not a short thick candle with a star on it. The middle. A flame without a wick, standing over a jar on a stone with a crown on it, that hadn’t gone out in three hundred years.

Suzu stood by the flat stone.

“Set one to watch,” he said.

“So the rest may sleep,” said eight people.

And very far away, at a long table in a round town, sixty more. And one old woman, on a bench on a roof, carried up two hundred and twelve steps by Tiger and Maigo at the second dusk. Who’d ask to be carried up every dusk now, Kei would write. Every one, till the longest night.

The egg took him at eight. The last thing he saw was the light, over the jar, standing straight up. And Ice at the north side of the ring, facing out, with his back to it.

He didn’t need to look. He knew it was there.
