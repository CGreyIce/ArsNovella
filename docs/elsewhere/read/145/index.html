Live from Elsewhere · Volume 6: There's a Chair

Chapter 145

The Other Side of the Road

3,703 words · 16 min read

Slep had never been very good at talking. He’d made his peace with it a long time ago.

Other people seemed to think it was a problem. Xyro thought it was a problem; Xyro talked the way other people breathed, and couldn’t imagine a person who didn’t. Tif thought he was shy, which was kind of her and also wrong. PenguChi was the only one who’d never said anything about it. Slep suspected that was because PenguChi was the same, only from the other direction. She didn’t talk because she was watching. He didn’t talk because he was waiting.

That was all it was. Waiting. Most things that needed saying got said, if you waited long enough, by somebody. Usually the right somebody. And if they didn’t, if nobody said it, then you said it. Once, plainly, and then you went back to waiting.

The game had worked that out about him in a week. He was still a bit annoyed about that. He’d had years and years of people not working it out.

[ Class: Second
One who takes the other side. ]

He’d laughed when it came up, at Millrace, in the shallows, with Xola reading hers over his shoulder. Tidewalker. One who goes where the water goes. And his, under it, like a footnote. He’d thought: well. That’s about right.

They went through Fernwick on Sunday at the first dawn, because Thea wanted to see Sedge.

Slep liked Fernwick. He’d liked it from the first night, coming in at dusk off the river road with Patience at his heels and the whole Landing lit up with lamps on poles. Herons on every roof. A town built on stilts over black water, where everybody said mind the step and meant it. The kind of place that had been the same for a very long time and didn’t see why it should stop.

Sedge was at her door when they came down the boardwalk. Of course she was. Slep had worked out that she was always at her door when people were coming, and never when they were going.

She looked at the six of them, and the sheep, and the packs.

“You’re going east,” said Sedge.

“We’re going to the round town,” said Thea.

“Hm.” Sedge looked at her for a long moment, the way she looked at everything, as if it were a pot that might or might not be ready. “The round one. Where the old woman writes it in.”

“Ama Corrie.”

“I know her name. I’ve had letters.” Sedge sniffed. “From the boy, mostly. Rafi. He writes like he’s frightened the paper’s going to run off. And one from her. Just one. Short.” Something happened in Sedge’s face that Slep couldn’t read. “She wrote my mother’s words into her book. The old book. In the margin, she said. So the rest may sleep. She said, tell her it’s kept.” Sedge was quiet a moment. “Nobody’s ever written anything of my mother’s in a book.”

Nobody said anything. Thea had her hand on her shield-strap, very still.

“Wait there,” said Sedge.

She went inside. They waited on the boardwalk. Patience looked at the water under the planks with deep suspicion. Xyro looked at the herons on the roofs and said they’re watching us and Tif said they’re herons, that’s what they do.

Sedge came back out with something long under her arm, wrapped in sacking and tied with string. She put it into Thea’s hands.

It was a broom head. A big one, bound tight, made of something pale and fine that Slep didn’t recognise until he touched it.

“Fen reed,” said Sedge. “Cut in the autumn, when it’s dry. There’s nothing sweeps like it. Not in the round town. Not anywhere. They’ve got stiff brooms in the round town, I’d bet on it. Birch. Birch is no good under a chair; it catches.” She folded her arms. “You give her that. You tell her Sedge heard it’s kept. And you tell her fen reed’s better for under the chairs, and she’s to use it on a Sunday and think of a fen. And you tell her—“ She stopped.

“Tell her what?” said Thea gently.

“Nothing,” said Sedge. “She’ll know.” She looked at Thea again, longer. “You were the one on the jetty. In May. The singing.”

Thea went pink.

“I heard it from my door,” said Sedge. “Half the Landing did. Nobody said anything. That’s Fernwick. We don’t say anything when it’s good.” She turned round and went back inside, and the door shut behind her.

They stood there.

“Is she—“ said Xyro.

“She doesn’t watch people go,” said Thea quietly. “Ice told me. She watches for them coming back.”

Slep looked at the shut door. He thought he understood Sedge, a bit. She was a waiter too, of a kind. She just did it facing the other way.

The road east out of Fernwick ran along the dykes for half a day, between the reed beds. Then it climbed a long slow rise onto what the map called the Levels, and went flat.

Properly flat. Flat in a way Slep hadn’t known land could be. He’d woken in the game on a cliff with sheep on it, and walked three and a half days down through hills to get to Xola, and he’d thought he knew what a landscape was. The Levels were a landscape somebody had ironed. Grass to the edge of the world in every direction, with the road ruled across it in a straight line. Every mile or so there was a single tree, as if somebody had put it there so you’d know you were moving.

“Flat roads make you lazy,” said Thea, at the top of the rise, looking at it. “Sedge said that. To Ice.”

“Then we won’t be lazy,” said Xola.

They set off. And without anybody saying anything about it, they fell into the order they always fell into.

Thea in front. Always, now. Shield on her back, hammer at her hip. She walked the way she fought, which was the way Jessamy had taught her: not braced, not planted. Ready to give a little and turn.

Xola at her right shoulder, half a pace back. Close enough to touch. The trident over her shoulder like a fishing rod, which, to be fair, it sometimes was.

And Slep on Xola’s other side.

Not next to Thea. Next to Xola. Xola was next to Thea, and he was next to Xola. He’d thought about that, at Millrace, the first time they’d all walked anywhere together. He’d thought: Thea’s at the front, and Xola’s on Thea’s side, and somebody has to be on Xola’s. Nobody else had noticed there was a gap there. He’d noticed. He’d filled it.

Behind them, PenguChi at the very back with the long spear, walking slower than everyone and somehow never falling behind. Xyro and Tif in the middle, Xyro talking and Tif saying mm at the right places. And Patience, wandering from one side of the road to the other, eating things.

“Eight days,” said Xyro, about an hour in. “How far is eight days?”

“Eight days,” said PenguChi, from the back.

“I know, but like. In miles.”

“In miles it’s further,” said PenguChi.

The hounds came at the second dusk on the first day, out of the long grass on the left. They came low, the way things on the Levels did. There was nowhere to come from except low.

[ Moor Hound · Lv 31 ] ×5

Slep saw them first. He usually did. Not because he had good eyes; Hyvred had good eyes, everybody knew that. He saw them first because he was always looking at the side nobody else was looking at. Thea looked forward. Xola looked at Thea. Somebody had to look left.

“Left,” said Slep.

That was all. One word. Thea turned on it without asking, the shield coming round off her back and down in one movement, which she couldn’t have done in April. The first hound hit the shield and she Turned it, right, into the space where Xola was.

Xola wasn’t there.

Xola had gone left. She’d seen the second hound coming behind the first, low in the grass, going for Thea’s legs while the shield was busy. And she’d gone for it without thinking, the way she always went toward Thea without thinking. And the first hound, Turned, went into the space she’d left.

It was coming at Thea’s back.

Slep was already there.

He didn’t think about it either. That was the thing about taking the other side: you didn’t have to think, because you’d already done the thinking. You’d done it on every road, every day, for ten weeks. When Xola goes, where’s the gap? When Xola goes left, who covers right? He’d worked it out so many times that his feet had stopped asking him.

The glaive came round long and low. It took the hound across the shoulder in mid-leap and put it in the grass.

“Thank you,” said Thea, without looking, already Turning the third.

“Mm,” said Slep.

PenguChi’s spear went past his ear, very close, into the fourth hound’s throat. She’d been holding it for twenty seconds, at the back, while everyone else did things.

[ Sentinel · Patience · held 22 s ]

Xyro’s arrows went out and came back. Tif rang Kindle on Xola a second before the second hound’s teeth closed on her arm, and the teeth took a seventh where they’d have taken a third.

And the fifth hound, the biggest, the one that had hung back in the grass watching, picked its moment. It went for the softest thing in the whole party.

Which was Patience.

Patience was standing in the middle of the road eating a thistle. She saw the hound coming. She looked at it, chewing. And then, with no warning at all, she put her head down and ran at it.

The hound hadn’t expected that. Nothing had ever expected that. A sheep, a ewe with a black face and opinions, going full tilt at a Lv 31 Moor Hound with her head down. She hit it square in the chest. It went over backwards into the grass, more from astonishment than damage.

Then Thea’s hammer came down on it.

There was a long silence on the Levels.

“Did the sheep just—“ said Xyro.

“She’s always been like that,” said Slep.

“She’s a sheep.”

“She might be,” said Slep. He went over and looked at her. Patience looked back at him, chewing, very pleased with herself. There was a little window over her head that hadn’t been there before, faint, the way windows were for things that weren’t players.

[ Patience · a ewe · Brave ]

“Huh,” said Slep.

They camped that night under one of the single trees, because it seemed rude not to.

Thea took the first watch. She sat with her back against the trunk and her shield across her knees, looking east. Slep, lying by the fire with his glaive beside him, could hear her talking.

Not to anyone. Just to herself, under her breath, in bits. She’d stop and start again. Sometimes she’d say a few words and then shake her head and say different ones. He couldn’t hear what. He didn’t try to. He knew what she was doing. She was practising. She was trying to find the first thing she’d say, when she got there and the woman with the hawthorn staff was standing in front of her.

He lay and listened to her try for an hour, and didn’t say anything.

Xola was beside him, not asleep. He could tell; her gills were moving the way they did when she was thinking, a little flutter at the edges.

“She’s practising,” whispered Xola.

“Yeah.”

“She’s been doing it since Brackenford. In the lodging-house. In the bath. I heard her in the bath.”

“Yeah.”

Xola turned over to face him in the firelight.

“I’m still angry,” she said. “Sometimes. It comes back. I’ll be fine, walking along, and then I’ll think about the chair, and it all comes back, all at once. Like a wave.”

“That’s allowed.”

“I know it’s allowed. I just don’t like it.” She looked at the fire. “I said I’d stand next to her. In the town. When it happens. I said I’d be so close she couldn’t lean anywhere without leaning on me.”

“I heard.”

“What if I’m angry? When it happens? What if I see her and it all comes back and I—“ She stopped. “What if I make it worse?”

Slep thought about it. He always did.

“Then I’ll be on the other side of you,” he said. “And you can lean on me. And Thea won’t have to hold you up as well as herself.”

Xola looked at him for a long time.

“That’s very good,” she said. “Did you think of that just now?”

“No,” said Slep. “I thought of it at Millrace.”

She laughed, very quietly, so as not to wake the others, and put her forehead against his shoulder, and went to sleep.

Over by the tree, Thea had stopped practising. Slep looked across, and she was looking at the two of them. She smiled, a bit, and turned back to the east.

On the second day it rained. Real rain, the kind the game did very well, cold and sideways across the Levels with nothing to stop it. The road turned to mud and the grass lay down flat and silver.

Xyro was miserable, loudly. Tif was miserable, quietly, with her cat ears flat to her skull and water running off the ends of them. She made everybody tea at the noon dusk under a bit of oilcloth that Slep held up with the butt of his glaive. It was Kotori’s recipe. It made you breathe slower. It didn’t make you any drier.

“This is the worst day of my life,” said Xyro, holding his cup.

“You said that about the channels,” said Tif.

“The channels were the worst day of my old life. This is the worst day of my new one.”

PenguChi was sitting on her pack in the rain with her hood up. She didn’t seem to mind it at all. “It’s just water,” she said. “It’s very slow water.”

Xola was in it, of course. She’d stopped wearing a cloak about an hour in and was walking along beside the road in the flooded ditch with her gills open. She was having the time of her life. “It’s like walking in a river that’s lying down,” she shouted.

Slep watched her and felt the thing he always felt, watching Xola be happy, which didn’t have a word that he’d ever found.

And Thea walked at the front through all of it, with the rain running off her shield, and didn’t complain once. Every so often she said something quietly to the east.

The footbridge came on the third day.

There was a river across the Levels. A real one, wide and brown and fast, swollen with the rain. The road went over it on a bridge that someone, a long time ago, had built for carts, and someone more recently had let fall down. All that was left was the footbridge beside it. Two ropes and a line of planks, swaying over the brown water, with gaps between the planks you could see the river through.

[ The Wester Ford · footbridge · Fair repair ]

Thea went across first, testing every plank. Then Xola, who didn’t need the bridge at all, and kept saying so, and went across it anyway to stay near Thea. Then Tif, holding the ropes with both hands. Then Xyro, saying I’m fine I’m fine I’m fine the whole way. Then PenguChi, very slowly, not touching the ropes at all.

Then Slep, and Patience.

Patience got to the first plank and stopped.

She looked at the gaps between the planks. She looked at the brown water going past underneath. She looked at Slep.

“Come on,” said Slep.

Patience planted all four feet and made a noise that said, very clearly, no.

“You walked down a cliff,” said Slep. “You walked three and a half days. You knocked over a Moor Hound.”

Patience said no again.

On the far side, five people were watching. Slep could feel them watching. He didn’t look.

He crouched down in the mud at the end of the footbridge, in the rain, so his face was level with the sheep’s. “You don’t have to,” he said. “If you don’t want to. You can stay this side. Somebody’ll come along.”

Patience looked at him.

“But I’m going over,” said Slep. “Because she’s over there.” He tipped his head at the far bank without looking at it. “And I go where she goes. That’s my whole thing. So you can stay. Or you can come.”

The sheep didn’t move.

So Slep did the only thing he could think of, which was to pick her up.

She was heavy. Much heavier than she looked, and she looked heavy. Wet wool and a lot of opinions. He got her in his arms, all four legs sticking out, her black face next to his ear, breathing. Then he stood up and walked across the footbridge with her. One plank at a time. Not holding the ropes, because he hadn’t got a hand free. The bridge swayed and the river went past brown underneath and the rain came down.

He didn’t look at the water. He looked at the far side, where Xola was.

Halfway across, Patience stopped struggling. Three-quarters of the way, she put her chin on his shoulder and sighed.

He stepped off the last plank onto the far bank and put her down in the mud.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Xola started laughing. Not the little laugh she’d been doing for three days, the one with an edge on it. The real one. The big one, the one from before. She laughed so hard she had to sit down in the mud, holding her trident, with the rain running down her face, and her gills fluttering, and she couldn’t stop.

“You carried her,” said Xola. “You carried the sheep.”

“She didn’t want to come,” said Slep.

“So you carried her.”

“She was going to stay on her own.”

Xola laughed harder. Tif was laughing too, behind her hand. Xyro said that’s the best thing I’ve ever seen, I’m going to tell everyone, I’m going to tell CHAT. PenguChi didn’t laugh, but her eyes did.

And Thea, at the front, was looking at Slep with an expression he couldn’t read. Then she looked at Xola, laughing in the mud. Then back at Slep.

She didn’t say anything. She just nodded at him once, the way you nod at somebody who’s done a thing you couldn’t have done yourself.

He took the third watch that night with Thea, because Xola had fallen asleep the second she lay down, worn out from laughing. Somebody had to.

They sat with their backs against a stone wall at the edge of a field, the only thing on the Levels taller than grass for miles. The rain had stopped. The stars had come out, more of them than there ever were at home, in a sky that went all the way down to the ground in every direction.

Thea didn’t practise. She just sat.

“Can I ask you something?” she said, after a long time.

“Yeah.”

“Is she angry at me? Still? For not telling her?”

Slep thought about it.

“No,” he said. “Not at you. She was angry that she wasn’t next to you. For six weeks. That’s a different thing.”

Thea was quiet.

“She thinks she wasn’t enough,” said Slep. “The first time. She stayed on the phone every night and it didn’t stop. You stopped it. She thinks that means she wasn’t enough.”

“She was everything.”

“I know,” said Slep. “You told her that. On the bridge. I heard.” He looked at the stars. “She’ll believe it in a bit. She’s slow about believing nice things. She’s quick about everything else.”

Thea laughed, very softly.

“Can I tell you a thing?” said Slep. That was unusual. He hardly ever said that. He felt Thea notice.

“Yes.”

“When we get there,” said Slep. “To the town. When it happens. When she’s in front of you.” He didn’t say who she was; he didn’t need to. “You’ll have Xola. On your side. She’s always on your side. She’s never been on any other one.” He shifted the glaive. “And I’ll be on Xola’s other side. So she can lean on me if she needs to. And then you won’t have to hold her up as well.”

Thea turned her head and looked at him in the starlight for a long time.

“That’s very good,” she said. “Did you just think of it?”

“No,” said Slep. “I thought of it at Millrace.”

Somewhere behind them, in the grass by the fire, Patience made a small noise in her sleep. Thea laughed again, properly. Then she leaned her head back against the wall and closed her eyes, and didn’t practise anything at all for the rest of the watch.
