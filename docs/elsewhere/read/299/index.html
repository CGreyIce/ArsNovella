Live from Elsewhere · Volume 12: Even

Chapter 299

Not to Watch You

3,204 words · 14 min read

Kestrel had stopped deciding things a year and a half ago, and she’d never once been so sure where she’d be on a Saturday.

On the north terrace, above the bridge over the third stream. At the rail, a little apart. Not watching the tank.

She’d written it to him in April. I’ll be there. Not to watch you. To watch him. She’d meant it when she wrote it. She meant it more now, after a month of first dawns on a bridge watching a man fall over. Four hundred and fourteen times. And then, one evening in June, not.

She stood on VANTA’s terrace on the east rim on the Tuesday at the first dawn with her bow. A gull sat on the parapet four paces off looking at her with one yellow eye. She thought about it while Fenn taught Linnet to look up.

Fenn was teaching. She still couldn’t get used to it.

He stood at the end of the terrace with his bow, nineteen and not as good as the others. He’d called slack water one breath early in a drowned city under the sea and been the sixth name on a world first. And beside him, younger, smaller, with a short bow, Linnet. She looked up. That was why Kestrel had taken her, in April, out of a crowd of forty on the terrace steps who all wanted to be in VANTA because of Saltreach. Thirty-nine of them had looked at Kestrel. Linnet had been looking at the gulls.

“Don’t decide where it’ll go,” said Fenn, to Linnet. “Look where it is.”

Linnet drew. Held. She looked at the gull. Not where it would fly to. Where it was, on the parapet, looking back at her.

She loosed.

The arrow went into the parapet a finger’s width from the gull’s foot. The gull looked at it. Then at Linnet. Then it turned round with enormous dignity and walked off along the parapet, in no hurry at all.

Linnet stared at the arrow.

“How long did that take you?” she said. To Fenn.

“Two weeks,” said Fenn. “And a gull.”

Kestrel, at the other end of the terrace, didn’t say anything. She’d said that, a year ago, to Fenn, about a pigeon. He’d been listening. He always listened to the one before.

She went down to the bridge on the Wednesday to watch Lucan practise.

She’d watched him every first dawn she could since June. The north terrace above the bridge over the third stream was steep stone steps down to a rail. From there you could see the whole span of the bridge and the gorge under it and the stream going down to the lake. The tank had stood at that rail every night last July on his way back from the Undercliffs. Watching Lucan train. Learning the river off him, from a terrace, in a month, while Lucan didn’t know he was there.

It wasn’t empty any more, the terrace.

It hadn’t been empty since June. Sable Court came, mostly, at first, to watch their leader fall over. Then other people. The fish man, from the quay. Two of the Ninefold, who’d come up from the south early. A boy from the netloft door with a big black sword who shouted things down at Lucan from Boss. By the Wednesday of the last week of July there were two hundred people on the north terrace at the first dawn, to watch a man practise.

He didn’t fall over.

Kestrel stood at the rail, a little apart, and watched him. He stood in the middle of the bridge with his sword, and Ves on the parapet with her slate, not counting falls any more. Counting something else; Kestrel didn’t know what. Lucan brought the sword back, low and long. Held it. Then started it. Not swung. Started. And went with it, all the way, the whole of his weight. The slow way. So slow you could see it the whole way.

At the end, where it ended, it stopped. And he was still standing.

The two hundred people on the terrace made a sound. Not a cheer. A sort of breath, let out all at once.

Lucan didn’t look up at them. He’d never once, in the month Kestrel had watched him, looked up at the terrace. He looked at where the sword was. And nowhere else.

“He’s not early,” said somebody beside her.

Kestrel turned her head.

The tank was standing at the rail beside her.

She hadn’t heard him come. Nobody ever did. He was just there, at the rail of the north terrace, where he’d stood every night last July, with his shield on his back. No Hyvred on his arm, for once. Watching Lucan on the bridge below with an expression Kestrel couldn’t read.

“He’s on time,” said the tank.

“I know,” said Kestrel. “I’ve been watching.”

“I know,” said the tank. “You wrote.” He didn’t look away from the bridge. “Not to watch you. To watch him.”

“Yes.”

“Good,” said the tank.

“Don’t,” said Kestrel.

He almost smiled. He didn’t look at her. He watched Lucan bring the sword back again, low and long, and hold it.

“He’s going to be very good on Saturday,” said the tank.

“He’s going to lose,” said Kestrel.

The tank thought about it. Properly. She’d seen him do it on a stream for a year and a half. She’d never once stood next to him while he did it. It took a long time.

“I don’t know,” he said.

Kestrel turned all the way round and looked at him.

“You’ve never said that,” she said. “About a fight. In two and a half years. On a stream, to three hundred thousand people. You’ve never once said you didn’t know.”

“I said it about Suzu,” said the tank. “In May. To Hyv. In a tunnel.” He was watching Lucan. “Nobody heard.”

“And about this?”

“About this,” said the tank. He was quiet. “He’s not early. I’ve never fought him when he wasn’t early. Nobody has.” He looked at Lucan below, standing on the bridge with the sword at the end of the slow one, perfectly still. “I don’t know what he does when he’s on time.”

Kestrel looked at him for a long time.

“That’s why you’re smiling,” she said.

He was. She hadn’t seen it start. All of it. The one from the bridge last July, the clip, LOOK, four million.

“Yes,” said the tank.

“Why are you watching him?” said the tank. After a while. “Not me.”

Kestrel thought about it. She didn’t decide anything; she just thought.

“Because I’ve seen you,” she said. “Everybody’s seen you. You walk into a fight and you’ve already learned the other person, and then it doesn’t matter what they do.” She looked at the bridge. “I fought you in a hall at the top of a long stair. It was like fighting a boss. I said so. You don’t fight it. You learn it, and it doesn’t give you time to learn.”

“I know.”

“I’ve never seen him learn,” said Kestrel. “Nobody had. He was always early. He’d learned it before anybody saw him try.” She was quiet. “And then he spent eleven months falling over in front of people. On purpose. Letting a woman hit him in the ear with a slate.” She looked at Lucan. “I wanted to see what that looks like. When it’s done. Somebody who was early his whole life, learning to be on time.”

“Why?”

Kestrel didn’t answer for a long time.

“Because I did it too,” she said. “Stopped deciding. A year and a half ago. In a ring in a market, with a man with two blades. Sixty-one misses the first morning.” She almost laughed. “Nobody watched me. Except Brann, who doesn’t say anything. And a pigeon.”

The tank didn’t say anything.

“I want somebody to watch him,” said Kestrel. “Who knows what it costs. When he gets there.”

“He’ll know you’re there,” said the tank.

“He never looks up.”

“He’ll know,” said the tank. And went back up the terrace steps toward the netloft, where Hyvred was waiting at the top with her hand held out sideways, not looking, at the height his hand would be.

Aurelie came to the netloft on the Thursday.

Kestrel was there because Brann had wanted to see the cellar, where the tank and Sketch had fought in May last year. Brann didn’t ask to see things, so when he did, you went. They were on the quay in front of the netloft when Meridian’s barge came along the lake shore. One of the three big blue ones with ten rowers each. Aurelie got off it onto the quay, on her own feet, with Vane beside her. Not a pace behind. Beside. And Harlan behind them both, enormous, waving at everybody.

“KESTREL,” shouted Harlan.

“Harlan,” said Kestrel.

“THIRD,” shouted Harlan. “AT SALTREACH. I WENT FIRST. IT WAS FINE.”

“I know,” said Kestrel. “I was there. I was first.”

“I KNOW,” shouted Harlan, delighted, and went to hug Gabe, who’d come out of the netloft to see what the noise was.

Aurelie came along the quay toward the netloft door. Small, in plain blue, walking. She stopped in front of Kestrel.

“Second,” said Aurelie. “At the Bell Above. Six seconds. To him.”

“Third,” said Kestrel. “To both of you.”

“And then first at the Low Tide,” said Aurelie. “And you didn’t decide it.”

“No,” said Kestrel.

“Good for—“

“Don’t,” said Kestrel and Aurelie together. And Aurelie laughed. Out loud, on the quay, which Kestrel had never once heard her do in six years.

“I’ve cut my chair down,” said Aurelie.

They were sitting on the netloft’s step, the three of them, Kestrel and Aurelie and Vane. A sword’s length off from the door. The boy with the big black sword had asked all three of them who’d stand next to you and then let them sit.

“I heard,” said Kestrel.

“Four inches,” said Aurelie. “So my feet touch.” She looked at her feet, on the quay. “It’s the first time in six years I’ve sat at the head of my own table and not been holding myself up.”

“How does it feel?”

Aurelie thought about it.

“Like being on time,” she said. “Instead of early.” She looked along the quay at the bridge, far off, over the third stream. “I sent Harlan to Saltreach. I didn’t go. I sat in the chair. And he came third and wrote IT WAS FINE.” She was quiet. “It was fine. I didn’t know things could be fine without me there.”

Vane, beside her, said nothing at all.

“He said we’re fine twice on the way here,” said Aurelie. “On the barge. On purpose.” She almost smiled. “That’s two more sentences than he needs.”

“I’m practising,” said Vane.

Brann went down to the netloft’s cellar that afternoon, and Kestrel went with him.

It was the reason they’d come. Brann had asked, on the Wednesday, on VANTA’s terrace. He’d said the cellar, the way he said names, so she’d hear it. And she’d heard it. Brann didn’t ask to see things. When he did, you went.

The cellar was low and dark and smelled of fish, and it breathed. You could hear the lake through the walls, a slow push and pull. Like the sea coming back into a drowned city, street by street, in an order. Brann stood in the middle of it with his dented tower shield on his back and looked at the floor for a long time.

“Here,” he said.

“Sketch and the tank,” said Kestrel. “May last year. The third one. Fourteen hits.”

Brann nodded. He looked at the stone floor, at the marks on it, old scuffs and one long scrape where somebody had been put across it by something heavy.

“Are you going to fight him again?” said Brann.

Kestrel looked at him. He’d never once asked her a question. Not in six years, in two games. He said names. He held doors and streets and the sea. He didn’t ask.

“The tank?”

“Yes.”

Kestrel thought about it. She didn’t decide anything; she thought.

“No,” she said. “Not this year.” She looked at the long scrape on the floor. “I’m going to watch. Somebody else.”

Brann was quiet for a long time, in the breathing cellar.

“Good,” he said. Which was Suzu’s. Even Brann had it now.

The terraces filled on the Friday.

Kestrel went up at the second dusk to look. Not just the north terrace above the bridge. All of them. The gorge of the third stream ran down between two walls of the crater’s inner rim, steep and narrow, with the bridge across the middle of it. And on both walls, all the way up, people had cut terraces over the years. Steps, ledges, little balconies off houses, a garden or two. Places to sit and watch the stream.

There were people on every one of them.

Not the stream’s usual people, the fish man and the netloft and Sable Court. Everybody. Meridian in blue, a hundred of them, on the west wall, with Aurelie in the middle in a chair somebody had carried up for her. VANTA, twenty, on a ledge below the north terrace, in black. The Ninefold, seven, in sea-blue, on a balcony off a house, very still. The Open Hand, with Marrok at the front with his hands at his sides. The Tidebound, from Saltreach, forty of them. They’d come up the coast road for it because Marisol said she wanted to see a river, once, that went into a lake instead of the sea. The Quiet Oar, who’d rowed half of them across the lake for nothing.

And Constellation. Fifty-nine. On the north terrace, at the front, at the rail. With a boy from the netloft door at the very front of the front, because he was a door.

Half of Masmark, somebody said. Kestrel didn’t count. Dennis was counting, at the back of the north terrace, with his ledger, twice.

She went down to the end of the bridge before the dark. She didn’t know why. Lucan was there, on his own, in the middle of it. Not practising. Just standing, with his sword point-down on the planks, looking at the stream.

He didn’t look up when she came to the end of the planks. He never looked up.

“You came,” he said. To the stream.

“I said I would.”

“I know.” He was quiet. “I don’t look up. At the terrace. I haven’t once, since March.” He moved his hand on the sword’s hilt. “If I look up I’ll start being early for them. For what they want to see.”

“I know,” said Kestrel. “I’ve been watching.”

“I know,” said Lucan.

They stood there, him in the middle of the bridge and her at the end, with the stream going under.

“Tomorrow,” said Lucan. “Where will you be?”

“At the rail,” said Kestrel. “A little apart. Where I always am.”

Lucan didn’t say anything for a long time.

“Good,” he said. “Then I’ll know where you are. Without looking.”

She went back up the terrace steps in the dark. She didn’t look back at him. She didn’t need to. She knew where he was.

She stood at her place at the rail, a little apart. She’d stood there every first dawn since June.

Linnet was beside her, looking up. At the gulls over the gorge. At the light going gold on the lake below.

“Who’s going to win?” said Linnet.

“I haven’t decided,” said Kestrel.

“You never decide.”

“No.”

Linnet thought about that, looking at the gulls.

“Then how do you know where to look?” she said.

Kestrel looked down at the bridge over the third stream. It was empty, in the last of the dusk. Tomorrow at this time there’d be two people on it, and half of Masmark watching. Most of them would be watching the tank. Three hundred thousand people would be watching through his eyes.

She’d be watching the other one.

“You look at the one who’s learning,” said Kestrel.

Linnet looked at her for a long time, the way the young ones did.

“That’s a good reason,” she said. Which was what Linnet had said herself, on the bridge in June, when she asked Lucan why he kept falling over and he told her. Everybody had everybody’s now, even their own back.
