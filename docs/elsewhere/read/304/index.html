Chapter 304

The Ninefold

3,388 words · 15 min read

Teo had decided every fight he’d ever been in before it started, and he’d never once been wrong.

That was the Ninefold. Nine from the southern isles, the far ones, past Masmark’s south shore, where the sea was warm and the rocks were black and nobody came. They’d started together in a fishing town on the last island, the first week of the game. Nine people who’d known each other out there and been told by the game, on the first morning, that a party was six. They’d said no. They’d said nine. They’d cleared every door in the south as nine, before anybody else in the world had got that far. And they’d never once taken a tenth.

Hundreds had asked. Thousands, maybe, by now. Take me. I’m good. You need a healer. You need a shield. Teo had looked at every one and said we’re nine. It wasn’t pride. It was arithmetic. Nine people who knew exactly where each other would be. You could decide a fight before it started, if you knew where nine people would be. A tenth was a person you didn’t know. A tenth was a fight you couldn’t decide.

They’d never lost a fight they’d decided.

Then, at Saltreach, the sea had come back at the third turn, and two of them had gone out with the water. And they’d come up the cliff stair seven. Teo had stood at the top of the hundred and forty steps and watched a woman in black with a bow sit on the cliff edge, resting. We rest first. She’d gone down on the fifth tide with a boy who listened to the one before. And won.

She hadn’t decided anything. Teo had watched her not decide it.

He’d been thinking about it ever since.

He’d watched the bridge from the balcony. Seven of them, very still. The other two were on the south coast still, back from the turn, waiting. He’d watched seventy-four minutes of the tank and the man from Sable Court. He’d seen something he didn’t have a word for. A man who’d been early his whole life, on time. A tank who didn’t know what the other man would do, and was happier about it than Teo had ever seen anybody be about anything.

He hadn’t watched the end.

He’d been watching the north terrace. A man with long silver hair asleep sitting up against the rail, with an empty cup in his lap. Through the last ten minutes of the best fight anybody in Masmark had ever seen.

Teo had seen the clip from May. Everybody had. HE SAT DOWN. Eleven million. A man with a scythe sitting cross-legged on the floor of a round pale room in front of Sketch of Riverbank. Sketch, who’d drawn with the tank. Sitting down, and then lying down, and then rolling into the place behind Sketch’s heel where Sketch had been a breath ago. Cutting him at rib height. Nobody cut at rib height with a scythe. It was a silly place to cut.

Teo had watched it forty times on the south coast, out there, trying to decide what the man would do.

He couldn’t. Every time he watched it, he decided, and every time the man with the scythe did something else.

He’d never once in his life watched somebody fight and not been able to decide what they’d do next.

So he’d come north. To ask.

He told the six on the Sunday afternoon, on the quay below the netloft, where they’d been sitting in a row since Monday, waiting.

They were the oldest friends he had. Out there and in here. Ofa and Sione, who’d started the game in the same fishing town on the last island with him, on the same morning. Mele, who did the counting. Tavita and Ana, who never spoke in a fight and never needed to. Losa, who’d fallen at Saltreach at the third turn and come back the next day with no day behind her and said again. Seven. The other two waiting on the south coast.

“I’m going to fight him,” said Teo. “Tonight.”

Nobody said anything. They’d known since the bridge. They’d seen where he was looking.

“You’ll lose,” said Mele. Not unkindly. She counted things. “Everybody does. Eleven million people watched him sit down.”

“I know.”

“Then why?”

Teo thought about it. He’d decided why on the south coast, watching the clip forty times. He’d decided it was to find out what the man would do, because he couldn’t decide it.

He found he didn’t believe that any more.

“I want to see what it’s like,” he said. “Not knowing.”

Ofa looked at him. Ofa had known him the longest. Since before the game, since before any game.

“That’s not what you’re going to ask him,” said Ofa.

Teo looked at the lake for a long time.

“No,” he said.

The six looked at each other. Nobody said we’re nine. It was the first time in two and a half years that nobody had said it.

“Go on, then,” said Losa. “We’ll be on the step.”

The boy at the netloft door had said Sunday. Teo had sat on the step for a while on the Monday, with the six in a row in the lane behind him, because the boy said that was the bit people forgot. Then he’d gone away and waited six days. The Ninefold had never once waited six days for anything.

On the Sunday, at the second dusk, he came back.

The boy was in the door. He asked who’d stand next to you. Teo said eight. The boy said that’s still an answer, and let him in.

The man with the scythe was in the cellar.

It was low and dark and smelled of fish, and it breathed. You could hear the lake through the walls, in and out. The man was sitting on the floor in the middle of it, cross-legged, with his scythe across his knees, eyes about a third open. A small woman with two cups stood by the wall. A few others sat on the cellar steps: Sketch, with a sketchbook. A man with a stick and a notebook. A tall woman from Sable Court with a slate, which Teo hadn’t expected. And at the top of the steps, in the dark, where the lamp didn’t reach, somebody. Teo couldn’t see who.

The man with the scythe looked up at him.

“You’re asking,” said Suzu.

“I’m asking,” said Teo. He’d practised it. It came out the way the boy at the door said it, a bit too fast. “Will you fight me?”

Suzu thought about it. Teo watched him do it. Like a cat deciding whether to get up.

“Okay,” said Suzu.

“I’ll call it,” said the man with the notebook, from the steps. “If it goes long.” He had his pencil out. “Until one yields or falls. In the cellar.”

“How long will it go?” said Teo. He didn’t know why he asked. He’d never asked anybody how long a fight would go. He’d always known.

The man with the notebook looked at him.

“I don’t know,” he said. He sounded pleased about it. “I’ve stopped saying.”

“Begin,” said the man with the notebook.

Teo decided.

He always did. He’d watched the clip forty times. He knew the man would sit down. So he decided what to do when he sat: come in low, both spears, at the floor. A man sitting on a floor can’t roll sideways and back the way he did against Sketch if both spears are already in the floor where he’d roll to. Teo decided where the roll would go and put both spears there.

The man didn’t sit down.

He stood there. Three paces off, in the middle of the breathing cellar, with the scythe low. And looked at Teo, about two thirds awake.

Teo’s spears were already moving. Toward the floor, where the roll would have gone if the man had sat. They went into the floor of the cellar with a sound like two nails into a board. Into nothing. Into the place a man would have rolled to if he’d done what he did in the clip.

Teo was bent forward over two spears stuck in a floor, in front of a man who was standing up.

The scythe came round. Not low. Not at the ribs. Gently, at the height of Teo’s shoulders, flat. The way you’d pat somebody on the back.

Teo pulled his spears out of the floor and stepped back. He looked at the man with the scythe.

“You didn’t sit down,” said Teo.

“You were ready for me to sit down,” said Suzu.

He tried everything.

He hadn’t meant to. He’d meant to decide one thing and do it, the way he’d done every fight in his life. But every time he decided, the man did something else. And he had to decide again. And again. Faster and faster. All of it, everything the Ninefold had ever done in the south, laid out in the breathing cellar one decision after another.

The spears together, high and low. The man stepped inside them, too close for either, where it was silly to stand. Shhhhk, short, the snath into Teo’s ribs.

But every time Teo got him, the man was somewhere else after. Nowhere anybody would put him. And Teo had to decide all over again, from nothing.

He’d never decided so many things in a row in his life. He’d never once been so tired.

At about nine minutes, he noticed the man was smiling.

Not much. About a millimetre, at the corner of his mouth. And then more. Teo saw it while he was deciding something, and stopped deciding, because he’d never seen anybody smile in a fight with him. People were frightened in fights with the Ninefold. They’d heard. Nine, never a tenth, never lost a fight they decided. They came in frightened and went out fallen and never once smiled.

The man with the scythe was having the best time of his life.

Teo could see it. All over his face, at about nine minutes, in the breathing cellar. And it made Teo so surprised that, for one breath, he didn’t decide anything at all.

He just stood there, with his spears, looking at the man. At where he was.

And the man, who’d been about to go somewhere silly, stopped. And looked back at him.

They stood there for a moment, in the middle of the cellar. Neither of them doing anything. The lake breathing through the walls.

“Oh,” said Suzu. Very quietly. Pleased. “You stopped.”

“I don’t know what you’ll do,” said Teo.

“Nobody does,” said Suzu. “I don’t either.” He shifted the scythe. “That’s why it’s good.”

It went on for eleven more minutes. Teo didn’t decide anything for most of them.

It was the strangest fight of his life. He’d always fought from a map. Now there wasn’t one. He just looked at where the man was, the way Sketch had in the second bout on the clip, and put a spear there. And sometimes the man was there, and tock, scythe and spear. And sometimes he’d gone somewhere silly, and the scythe came out of it. Shhhhk.

He got better at it. He could feel himself getting better. He’d never once felt that in a fight. He’d always been as good at the start as at the end, because he’d decided it all at the start.

At twenty minutes, the man sat down.

Teo had been waiting for it for twenty minutes. He’d stopped waiting about eleven minutes ago. So when it came, he didn’t do anything. He didn’t put his spears where the roll would go. He didn’t decide anything. He just looked at the man, sitting on the floor of the cellar in front of him, cross-legged, with the scythe across his knees.

And sat down too.

He didn’t know why. It came out of him the way, he’d heard, good came out of everybody in the tank’s guild now. He sat down on the floor of the breathing cellar, three paces from the man with the scythe, cross-legged, with his spears across his knees.

On the steps, Sketch made a noise like a man trying not to laugh.

Suzu looked at Teo, sitting on the floor across from him.

“That’s Xav’s,” said Suzu.

“I watched the clip,” said Teo.

“It worked for him,” said Suzu. “Once.” He thought about it. “It won’t work for you.”

“Why not?”

“Because he did it first,” said Suzu. And rolled.

It was over in about four seconds after that. Teo couldn’t have said how. A roll, sideways and back, along the floor. The scythe came up across both of Teo’s knees from a place he wasn’t looking. Then it was resting along the side of his neck, the way it had rested on Sketch’s in the clip. Gently. Like a hand.

“Yield,” said Teo.

Nobody on the steps made much noise. It wasn’t that kind of crowd. The man with the notebook wrote something down. The woman with the slate wrote something down too. Sketch said rib height very quietly to himself, and drew. The small woman with two cups came across the floor. She gave one to Suzu and one to Teo, who didn’t know what to do with it, and held it.

Suzu took the scythe off Teo’s neck. He didn’t get up. He sat there on the floor, cross-legged, three paces off, with his tea.

“You stopped deciding,” said Suzu. “At nine minutes.”

“You were smiling,” said Teo. “Nobody smiles. In a fight with us.”

“Why not?”

Teo thought about it.

“Because we’ve decided,” he said slowly. “Before they come in. Where they’ll be, what they’ll do, how it ends.” He looked at the tea. “They can feel it. That it’s already over. That they’re just walking through something we’ve already done.” He was quiet. “You can’t smile at that.”

“I could,” said Suzu. “Because you were wrong.”

Teo looked at him.

“You decided I’d sit,” said Suzu. “So I didn’t. You decided where I’d roll. So I didn’t.” He drank some tea. “Everything you decided, I just didn’t do. It was least effort.” He looked at Teo. “And then at nine minutes you stopped. And then you were good.”

“I lost.”

“You were good,” said Suzu. “For eleven minutes.” He put his cup down. “Nobody’s ever been good against you, have they. You never let them.”

Teo didn’t say anything for a long time.

He thought about the south. About nine people who knew exactly where each other would be, and never once took a tenth, because a tenth was a fight you couldn’t decide. About every door in the south, cleared before anybody else got there, decided before they went in. About a woman with a bow on a cliff edge at Saltreach, resting, not deciding. Winning anyway.

“No,” said Teo.

He sat in the cellar after the others had gone up. The man with the scythe had gone to sleep against the wall, sitting up, with his empty cup in his lap. The small woman had put a blanket over him and gone. Sketch had stopped drawing at last. The somebody at the top of the steps, in the dark, had gone too. Teo still hadn’t seen who it was.

He sat on the floor in the middle of the breathing cellar with his spears across his knees and thought.

Nine. Never a tenth. A tenth was a person you didn’t know. A fight you couldn’t decide.

He’d just been in one. With a man he didn’t know, in a fight he couldn’t decide, for twenty minutes. It had been the best twenty minutes of his life. He’d lost, and he’d got better while he lost, and he’d never once got better in a fight before.

He looked at the man asleep against the wall. Long silver hair over one shoulder. Scythe across his knees. Smiling a bit, still, asleep.

Teo had never once in his life asked anybody to join the Ninefold. It wasn’t a thing you did. People asked you, and you said we’re nine.

He sat on the cellar floor until the third dawn, deciding whether to.

He couldn’t decide.

So he stopped trying. And in the grey light, coming down the cellar steps from the netloft above, he found he’d already decided. Somewhere in the eleven minutes, without noticing. The way the man with the scythe said you went where it was least effort.

Tomorrow. At the door. Properly.
