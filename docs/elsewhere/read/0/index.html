Live from Elsewhere · Volume 1: Moonlight Harvest

Prologue

Ten Viewers

2,644 words · 11 min read

The pod had been in the spare room for two days, and Ice still hadn’t decided what it looked like.

Salty had seen one photo and said a fridge, lying on its back. Niko had said a coffin, and then, because Niko was kind, a nice coffin. Aleaka had said it looked like the pod a spaceman wakes up in at the start of a film, right before everything goes wrong, and then she’d sent three crying-laughing faces and asked if she could see it on camera.

Ice stood in the doorway with a mug of coffee going cold in his hand and thought it looked like a very expensive egg.

It filled most of the room. It was white, with soft corners and a long smoked-glass lid, and it sat on a low base that hummed so quietly you only heard it when the ceiling fan was off. Inside was a bed of grey gel that warmed when you touched it and forgot your handprint a second later. By the head end, set into the shell, was a small glass square with a fingerprint printed on it in thin grey lines, the way a hotel lift shows you where the button is. Along the side, in letters so thin they were barely there, it said ELSEWHERE.

It had cost two thousand, four hundred and ninety-nine US dollars, which was a little over ten thousand ringgit, which was more than he’d ever spent on anything that wasn’t the house.

He’d done the maths four times on Friday when the delivery men carried it up the stairs, and twice more on Saturday, and once in bed last night, as if the number might change if he caught it by surprise. It didn’t. It was fine. It was more than fine. Years ago, at the right time, he’d been lucky with a market most people didn’t understand, and then he’d been sensible with the luck, which was rarer. There was enough in the bank to keep this house and keep him in it for a good few years without a job.

A good few years was not forever. He knew that. He thought about it sometimes, late at night, in the same part of his brain where he kept the electricity bill.

But it was enough for an egg.

He drank the cold coffee, made a face, and went to set up the stream.

The spare room had been a storeroom until Thursday. Now it had the pod, a folding chair, a desk with his laptop on it, and a ring light he wasn’t going to need, because he wasn’t going to be in the room. He’d moved the boxes into the hall. They were still in the hall. He’d step over them for a month and then one day move them somewhere worse.

The Cradle, which was what the company called the pod, streamed for you. That was one of the things that had made people at the trade shows go quiet. You didn’t need a capture card or a second machine. You linked your channel once, picked a camera, and whatever the game saw went out live while your body lay in the gel.

There were four cameras. Follow was the default. It hung a little behind and above you, so your viewers watched your character the way they’d watch anyone in any game: the back of your head, your whole body moving, the world wide around you. Every launch trailer had been shot in Follow. It looked like a game.

Over-shoulder, closer. Fixed, for people who wanted to stand somewhere pretty and talk.

And POV: no camera at all, just your eyes.

He put it on POV.

Then he opened the advanced settings, found the broadcast delay, and set it to sixty seconds. Nobody would see anything until a minute after he did.

He did that before he even said good morning. It was a habit from another game, one where anyone who knew exactly where you were would come and take everything you owned. Fifteen thousand hours of that did things to a person. He’d never once regretted a delay. He’d regretted not having one plenty.

At twenty-two minutes past seven on Monday morning, he pressed GO LIVE.

The title said: ELSEWHERE launch day | POV | trust.

He did know these people. That was the funny part about ten viewers. The streamers his friends watched had numbers with a K after them and chat that went past too fast to read. His went past at walking pace, and every name in it was a name he could put a voice to. Teh Peng had been watching him for four years and still had never said where he lived. Frog had once stayed up until four in the morning to watch him lose the same fight thirty-one times and had typed again after every single one. Mangoes was a student in Ipoh who watched with her lectures muted in another tab. These were his regulars. On a good month he streamed five or six times, and on a good night ten of them came.

That was fine. He wasn’t doing it to be big. He liked the company.

He smiled at the phone.

He’d known Deston since they were both thirteen, in the back row of the same school choir, both of them mouthing the high bits. He’d known Xav since the first week of poly, when he’d talked him into one game of Counter-Strike and they’d ended up playing every game together for years after. These were the two people in the world most like him about this. They’d been waiting just as long, maybe longer. They’d read every dev diary, watched every trade-show clip frame by frame, and argued with strangers on forums about drop rates in a game that didn’t exist yet.

And neither of them had bought one.

Deston had finals. Xav was in Japan, in his second year, on a student’s money. US$2,499 was a lot of money to spend on a rumour. That was what the comment sections said under every trailer: looks amazing, I’ll wait and see. Most of the world was waiting and seeing. Pods were expensive. Full dive was new. Everybody remembered a game that had looked amazing and turned out to be a menu with a nice background.

So somebody had to go in first and say how it was.

There were names he didn’t know, too. One by one they came, curious, and some of them left again. A launch-day stream had that, even a small one. People typed ELSEWHERE into a search bar, clicked whatever was live, and checked whether anyone had said yet if it was worth the money.

“Good question.” He leaned back in the folding chair. “Everyone’s going to stream it in Follow, because Follow looks like a game. You can see the whole character, the outfit, the big swings. It looks great in a clip.” He tapped the setting on the screen. “POV is just what I see. If I make a mistake, you’ll see exactly when I make it, because you’ll be looking where I was looking. If I miss something on the ground, you’ll miss it too. Then you can tell me. It’s more honest.” He shrugged. “I like it. That’s the real reason. It feels like having people in the room.”

At seven fifty-five, he turned the webcam off.

He’d done it in his head a hundred times, and it still felt strange. He took off his slippers. He took his phone out of his pocket and put it on the desk. He climbed over the lip of the pod and lay down in the gel, which was warm already, warmer than skin, and settled under his shoulders and the back of his head like a hand.

A soft voice came from somewhere near his ear.

This Cradle registers to one person, permanently. Only that person’s fingerprint can open it. Place your finger on the glass to continue.

He held his right index finger above the little square for a second. Then he pressed it down.

The glass was cool. A thin line of light ran across it, once, the way a scanner reads a page.

Registered. Welcome.

The lid came down slowly on its long arm, and the room went dim and blue through the smoked glass. The hum rose until he felt it more than heard it, in his back teeth and the bones of his ears. A small display glowed on the inside of the lid, just above his face.

07:58:12.

On the laptop across the room, where he couldn’t see it now, chat was still going.

He couldn’t read it. He knew what it would say anyway.

“Okay,” he said, to the inside of the lid, to twenty-one people and a microphone somewhere in the shell. “I’ll tell you how it is. Trust.”

07:59:50.

He closed his eyes, because it seemed polite.

The hum went up and up and then, all at once, stopped, and for a moment there was no pod and no room and no Malacca, no Monday, no bank balance, no body at all, only a stillness so complete that it wasn’t even dark.

Then he smelled rain.
