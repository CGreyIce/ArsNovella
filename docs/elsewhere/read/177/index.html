Live from Elsewhere · Volume 7: Keep the Lamps

Chapter 177

Conservation Efforts

3,465 words · 15 min read

The northern moor in November, thought Hako. A harsh and unforgiving landscape. Few creatures survive here. Fewer still would choose to.

He was lying on his back in the heather at the third lantern ring, with his spear across his chest and his hands behind his head. He was narrating. He did it in his head when he didn’t do it out loud. It had started as a joke on his stream in the first week, in Otterbank, with the otters. And here, the young spearman attempts to befriend a river otter. The otter is unimpressed. Then his chat had liked it, so he’d kept doing it. Now he couldn’t stop. It was how he thought.

And yet, thought Hako, three brave adventurers have come. Their purpose: to light a road that has been dark for three hundred years. Their chances: honestly not bad.

“You’re doing it,” said Maigo, from the fire.

“I’m not doing anything.”

“You’re doing the voice. In your head. I can see your face doing it.” Maigo stirred the pot. It was barley again, and moor rabbit, and something green Maigo had found in a ditch and said was fine. “You’ve got your nature documentary face on.”

“The Maigo,” said Hako, out loud, “is a perceptive creature.”

“Thank you,” said Maigo.

Ina was at the edge of the ring, sitting with his back against one of the twelve iron posts. The cage above him had a lamp burning in it, hooded to the south. He had his little book open on his knee and he was counting. He’d been counting all week.

“Sixty-eight,” said Ina.

“Sixty-eight lamps?”

“Sixty-eight lit, between here and the north door. Twenty-six to go. Then the twenty-two.” He shut the book. “We’ll do the rest tomorrow and the day after. Then the old hall. Then the edge.”

And so, thought Hako, our heroes pause, two-thirds of the way along their journey. Night falls on the northern moor. Somewhere out in the darkness—

He stopped narrating.

He sat up.

“What,” said Maigo.

Hako didn’t answer. He was looking past the ring, north-west, out across the heather toward a place where the ground dipped. He’d noticed it on the way in, because he noticed water. You grew up in a river town of trappers and you noticed water. There was a pool there. A moor pool, black and flat and still, about the size of the banner room. There’d been a mist on it in the afternoon.

There wasn’t a mist on it now.

Something was standing up out of it.

He’d seen Unlit before. Everybody had. On the first Long Night, in Otterbank, they’d come up out of the deep bends of the river all night long, low and flat like otters made of ink. He’d killed eighty-one of them with his spear before the dawn. He’d counted. He’d thought he knew what they looked like.

These were different.

They were tall. Thin, very thin, the height of a man and a half, the colour of nothing. Like somebody had cut a shape out of the night. They didn’t have legs. They flowed. They came up out of the still black pool one after another, slowly, the water closing behind them without a ripple. And they stood on the edge of it in a row, swaying very slightly, like reeds.

Eleven of them.

“Unlit,” said Ina, very quietly. He’d stood up. His wand was in his hand.

“It’s November,” said Maigo. “It’s not the longest night. It’s not even near the longest night. They’re not supposed to be out.”

“They’re out,” said Ina.

The Unlit, thought Hako, without meaning to. Rarely observed outside the longest night. Their behaviour on other nights is almost entirely unknown. What will they do?

He watched them.

They leaned.

All eleven of them, at the same moment. Not toward the ring, where three people were sitting round a fire inside twelve lit lamps. Away from it. They leaned east. Toward the road. Toward the nearest waystone, a quarter of a mile off, with a new black cage in its north face and a little flame burning in it, shining north across the moor in a thin bright line.

Toward the light that was facing them.

“They’re going to the lamp,” said Hako.

They went.

It was the strangest thing he’d ever seen in a game, and he’d once seen Gabe try to fish.

They didn’t come for the three of them. They didn’t even look. They flowed past the ring at a distance of about forty paces. Eleven tall dark shapes in a line, going across the heather toward the waystone as if they’d been called. As if somebody had said their names. They didn’t hurry. They didn’t stop. They went to the lamp.

And when the first one got there, it bent over.

It bent over the waystone the way you’d bend over a candle to blow it out. It folded itself round the cage and the little flame, like a hand closing round a moth. The thin bright line going north across the moor went out.

Ina made a sound Hako had never heard him make.

“That’s mine,” said Ina.

He was already moving. Out of the ring, past the posts, into the dark. Wand up, the oni mask coming down off his shoulder onto his face in one movement, the way it had on the hill at Emberly in December. Maigo said something sharp and grabbed his staff. And Hako picked up his spear, and went, because there were three of them and that was the rule.

Nobody alone.

He’d tell it on his stream afterward, in the voice, and his chat would laugh. The lesser-spotted Ina, enraged, charges a group of eleven Unlit with a stick. Experts are divided on whether this is wise. But it wasn’t funny at the time.

The Unlit didn’t fight them. That was the strange part. Not at first. Ina got to the waystone and put his wand into the dark shape folded over the cage, and said Kindle. The shape burned, gold fire running up it like up a curtain. And it unfolded off the stone and fell back into the heather, writhing. And the other ten didn’t turn on him. They just kept going.

Past him. Past the waystone, now dark. North along the road to the next one. A mile on, a little grey stub on the skyline with a flame in its north face.

“They’re not interested in us,” said Maigo. He’d stopped running. He was standing in the heather with his staff, staring after them. “They’re not interested in us at all.”

“They’re interested in the lamps,” said Hako.

He watched them go. He couldn’t help it; it was what he did. He watched them flow up the road toward the next light, in their swaying line. And he saw something he’d tell Kei about later, and Kei would write it on the map in very small letters.

They didn’t go north.

He’d thought they would. North was where they came from, the dark, the black past the twenty-two stones. If you’d asked him an hour ago, he’d have said the Unlit went north. But they were going up the road toward the next lamp, and the next lamp was north. And then, when they’d put that one out, the next lamp would be north again, a mile further on.

No. Wait. He’d got it the wrong way round.

He stood in the heather and turned the map in his head the way Kei would.

They’d come up out of the pool to the north-west. They’d gone to the nearest lamp. The nearest lamp had been south of the pool, back toward the round town. Then they’d turned and gone to the next one. Not the next one north. The next one they could see. And the lamps faced north. So the only ones they could see, from where they were, were the ones south of them. Every lamp on the road, all ninety-four, faced north. Toward them. Toward the pool. Toward the dark.

They could only see the lamps that led south.

“Ina,” said Hako. “Maigo.”

“What?”

“They’re not going north.” He pointed. “Look. They put that one out, and now they’re going to the next one they can see. And they can only see the ones between them and the town. Because the lamps face this way.” He was talking fast, the way he never talked, not in the voice. “It’s a road. It’s a road. For them. You put a lamp every mile facing north, and anything that comes out of the north sees the first one and goes to it. And then it sees the next one, and goes to that. All the way. Mile after mile.” He took a breath. “Down the road. To the round town.”

Ina had taken his mask off. He was holding it in his hand, looking at Hako.

“To the north door,” he said.

“To the north door,” said Hako.

Nobody said anything.

Out on the heather, the ten Unlit had reached the next waystone, a mile off. Hako saw the little flame there go out.

And then, very slowly, the way the tide turns, the ten tall shapes began to sink. Down into the heather, into the ground, the way they’d come up out of the pool. The night was still the night, but something in it had changed. A sort of thinness in the dark. It wasn’t the longest night. It was only November. They’d come up too early, and they couldn’t stay.

They were gone.

The moor was quiet. Two waystones dark. The ring behind them, still lit, twelve lamps burning on their posts. A kept place. Nothing had even tried to come inside.

They relit both lamps before they slept. Ina did it, with the one word. He stood in front of each one for a long time afterward, with his mask in his hand, not saying anything.

“It works,” said Maigo, back at the fire. He was stirring the soup, which had gone a bit thick. “Doesn’t it. That’s what that was. It works. The road. It’s doing what it’s for.”

“It’s doing what it’s for,” said Ina.

“Already,” said Hako. “In November. With eleven.”

They all thought about that.

On the longest night, thought Hako, and found he wasn’t doing the voice any more. Not eleven. All of it. All at once. Everything that lives in the black past the edge of the world, for a day and a half. And every one of them sees the first lamp, and goes to it, and then the next. All the way down the road of lights.

To us.

“We should tell Kei,” said Maigo.

“We’ll tell Kei,” said Ina. “When we get back.”

“We should tell him now.”

“He’s asleep. It’s two in the morning in Australia.” Ina looked at the fire. “And it won’t change anything. We’d still have to light the rest. That’s the job.” He looked at Hako. “Isn’t it?”

Hako thought about it.

He thought about Otterbank on the first Long Night, with the river lit. Eighty-one Unlit coming up out of the deep bends like ink otters, and him on the bank with his spear, alone. He’d been fine. He’d been fine. He’d been upbeat about it the whole night, on Kei’s call, telling everybody it was going to be all right. It had been all right. But he’d been alone on that bank for eleven hours, and he hadn’t told anybody what that was like. Because it was all right, and you didn’t complain when it was all right.

Kei’s list on the board said nobody alone now. Underlined.

“It’s the job,” said Hako. “Yes.”

And so, he thought, quite deliberately this time, in the voice, because the voice helped, our three adventurers, having learned something terrible, decide to do the exact same thing they were going to do anyway. Experts agree: this is very on brand.

He went to sleep inside the ring with his spear across his chest, between Maigo and Ina, warm. He didn’t dream.

They finished the road on Wednesday.

The last lamp was a mile short of the old hall, at the top of a long slope of heather. From there you could see the ruin for the first time: half a dome on a hill, with a birch tree growing up through the fallen part. Ina put the cage in. Maigo poured the oil. Hako set the wick, and stepped back, and Ina said the word.

[ A lamp, north. Lit. ]

They didn’t cheer. They were too tired. They stood round the last stone in the wind and looked back down the road, south, toward the round town four days away. They couldn’t see a single one of the ninety-four. They all faced the other way.

“Four days,” said Maigo. “Ninety-four lamps. And you can’t see any of them.”

“That’s the point,” said Ina.

“I know it’s the point. I’m just saying.” Maigo put his sunglasses on. “It’s a bit sad.”

They went on to the old hall, and through it, past the birch, out of the broken wall on the north side. And up the line of the twenty-two stones, every fifty paces, toward the edge of the world.

The twenty-two were still lit. Hako hadn’t expected that. Tiger had set them in September with his lanterns, and Thea had set the last one, and Kei had come home and written in the Roll. Tiger’s lanterns should have burned out in two nights. They hadn’t. Every one of them was burning, small and steady in its soot-black hollow, twenty-two lights in a line going up the slope into the black.

“Somebody’s been here,” said Ina.

He crouched by the first one. Tiger’s tin lantern was in it, the same one, a plain market lantern. Its oil dish was full. Somebody had filled it.

“Who?” said Maigo.

Nobody knew. There was nobody out here. There was nothing out here. There was the old hall and the twenty-two stones and the black.

Hako looked up the line to the last stone, the twenty-second, where Thea had set her lantern in September. And past it.

The light was there.

He’d seen it from the roof of the dome. A gold point on the edge of the world. It wasn’t a point any more. It was much closer than in September, much closer than it had been from the roof last week. It was a light. A lamp, a real one, the size of a lamp, low down, steady, gold. Out in the black past the last stone, not very far now. He thought a mile. Perhaps less.

And as he watched, it moved.

Not toward them. Sideways. A little way to the left, and then a little way back. The way a lamp moves when someone’s carrying it and stops, and looks around, and goes on.

“Oh,” said Maigo, very softly.

Nobody said anything else. They stood at the bottom of the twenty-two stones in the wind and watched it. A lamp, in the black, being carried. Stopping. Going on. A long way out. Not coming closer, not tonight. But there.

Somebody’s carrying it, thought Hako. Somebody’s out there carrying it. On their own.

He didn’t do the voice. He didn’t want to.

“Hello,” he said instead. Not loudly. Into the black. The way you’d say it to somebody across a river, the first time you saw them on the other bank. “We lit the road. It’s lit. All the way home.”

The light didn’t answer. He hadn’t thought it would.

But after a moment it moved again, a little way to the left and back, and then it was still. And it stayed still for a long time, the whole time they stood there. As if somebody out in the black had set the lamp down, and sat beside it, and was looking back toward them, up the line of twenty-two stones. To where three people were standing in the light.

They came home on Saturday by the north road, four days, with every lamp at their backs. At the edge of the round town they went round, the long way, to the south. Everybody came in through the gate. Even if you lived there.

Wendy was on the south steps with her hands in her pockets. Kei was beside her, with his map under his arm, which he never was. He’d been waiting.

“Who’d stand next to you?” said Wendy.

“These two,” said Hako. “All the way up and all the way back.”

“Fair,” said Wendy.

When Hako told Kei about the eleven at the third ring, and the road, and the lamps they could only see going south, Kei didn’t say anything at all. He unrolled the map on the step, right there, in the cold, with Wendy holding one corner down with her boot. He took his pen. Down the line of beads from the edge of the world to the round town, he drew an arrow.

Pointing south. Down the north road, into the round market, to the Hall of Roads. To the north door, on the far side of the dome from where they were standing.

Then he drew a small circle round the gold mark past the twenty-second stone, and wrote beside it, very small: being carried.

“Tuesday’s the anniversary,” said Kei, after a while. “Did you know? A year. Since the first day.”

“I didn’t know,” said Hako.

“Nor did I, till Tif said.” Kei rolled up the map. He looked over the roofs, north, toward the dark, where ninety-four lamps were burning that none of them could see. “A year, and five weeks after it, the longest night. I’m going to need a lot of threes.”

“You’ve got sixty people.”

“Twenty threes,” said Kei. “I’ve counted.”

He went up the steps and in. Hako followed him, with Maigo and Ina, through the Hall and past Ama Corrie at her counter, to the banner room. At the far end of it, set apart by the north door, facing north, the chair stood empty, the way it always did.

Ama Corrie came and stood beside them in the doorway with her broom.

“Ninety-four,” she said.

“Ninety-four,” said Ina.

“And somebody carrying one,” said Ama Corrie. It wasn’t a question. Hyvred had called it down from the eye an hour ago, apparently, the moment they’d come into sight: three on the north road, and they’ve seen something. Ama Corrie looked at the chair for a long time over her spectacles.

“Hm,” she said. And she went and swept under it, under not round, though there was nothing under it at all.
