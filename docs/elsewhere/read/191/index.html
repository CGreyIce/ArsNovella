Live from Elsewhere · Volume 8: Somewhere Else

Chapter 191

The Unit Opposite

3,279 words · 14 min read

The sticker on the grille opposite said DIJUAL, and it had said DIJUAL for nineteen months, and Gabe had walked past it every morning for a year.

He’d counted once. You couldn’t help it, living across a corridor from a thing. The unit opposite Dec’s, on the same floor, at the end of the same long corridor. Grille padlocked. A dead plant in a pot by the door, very dead, so dead it had gone past brown into a sort of grey lace. Post piled up behind the grille, a drift of it, years of it, flyers and bills and somebody’s free newspaper. And a red-and-white sticker on the grille at eye height, faded pink now by the sun through the corridor window: DIJUAL. For sale. And a phone number, which nobody had ever rung.

Deston rang it on a Thursday night in January, from Singapore, on speaker, with Gabe and Dec listening on the other end of a group call.

“Hello,” said Deston, in his best voice. The one he used for networking, the relaxed one that made people like him in the first ten seconds. “I’m calling about the unit. The one with the sticker. On the—“ He said the floor and the block. “Is it still for sale?”

There was a long pause on the line.

“Lah,” said a man’s voice, at last. Surprised. “Still for sale. Nobody call for one year. You want to see?”

“Yes,” said Deston. “Saturday?”

“Saturday can,” said the man. “Ten o’clock. I bring the key.”

Deston hung up. On the group call, there was a short silence.

“Nineteen months,” said Gabe.

“He sounded so happy,” said Deston.

“Nobody’s called him in nineteen months, Dest. He’s probably going to cry.”

On the third square of the call, Dec’s square, the camera was off. It had been off all evening. He’d been in the egg till eight and then come out and joined the call and said hi and nothing else. Gabe could hear him, though. The small sounds of somebody in a kitchen, making tea. And under it, every few minutes, the buzz of his phone on the counter. Messages. A lot of them.

“Dec,” said Gabe. “You coming Saturday?”

“Yes,” said Dec. And then, after a moment, as if he’d just heard it: “Ten?”

“Ten.”

“Okay.”

His phone buzzed again. Twice. Gabe heard him turn it face down on the counter.

Deston came up on the six o’clock bus on Friday. Of course he did. He always came up on the six o’clock bus, with something in a box from Singapore that the kopitiam auntie downstairs would be suspicious of. This time it was kueh, in a pink box. She held one up to the light at breakfast on Saturday and said this one, Singapore kueh, too sweet, and ate three.

At ten to ten they were in the corridor. All three of them. Dec in an old T-shirt with his hair flat on one side from the pod. Gabe in the shorts he wore every day. Deston in a collared shirt, because he was Deston and it was a viewing, and you wore a collar to a viewing.

The agent came up the lift at ten exactly. A small round man in a batik shirt with a lanyard and a key ring with forty keys on it, and the face of a man who’d been waiting nineteen months for this. He shook Deston’s hand twice. He shook Gabe’s hand. He looked at Dec, who was standing at the side by the window, out of the way, and shook his hand too, uncertainly, as if he wasn’t sure who Dec was for.

“Neighbour,” said Gabe, helpfully. “He lives there.” He pointed at Dec’s front door, at the other end of the corridor. “And there.” The other door, the parents’ side, the long home with two front doors. “He’s the reason.”

“Ah,” said the agent, as if that explained something, which it did.

He found the key on the third try. The padlock came off the grille with a scream of rust. The grille slid back. The post fell over in a drift onto the corridor tiles, and the agent stepped over it, unlocked the door, and pushed it open.

It smelled of nobody.

That was the first thing Gabe noticed. Not a bad smell. Dust and closed windows and the faint ghost of somebody else’s cooking from a long time ago. A unit nobody had lived in for two years. Bare walls, bare floor, the white tiles, the same as Dec’s side. A living room, a kitchen through an arch, a corridor going back to the bedrooms. A sliding door at the end onto a small balcony, its glass gone grey with dust, with Malacca beyond it, the roofs and the river and a long way off the sea.

The same layout as Dec’s. Exactly. Mirrored. Like standing in a reflection of a room you knew.

Deston walked into the middle of the living room and stood there.

He didn’t say anything. He didn’t look round. He just stood in the middle of the empty white room with his hands in his pockets, looking at the grey balcony door and the light coming through it. Gabe had known him for eleven years, since a school choir. He knew that face. It was the face Deston had worn on the edge of Kai’s chair in the banner room when the Fourth Verse came. A song he’d been trying to find for months, and suddenly there it was.

“Dest,” said Gabe.

“Mm.”

“You all right?”

“Yes,” said Deston. “Yes. I’m just—“ He took his hands out of his pockets. “I’m just looking.”

The agent did the numbers in the kitchen, leaning on the counter, with a calculator on his phone.

Four hundred and thirty thousand ringgit. That was what the owner wanted. The owner lived in Penang now, the agent said, with his daughter. He’d been asking four-eighty at first, and then four-sixty, and then, last June, four-thirty. He just wanted it gone. He was tired of paying the maintenance on a unit nobody lived in.

“Four-thirty,” said Deston. “Okay.”

The agent looked at him. Then at Gabe. Then, carefully, back at Deston.

“You’re from Singapore?” he said. “Your voice.”

“Yes.”

“Singapore citizen?”

“Yes.”

The agent put his phone down on the counter. He looked, suddenly, very sad. Like a man who’d been waiting nineteen months for a phone call and had finally got one, and was about to have to say the thing he’d hoped he wouldn’t.

“Foreigner cannot buy at four-thirty,” he said.

Silence in the kitchen.

“What?” said Gabe.

“Foreigner buying in Melaka,” said the agent, “must get state consent. The state government. And must buy above the minimum. For condo, last I check, five hundred thousand.” He spread his hands. “Below five hundred, consent board say no. Doesn’t matter what the unit worth. The price must be above.” He looked at the empty white room through the arch, sadly. “That’s why nobody call. Locals don’t want this floor, too old. Singaporeans want, but cannot. Price too low.”

Deston stood in the kitchen and didn’t say anything.

Gabe looked at him. He’d seen Deston take bad news before. He took it the way he took everything, relaxed, nodding, already thinking about the next thing. He wasn’t doing that now. He was looking through the arch at the living room and the grey balcony door. At the corridor beyond the front door, and Dec’s door at the end of it. As if somebody had just told him the song he’d found had a verse missing.

“So it’s too cheap,” said Deston slowly.

“Too cheap,” agreed the agent, miserably.

“For me to buy.”

“For a foreigner. Yes.”

Dec, at the window, hadn’t said anything the whole time. He was looking at Deston.

Deston turned round. He looked at the agent. And Gabe saw the relaxed face come back, the networking one, the one that made people like him in the first ten seconds. But underneath it, for the first time in eleven years, Gabe saw something he’d only seen on Deston once before. On the altar step of a drowned church, in December, a year ago, singing a verse nobody had sung in three hundred years.

“Then I’ll pay five hundred,” said Deston.

The agent dropped his phone.

It hit the counter and bounced and went under the edge of the cabinet. He went down on his knees to get it, and stayed down there for a moment longer than he needed to. Gabe was fairly sure he was composing himself.

“Five hundred,” said the agent, from the floor.

“Five hundred thousand. The minimum.” Deston’s voice was very calm. “If the owner will take it. Seventy more than he’s asking. So it’s above the line. So the state can say yes.”

The agent came up from behind the counter with his phone and looked at Deston as if he’d grown a second head.

“The owner,” he said, “will take it.”

“Good.”

“The owner will cry.”

“That’s fine,” said Deston.

Gabe found his voice.

“Dest,” he said. “Dest. You’re paying seventy thousand ringgit extra. To live across the corridor from Dec.”

Deston looked at him.

“Yes,” he said.

“Seventy thousand.”

“Yes.”

“That’s—“ Gabe did the sum, badly, in his head. “That’s like twenty thousand Singapore dollars, Dest! That’s a car! That’s a—“

“I know what it is,” said Deston.

Gabe stopped.

Deston was looking past him, through the arch, at the empty white living room. The same layout as Dec’s, exactly, mirrored. A room you knew from the other side. And the grey balcony door with Malacca beyond it, the roofs and the river and the sea.

“I’ve been coming up on the six o’clock bus for a year,” said Deston. “With kueh. And sleeping on his sofa. And going back on Sunday.” He was quiet for a moment. “I want a door. Opposite his. Where I can go home and still be here.” He paused. “And one day, when I ask her, I want her to have a door too.”

Nobody said anything.

The agent looked at the counter.

At the window, Dec turned round. He’d been looking out at the corridor the whole time, at his own door at the end of it, and his parents’ beside it. He looked at Deston for a long moment across the empty kitchen.

“Okay,” said Dec.

That was all. Okay. But Gabe had known him eleven years too, and he heard what was in it. So did Deston. He went very slightly red, and looked at the agent, and said, in the networking voice, slightly too loudly, “So. What happens now?”

What happened now, it turned out, was Vivian.

Deston rang her from the corridor, standing by the drift of old post, because Dec said she’d want to know first. He’d been right. She answered on the first ring, on a Saturday, at eleven.

“Mr Deston,” said Vivian.

Deston explained. The unit. The price. The minimum. The state consent. Five hundred.

There was a pause on the line.

“You are paying above the asking price,” said Vivian, “in order to be permitted to purchase a property that is worth less than you are paying for it.”

“Yes.”

“So that you may live across a corridor from Mr Declan.”

“Yes.”

Another pause. Gabe, beside Deston, could hear her breathing. He’d met Vivian four times in a year and she’d frightened him every time.

“That is the most sentimental thing I have heard this year,” said Vivian. “And it is the eighth of January.”

“It’s the fifteenth.”

“I have been counting from the first business day,” said Vivian. “I will find you a conveyancing lawyer. A good one. Not my cousin; my cousin is not good. The lawyer will prepare the application to the state. It will take some months. They will want documents. They will want them in a particular order. I will make sure they are in that order.” She paused. “There will be fees. There will be a stamp duty that is higher for you because you are not Malaysian. I will send you the figures on Monday at eight.”

“Thank you, Vivian.”

“Do not thank me. I am a company secretary. This is not company business.” Another pause, a longer one. “I am doing it anyway. Do not tell the auntie. She will want to come and see the unit.”

She hung up.

Deston stood in the corridor with his phone and looked at it.

“She’s doing it anyway,” he said.

“She likes you,” said Gabe.

“She’s never said a nice thing to me in her life.”

“That’s how you know,” said Gabe. “She doesn’t say them to people she doesn’t like. She just declines their nine companies.”

The agent left at half past eleven, with the key ring jangling and a face like a man walking out of church. He shook all their hands again on the way to the lift, three times each. He gave Deston his card. He gave Gabe his card. He tried to give Dec his card and Dec said I live there and pointed, and the agent laughed far too hard and pressed it on him anyway.

When the lift doors closed, the corridor was quiet.

The grille of the unit opposite was still open. The agent hadn’t padlocked it again. He’d left the key with Deston, for measuring, he’d said, since you’re going to be the owner, la. The front door was open behind it. You could see into the empty white living room from the corridor. The dust in the sunlight from the balcony. The tiles.

Deston bent down and started picking up the post.

He did it slowly. All of it, the whole drift, nineteen months of it. Flyers for a durian seller, and a cleaning service, and a restaurant that had closed a year ago. A free newspaper with a headline about the ringgit. Bills for somebody who lived in Penang now. He stacked it all very neatly in his arms.

Gabe watched him. Then he bent down and helped.

Dec didn’t. He stood by the corridor window, out of the way, the way he always stood. When the post was all picked up, he went to the dead plant by the door, very dead, grey lace. He picked up the pot and carried it down the corridor to the bin chute and dropped the plant in. Then he brought the pot back, empty. He put it down again by the door, exactly where it had been.

“For a new one,” said Dec.

Deston looked at the empty pot for a long time.

“I’ll get a plant,” he said.

“Get one that doesn’t need you,” said Dec. “You’re going to be in the egg.”

“I’ll get a cactus.”

“Get a cactus,” agreed Dec.

That night, back in the back room at Melaka Raya, with the fan going round and the pods side by side, Gabe lay in his egg with the lid up and didn’t go in yet.

He’d been in Malacca for a year. He’d come on Christmas Eve, the year before last, off a plane from Melbourne with his own grey pillow. He’d taken the spare room in Dec’s flat, the one where the first egg had been, and put his own grey pillow on the bed and said it’s the right decision. He’d never once regretted it. He’d watched Dec go into the egg every morning at nine and come out every night at nine. He’d made the kopi too sweet every day for a year and Dec had never once said anything.

He’d thought it was enough. Him and Dec and Suzu, the three of them, in the flat and the shoplot, the gardening boys, the auntie called them. And Deston on the six o’clock bus.

Now there’d be a door opposite.

He thought about a school choir, a long time ago. The back row: Dec and Deston, and him at the end of it. Standing next to each other on the risers, singing something none of them could remember now. It had been eleven years. They’d stood in a lot of rows since. A fen, and a drowned church, and a line on the longest night.

They’d always been in the same row.

A door opposite, he thought. The back row, again. Across a corridor.

On the pod beside his, Dec was already in. The lid was down and the little light was on. His phone was face down on the shelf by the pod, the way he’d left it, and it was buzzing.

Gabe looked at it.

It buzzed again. And again. Three in a row. He couldn’t see the screen. He didn’t need to. He’d heard it buzzing all week, on the counter, in the kitchen, on the desk. Every evening, from about nine. He’d seen Dec look at it once, on Wednesday, and read whatever it was. And for a moment, just a moment, Dec’s face had done something Gabe had never seen it do in eleven years.

He’d gone pink.

Dec. Pink. At a phone.

Gabe had stood in the kitchen with the kopi and looked at him and said absolutely nothing. It had been the hardest thing he’d ever done. He’d wanted to say DEC. He’d wanted to say WHO IS IT, and IS IT WHO I THINK IT IS, and ARE YOU OKAY. He’d wanted to tell Deston, and Suzu, and the auntie, and probably the whole of Malacca.

He hadn’t. He’d made the kopi.

Because it was Dec’s. Whatever it was. It was his.

The phone buzzed again.

Gabe reached over, very gently, and turned it so it was face down a bit more squarely on the shelf, so it wouldn’t buzz itself off the edge. Then he lay back in his own egg and pulled the lid down.

Somebody’s in his chat, he thought, as the dark came. For once. Somebody’s in his.

He was smiling when he went in.
