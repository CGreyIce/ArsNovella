Live from Elsewhere · Volume 3: Constellation

Chapter 64

Properly

2,708 words · 12 min read

Pell decided about Drew in about four seconds.

She was sitting on the top beam of the Top Lock when they came up the towpath on Thursday evening, the way she always sat, with her legs over the drop and the red runner’s satchel on her shoulder. She was sixteen, and the best runner in Sevenlocks, and she thought everyone who wasn’t from Sevenlocks was slightly stupid. She looked at Drew coming up the cobbles beside Tiger. She looked at his face, and his hair, and the iron knuckles on his belt. Then she looked at his feet.

“Huh,” she said.

“Hi,” said Drew. “You’re Pell.”

“You’re the one he stood at the bottom lock for. For two hours. Like a dog.”

“Like a tiger,” said Tiger.

“That’s worse,” said Pell and Drew, at exactly the same moment, and then looked at each other.

Tiger stared at both of them.

“No,” he said. “No. You don’t get to be friends. You’ve known each other one second.”

“He’s got runner’s feet,” said Pell, ignoring him. She swung her legs. “Not runner runner. You’d never run the Top. But you put them down like you’ve thought about it first.”

“I watch feet,” said Drew. “It’s sort of my whole thing.”

“Huh,” said Pell again, and Tiger could tell from the way she said it that Drew had passed something, some Sevenlocks test that Tiger had taken three weeks to pass. Drew had passed it standing still.

He wasn’t even annoyed. He was too happy.

Drew told him properly on the roof of Gaspard’s shop, at the first dark.

Tiger had known it was coming. He’d known since Monday night on the call, when Drew said Tiger’s in if I’m in. I haven’t asked him. I don’t need to. Tiger had heard it, and grinned at the ceiling of his room, and thought yeah, obviously. He’d been waiting for Drew to ask him anyway, because Drew always asked anyway. That was Drew. Drew didn’t need to, and asked.

So they sat on the roof of the chandler’s with their backs against the chimney, looking down at the locks. The lamps were coming on along the gates one by one, and the boats were tying up for the night in the basin. Drew told him all of it. Slowly, the way he did, so you could see each bit before the next one came.

The writ. Twenty names. Fifteen. Thirty days. One Hall. The gate, and Nell, and every time. Kei on the steps of a round hall where seven roads met, with a map. Ice saying not yet. Xav saying ask me when it’s big. Thirteen people on a call saying here.

“Carrow Cross is nine days from here,” said Drew. “Kei’s sums. Probably eleven.”

“Kei’s sums said twenty-four to me,” said Tiger. “It was forty.”

“There was an inn.”

“There’s always going to be an inn with you.” Tiger grinned. “Okay. Nine days. Eleven. Fine.”

“Early February,” said Drew. “When Kei’s fifteen. Then thirty days to get twenty names into the same room.”

“Fine.”

“You’d have to leave Sevenlocks.”

Tiger stopped grinning.

Drew didn’t say anything else. That was the properly. He’d said the thing, and now he was waiting, looking out at the locks, letting Tiger look at it too.

Tiger looked.

He looked at the seven locks going down the hill, the black gates and the gold water. At Pell on the Top, legs dangling. At the bottom four locks that were his, that he’d run every night for six weeks, that he’d carried lamps up all through the longest night until the sky went out and he fell in. He looked at Gaspard’s doorway below, and the lock-keeper’s hut, and the old runner’s window, where the old runner had given him a brown satchel. Not the red one. You had to be Sevenlocks-born for the red one.

Sevenlocks remembers who ran.

“Do I have to leave leave?” he said. “Or just—go?”

“Just go,” said Drew. “For the signing. It’s twenty names in one room, by hand. After that you can run wherever you like.”

“I could come back.”

“You could come back.”

“Every longest night,” said Tiger. “I could come back every longest night. For the lamps. Pell can’t do all seven on her own. Nobody can.” He was talking faster now, the way he did when something was getting clearer. “I’d run to Carrow Cross and sign it and run back. I’m fast. I’m fast now. I could do nine days in six. I could do it in five if I didn’t fall in.”

“You’d fall in.”

“I’d fall in once,” said Tiger. “Probably once.”

Drew laughed. It was the quiet laugh, the one that hardly made a sound, that Tiger had been hearing down a phone line for nine years.

“So that’s a yes,” said Drew.

“It was a yes on Monday.” Tiger knocked his shoulder into Drew’s. “It was a yes before you said it. You knew it was a yes.”

“I knew.”

“Then why’d you ask?”

Drew looked down at the locks for a moment. A boat was coming in late to the bottom basin, very slowly, with a lamp at its bow, and the lock-keeper was going out with his long pole to meet it.

“Because you had to see it first,” he said. “The leaving. Before you said yes. Otherwise it’s not a yes, it’s just you being happy.”

Tiger thought about that.

“I am happy, though,” he said.

“I know,” said Drew. “That’s the best bit.”

The lock-keeper came up the ladder to the roof at nine.

He was a broad, slow man called Wat, with a face like a walnut and the long pole always in his hand. He’d lost his bet on Wednesday night and been very gracious about it. He didn’t usually climb ladders. He looked at the two of them sitting against the chimney, and then down at the bottom basin, where the late boat had tied up.

“Barge,” he said. “From Lowmarsh. Wants to go up tonight. All seven.”

“Tonight?” said Tiger.

“Tonight.” Wat leaned on his pole. “Grain for the Top before the market. Bargeman says he’ll pay double.”

Tiger sat up. Drew watched him do it. His ears came forward and his whole face changed, the way it did on stream when something was about to happen.

“The crabs,” Tiger said.

“The crabs,” agreed Wat.

There were crabs in the walls of Sevenlocks.

Tiger had found out about them in the first week, the hard way, the way he found out about most things. In the day they lived in the gaps between the big stones of the lock chambers, down near the water, where nobody looked. They were grey and flat, and the size of a large dog, with one claw much bigger than the other. At night, when a barge went up through the locks, they came out. They climbed the walls of the chamber, sideways and fast, and went for the ropes. One snip and the barge swung loose in the chamber, crashed into the gates, and the grain went into the canal. Nobody took barges up at night.

Except for double.

“Eight feet,” said Drew, at the first lock, looking down into the chamber as the water started to rise.

They were standing on the walls, one on each side, with the barge coming up between them on the rising water. Wat was on the gate with his pole. The bargeman was very nervous at his tiller. And all along the wet stone sides of the chamber, in the lamplight, grey shapes were starting to move.

“Eight feet,” said Drew again. He sounded pleased, which was a strange thing to sound about crabs.

“So?”

“So everything starts in the feet.” Drew had his knuckles on. He was watching the nearest one come up the wall toward the barge rope, sideways, very fast. “And they’ve got eight. That’s eight tells.”

It came over the edge of the wall at him.

Drew didn’t move until it was almost there. Then he moved half a step, sideways, the way Tiger had seen him move on stream against a frost bear, and the big claw closed on the air where his arm had been. And his fist was already there. Not where the crab was. Where it was going to be.

The crab went back down the wall the way it had come, upside down, into the rising water.

“Left side,” said Drew, calmly. “Front left foot goes first. Every time. Watch for it.”

Tiger watched for it.

And then he went.

That was the thing about Skim. On the lock walls it was nothing much. You could run along a wall; anyone could. But the barge was coming up between them, and the crabs were on both sides, and the ropes ran from the barge to the bollards on both walls. To get from one side of the chamber to the other you had to cross the water. And Tiger didn’t cross water. He went over it, from the wall to the barge’s rail to the top of the grain sacks to the other wall. Never landing. Leaving every place he touched before it knew he’d been there.

A crab reached for the bow rope. He was on it before the claw closed, both short swords, one-two, the way Pell had made him do it on the gates a thousand times. Kiss it, don’t marry it. He was gone again before it fell.

“Behind you,” said Drew. “Two. Front left.”

Tiger didn’t look. He trusted it. He went up, off the rail, and two claws closed under him on nothing. When he came down he was on the far wall, and Drew was there, and the two crabs that had reached for him were in the water.

“Drew,” said Tiger, delighted, breathing hard.

“Keep going,” said Drew. “Six more locks.”

They took the barge up all seven.

It took three hours. Lock after lock, the water rising, the gates swinging open, the crabs coming up out of the walls. And all the way up the hill Drew read their feet and called them, and Tiger went where he called, and the crabs went back into the canal.

At the fourth lock, Tiger’s top one, the townspeople started coming out onto their roofs. At the fifth, Gaspard was on his, with a lamp, cheering. At the sixth, Pell came down off the Top and sat on the gate with her legs dangling and watched, and didn’t say anything at all. Which from Pell was a speech.

At the Top Lock, the old one came up.

She was huge. Twice the size of the others, black with age, with weed growing on her back and one claw so big she had to drag it. She came up the wall of the Top chamber slowly, which was worse than fast, and she didn’t go for a rope. She went for the gate. For the great beam where the two gates met, and the hinge-iron beside it. One snip there, and the Top gate would swing loose, and the whole hill of water behind it would come down.

“Tiger,” said Drew. His voice had changed. “She doesn’t—“

“What?”

“She doesn’t move her feet.” Drew was staring at her. “She drags. She’s not stepping. She’s just pulling herself. There’s no tell.”

Tiger looked at her. Then at the gate. Then at the gap between the two gates, where the beams met, the gap that opened when the gates swung. The gap he’d jumped on the twenty-fifth day, with a letter in his hand, while the whole town watched from the roofs.

“Wat,” he shouted. “Open the gates.”

“What?”

“Open them! Just a bit! Just the gap!”

Wat stared at him. Then, because he was a lock-keeper and he’d seen this boy do this before, he leaned on his pole. The great Top gates groaned, and began to swing.

And the Matriarch, halfway along the beam, felt the gate move under her. She did what anything does when the ground moves. She shifted her weight. She lifted a foot.

“Front right!” shouted Drew. “She’s going left—“

Tiger was already in the air.

He went off the wall, over the opening gap, twelve feet of air above churning water, with both short swords out. He didn’t look down. You don’t land. You leave. He went across the Matriarch’s back like a stone skipping a pond. He touched it once, on the hard black shell, and was gone. Both swords went in the soft place behind her big claw as he passed, in and out, kiss, don’t marry.

She lost her grip.

She went off the beam into the Top chamber with a splash that soaked the whole lock, Wat, Pell and Gaspard on his roof included. She didn’t come up.

Tiger landed on the far wall.

He landed. Both feet. On the stone, at the top of the Top Lock, in the lamplight, with his swords out and his ears straight up.

He didn’t fall in.

He stood there for a second, swaying, looking down at the churning water in the chamber below him. Then he looked across the gap at Drew, who was standing on the other wall with his knuckles still up, looking back at him.

“I didn’t fall in,” said Tiger.

“You didn’t fall in,” said Drew.

“I didn’t fall in!”

On the Top gate, Pell, soaking wet, had her hand over her mouth. Tiger couldn’t tell if she was laughing. Then she took it away, and she was.

Later, much later, after the bargeman had paid double and then a bit more, and Gaspard had stopped crying, they sat on the Top gate. Pell had gone down to the old runner’s house. The whole town was lit below them like a staircase of gold.

Tiger turned his stream to face himself.

“Okay,” he said. He was grinning so widely it hurt. “Okay, chat, so. News. I’m going to be in a guild.”

“It’s not the swamp guy’s,” said Tiger. “He said. It’s Kei’s. And Drew’s. And Maigo’s. And Niko’s. And mine. And, like, everybody’s. It’s called—“ He stopped. He looked at Drew. “What’s it called?”

“It hasn’t got a name,” said Drew.

“It’s called nothing yet!” said Tiger happily, to twenty-two thousand people. “It’s going to be the best guild in the world, and it’s called nothing!”

Drew put his face in his hands. He was laughing. Tiger could tell. He could always tell.

Later, when the stream was off and Drew had gone down to the inn by the basin to sleep, Tiger sat on the Top gate on his own. He sent one message to Kei, who was nine days and eleven and forty away, on the steps of a round hall with a map.

drew told me properly. i’m in. properly.

Kei answered in a minute.

Fourteen.

Tiger looked at it for a while, grinning at the dark. Then he swung his legs over the drop and sat there, on the gate he’d jumped twice now, above the gold staircase of the town. He had two falls in six weeks, and none tonight.
