Chapter 146

The Back of the Line

3,866 words · 17 min read

PenguChi had always walked at the back. In the game and out of it.

It wasn’t that she was slow, exactly, although she was. It was that from the back you could see everything. From the front you saw the road. From the middle you saw the people in front of you. From the back you saw the whole line: who was walking with whom, who was limping, who’d gone quiet. You saw who kept looking over their shoulder and who never did.

She’d told Thea that once, years ago, back when they’d all streamed together more. Thea had laughed and said you’d make a good shepherd. PenguChi had thought about it for a week and decided it was the nicest thing anyone had ever said to her.

Now there was an actual sheep, and she was still at the back. She thought that was probably fair.

By the fourth day on the Levels she’d learned the whole line by heart.

Thea at the front had stopped practising. PenguChi had noticed that on the morning after the footbridge. For three days Thea had walked with her lips moving a little, her head tipped, trying out first lines on the east. Then on the fourth morning she just walked. Her shoulders were down where they’d been up round her ears. Something had happened on the third watch, between her and Slep, by a wall. PenguChi didn’t know what. She didn’t need to. She could see it in Thea’s feet. They’d stopped trying to get anywhere and started just going.

Xola at Thea’s right, half a pace back. Xola was the easiest to read and the hardest to watch. The anger still came, PenguChi could see it. It came in waves, the way Xola had said. She’d be walking along fine, swinging the trident, and then something would cross her face, and her gills would flare. Her hand would tighten on the haft and she’d walk a bit faster, closer to Thea. But the waves were getting smaller. On the first day there’d been one every hour. On the fourth there were two all morning. And after each one, Slep’s hand would come up and rest on the back of her shoulder for a moment, and go away again, and Xola would breathe out.

Xyro had blisters. He wasn’t being brave about them. Tif was being brave about her ears, which hadn’t dried properly since the rain, and kept flicking water at everyone.

And Patience wandered from one side of the road to the other, eating thistles. Since the footbridge she’d stayed within ten paces of Slep at all times, which she’d never done before.

PenguChi walked at the back and saw all of it, and didn’t say anything, and was happy.

On Wednesday they hit the twelve hours.

You forgot about it, in the game. You walked and fought and ate and slept, three game days to every real one. Then a little window came up in the corner of your eye and told you your body had been lying in a pod for twelve hours, and it would be getting out now, thank you, whether you liked it or not.

[ Twelve hours. Your body needs you. Logging out in 10 minutes. ]

They all hit it within an hour of each other, because they’d all gone in within an hour of each other. Xyro went first, in the middle of a sentence, which was very Xyro. Then Tif. Then PenguChi, at the noon dusk, sitting on a milestone. She watched the Levels go grey and then white, and then she was lying in her own pod at home with the lid humming open over her.

It was the middle of the afternoon. The light through the window was the real kind, flat and hot. She lay there for a while, getting used to having a back that hurt. Then she got up and made something to eat, and sat on the floor of her room with it, and got her phone out.

There was a message in the group chat from Tif. Their real one, the one outside the game, with all six of them in it.

She didn’t watch Ice’s stream. She never really had. He’d raided her once, years ago, with eleven people. She’d lasted forty minutes of his stream in December, the first time she’d ever looked, before she’d decided he was boring and turned it off. Then in May, out of the egg on a night off from Millrace, she’d watched him brew potions for an hour on his stream. She’d thought, oh. He’s slow too. And then, a week later, he’d called her Penguin on the Landing at Fernwick, and she’d chased him round it four times and kicked him in the back of the knees. She’d decided he wasn’t slow at all. He just chose when not to be.

She didn’t watch his stream. But Tif had said come watch me behave.

PenguChi watched the chat go mad for a whole minute. TIF in capitals, over and over, from people with names she didn’t know. The VIPs with their little diamonds and the mods with their little green swords. It was like watching a town come out of its houses because somebody it loved had come home.

On the stream itself, Ice was underground.

She didn’t know where at first. A stone tunnel, round, old, with roots coming through the ceiling and a line of faint light running along the floor. He was walking with his shield up, the way he always walked, slow and careful, with people behind him. She could see a scythe at the edge of the picture, and a white staff, and a hammer. He streamed from inside his own eyes, so you never saw him. You only saw what he saw, and his hands, and his shield.

“Hi, Tif,” said Ice, without stopping.

What came out of the dark was a mole. PenguChi wasn’t sure, at first, because it was the size of a pony. It had hands like shovels and no eyes at all, just a pink star of feelers on the end of its nose, twitching.

Ice laughed. Not much. Just once, out loud, through his nose.

“Good,” he said. “Tell Slep good.”

PenguChi watched for two hours. Longer than she’d ever watched him before.

She didn’t really know why. Some of it was Tif, typing away happily in the chat with her little green sword, telling forty thousand strangers about blisters and rain and Kotori’s tea recipe. Some of it was the tunnel. It went on and on under a road she’d be walking down in four days, with things in it that came out of the dark. She wanted to know what was under the road.

But most of it was watching how he walked.

She’d watched a lot of people walk in the last ten weeks. She was good at it now. She could tell you which way Thea would Turn a thing just by Thea’s back foot. She could tell you when Xola was about to go under by the way her shoulders dropped. And Ice walked like somebody who’d already looked at everything. Not quickly; she’d been right about that, in May. He was slow. But it wasn’t the slowness of someone who didn’t know. It was the slowness of someone who did know, and was waiting for everybody else to catch up.

He just chose when not to be.

At the end, at about eleven, the people behind him said goodnight and went up a stair. Ice stayed at the bottom of it for a minute, alone, in the round room with the seven tunnels and the star on the floor. He didn’t do anything. He just stood there. Then he looked up the stair, at a square of light at the top, and went up.

Then she went to bed.

They were back in by Thursday’s first dawn, and the Levels ran out by noon.

It happened all at once, the way the Levels did everything. One minute grass to the edge of the world. The next, the road began to climb. The grass got rougher and browner and full of stones. Gorse came in, yellow and spiky and smelling of coconut, which Xyro refused to believe until he’d smelled it himself. Then the sky got bigger, if that was possible, and they were on a moor.

“This is the north-west road,” said Thea, looking at the map in the corner of her eye. “The last bit. Three days.”

“Three days,” said Xyro, in the voice of a man who had been promised eight and was now being told about a different three.

“It’s on the map,” said Thea.

“Everything’s on the map. That’s not the point.”

PenguChi walked at the back and watched the moor.

She didn’t like it. She couldn’t have said why, at first. It was beautiful, in a big bare way, with the clouds going over very fast and their shadows going over the ground even faster. But there were too many places for things to be. Gorse, and heather, and stones that might be stones. The road went between them in a long curve. And from the back of the line she kept getting the feeling you get when someone’s looking at you from across a room.

She didn’t say anything. Not yet. It was a feeling, and you didn’t say feelings. You waited until they turned into something you could point at.

On the fifth day they came to a stone cross.

It stood where two roads met, out on the open moor, a good way from anything. It was old, and leaning, with lichen all over it and a little ledge at the bottom where somebody had left a cup. Thea stopped in front of it. So did everyone else, because Thea had.

“I know this,” said Xola slowly.

“Ice,” said Tif. “February. Nightjar.”

PenguChi remembered then. She’d seen the clip, or part of it. Everybody had. Ten people at a stone cross at dusk, and eight in dark collars with a little bird stitched on them. A woman with two curved blades fighting the gaps instead of the line. And Ice at the front, being cut twice and not flinching. Then, at the end, the woman on the ground. Ice standing over her. And not finishing it.

Tell whoever’s paying you that every name on that road has friends on it.

“He let her go,” said Xola. “Here. He had her, and he let her go.”

Thea put her hand on the stone. She didn’t say anything. She stood there for a long time with her palm flat on the lichen.

PenguChi watched her from the back and thought about what it meant to have somebody at your mercy, and let them get up. She thought Thea was thinking about it too. She didn’t think Thea was thinking about Nightjar.

The thing that had been watching them came on the evening of the fifth day.

PenguChi had been right. She’d been right since the edge of the Levels. She’d felt it for two days and not said anything, because a feeling wasn’t a thing you could point at. Then at the second dusk, as they came down off a rise into a hollow full of bracken, she finally saw it.

Behind them. Of course behind them. Everything that was any good at hunting came from behind.

It was long and grey and lean, like a dog that had been stretched. It moved through the bracken without moving the bracken, which was the wrong thing for anything that size to be able to do. It had been following them, she realised, since the stone cross. Probably since before. It had been waiting for the hollow, where the light went first.

[ The Grey Lurcher · Lv 33 · Elite ]

She didn’t say anything.

That was the hardest thing she’d ever done in the game. Harder than the first spear thrust at Millrace, harder than a hundred buck hides. She saw it, and she knew what it was going for. It was going for the softest thing at the back of the line. That was Tif, walking just in front of her with her ears up, talking to Xyro about tea. And PenguChi didn’t say anything, because if she said anything the Lurcher would hear her, and know it had been seen, and go back into the bracken and wait for another hollow.

Instead she held.

[ Sentinel · Patience ]

She held the spear low and still and didn’t look at the thing. She walked, very slowly, with everyone else. She held, and held. She felt the strike building in her arms the way it did, the way water builds behind a sluice gate. Ten seconds. Twenty. Thirty. In front of her Tif was laughing at something. Forty. The Lurcher came closer through the bracken without moving it. Fifty. Sixty.

[ Sentinel · Patience · held 60 s ]

She’d never held for sixty. Forty-one had been her best, on a Sallow Buck, and it had nearly cleaved the thing’s head off.

Seventy.

The Lurcher stopped. She felt it stop more than saw it. It sank down into the bracken, the way cats sink before they go.

Eighty.

It went.

It came out of the bracken at Tif’s back in a single long grey leap. Its jaws were open, and it was very, very fast, faster than anything on the Levels. Tif heard it at the last second and started to turn, ears flat, eyes going wide.

PenguChi had been waiting for eighty-four seconds.

[ Sentinel · Patience · held 84 s · First strike ]

The long spear went into the Lurcher in mid-air. It went in under the front leg, where the grey fur was thinnest, and it went in with eighty-four seconds behind it. The thing stopped in the air. It actually stopped, the way a thrown stone stops when it hits a wall. It hung there on the end of the spear for a moment that went on and on. Then it fell, sideways, into the bracken, and the bracken moved this time.

[ The Grey Lurcher · 31% ]

“CHI,” shrieked Tif.

And then everybody was there. Thea’s shield was coming round, and Slep’s glaive, and Xola’s trident, and Xyro’s arrows already in the air. The Lurcher got up and tried to go back into the bracken, and found Slep there. It tried to go left, and found Thea there, and Thea Turned it right into Xola. Xola had been watching the water.

It took a minute. It was an elite. It cut Slep across the arm and put a long gash down Xola’s leg that Tif Kindled shut before it was finished bleeding. It knocked Xyro flat in the bracken. Xyro lay there going ow ow ow. But it had come into the fight with a third of itself left, and they were six, and it was one.

When it was done, PenguChi stood in the bracken with her spear in her hand and found that her arms were shaking.

“You saw it,” said Tif. She was standing very close to PenguChi with her ears all the way back. “You saw it. You knew it was behind me.”

“Since the stone cross,” said PenguChi.

“Since the stone cross?”

“I thought it was a feeling,” said PenguChi. “You can’t say a feeling. You have to wait for it to be a thing.” She looked at the grey shape in the bracken. “Then it was a thing. But if I’d said, it would have run. So I held.”

“For how long?”

“Eighty-four seconds.”

Tif stared at her. Then, without any warning at all, she hugged her. Ears and all. The ears went right up PenguChi’s nose.

“Never do that again,” said Tif, into her shoulder. “Do it every time. But never do that again.”

PenguChi didn’t know what to say to that, so she patted Tif on the back and didn’t say anything. From over Tif’s shoulder she could see Thea looking at her, and Xola, and Slep, and Xyro sitting up in the bracken. Even Patience was looking at her. The sheep was standing on a rock with her black face very serious.

“I was at the back,” said PenguChi, as if that explained it.

It did, a bit.

That night Thea came and sat beside her at the edge of the camp. PenguChi was mending the rip the Lurcher had put in Xola’s trousers.

They didn’t say anything for a while. PenguChi sewed. Thea watched.

“What do you see?” said Thea. “From the back.”

PenguChi thought about it.

“Your feet,” she said. “Mostly. You’ve stopped practising.”

“Slep said something.”

“He does that,” said PenguChi. “About once a month. It’s usually the thing.”

Thea laughed, quietly.

“What if I get there,” said Thea, “and I’ve got nothing to say? What if she’s standing there, and I’m standing there, and I open my mouth, and there’s nothing in it?”

PenguChi bit off the thread.

“Then say nothing,” she said. “Stand there. You’re good at standing there now. You’re a wheel.” She smoothed the patch flat with her thumb. “You said in May it was going to take a while. You don’t have to be okay on a schedule. You don’t have to have the words on a schedule either.”

“You said that in May,” said Thea. “On the Landing.”

“I’m still saying it,” said PenguChi. “I’m slow. I say things a long time.”

Thea leaned her head on PenguChi’s shoulder, the way she used to years ago, when they all streamed together and stayed up too late in calls. PenguChi let her. She went on sewing with Thea’s head on her shoulder, very carefully, so as not to move.

Out past the fire, on the dark moor, she could see the whole line of them, the way she always could. Xola asleep with her head on Slep’s arm. Slep awake, looking left. Xyro and Tif in a heap. Patience on her rock, keeping watch on nothing. And Thea, here, resting.

Three more days.

PenguChi sewed and watched, and waited. She was good at waiting. She’d waited eighty-four seconds once, in a hollow on a moor, with something coming. She thought she could wait three days.
