Live from Elsewhere · Volume 8: Somewhere Else

Chapter 204

Lazy

3,369 words · 15 min read

The clerk at the oldest counter in the Rim Hall looked at Kei over her spectacles for a long time and said, “Never heard of it.”

“Constellation,” said Kei again. Patiently. “Carrow Cross. The round town. Sixty.”

“Sixty what?”

“Sixty people.”

The clerk looked at him. She was very old, with spectacles on a chain and a pen behind each ear, and she’d been at this counter, Ice thought, since before the city had a name. She looked like Ama Corrie, if Ama Corrie had been put in a ledger and left there for a hundred years.

“There are four hundred and nineteen guilds registered in Masmark,” said the clerk. “As of this morning. The smallest is a company of five from somewhere in the south who keep bees.” She ran her finger down a ledger the size of a door. “The largest is the Open Hand. Eleven thousand two hundred.” She looked up. “Sixty is not a guild, young man. Sixty is a wedding.”

Behind Kei, in the queue, Hyvred made a small noise.

Ice, beside her, didn’t look at her. He could feel her not looking at him. The warmth came up the sides of his neck. He’d stopped trying to stop it a month ago.

“Sixty,” said Kei, “is us.”

The clerk looked at him for a long moment more. At the map rolled under his arm. At the ten behind him in the queue, in plain gear, dusty from fourteen days of road. At the banner on the winged girl’s back, which they’d brought with them, rolled. You brought your banner to register.

“Show me,” said the clerk.

Bee unrolled it on the counter.

The clerk looked at it. Sixty gold dots on deep blue, and the silver lines between them, and one white dot in the corner. And along the top edge, in the middle, where it had moved to in the netloft, a faint old gold star.

The clerk stopped.

She leaned forward. She looked at the star for a long time through her spectacles, very close. Then she took the spectacles off, which Ice thought she probably hadn’t done in public in fifty years, and looked at it without them.

“Where did you get this?” she said.

“Bee painted it,” said Kei.

“Not the banner,” said the clerk. “That.” She pointed at the star with one finger, not quite touching it. “That’s not paint.”

“It came,” said Bee. “By itself. When Curse wrote what it was.”

The clerk looked at the star for a long time. Then she put her spectacles back on. She took one of the pens from behind her ears and opened the ledger the size of a door at a new page. She wrote.

Constellation. Carrow Cross. Sixty.

And then, under it, in a different hand, smaller, older, as if somebody else were writing through her:

It was carried here.

She looked at what she’d written for a moment. Then she closed the ledger.

“Sixty,” she said, “is a guild.” She looked at Kei. “Next.”

Aurelie came to the netloft that afternoon.

Ice knew she would. He’d known since Vane sat down beside him on the old bench that morning and said, very quietly, she’ll come today. Vane had stayed all morning. He’d sat on the end of the bench and watched the ten unpack, and eaten a fish, and not said much. When Wendy had gone out to the harbour for an hour, he’d stood in the door himself, without being asked, and watched the lane. In case somebody comes, he’d said. Somebody always does.

She came at the first dusk, down the lane from the north terrace, with Harlan. In plain blue, no gold at all. Small and quick and quiet. Harlan behind her looked enormous and nervous, which Ice had never seen Harlan look in his life.

Wendy was back in the door.

Aurelie stopped in front of her. She looked at Wendy, at her bow and her hands in her pockets and her face, for a long moment. The way, Ice thought, she’d looked at him for a week from harbour walls and cave mouths and the back of the Rim Hall.

“I’ve heard about you,” said Aurelie. “Harlan told me. You’re the gate.”

“I’m the gate,” said Wendy.

“Everybody gets asked.”

“Every time. Doesn’t matter who.” Wendy took her hands out of her pockets. “Who’d stand next to you?”

Aurelie thought about it. Ice watched her think. It was like watching Kei think, or Vane: careful, slow, the whole thing turned over before anything came out.

“Ten thousand people,” said Aurelie.

On the bench inside, Harlan, who’d come in past her, put his face in his hands.

“That’s not a person,” said Wendy.

Aurelie looked at her. Ice saw the corner of her mouth go.

“No,” said Aurelie. “Harlan said you’d say that.” She paused. “Vane.”

Wendy looked past her, into the netloft, at Vane on the end of the bench. Vane looked back at her, very still.

“Fair,” said Wendy.

She didn’t sit at the head of anything. Ice noticed that. There wasn’t a head; the netloft had one old bench and a stone floor and a window. But the ten had sorted themselves the way they always sorted themselves, without anybody deciding. Kei in the middle of the bench with the map. Higu beside him. Gigi a pace off from Thea, at the end. Xola on Thea’s other side. Drew by the door, watching feet. Salty and Wendy on the floor against the wall. Bee under the banner.

And Ice at the far end of the bench, by the window, with Hyvred.

Not at the head. At the end. Where he always sat.

Aurelie came in and stood for a moment and looked at all of them. At where everybody was. Then she sat down on the stone floor, cross-legged, in the middle of the room. Not on the bench. On the floor, in front of all of them, where she could see every face. Like a visitor at somebody else’s fire.

She looked at the ten for a long time. Then at Ice.

“I watched you for a week,” she said.

“I know.”

“You nodded at me. From the bottom of the road. On the first day.”

“You were on the wall,” said Ice. “With Harlan. Harlan’s very big.”

Harlan, on the bench, made a wounded sound.

“I saw you go into the Undercliffs alone,” said Aurelie. “Every day. For eleven hours. I saw you queue for an hour at the oldest counter in the Rim Hall when you could have gone to the front. I saw you rent the smallest Hall in Masmark, the one nobody wanted, for ten people who weren’t here yet, and sweep it.” She paused. “I saw the Open Hand offer you anything you wanted on a harbour in front of half the city, and you said sixty chairs.”

Ice didn’t say anything.

“I saw you stand in front of a boy you didn’t know,” said Aurelie, “in a cave, with a thing coming. You took it until you were at thirty-one per cent, and walked him home backwards. And when he asked why, you said you were on your own.” She looked at him. “And I saw you read a letter on a harbour wall and go pink.”

On the bench beside him, Hyvred made a noise like a kettle. Xola, three people down, made a louder one.

“Vane told me about you for six years,” said Aurelie. “Every time somebody in my guild said the GM quit, he said the GM doesn’t quit. He goes somewhere else. I didn’t believe half of it.” She was quiet for a moment. “I believe it now. All of it. You’re the best I’ve ever seen. In six years. In any game.”

The netloft was very quiet.

“So I want to ask you something,” said Aurelie. “Just one thing. And then I’ll go.”

“Okay,” said Ice.

Aurelie looked at him. At where he was sitting, at the far end of the bench by the window, not at the head. At Kei in the middle with the map. At the banner on the hooks with the star in it.

“Why aren’t you running it?” she said.

He thought about it.

He thought about it properly, the way she’d asked it. She’d asked it properly. She’d walked down a lane to the smallest Hall in Masmark and been asked who’d stand next to her at the door, and sat on the floor.

He thought about Harlan on the top step of the Hall of Roads, in October. You always run it. You’ve never not run something in your life. About Vane on the harbour wall. You were always better at the front than at the top. You hated the top.

He thought about the year.

The duel, and the seven, one a night, until his hand shook. The spar with Xav, twenty-six minutes and then forty-one. The road of lights, ninety-four lamps, and the cold place at the end of the north tunnel every morning for months. The longest night, under the floor, with a lake standing up. Fourteen days of road to here, and a goose, and a ferry. And somewhere in all of it, a man who’d been at the front of everything since he was a boy in a game with a black sky, finding out that he was allowed to have somebody’s tail on his boot.

He was tired. That was the truth of it. Not the kind of tired you fixed with sleep. The other kind. The kind where you’d been holding a door shut with your shoulder for a very long time, and somebody had finally told you that you could let go of it, and you had. And now you just wanted to sit on a bench at the end of a table and hold the cup the same as always, and sweep under the chairs on Sundays, and go down the tunnel in the morning, and come back up.

He’d run things. He’d run a war against five thousand guilds when he was too young to name it properly. He’d run Moonlight Harvest’s line in a drowned church and the Long Watch and the Elm Below. He knew how. He was good at it.

He didn’t want to. He didn’t want to be the one who decided where everybody stood, and wrote the lists, and counted the threes, and lay awake doing the sums. Kei did that. Kei was good at it, and liked it, and was better than him.

He wanted to be the one who stood where Kei put him.

He thought about how to say all of that to a woman sitting cross-legged on the floor of a netloft, who’d run ten thousand people for six years.

“I’m lazy,” said Ice.

The netloft went completely silent.

Aurelie looked at him.

He looked back. He didn’t say anything else. He didn’t need to. It was true. Not the whole truth. But it was true enough that it would take, the way Curse’s ink took, the way Niko’s words took on the cut. And the rest of it was his.

Then, on the bench beside him, Hyvred started to laugh.

She tried not to. Ice felt her try. She put her free hand over her mouth and her shoulders went, and she leaned forward over her bow, and it came out anyway, through her fingers. And then Xola, three people down, with her gills flaring. And then Wendy, on the floor, with her head back against the wall. And Salty, who never laughed, beside her. And Thea, with tears in her eyes. And Gigi, very quietly, at the end of the bench. And Higu. And Drew, by the door.

And Kei, in the middle of the bench, with the map on his knee. Laughing so hard he had to put the map down.

“He’s not lazy,” said Gigi, through it.

“He’s a bit lazy,” said Kei.

“He’s very lazy,” said Hyvred, from behind her hand, and leaned her head on his shoulder. She didn’t seem to notice she’d done it. “He walked round the goose.”

Ice looked at Aurelie, sitting on the floor in the middle of all of it.

She wasn’t laughing. Not yet. She was looking at him, and then at the ten, laughing on a bench and a floor in a room that smelled of fish. At Hyvred’s head on his shoulder. At Kei with the map. At the banner on the hooks.

At where he was sitting. The end of the bench.

Then something happened to her face. It wasn’t big. But Ice had spent a week being watched by her, and he’d watched back. He’d never seen it do anything.

It did this.

“So am I,” said Aurelie.

The laughing stopped.

“I’m very lazy,” said Aurelie. She said it quite seriously. “I’ve been lazy for six years. I’d like to be lazy for at least six more.” She glanced at the bench, at the end of it, where a man in deep blue with not much gold on him was sitting very still. “That’s why I have Vane.”

Vane didn’t move. But Ice, who’d known him two years in a war and a week on a harbour, saw his ears go faintly red.

“Vane does the numbers,” said Aurelie. “So I don’t have to. And Harlan does the shouting. And ten thousand people do everything else.” She looked back at Ice. “I just sit at the end of a very long table, and decide where people stand, because nobody else wants to. And every so often somebody tells me the GM quit, and Vane says no, and I don’t believe him.” She paused. “I believe him now.”

She got up off the floor. She dusted off her knees. She stood in the middle of the netloft, small, plain, in blue, and looked at all of them.

“Thank you,” she said. “That’s what I wanted to see.”

“What?” said Kei.

“Where he stands,” said Aurelie. “Vane told me he was always better at the front than the top. I wanted to see what that looked like.” She looked at Ice, at the end of the bench, with Hyvred’s head on his shoulder. “It looks like that.”

She stopped in the door on her way out. Wendy moved aside for her.

“There’s going to be a race,” said Aurelie, to the room.

They looked at her.

“Tonight,” she said. “At the third dawn. It’ll go up in every Hall in Masmark at once. The island in the lake, with the tower. There’s a door at the bottom of it, under the water. It’s been shut since the city woke.” She looked out at the lake through the door. “It’s going to open in ten nights. A hall for twenty. The first great door of Masmark. And every guild in this city is going to want to be first through it.”

“Meridian?” said Kei.

“Meridian,” said Aurelie. “And Sable Court, who came down from Dawnmere on Tuesday and haven’t stopped training since. And the Open Hand. And VANTA, who are three days out on the east road. And a hundred others.” She looked at Ice. “I’d like you to be there.”

“We’re ten,” said Kei. “It’s twenty.”

“You’ve got sixty,” said Aurelie. “Haven’t you?”

She went out. Harlan went after her, with a last wild look back at Ice that said GM, GM, GM. Then Vane got up off the end of the bench, slowly. He stopped in front of Ice on his way to the door.

“You told her the truth,” said Vane.

“Most of it.”

“Most of it is more than most people tell her.” Vane looked down at him. At Hyvred, asleep now, or pretending, on his shoulder. “She doesn’t like anybody, GM.”

“I know. Harlan said.”

“She likes you,” said Vane. He went to the door. He stopped there, by Wendy, and looked back. “So do I. I never said.” He paused. “I’m lazy too.”

And he went out into the lane before anybody could answer.

The notice went up at the third dawn, the way Aurelie said it would. In every Hall in Masmark at once. In the netloft, too, on the wall beside the banner, in fresh ink, the way the old notices had gone up in the round town.

[ The Crown Below
Masmark · the island · a hall for twenty · Lv 52
Opens at the tenth dusk. ]

Kei read it. He read it twice. Then he unrolled the map and looked at the round town at the top of the old sheet, a long way north and west. And the line of road coming down to here.

“Riverbank left on the twenty-eighth,” he said. “Xav and the seven. And Xyro. They hit fifty the weekend before. Nine of them. They’ll be here the night before it opens.” He did the sum on his fingers. “Eleven. And nine. Twenty.”

“Twenty,” said Higu.

Kei looked at the notice again. Then down the bench. At Ice, at the far end, by the window. Hyvred asleep on his shoulder, really asleep now, with her bow across her knees and her tail across his boot.

“Ice,” said Kei. “Will you tank it?”

Ice looked at him.

“If you put me there,” he said.

“I’m putting you there.”

“Then yes,” said Ice.

Kei nodded. He wrote something on the map, very small, at the bottom of the new sheet, by the little square that said ours. Ice couldn’t see what. He didn’t need to.

He looked out of the wavy green window at the lake, at the island in the middle with its tower, very small in the dawn. And he thought, quite clearly, that he’d told Aurelie the truth. He was lazy. He was tired, and he wanted to sit at the end of the bench.

And he wanted to be at the front, when Kei put him there.

Both. It turned out you could be both.

He sat very still so as not to wake her, and held the cup the same as always.
