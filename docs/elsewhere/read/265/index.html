Chapter 265

It'll Be March Again

4,209 words · 18 min read

Malcolm had a new notebook, and on the first page, in his small careful hand, it said XAV V.

He’d started it in September, the night after the fourth one. He’d sat on the step above the loose one in the Seven Below and written down what he could remember of the fourth fight. Nineteen hits. Fifty-two minutes. BOOM. Two people against a wall. Then he’d turned the page and written XAV V at the top of a clean one and underlined it twice. He’d known there’d be a fifth. There was always going to be a fifth.

He’d thought it would be March.

It was the twenty-seventh of January. A Saturday. He was sitting on the same step, above the loose one, with his stick across his knees and the notebook open on top of the stick. And for once in his life he’d been wrong about something taking too long.

“You’re early,” said RX, beside him. “For you.”

“I’m on time.”

“You’ve never been on time. You’re always on time for March.” RX was eating something out of a paper twist that smelled of sugar and burnt. “What changed?”

Malcolm thought about it.

“He asked me,” said Malcolm.

Xav had asked him in October.

On the road, in the rain, on the way back from Masmark to the round town for the longest night. They’d stopped at an inn with a dripping thatch. Xav had sat down across from him at the table with his sketchbook shut and said, How do you hold still?

Malcolm hadn’t understood the question.

When you harvest, Xav had said. Moonbells. The wind on the high moor. That old dog in the farmhouse. You go up to a thing and you take a breath of what it is. And it lets you. How?

You hold still, Malcolm had said. Until it forgets you’re there.

How long does that take?

As long as it takes. Sometimes March.

Xav had looked at him for a long time across the table at the inn with the dripping thatch.

Teach me, he’d said.

So Malcolm had. All autumn. In fields, on the way home, at dawn. Sitting by moonbells in the wet for an hour without moving, until the moonbells stopped noticing them and opened. Sitting on a fence by a field of sheep until a ewe came and put her nose on Xav’s boot. Sitting by the still black pool on the moor north of Brackenford where Malcolm had once taken a breath of the dark in the water and been sick after, and not taking anything. Just sitting. Until the pool forgot them.

Xav had been terrible at it. For six weeks. He’d fidget. He’d look round. He’d move his hand to his sketchbook and the moonbells would shut.

And then, one morning in November, he hadn’t.

He’d sat by a patch of moonbells in a ditch for an hour and ten minutes without moving at all. And they’d opened round him, all of them, one by one. And he’d sat in the middle of them, open, not taking anything, with his hands on his knees. And he’d looked at Malcolm across the ditch and not said anything.

He hadn’t had to.

It’s not the fen, Malcolm had said, on the way back. The fen’s his. That’s waiting for something to come. This isn’t waiting. This is just being still.

I know, Xav had said. That’s why I wanted it.

The step was full.

RX and Malcolm. Gabe, too big for it. He’d come down from the long table with Rae, which was new. Rae sat on the step beside him with her staff across her knees. Not a pace off. Not behind. No paces at all. Azac and John had come in from wherever they’d been, with Ryan, and Val with her pages. And at the top of the stair, by the hatch, sitting on the top step with his hands on his knees, Vane.

Vane had stayed. Through January. He and Aurelie had come up for the longest night with Harlan and twenty, and the twenty had gone home to Masmark. Aurelie and Vane hadn’t. Aurelie had said we’ll walk home after the fifth one, and nobody had asked her why, and Malcolm thought he knew.

And on the bottom step, with her knees up and her tail round her feet: Hyvred.

She wasn’t calling. Again. Xav had asked her not to. But she wasn’t holding Dec’s arm either, because Dec was in the ring. So she was holding the edge of the bottom step with both hands, very tightly, as though it might go somewhere.

Malcolm wrote, in the notebook: Hyv. Both hands. The step.

He’d been writing things like that for a fortnight. Since they came home through the north door. A small list, at the back of the notebook, where he kept the things that didn’t go anywhere else. Hyv. Arm. Both hands. Banner room. Hyv. Cheek. Long table. Gabe screamed. Hyv. “He’s mine.” Ama Corrie. “I’ve got eyes.” He didn’t know why he was keeping it. He thought somebody ought to.

Dec had come down the stair ten minutes ago. Hyv had stood up off the bottom step as he went past. She’d put her hand flat on his chest, stopped him dead on the stair in front of everybody, and kissed him on the cheek. Then she’d sat back down and taken hold of the step.

Hyv. Cheek. The stair. Before a fight.

Dec had gone pink. He’d gone down into the ring pink. Malcolm had never once seen him go into a fight anything but still.

“That’s not fair,” said RX, through the sugar. “She’s done that on purpose. That’s a tactic.”

“It’s not a tactic,” said Hyvred, from the bottom step, without turning round. “It’s a kiss.”

The Seven Below was round and pale and very still. Seven silver lines in the floor meeting in a ring in the middle. Seven tunnels going out into the dark. In the north-east one, the mark on the wall from a year ago January, where Xav’s right blade had gone in under a Falling Cut.

In the ring, two people.

Dec, with his shield on his arm and his sword low. Lv 64 since Tuesday. He’d come home from the hill with a whole new kind of quiet on him. Malcolm had seen it at the long table: a man who’d been at the middle of something and come back.

And Xav, across from him, with his blades out and down. Lv 61. A new sketchbook inside his coat, with three drawings in it. Val’s two pages, flat against his chest. And a whole autumn of sitting in ditches.

That was the other new thing. Malcolm was the one who called it.

Xav had asked him that too, in December. If it goes long. If neither of us can. Somebody has to say. And Malcolm had said why me, and Xav had said because you’ll say it’s March. And you’ll be right. And we’ll listen.

Malcolm had put his name in the window. He’d never called anything in his life.

Dec went first.

He always went first. He’d sat still in the fen for four minutes at the start of the fourth one, and Xav had sat still back, and Dec had come all the way in and found nothing to meet. Oh. He wasn’t going to do that again. So he went. Straight in, on the first breath, before Xav had finished settling. Not fast. He never looked fast. He was just already there, a breath before you’d thought he would be. Shield first, the rim low. The river.

Xav did the river back.

They’d both had it since September. Dec had it off Lucan, on a bridge in Masmark. Xav had it off Dec, and had practised it on RX all the way down the road until RX dreamed about it. So for the first nineteen minutes it was two rivers. Each of them already where the other was going to be, and then not, and then somewhere else. Two people in a round room who never once stood still and never once hurried. And who hit each other, every so often, when both rivers got to the same place at the same time.

Three, wrote Malcolm. Five. Eight.

He kept two columns. D and X. He’d never kept two columns before. In the first four, there’d only ever been one worth keeping.

Every exchange was Dec’s. Malcolm could see that much from the step. Dec chose where and when, every time; he always had. Xav only ever answered. But for the first time, he answered every one.

At nineteen minutes, the columns were 11 and 10.

“He’s ahead,” said RX. “Xav’s ahead.”

“No,” said Malcolm. “Dec’s eleven. Xav’s ten.” He looked at the columns. “They’re even.”

“They’ve never been even.”

“I know,” said Malcolm. He wrote 19 min. 11 / 10. and underlined it.

The rain started at twenty-three minutes.

Dec changed it. Malcolm saw him decide. One beat he was the river, and the next he was something else. Faster, and lighter, and from everywhere. Three cuts where there’d been one, each from a different side, none of them meant. Then the fourth, meant, from wherever you weren’t looking. It was what he’d learned in the war, in two years of it, at the front of a guild called MotU. Every day it had rained.

And Xav read it.

Not with him. That was Val’s line, in the margin of her second page, from the bridge. Don’t read it with him. Read the one after. Xav didn’t watch the three cuts coming. He watched where the fourth would have to come from, after them, and was there. And put a blade on it. Not to block it. To meet it.

Fifteen, wrote Malcolm. Fifteen. Seventeen. Eighteen.

“That’s Val’s,” said RX, very quietly.

On the step below Malcolm, Val had her pages on her knee and her face in her hands. Not crying. Holding it in, so as not to make a noise.

It went on for twenty minutes. Two people in the rain, both reading the one after. Dec laughing in it. Malcolm saw him. A small open laugh, in the middle of a fight, the kind he only did in a fight with somebody who could keep up. Xav laughing back.

At forty-three minutes, Dec was at twenty-two per cent and Xav was at nineteen. The columns were 21 and 21.

Malcolm wrote it down and found his hand was shaking.

At fifty minutes, Dec brought the weight.

Malcolm knew it when he saw it. They all did. Tif’s clip of the fourth one had been watched four million times. Dec stopped. Planted his feet. Brought the shield in close and the sword back, low and long behind him, and stood there. Not the fen. The fen was waiting for something to come. This was waiting for you to come into it. The Weighted Cut, sitting there on the end of his arm like a held breath. If you went into it, BOOM, and the wall.

In the fourth one, Xav had gone into it on purpose. Just to see it come round from close. And gone across the floor into the wall at three per cent.

He didn’t go into it now.

He stopped too. Three paces off, at nineteen per cent, with his blades out and down. And he held still.

Not the fen. Not waiting. Malcolm knew exactly what it was, because he’d sat in a ditch beside it for an hour and ten minutes one morning in November. It was just being still. Xav stood in the Seven Below three paces from the Weighted Cut and held still until the room forgot he was there.

Dec looked at him.

A minute. Two. Neither of them moved. The whole of the step held its breath. Gabe had both hands over his mouth. Hyvred had let go of the step and put her hands flat on her knees, and was looking at the two of them with her eyes that saw everything, not saying anything. Not because she’d been asked not to. Because there was nothing to say.

At four minutes, Dec smiled.

Malcolm saw it from the step. A small, sudden smile, the one he did when somebody did something he hadn’t seen before. He let the Weighted Cut go. Not into anything. Just let it go, the way you’d put down something heavy you’d been holding out for somebody who hadn’t come to take it.

And went in again. The river. Because he wasn’t going to stand there. He was never going to be the one who stood there.

Xav met him.

Twenty-two, wrote Malcolm. Twenty-three. Twenty-four.

At sixty-one minutes, they hit each other at the same time.

Malcolm saw it coming. Both of them, in the rain again, both reading the one after. The one after was the same place for both of them. They got there together and they both went, and it landed both ways. Dec’s sword across Xav’s shoulder. Xav’s right blade under Dec’s shield, across his ribs.

The step made a noise all at once, like a lot of people trying not to scream.

The two of them stood in the middle of the Seven Below three paces apart. Dec with his shield up. Xav with his blades out and down.

Twenty-seven, wrote Malcolm. Twenty-seven.

Twenty-seven each. One per cent each. One more hit, either way, and it was over.

And neither of them moved.

It was obvious, after the first minute. Malcolm could see it from the step, plain as a page. Whoever went first would lose.

Not because of the weight, or the river, or the rain. Because they both knew all of it now. Every one. Dec had taught Xav the river, without meaning to, on a road. Xav had taught Dec what it felt like to be read, the one after. They’d each seen everything the other had. At one per cent, three paces apart, whoever moved first was giving the other one the next beat. And the other one would know exactly what to do with it.

So Dec didn’t move. That was the fen. He could out-wait anybody. Nobody had ever out-waited him.

And Xav didn’t move. That was the ditch. He’d learned to be still until the moonbells forgot him.

Five minutes. Six.

Gabe had stopped breathing. Rae, beside him, had put her hand on his knee without looking. RX had dropped the paper twist. On the top step, by the hatch, Vane was sitting forward with his hands clasped between his knees, very still. The way, Malcolm thought, a man might sit who’d watched the same thing every day for two years in a war and never once seen this.

Seven minutes. Eight.

Dec could have gone. Malcolm knew that too. He could see it on Dec’s face, the one he’d known since poly. He could have gone, and taken his chance, and maybe won. He always went first. He’d always had the next beat.

He didn’t take it. Malcolm watched him not take it. Watched him stand there at one per cent with the next beat in his hand, looking at Xav, and decide to keep holding it. To see if Xav would.

Xav didn’t.

Nine minutes.

Malcolm looked down at his notebook. XAV V. Two columns. 27 / 27. Seventy minutes. And under it, all the space left on the page.

He thought about how long they’d stand there.

He knew exactly how long. He’d known his whole life. It was the one thing he’d always been right about.

He closed the notebook.

“It’ll be March,” said Malcolm.

He said it out loud. Not to the notebook. Into the Seven Below, in his ordinary calm voice. And both of them, in the ring, at one per cent, three paces apart, turned their heads and looked at him.

“It’ll be March,” said Malcolm again, “before either of you goes. I know it will. I’ve sat in ditches with him.” He looked at Xav. “And I’ve known you since poly.” He looked at Dec. “You’ll stand there till March. Both of you. And then the egg’ll come at eight and you’ll go out at one per cent and come back tomorrow and stand there again.” He opened the window. His hand was shaking. “So I’m calling it.”

Dec said yes first. Of course he did. He always went first. He’d pressed it before Malcolm had finished reading the window out loud. As though he’d been waiting for somebody to say it.

Xav looked at the window in front of him for a long time.

Nobody made any noise for about three seconds.

Then Gabe did, and it set everybody off.

Malcolm didn’t hear most of it. He was watching the ring.

Xav put his blades away. Slowly, one and then the other. He looked across the three paces at Dec, who’d lowered his shield, and was looking back.

“Closer,” said Xav.

“Yes,” said Dec.

“Again,” said Xav.

“Yes,” said Dec.

And Xav sat down. Not fell; sat. On the floor of the Seven Below, where he was, and then shuffled back on his hands to the wall, the north-east wall, under the mark. And put his back against it and his head back against the stone, and closed his eyes.

Dec came and sat down beside him. Against the wall. Shoulder to shoulder. The way they had in September. Two people who’d fought each other for a very long time and hadn’t finished.

“You learned a fifth one,” said Dec. “I told you not to.”

“Off Malcolm,” said Xav, with his eyes shut. “Not a goose.”

Dec turned his head and looked up the step, at Malcolm, with the notebook shut on his knee.

“It’s not the fen,” said Dec.

“No,” said Malcolm. “It’s a ditch.”

“It’s better than the fen,” said Dec. “For him. The fen’s for waiting for something. He’s not waiting for anything.” He looked back at Xav, against the wall, eyes shut, still. “He’s just there.”

Xav opened one eye.

“Don’t,” he said.

“You’re the best I’ve ever fought,” said Dec. “I said it in September. It’s still true. It’s more true.”

“Dec.”

“I’m allowed,” said Dec. “Somebody told me.”

Xav put his arm over his eyes. Malcolm saw his shoulders go. The laugh, the one from September, that wasn’t quite a laugh.

Hyvred lasted about a minute.

She’d let go of her knees. She came down off the bottom step and across the floor of the Seven Below, through the ring, past the silver lines. She stood in front of the two of them against the wall for a moment, looking down. Then she sat down on Dec’s other side, against the wall, and took hold of his arm with both hands. And put her head on his shoulder.

“Can I have him back,” said Xav, from under his arm, “for one minute?”

“No,” said Hyvred.

“Thirty seconds.”

Hyvred thought about it.

“Fine,” she said. And didn’t let go.

On the step, RX made a noise like a kettle. Gabe had his face in Rae’s shoulder and was crying, loudly, in a way that sounded a lot like screaming. Rae was patting him on the back and looking at the ceiling with an expression of great patience.

Malcolm opened the notebook again. He turned to the back, to the small list. He wrote: Hyv. Both hands. The wall. After a draw. Xav asked for thirty seconds. Didn’t get them.

Then he turned back to the front, to XAV V, and under 27 / 27 and 70 min, he wrote: Drawn.

And under that, after a moment, smaller: Not March.

Vane came down the stair.

He’d never come down before. In September he’d sat at the top. Today he’d sat at the top too, through all seventy minutes, without moving. Now he came down the steps past all of them, past RX and Gabe and Rae and Val and Malcolm, tall and still. He stopped at the bottom, at the edge of the ring, and looked at the two of them against the wall. Three of them, with Hyvred.

“GM,” said Vane.

Dec looked up.

“Two years,” said Vane. “In the war. Every day. I never saw anybody stand in front of you at one per cent.” He was quiet. “I never saw you not take it.”

“I didn’t want to,” said Dec.

“I know,” said Vane. “I saw.” He looked at Xav, under his arm. “Next one?”

“May,” said Xav, from under his arm. “Malcolm says.”

“I didn’t say May,” said Malcolm.

“You were going to,” said Xav.

Malcolm thought about it.

“May,” he said.

Vane nodded. He turned and went back up the stair, past all of them. At the top, at the hatch, he stopped. He said something to Aurelie, who had been standing behind Ama Corrie’s counter the whole time where nobody could see her, watching down the stair. Malcolm didn’t hear what. But he saw Aurelie’s face, and he saw her nod. And he thought that whatever they’d stayed for, through January, they’d seen it.

Afterwards, RX made tea. It was terrible.

Malcolm sat on the step above the loose one with his cup. He watched Xav, against the north-east wall, with the new sketchbook open on his knee. For the first time since September, he was drawing.

Two people against a wall. And a third, on the far side of the one in the middle, holding on to his arm with both hands.

And on the step above them, very small, in the corner of the page: a man with a stick across his knees and a notebook shut on top of it. Malcolm hadn’t known Xav had seen him there.

“Is that me,” said Malcolm.

“It’s the one who called it,” said Xav, not looking up. “Somebody had to.”
