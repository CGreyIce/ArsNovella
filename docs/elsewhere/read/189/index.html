Live from Elsewhere · Volume 7: Keep the Lamps

Chapter 189

Keep the Lamps

4,088 words · 18 min read

Vivian’s message came at eight on Christmas Eve, on the dot, the way they always did.

[ Messages · Vivian
Vivian: Mr Declan.
Vivian: A crate has arrived at the office from Jonker Street. Twenty-four bottles. There is a card.
Vivian: The card says “From Lim Brothers, for the longest night. It is bitter. We are very proud.” It is signed by Mr Lim and, separately, by his daughter, in purple ink.
Vivian: I will bring it on Monday.
Vivian: The auntie also wishes you to know she watched the clip of the old lady and cried into the char kway teow.
Vivian: Merry Christmas.
]

He read it on the edge of his pod in the back room at Melaka Raya, with the fan going round. He read it twice.

[ Messages · Vivian
Ice: thank you
Ice: tell the auntie i didn’t see it
Ice: i was under the floor
Vivian: I will tell her. She will say that is very like you.

He laughed, a bit, on his own. Gabe was in the kitchen making the kopi too sweet. Suzu had gone to Singapore on the six o’clock bus for Christmas with his mum. He’d left his pod with its lid down and the little light off, and his jar on the windowsill beside the empty bottle from Jonker Street. He’d said he wouldn’t need it till Monday.

He’d been wrong about that.

He went in at nine, and the old woman in the chair by the north door was awake, and looking at him.

He came out of the dark into the first of the nine, the way he always did. The banner room was quiet. Friday morning, Christmas Eve, two days after the longest night. Most of the guild was still asleep in the real world, or out on the roads, or in the gallery, or at the Hart. A few chairs sat in. Kotori by the window with a pot, waiting for something to need tea. Bee drawing. Hyvred beside her, not looking at him, very carefully.

And at the north end of the room, by itself, facing north, the chair.

She was sitting in it with Ama Corrie’s grey blanket round her shoulders and her stick against the wall and the lamp on her knees, still lit. Thea was asleep on the floor beside the chair, sitting up, with her back against the wall and her tower shield across her knees. She’d been there since Wednesday night, Kotori said. Sitting watch. She hadn’t been asked. Nobody had needed to.

The old woman looked at him down the whole length of the long table.

He got up and went down to her.

He didn’t know why. He’d sat at the end of the table on Wednesday night and watched her tell it, and not come forward. It hadn’t been his. He’d been under the floor. He hadn’t seen her come home. He’d run for the hatch at seven fifty-nine and the egg had taken him halfway across the Seven Below, with Bee’s hand reaching for his.

But she was looking at him, and he went.

He stopped a pace off. Thea didn’t wake.

“You’re the one under the floor,” said Isa Corrie.

“Yes.”

“I felt it go,” she said. “The still water. On the longest night. At six, under the round town. I was eleven miles up the road. I felt it stand up, all of it, the whole lake. My grandmother told me about it. There’s a lake under the round town, she said. It’s been there since before the town. One day it’ll stand up.” She looked at him with her bright tired black eyes. “And then I felt it go. Into the running water. All at once. Like a bell.”

“It went into the stream,” said Ice.

“I know,” said Isa. “Somebody put it there.” She looked at his shield, on his back. At his hands. “Nobody’s ever done that. In three hundred years. It’s always just stood there under the town, waiting.”

He didn’t say anything.

“What’s your name?” said Isa.

“Ice.”

She thought about it, the way old people think about a name, turning it over.

“That’s not a name,” she said.

“It’s the one I’ve got.”

“Hm,” said Isa Corrie. It sounded exactly like Ama Corrie. He nearly laughed.

He sat down on the floor, on the other side of the chair from Thea, the way you’d sit beside somebody’s grandmother at a wedding. Cross-legged, with his shield across his knees. Isa looked down at him. She seemed to approve.

“What did you do on the last one?” she said. “The longest night. A year ago. Before the road.”

“A ford,” said Ice. “In a fen. Fernwick. There’s a ring of stones. Nine herons.” He thought about it. “One of them faces out. Because somebody has to watch.”

Isa was quiet for a long time.

“Set one to watch,” she said.

“So the rest may sleep,” said Ice.

“That’s ours,” said Isa. “That line. That’s the old ones’ line. Before they went.” She looked at the lamp on her knees. “We left it in every town we went through, going north. Everywhere. One line, in one house, with one person. So that if we never came back, somebody would still be saying it.”

“A woman in Fernwick said it,” said Ice. “Every night. To her son. He told me.”

“Then it worked,” said Isa.

She looked down at him for a long time. He let her. He’d learned that from Ama Corrie, and from Suzu, and from a man in a fen called Hollis who used to look at him like that over a dummy he was mending. You let old people look.

“You held a ford,” she said at last. “And then you went under a town. And you never stood where anyone could see you.”

“Not always,” said Ice.

“No. Not always.” She nodded. “That’s what the watching heron’s for. It faces out. Nobody sees its face.” She smiled her small walnut smile. “My mother would have liked you. She’d have said you were too quiet. But she’d have liked you.”

He didn’t know what to say to that. So he didn’t say anything, and sat on the floor by the chair, and let it be true.

“I can’t carry it any more,” said Isa, after a while.

She said it plainly. The way you’d say it was raining. She was looking at the lamp on her knees, the little black iron cage with the hood and the star, and the flame in it, gold, steady, that had been lit for three hundred years.

“I’m the last,” she said. “There’s no daughter. I walked home on it. That was the last walk I had.” She turned the cage a little in her root hands. “I’ll keep it till I can’t. But somebody’ll have to keep it after.”

Ice looked at the lamp.

“Not me,” he said.

Isa looked at him sharply.

“Not because I won’t,” said Ice. “Because it’s not mine.” He thought about it. “It’s not always me.”

Isa looked at him for a long moment. Then her face did something he hadn’t seen it do yet. Something under the walnut, very old, very pleased.

“No,” she said. “It isn’t. Good.” She looked at the lamp again. “Then whose?”

He knew.

He’d known since he came in, since he saw her looking at him down the long table. He’d known since a corner table at the Hart three weeks ago, a jar between two people and a cat. Malcolm writing It wasn’t March at the bottom of a notebook. Suzu with his hand on the lid, saying it’s still not empty. It’s ready. For something else. I’ll know when it’s time.

He got up off the floor.

“Wait,” he said.

Suzu came in at eleven, from a booth at the Cradle Commons in Singapore, on Christmas Eve, because Ice had messaged him.

He came out of the dark into the first of the nine’s neighbour, the second chair, his chair, beside Ice. He sat there for a moment, looking down the long table at the north end of the room. At the chair, and the old woman in it, and Thea asleep on the floor beside her, and the lamp.

Then he got up and went down the room, with his pack.

Ice went with him. He didn’t go all the way. He stopped three paces back, beside Kotori, who’d come over from the window with her pot and stood waiting. It was Suzu’s. It had always been Suzu’s.

Suzu stopped in front of the chair.

Isa looked up at him. At his face, which was doing what it always did, which was nothing. At his hands, which were very still.

He took the jar out of his pack.

A plain clay jar, about the size of two hands cupped together. A lid. A woman called Tamsin had given it to him in Larkhollow on the chalk downs, a year ago, in the first weeks. For keeping old lights in.

Isa saw it and stopped breathing.

“Tamsin’s,” she said.

Suzu looked at her.

“Larkhollow,” said Isa. “On the downs. The chandlers.” Her root hands had tightened on the lamp. “They made those. For us. For the old ones. To carry the lights in, when the lamps were too far apart. A jar from Larkhollow remembers keeping.” She was looking at it the way Ama Corrie had looked at the old Roll in September. “My grandmother had one. It cracked. On the way back from the edge, the year I was born. She cried for a week.” She looked up at Suzu. “Where did you get it?”

“Tamsin,” said Suzu. “Larkhollow. She said it was for keeping old lights in.”

“What lights?”

“Eighty-one,” said Suzu. “From a barrow on the downs. I carried them all spring. Then I let them go. One at a time. In a gallery in the dark. Into eighty-one lanterns.” His face was doing nothing. His thumb was resting on the lid, very still. “Kept: none. Let go: eighty-one.”

Isa looked at him for a long time.

“And then,” said Suzu, “a hundred and sixteen wicks. For the road. One at a time. In December. So they’d remember being kept.” He paused. “I said every number.”

“You lit the road,” said Isa slowly.

“Malcolm did. And Ina, and the smiths, and Maigo and Hako. And everyone.” Suzu looked at the jar. “I just had the jar.”

“Hm,” said Isa Corrie.

She held out the lamp.

Not to him. Not yet. She held it out between them, in both hands, the little black iron cage with its hood and its star and its gold flame. She held it out the way you’d hold out something you’d been carrying for longer than you’d been alive, to see if somebody would take it.

“It’s three hundred years old,” said Isa. “The flame. It’s never been out. Not once.” Her voice was very thin. “If it goes out, it goes out. There isn’t another one.”

“I know,” said Suzu.

“It’s very heavy.”

“I know,” said Suzu. “Things that have been kept a long time are.” He took the lid off the jar. “It doesn’t have to go anywhere. Not yet. It can be home for a bit.” He held the jar out, open, beside the lamp. Not reaching. Just there. “Go on,” he said.

That was what Sedge had said. Ice remembered. In Fernwick, a long time ago, to a boy with a jar full of lights who didn’t know where they went. Go on.

Isa looked at the open jar for a long time.

Then she opened the little door of the cage.

He’d tell it to nobody. Not on stream; he hadn’t been streaming. He’d gone dark at half past ten, before Suzu came in, because it wasn’t theirs. Tif’s counter would say four hundred and nineteen in the morning, and the chat would say he found something, and he had. He’d tell Gabe a bit, at the mamak on Christmas night, over roti. That was all.

But he’d remember it the way he remembered the first night in the fen. All of it, every second.

The flame came out of the cage.

Not on a wick. On its own. A small gold light, the size of a thumb, the colour of the dawn coming through the eye of the dome. It came out of the little open door of the black cage and hung in the air between the lamp and the jar for a moment, very still.

“One,” said Suzu, very quietly.

It went into the jar.

He put the lid on. He put his hand flat on top of it, the way he’d sat at the corner table in the Hart for eleven nights. He stood there in front of the chair by the north door with the jar against his chest and his hand on the lid.

The jar glowed. Faintly. Through the clay. The way the silver lines in the Seven Below glowed, as if the stone remembered something.

Suzu looked at the window for a long time.

His face did something.

Ice saw it from three paces back. Kotori, beside him, saw it too; he heard her breathe in. It wasn’t much. You’d only have seen it if you’d known him a long time. A small change at the corners of his mouth and round his eyes, all at once, like a door opening somewhere a long way off, and light coming through.

“Okay,” said Suzu. Very softly. To the jar.

Isa sat in the chair with the empty cage in her lap and looked at him.

“There,” she said. “Kept.”

And then the last of the Corries who went north, who had walked home along a road of lights on the longest night, behind the dark, lighting every lamp it put out, put her head back against the chair by the north door and closed her eyes. She went to sleep. Just like that. Like somebody who’d put down something very heavy at last and found there was nothing left that she had to do.

Thea woke up.

She opened her eyes on the floor beside the chair, with her shield on her knees. She looked up at the old woman asleep. At the empty cage in her lap. At Suzu, standing there with a glowing jar against his chest and his face doing something. At Ice and Kotori, three paces back.

She didn’t ask what had happened. She looked at all of it for a moment. Then she looked at Ice.

“Did I miss it?” said Thea.

“Yes,” said Ice.

“Was it good?”

He thought about it.

“Yes,” he said.

Thea nodded slowly, as if that was enough. Then she shifted her shield on her knees, and leaned her head back against the wall beside the chair, and went on sitting watch.

Ama Corrie hung the empty cage on a hook by the north door that afternoon.

She did it herself, standing on a stool from the kitchen, with Ina holding the stool. The hook had been there for three hundred years. Nobody had known what it was for. It was the right height, exactly, the way the hollow in the first waystone had been the right size for a cage. She hung the little black lamp on it, hooded side to the room, open bars to the north door. Empty. No flame.

“So they know,” said Ama Corrie, getting down. “Whoever comes in that way. That somebody came home.”

“Will anyone else?” said Ina. “Come in that way?”

Ama Corrie looked at the north door. At the hook, and the empty cage on it. At the chair beside it, and the old woman asleep in it.

“There’s a ring of stones on a hill,” she said, “in the middle of the dark. A kept place. She told me. With nobody in it now.” She sniffed. “Somebody’ll want to go and look at it. One day. Somebody always does.” She looked at Ina over her spectacles. “Not this year.”

“Not this year,” agreed Ina.

On Christmas morning, at the first dawn, Xav was in the ring in the market, practising a back foot.

Ice saw him from the south steps. The chalk mark on the paving, worn now, redrawn. The two blades. The step, the back foot staying, a breath, coming round. Over and over. The pigeon asleep on its dark stone, as it had been since before Ama Corrie’s mother.

He went down the steps the long way round, by the east side, so he wouldn’t walk past.

At the corner by the chestnut stall, Xav said, without stopping, “January.”

“January,” said Ice.

“Second week. Downstairs.”

“Okay.”

“Don’t make it a thing.”

“I won’t,” said Ice. And then, because it was Christmas, and because Xav had sat cross-legged at the edge of every ring that year with a sketchbook on his knee: “You’ve got better.”

Xav stopped.

He stood in the ring with his blades in his hands and his back foot on the chalk mark, and didn’t move. Then he looked up, across the market, for the first time.

“Have I?”

“The back foot,” said Ice. “You wait on it now. A breath.”

Xav looked at him for a long moment.

“I stole it,” he said.

“I know,” said Ice. “I borrow things. You steal them.” He shrugged. “It’s yours now.”

Xav laughed. Out loud, in the empty market, on Christmas morning, the way he’d laughed on the floor of the Seven Below with seven per cent and a headache. And then he went back to stepping. And Ice went on to the north door, a little faster than usual.

He sat in the first of the nine that night, at the second dusk, and the room filled up round him.

It filled up the way it always did. Gabe telling somebody about the second heart, and the lake, and Salty’s hundred-and-four, all in one breath. Leaka and Xola shouting ICEY BOI at each other in the doorway. Hana and Milli by the window with their hands on the arm between their chairs. Salty with a black sword the right size across the back of his chair and Wendy asleep on his shoulder. Tiger, who’d kept the last lamp on the road for twenty minutes, being made to tell it again by Riverbank and going redder each time. Chernn on her stool by Deston, dipping a candle in a little pot of wax Mother Dipp had given her, very straight. Riverbank at the long end of the second table, Malcolm with his notebook, the last page of it showing: It wasn’t March. Suzu in the second chair beside him with a clay jar in his lap, glowing faintly through the clay. His hand flat on the lid.

Kei at the head with his map unrolled, the road on it. Ninety-four dots and twenty-two, every one with a cross through it and a circle round the cross. And at the top, past the last stone, where he’d written being carried in November, one more word in very small letters. Home.

And Hyvred, beside Bee, halfway down the table.

She looked at him. Openly, for once. Across the room. She didn’t look away when he looked back.

He knew. He’d known for a while.

“Hi, Hyv,” he said, down the table.

“Hi,” said Hyvred.

Her tail did a thing. He saw it. Bee, beside her, without looking up from her drawing, moved one wing.

He held the cup Kotori had brought him the same as always.

At the far end of the room, at the north end, by the north door, the chair was not empty.

The old woman was asleep in it, with Ama Corrie’s grey blanket round her and her stick against the wall. Above her, on its hook, an empty black lamp with a star on top, its open bars toward the door she’d come home by. And Ama Corrie, with the fen-reed broom, the one with KEPT on its tag in blue ink, going round under it.

Under, not round. Slowly. With her humming the tune that wasn’t a tune.

He watched her for a long time.

He thought about a light on the edge of the world that hadn’t gone out for three hundred years. About ninety-four lamps on the backs of grey stones, facing the wrong way, so only the dark could see. About sixty people and a stool, in threes, on the longest night, so nobody was alone. And a hundred and ninety-one candles in a hundred and ninety-one windows, so nobody would be sitting in the dark. And a woman in a long grey coat walking down a road behind the night with a lamp, lighting every one it put out.

So the rest could sleep.

Keep the lamps, the old ones had said. Not light them. Anyone could light a lamp. Keep them. Mother to daughter. Night after night. Whether anyone came home or not.

He looked down the long table at everybody he’d known for years, and everybody he’d known for a few months, and one old woman he’d known for two days. All in one room, on one map, at one table, with a chair at the end that somebody was sitting in.

Then he got up, and went to the corner by the north door, and held out his hand.

Ama Corrie looked at him over her spectacles for a long time.

“Six,” she said.

But she gave him the broom.
