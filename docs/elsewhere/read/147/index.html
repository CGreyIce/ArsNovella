Live from Elsewhere · Volume 6: There's a Chair

Chapter 147

Six and a Sheep

3,557 words · 15 min read

On the seventh day the weather cleared, and the moor went on for ever in every direction under a sky like washed glass. Xyro asked Slep a question he’d clearly been saving up.

“How do you know,” said Xyro, “when to say things?”

They were walking at the side of the road, a bit behind the others, because Xyro’s blisters had got better and his pace had got worse. Slep had dropped back to walk with him without deciding to. Somebody had to be on that side too.

“What things?” said Slep.

“Things. Any things. You hardly ever say anything, and then when you do, it’s like—“ Xyro waved his bow at the sky. “It’s like the thing. Like at the footbridge. She was going to stay on her own. Or the wall, with Thea, whatever you said to her, because she’s been different ever since. How do you know?”

Slep thought about it for nearly a minute. Xyro waited, which was unusual for Xyro and a sign that he really wanted to know.

“I wait,” said Slep, “until nobody else has said it.”

“That’s it?”

“Mostly.” Slep shifted his glaive to the other shoulder. “Most things get said. If you wait. Somebody says them. Usually the right person. Xola, or Thea, or Chi, or you.” He looked at the road. “And if nobody does, then that’s the one that’s left. So I say that.”

Xyro walked for a bit, thinking. You could actually see him do it.

“I never wait,” said Xyro.

“I know.”

“I just say everything. Straight away. All of it. Even the wrong stuff.”

“I know.”

“Is that bad?”

“No,” said Slep. “Somebody has to say things first. Then I know what’s left.” He nodded up the road, at Tif, who was walking with PenguChi and talking about tea. “And she likes it. When you say everything. She told Xola. She said it’s like living next to a radio.”

“Is that good?”

“She said she likes the radio,” said Slep.

Xyro went very red and walked faster, and caught up with Tif, and didn’t say anything at all for nearly ten minutes. Slep thought it was probably the longest Xyro had ever gone without talking, in the game or out of it.

Xola had stopped having the waves.

Slep noticed on the seventh morning. Not all at once. It was more that he’d been waiting for one since they broke camp, and it didn’t come. Then another hour went by, and it didn’t come either. By noon he realised his hand hadn’t gone to her shoulder once since dawn.

She was quiet instead. That was new. Xola was never quiet; Xola narrated. She’d tell you what the clouds looked like, and what the road was doing, and what she’d have for dinner if dinner were up to her. She’d been doing it since the first day at Millrace, when she’d come up out of the mill pond shouting about her gills. She’d done it the whole way across the Levels, even in the rain. Even on the bad days.

Today she walked at Thea’s right shoulder with the trident over her own and didn’t say anything for hours.

Slep walked on her other side and didn’t say anything either. He was good at that. He waited.

It came at the noon dusk, when they stopped by a stream to eat. Xola sat down on a rock with her feet in the water. Her gills opened, the way they always did near running water, as if they couldn’t help it.

“I’m not angry any more,” she said.

“I know.”

“I’m—“ She stopped. She looked at the water going over her feet. “I’m scared, Slep.”

“I know.”

“You keep saying I know.”

“I keep knowing,” said Slep.

She laughed, a bit, and wiped her face with the back of her wrist.

“It was easier being angry,” she said. “Angry’s loud. You can hear it. You can see where it is. Scared’s just—“ She made a shape with her hands, a sort of fog. “It’s just there. All over. It doesn’t go anywhere. I can’t push it.”

“No,” said Slep. “You can’t push scared.”

“Then what do you do with it?”

He sat down on the rock next to her. The stream went over both their feet now. Patience came and stood in it too, which she’d never done before. She looked very surprised by the water, and stayed anyway.

“You give it to somebody,” said Slep. “For a bit. So you can put it down.”

“Who?”

“Me,” said Slep. “I said. At Millrace. I’ll take the scared side.” He looked at the water. “You can’t push it. But you can hand it over. I’ll carry it for a while. You carry Thea.”

Xola didn’t say anything for a long time. Then she put her head on his shoulder, the way she had by the fire on the first night.

“How do you know all this stuff?” she said into his sleeve.

“I wait,” said Slep. “And then it’s what’s left.”

They found the walkers in the middle of the afternoon.

There were three of them, which was the first thing that was wrong, because three people alone on the moor this far out was not a good number. The second thing was that they were running. The third thing, when the six came over a rise and saw them properly, was the hounds.

Thea didn’t stop. She didn’t look round to check. She came off the rise at a run with the shield coming off her back, and everybody went with her, because that was what they did now. There’d been a time when they’d have stopped and said should we. There wasn’t any more.

“Down,” Thea shouted at the three, and they went down, all at once, flat in the heather, and the first hound went over them and into her shield.

She Turned it into Slep. He was there.

It was a short fight. Four hounds, Lv 31, against six people who’d been killing Lv 31 things for a fortnight and an elite the day before. Xola went under the hounds, low, the way she went under water. PenguChi held for twelve seconds, which for her was practically a sneeze. Xyro’s arrows came back. Tif rang Kindle on the one called Fennimore before a hound’s teeth found him, because she’d seen it looking at him. And Patience stood on a rock and watched, with the air of a sheep who’d done this before and didn’t need to prove anything.

When it was over the three walkers sat up in the heather, very slowly, and looked at them.

“Oh my god,” said the one called CaseyWren. She was young, with a short sword she clearly didn’t know how to use, and a lot of mud. “Oh my god. You’re AltheaVT.”

Thea blinked.

“I watch you,” said CaseyWren. “Every night. The wheel thing. I love the wheel thing.” She looked at the others. “And you’re xolalotl. And PenguChi. And—“ She looked at Slep, and at the sheep, and her face did something. “Patience,” she said, the way you’d say the name of a celebrity.

Patience looked at her and chewed.

They were going to the round town. Of course they were. Everybody on this road was going to the round town. They’d heard about the gate. The famous gate, where a woman asked you a question before she’d let you in. Not a test, exactly. Just a question. And everybody had a theory about what you were supposed to say.

“Who’d stand next to you?” said dot_and_carry. He was a small anxious boy with a bow even worse than CaseyWren’s sword. “That’s what she asks. Wendy. Everybody knows. And you have to have an answer, or they don’t—well. They let you in anyway, I think. But you have to have one.”

“We haven’t got anybody,” said Fennimore. He said it very matter-of-factly. “We met at a bus stop. In the game. Five days ago. That’s all. We don’t know who’d stand next to us.”

“I’d say her,” said CaseyWren, pointing at dot_and_carry. “Because she’s got the map.”

“I’ve got the map because you lost it.”

“Still.”

Slep watched Thea listen to all this.

She was very still. She was crouched down in the heather beside them, with her shield on the ground and her hand on its rim, and she was listening the way she listened to Higu on the phone.

“Are you going to the round town?” said CaseyWren. “Are you going to join? Constellation? Everyone says you are. Chat says. Everyone’s chat says.”

Thea looked at her for a moment.

“We’re going to see a chair,” said Thea.

CaseyWren obviously didn’t understand that at all, and was much too polite to say so.

“Who would you say?” said dot_and_carry. “At the gate. If Wendy asked you. Who’d stand next to you?”

And Thea opened her mouth. Slep saw her open it. He saw her look at Xola, and then at the rest of them. Then her eyes went past them all, east, along the road, toward a place she couldn’t see yet.

She closed her mouth again.

“I don’t know,” said Thea. “I’ll find out when I get there.”

They walked the three of them to the next waystone, because it was getting dark and three people at Lv 22 on the north-west moor at night was a number Slep didn’t like at all. CaseyWren talked to Xola the whole way about the wheel thing and the river thing and how she’d watched the trident clip forty times. Xola talked back, more than she’d talked all day. Slep watched her do it and was glad.

At the waystone, Thea said goodbye. Then she stood there watching them go off up the road in the dusk, three small shapes with a bad sword and a worse bow and a map, going toward a gate.

“They don’t know who’d stand next to them,” said Thea.

“No,” said PenguChi, beside her.

“Five days. A bus stop. And they’re walking eight days to a gate to be asked a question they can’t answer.” Thea’s voice was very quiet. “And they’re going anyway.”

“Yeah,” said PenguChi. “That’s sort of the point. I think.”

Thea didn’t say anything else. But Slep saw her hand go to her shield-strap, and stay there, and tighten.

That night, Slep had the second watch. He heard Thea and Xola talking by the fire, low, when they thought he was looking the other way.

He was looking the other way. Left, the way he always did. But he could hear.

“I’m not angry,” said Xola. “I told Slep. I’m scared.”

“Me too,” said Thea.

“What of?”

There was a long pause. The fire spat.

“That I’ll go back to being small,” said Thea. “That I’ll see her and I’ll be—the old size. That I’ll start looking for the right answer again. In my head. Before she’s even said anything. Just out of habit.” Her voice was steady, the way Thea’s voice got when she’d thought about a thing for a long time and decided to say it straight. “I’ve spent ten weeks at the front of a line, Xo. With a shield. I’ve been a wheel. And I’m frightened I’ll walk through that gate and be the one behind again, in about a second.”

Xola didn’t say anything at first.

“You won’t,” she said. “You’re at the front now. I’ve seen you. You’re not even the same shape.”

“What are you scared of?”

“That I won’t be enough,” said Xola. “Again. That something will happen, and I’ll be standing right next to you, and it won’t be enough. Like before.”

“You’ll have Slep,” said Thea.

There was a silence.

“He told you that too?” said Xola.

“By a wall,” said Thea. “On the Levels. He said he’d be on your other side, so you could lean on him. So I wouldn’t have to hold you up as well.”

“He said that to me,” said Xola. “By the fire. The first night. I asked if he’d just thought of it, and he said—“

“At Millrace,” they said together.

And then the two of them were laughing. Trying not to, because the others were asleep, and failing completely. Slep sat on his rock looking left with the glaive across his knees and felt his ears go hot. He hadn’t known it was possible to be that embarrassed and that happy at the same time.

“He’s been planning this since Millrace,” said Xola, through it.

“He plans everything since Millrace.”

“He carried the sheep.”

“I know,” said Thea. “I was there.”

Their laughing ran down slowly, into the fire. When it had stopped, Thea said, in a different voice, “Xo. Whatever happens. When we get there.”

“Yeah.”

“I’m glad it’s you. On my side. I’m glad it’s always been you.”

“Shut up,” said Xola, thickly. “You’ll make me cry, and I’ve only just stopped.”

Slep looked left, at the dark moor, for a long time, and didn’t turn round.

At noon on the eighth day a cart caught them up.

It was the first cart they’d seen in four days. A low flat thing behind a brown pony, loaded with sacks of something lumpy, and a man on the front in a hat that had given up being a hat some time ago. He slowed down to walk beside them. Then he looked at the six of them, and at the shield and the trident and the spear, and lastly at the sheep, and something in his face went sharp and pleased.

“You’ll be the six,” he said.

Thea, at the front, nearly tripped.

“Turnips,” said the man, nodding at his sacks, as if that were the real question. “For the round town. Every eighth day. I’m Hobb.” He looked at them all again. “Six, and a sheep. They said there’d be a sheep. I didn’t believe it.”

“Who said?” said Xola.

“Everybody,” said Hobb. “Whole town knows. Ama Corrie’s set out six chairs by the window in the banner room, and she don’t set out chairs for nobody. When she sets one out, they come. That’s what folk say.” He clicked his tongue at the pony. “There’s a book on it at the Hart. Not a book book. A betting book. When, not if. I’ve got the end of the month.”

Slep watched Thea’s face go through about four things at once.

“What if they don’t?” said Thea. “Come.”

Hobb looked at her for a while, the way NPCs sometimes looked at you, as if they were reading something on your face that you hadn’t put there on purpose.

“Then she sweeps under them,” he said. “Every Sunday. Same as she did the seventeen. Same as her mother did, and hers. There’s chairs in that Hall been swept under for longer than I’ve been alive.” He shrugged. “Nobody minds. Chairs don’t mind. That’s the thing about a chair. It just stands there till you want it.”

Nobody said anything for a bit. The cart creaked. Patience walked alongside the pony, and the pony looked at her with deep suspicion.

“You’ll be in by tomorrow,” said Hobb. “If you don’t dawdle.” He touched the brim of his ruined hat, to Thea in particular. “I’ll tell the Hart. They’ll want to know. I’ve got the end of the month, mind. Dawdle a bit.”

He clicked to the pony again and the cart went on ahead of them, faster, up the long hill. They watched it go until it was a dot.

“There’s a betting book,” said Xyro.

“On us,” said Tif.

“On when,” said PenguChi. “Not if.”

Thea didn’t say anything at all. But Slep, walking on Xola’s other side, saw her look at the dot of the cart for a long time. Then she looked at the sheep, who was walking along very pleased with herself, as if she’d known about the book all along.

“It just stands there till you want it,” said Xola quietly.

“Yeah,” said Thea.

They didn’t dawdle.

On the eighth day they came over the last rise.

It was evening. They’d walked all day without stopping much, because everybody wanted to and nobody said so. The road had been climbing for hours, slow and steady, up a long hill of heather with a wind on it. And then, at the second dusk, with the light going purple and gold in the west behind them, the hill stopped going up.

They stood at the top in a line. Not the walking order; just a line, side by side, the way people stand at the edge of something.

Below them, a long way off, a day’s walk at least, there was a town.

It was round. That was the first thing. Slep had heard it called the round town so many times that he’d stopped hearing the word. But it was round, a perfect circle of roofs on a plain where seven roads came in from seven directions, like spokes. And in the middle of the circle there was a dome. A great grey dome, bigger than anything Slep had seen in the game. It was so big it looked less like a building and more like a hill someone had decided to put a roof on.

And there were lights on it. A ring of them, round the dome’s middle, like a necklace. Lamps on chains. Somebody had mended them, Slep remembered vaguely, in the spring; there’d been a thing about it on someone’s stream.

And at the very top, right at the top, where the dome had a hole in it, there was one more light. Very small. Much smaller than the others. Just a single point, like a candle somebody had carried up two hundred steps and put down on a ledge.

Thea had stopped breathing. Slep could see it. Her shoulders had gone still.

“That’s her place,” said Thea. “The top. Higu’s place. Where she sits.” Her voice was very small. “Somebody’s up there.”

Slep looked at the light for a long time.

“Two somebodies,” he said.

He didn’t know how he knew that. You couldn’t see that far, not even in the game. It was just a point of light on a dome a day away. But he’d been waiting all his life, and he knew what it looked like when somebody was sitting up late on a roof, waiting for somebody else. You sat close to the light so it would show. And you didn’t sit alone.

Xola’s hand came out sideways, without her looking, and found Thea’s, and held on.

And Slep’s hand came out the other way and found Xola’s.

They stood like that on the top of the hill in the purple light: Thea, and Xola, and Slep. Then PenguChi came and stood on Thea’s other side without saying anything. Then Tif. Then Xyro, who put his arm round Tif and for once in his life didn’t say a word. And Patience walked along the front of the line and looked up at all six of them, one after the other, as if she were counting.

Thea read it out loud, standing on the hill, holding Xola’s hand.

Then she read the last line again.

“We’ll be here,” said Thea. “She said we.”

Nobody said anything. Slep didn’t need to. It had already been said, by somebody, the right somebody. That was usually how it went.

They camped on the top of the hill that night, where they could see the light. It stayed on, the little one at the very top of the dome, until nearly the third dawn. Thea didn’t sleep. She sat at the edge of the camp with her shield across her knees and watched it the whole time. And Slep, on the second watch, with nothing to look at on the left but dark heather, kept looking at it too.
