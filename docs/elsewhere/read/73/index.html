Chapter 73

Newish had decided to be annoying about it, and she was being annoying about it, and she was having the best week of her life.

“Day four,” she said on the call on Monday morning, her time, which was Monday afternoon in Malaysia and Monday evening in the Iron Hills and some ridiculous hour in America. “Day four of walking to Chris’s ugly iron town. For Chris. Who is level ten. Chris, are you listening? I want you to know I’m doing this. For you. Personally.”

“Ten and ninety,” said Chris.

“Ten and ninety! For you! I’ve walked through a forest, Chris. A whole forest. There was a bear. I hid from it in a tree, because I’m twelve and it was seventeen, and I sat in that tree for an hour, for you.”

“Thank you,” said Chris.

“Don’t thank me, I’m being annoying,” said Newish. “You’re supposed to be annoyed.”

“I’m not annoyed.”

“That’s worse,” said Newish, delighted, and kept walking.

She’d left Meadmere on Friday, with her drum on her back and a basket of the innkeeper’s cheese pies on her arm, because the innkeeper wouldn’t let her go without them.

Meadmere was a brewing town on a lake, full of orchards and beehives, and she’d loved it from the first night. She’d never told anyone that except the call, once, at four in the morning, in her other voice. The innkeeper was a big slow woman called Hedda, with flour on her forearms and a laugh like a barrel rolling. On the first night she’d offered Newish a pork pie, and Newish had said, very politely, no thank you, I don’t eat pork. Hedda had said ah, and brought her a cheese one instead, and remembered. Every day for five weeks, without being asked, without making anything of it. Ah. Cheese.

Hedda had taught her the drum.

Not taught, exactly. Hedda didn’t teach things. She just played it, in the evenings, in the inn, while the brewers drank. A small round hand drum with a goatskin head. And Newish had noticed that when Hedda played, everybody in the inn moved a little faster. The pot-boy collected the mugs faster. The brewers drank faster. Two old men playing a game with pegs on a board in the corner played faster, and argued faster, and laughed at the same moment, on the beat.

You can play people faster, Hedda had said, when Newish asked. If they’ll listen.

What if they won’t?

Then you can’t. Hedda had shrugged. Can’t make anybody hear a drum. They have to want to.

Newish had taken a small hand drum off the rack on the first day, because it was there and because she was contrary. She’d been playing it at bees for five weeks. The bees didn’t care. Bees had their own tempo.

She met Winter at a crossroads on Monday evening, two days out of Ferrowmere, in the rain.

She’d known Winter was coming down the north road. Winter had said so on the call, in about four words. But she hadn’t known she’d be standing at the crossroads under a dead tree when Newish came round the corner. Two short black horns, silver-white hair to her waist, soaked flat by the rain. A harpoon over her shoulder with a coil of rope on it. One pale blue eye and one pink, half-lidded, looking at Newish as if she’d been waiting for some time and wasn’t going to mention it.

“You’re late,” said Winter.

“I’m not late, I’m on time, you’re early, that’s a you problem,” said Newish. She stopped in front of her. She looked up at her, because Winter was tall in here, taller than her model ever looked on stream. “Your horns are very stupid.”

“I know,” said Winter.

“I love them.”

“I know,” said Winter, and turned and walked down the east road toward the Iron Hills, without waiting to see if Newish was coming. Newish came, talking. She talked the whole two days. Winter said about eleven words. On the second night, sitting under an overhang with the rain coming down and a fire that Winter had lit with flint, Winter said a twelfth.

“Thanks.”

“For what?”

“For talking,” said Winter, looking at the fire. “I don’t. You do. It’s nice.”

Newish didn’t say anything at all for nearly a minute. It was the longest she’d been quiet since she came into the game.

Ferrowmere was ugly. She’d said so on the first day without seeing it, and she’d been wrong, and she wasn’t going to admit it.

It was a town of iron and stone in a valley of iron-coloured hills. Every hill had a mine in it, with a wheel on top turning. From the wheels, high up, cables ran across the valley from hill to hill. Thick black iron ropes, a hundred feet above the river, with little wooden carts hanging off them on hooks. The carts went back and forth all day, slowly, full of ore and miners and goats, swinging gently over the drop. The whole valley creaked. It smelled of rain and rust and coal.

Chris was waiting at the bottom of the main cable tower.

He looked exactly like himself. That was the thing about Chris. He’d never been anything but himself on any screen, and in here he was just Chris. A plain longsword on his hip, and an expression of slightly anxious delight, as if he’d been expecting them for a long time and had prepared some notes.

“You came,” he said.

“I’ve told you I was coming every day for six days,” said Newish. “I’ve told you about the bear.”

“I know. I’ve got it in the sheet.” Chris looked at both of them, at Winter’s horns and Newish’s drum and the basket of pies, which were nearly gone. He seemed to be having trouble with his face. “I just didn’t—I didn’t actually think you’d—“

“Chris,” said Winter.

“Yeah?”

“Shut up,” said Winter, and hugged him. Briefly, one-armed, with the harpoon still on her shoulder, so that the rope swung. Then she let go and stood back as if nothing had happened.

Newish stared at her.

“You hugged him?”

“He looked like he needed it,” said Winter.

“I’ve been walking for a week—“

“Then hug him,” said Winter.

So Newish did, very hard, basket and drum and all, until Chris made a small noise. And she said into his shoulder, “Your town’s ugly,” and he said, “I know,” and she said, “I love it,” and he said, “I know that too.”

Niko came over the west ridge the next morning with his sword and his book. He said “hey” and sat down on the cable-tower steps next to them as if he’d been there all along.

They went up on Thursday night.

Chris had found it. Of course he had. He had a tab. Ferrowmere — things above level 14, by location and time. At the top of the tab, highlighted in yellow, was a line that said the strix. Cable 3. Night only. DON’T.

“So we’re doing the strix,” said Newish.

“We’re doing the strix,” said Niko.

“It says don’t,” said Chris. “In capitals. I wrote that. I know what it says.”

“It’s seventeen,” said Winter. “You’re eleven. That’s six levels. That’s the bonus.”

“It’s also a hundred feet up on a cable at night, and it kills carts,” said Chris. “It doesn’t kill people. It kills carts. It knocks them off the cable. Everybody in them goes into the river. Three people last week.”

“Then we stay in the cart,” said Niko.

It lived in the dark above Cable 3, the longest cable, between the two highest mines. A bird. A huge one, an owl with a wingspan as wide as the cart and a beak that rang when it hit things, like a hammer on an anvil. It came down out of the night onto the carts with its talons out, and it rocked them, and rocked them, until they came off the hook.

They got into a cart at the top of the east mine at midnight. The four of them, standing up, with the drop under the floor and the black cable creaking over their heads. The cart lurched off the platform and out over the valley, swinging. Far below, the river was a thin silver line in the dark. Above, nothing. Just the cable, going away into the night.

Newish took the drum off her back.

She didn’t know why. She just did it. She sat down on the floor of the swinging cart with her back against the side, put the drum between her knees, and started to play. Not loud. A slow steady beat. The beat Hedda played in the inn. Doum. Tek. Tek. Doum. Tek.

“What are you doing?” said Chris.

“Listen to it,” said Newish.

“Why?”

“Because Hedda said I can’t make you,” said Newish, and kept playing. “You have to want to.”

The strix came down at a quarter past.

It came out of the dark above them without a sound, which was the worst thing about it. Owls didn’t make a sound. One moment there was nothing over the cart but the cable. The next there were wings as wide as the night, and two great yellow eyes, and talons the size of Newish’s forearm closing on the rim of the cart. It hit, and the whole cart swung sideways on its hook, and the floor tipped, and the river was suddenly underneath them a hundred feet down.

“Hold,” said Niko.

He said it on the cut. His sword came up and over and into the strix’s foot where it gripped the rim. The word went down the blade, and the foot froze where it was, held on the wood for one heartbeat, so that the next swing of the cart didn’t come.

Winter threw.

Newish had never seen her throw the harpoon before, not properly. She did it the way she did everything: without a word, without any fuss, all at once. The harpoon went up out of the swinging cart into the dark with the rope paying out behind it, and into the strix’s wing, near the shoulder. Winter braced her feet and pulled. The great bird came down out of the air onto the rim of the cart with a shriek, its wing caught on the line, half in and half out.

“Chris,” said Niko.

Chris had his sword up. Newish could see him doing it. The thing he always did, the thing he’d written down in the forty-second sheet on the first day. He was thinking in a list. Back foot. Shoulder. Breathe out. Bring it down. One, two, three, four. She could see it on his face, every step, one after another. His Falling Cut was fifty-eight per cent. It had been fifty-eight per cent for five weeks.

Doum. Tek. Tek. Doum.

Newish played louder.

“Chris,” she said. “Stop listing. Listen.”

He looked at her. For a second, with the cart swinging and the bird screaming on the rim and the river a hundred feet down, he looked at her. She played. Doum. Tek. Tek. Doum.

And he heard it.

She saw it happen. She saw the list go out of his face. She saw his shoulders drop onto the beat, and his back foot turn on the beat, and his breath go out on the beat, all at once. Not one, two, three, four. Just doum. And the sword came down on the doum. Not a step behind it, the way it had been coming down for five weeks. Exactly on it.

The strix came off the rim.

It went backwards off the cart into the dark with Winter’s harpoon still in its wing. Winter let the rope go just in time, before it took her with it. And they heard it, a long time later, hit the river a hundred feet down.

The cart swung on its hook. Slowly, back and forth, over the valley, in the dark, with the four of them in it. Nobody said anything. Newish stopped playing.

Then Chris sat down on the floor of the cart. Quite suddenly, the way people did when their legs had decided.

“Eighty-four,” he said.

“Eighty-four,” said Niko.

“It’s been fifty-eight for five weeks.” Chris was looking at his hands. “Fifty-eight. I’ve got a sheet. Every cut. Every day. Fifty-eight, fifty-six, fifty-nine, fifty-eight.” He looked up at Newish. “What did you do?”

“I played a drum,” said Newish.

“You played me faster.”

“Hedda said I couldn’t make you,” said Newish. Her voice had gone strange, and she didn’t care. “She said you had to want to. You have to listen. If you won’t listen, it doesn’t work.” She looked at the drum between her knees. “You listened. All three of you. On a cart. A hundred feet up.”

Winter took the harpoon rope off the rim and coiled it, very carefully, without looking at anyone.

“It’s a good drum,” she said.

“It’s a great drum,” said Newish. And then, because she couldn’t help it, because it was her: “Your town is so ugly, Chris. I’m so glad I came.”

“Fine,” said Winter, to her stream. And then, after a long pause, quietly: “That was good.”

They rode the cart the rest of the way across the valley. It took twenty minutes, swinging, with the cable creaking over their heads and the lights of the mines going by. Chris sat on the floor with his sword across his knees and stared at nothing. Every so often he took out his phone in the air and wrote something down, and then crossed it out, and wrote it again.

Newish leaned over to look. He let her.

It was the forty-second sheet. ELSEWHERE (playing). At the bottom of the list, under Ice: knows I know, in the column where he wrote down the things that had happened, there was a new line.

Day 88. Newish played a drum. I stopped listing. 84.

And under it, smaller, as if he’d had to think about whether to put it in:

They came to me.
