Chapter 104

The City With No Name

2,299 words · 10 min read

The doors appeared in the round market at Carrow Cross at ten o’clock on Saturday night, Tiger’s time, and Tiger was the first person through his, because of course he was.

It came out of nothing. One minute he was sitting on the edge of the fountain in the middle of the market with Drew, swinging his feet, waiting. The next there were doors standing on the flagstones all round the fountain. Nine of them, one in front of each of the nine people who’d been sitting there all evening pretending not to wait: him and Drew, Kei and Higu and Gigi, Maigo, Winter, Hyvred and Bee. Just doors, on their own, with no walls round them. Plain grey wood, each with a little egg carved on it and a line through the egg like a horizon. The same as the envelopes they’d all got in March.

“Oh, nice,” said Tiger.

“I’m not going to fall in anything,” said Tiger to his chat. “It’s a door. You don’t fall in a door.” He grinned at the orb. “Right. That’s it from me. Stream’s off on the other side, they said. Nobody’s allowed to see. I’ll tell you everything I’m allowed to, which is nothing. Love you. Bye. Drink water.”

He turned the stream off. He looked at Drew.

Drew was looking at the door with his hands in his pockets, the way he looked at most things. Calmly, as if he’d seen one before. He probably had. Drew had been a Partner for years. He’d been to things like this on Earth, with name badges and a buffet and somebody from a company saying we’re so excited. He said they were always the same.

“After you,” said Drew.

Tiger went through the door at a run, and fell in a fountain.

He came up spitting.

It was a big fountain. Much bigger than the one in the market at Carrow Cross, which he’d fallen in four times, a record. This one was white stone, wide as a room, with a statue in the middle of a woman holding up a bowl. Water poured out of the bowl in a clear sheet all the way round, so she stood inside a falling curtain of it. The water was cold and very clean and came up to his chest.

He stood up in it and pushed his wet hair out of his eyes and looked round.

And stopped.

It was a city.

Not a town. Not a round town where seven roads met, or a canal town going up a hill in locks, or anything he’d seen. A city. White and gold and enormous, going away from the fountain in every direction. Wide streets of pale stone. Towers, dozens of them, tall and thin, with gold on their tops catching the light. Gardens on the roofs. Bridges between the towers, high up, so high they looked like threads. And in the middle of it, rising over everything, a dome so big it made the sky look smaller.

The light was the light from just before sunset. Long and gold and soft, lying across everything. It didn’t move. Tiger would find out later that it never moved here. It was always the hour before sunset in this city, and always would be.

And there was nobody in it.

Not one person. Not on the streets, not on the bridges, not in the windows. No stalls. No carts. No dogs. Just the fountain, and the gold light, and the whole enormous white city standing round him in perfect silence. Waiting.

No name. Just three question marks where the name should be.

Drew came through the door behind him, walked to the edge of the fountain, and looked down at Tiger standing in it up to his chest.

“First person in history to fall in that,” said Drew.

“Look at it, Drew.”

“I’m looking.”

“Look at it.”

Drew looked up. At the towers, and the threads of bridges, and the dome. He stood there for a long time with his hands in his pockets. Tiger watched his face. It was the face Drew made about twice a year. Once, on the Long Night, when the gate at the Waymeet held. Once on the first night of the game, in the snow, when he’d said Huh. Cold.

“Huh,” said Drew, very quietly.

Tiger climbed out of the fountain and stood dripping on the white stone, grinning so hard his face hurt.

There was a terrace at the end of the square, under the great dome. Wide steps going up to it, and a long stone balustrade along the edge, and a view over the whole city falling away below. People were gathering on it.

Seven, the window had said. Then nine. Then fifteen, as Constellation came through behind him, one grey door at a time, out of the air at the edge of the square.

Kei first, with his map under his arm, who stopped at the edge of the fountain and looked up and didn’t say anything at all. Higu, with her hand already going inside her coat for the sheets. Gigi, who looked at the whole white city for a long moment and said Oh, no. It’s nice, exactly the way she’d said it the first time she saw Carrow Cross. Maigo, who said THIS IS THE BEST TOWN at a volume that came back off three towers. Winter, who looked round once and said Fine. And Hyvred and Bee, who came through together and stopped dead at the bottom of the steps. Hyvred’s head was tipped all the way back to look at the dome. Her gold eyes had gone very wide, the crosshair in the left one swinging slowly round from tower to tower. Bee had her book out already and was drawing before she’d finished walking.

He didn’t know the rest. Not to talk to. He knew their names, the way everyone in the game knew some names, off the Tally and the clips and the news.

A tall Brazilian man in green with a laugh you could hear across the square. Tavinho. Tiger had watched his clips; he fought like he was dancing, and he talked to his chat in three languages at once.

A small precise Korean woman in white with her hair pinned up, who looked at the city as if she were working out how long it would take to walk. Mirae. She’d been on the Tally at Brackenford for months, never higher than sixth and never lower than eighth, exactly the same every week.

A quiet young man who hadn’t said anything at all, and who Tiger only knew was sori because he’d looked at the name over his head. He was sitting on the balustrade with his legs over the drop, very calm, looking at nothing.

Two more he didn’t know at all: a girl who made animated clips of other people’s fights, and a man who wrote the guides everybody read and nobody thanked.

And at the far end of the terrace, on her own, a short woman in plain black with a short bow on her back and a long knife at her hip. She had short dark hair and the face of somebody who’d decided not to waste anything.

Tiger knew who that was. Everybody knew who that was.

He went over. Of course he did.

“You’re Kestrel,” he said.

She looked at him. Up and down. At the water still running out of his coat onto the stone.

“Yes,” said Kestrel.

“I’m Tiger. Sevenlocks. Constellation.” He stuck his wet hand out. “I’ve watched you since December. You’re really good. You’re shorter than your name.”

Kestrel looked at the hand for a moment. Then she shook it, once, firmly.

“You fell in the fountain,” she said.

“Everyone’s going to say that.”

“Everyone’s going to say it because you did it.” Something happened at one corner of her mouth. It wasn’t a smile. It was the place a smile would go, if she’d let it. “You run the lamps. In the canal town. On the Long Night. I watched that.”

“You watched—“

“Everybody watched it,” said Kestrel. “Thirty lamps up a hill in the dark. Two short swords, no armour. You fell in the canal twice.” She looked out at the city. “VANTA wanted you, after. I said you’d say no.”

“Why?”

“Because somebody walked a long way to you,” said Kestrel, and looked across the terrace at Drew. “People don’t walk away from that.”

Tiger stood there with his mouth open. He didn’t know what to say. So for once in his life he didn’t say anything.

More doors.

Suzu came through one at the edge of the square. He had his scythe on his back and a pack on his shoulder that glowed faintly on one side through the canvas. He stopped and looked up at the dome, and stood very still. Tiger liked Suzu. He’d only talked to him three times, and Suzu had said about eleven words across all three. But he stood still in a way that made you want to stand still too.

Then Hana. Tiger knew Hana the way everyone in Ice’s chat knew Hana, from a hundred hours of her being in it. She came out of her door and stopped, and put both hands over her mouth, and stood there with her eyes going wet. Of course they were. Hana cried at everything. It was one of the best things about her.

“It’s so big,” said Hana, through her hands, to nobody.

“I fell in the fountain,” Tiger told her, coming down the steps.

“Oh, Tiger.” She laughed, and the tears ran down anyway. “Of course you did.”

Then a door on the far side of the square banged open so hard it nearly came off its nothing, and Leaka came through it. Leaka, with an axe taller than she was and a headset round her neck, who’d only been in the game a week. Her letter had come on Wednesday, after the team had watched her first stream, and she’d screamed so loudly that Curse had heard it from the other side of the fair. She stood in the white square and looked up at the towers and shouted HELLO, and the empty city shouted it back at her four times.

“OH, IT’S LOUD,” said Leaka, delighted, and ran at Hana.

Then a man in a plain grey coat with a lanyard round his neck. The lanyard said TEAM on it in small letters. He came out of a door that was bigger than the others and went up the terrace steps and stood at the top, looking pleased and nervous at the same time, the way people from companies looked at things like this. Drew caught Tiger’s eye across the terrace and very slightly raised his eyebrows. Told you.

“Just one more,” said the man in the grey coat, to everyone, looking at a window in front of him. “And then we’ll—“

The last door opened at the edge of the square.

Ice came through it.

He came out the way he did everything, without hurrying, and stopped on the white stone. In charcoal and dark leather, with a shield on his back and a teal cord on his laces. He looked up at the city. Tiger watched him do it. Ice didn’t say oh or huh or put his hands over his mouth. He just stood there and looked at all of it, the towers and the bridges and the dome. Very still, and for a long time. Tiger thought he looked like somebody who’d been waiting a long time to see a thing, and wasn’t sure yet whether it was the thing.

Then Ice looked down. Across the square, up the steps, at the terrace and the nineteen people on it. His eyes went along them one at a time. Drew. Tiger, dripping. Kei, who nodded. Suzu, who lifted a hand. Hana, who waved with one arm and held onto Leaka’s collar with the other. Leaka, who opened her mouth, and then saw something and shut it again. Kestrel, at the far end, who didn’t wave at all and didn’t look away either.

And Hyvred.

Hyvred, at the top of the steps, had seen him first. Tiger saw her see him. He saw her gold eyes find Ice at the edge of the square and the crosshair lock on, the way it did when she really looked at something. He saw her go perfectly still.

Then she started walking.

Down the steps. Across the square. Not fast. Very straight. With her long thin tail flicking behind her, and her hands at her sides in fists.

“Oh, no,” said Drew, beside Tiger, sounding absolutely delighted.

“What? What’s happening?”

“I’ve been in her chat,” said Drew. “Last week. Every night.” He folded his arms and leaned on the balustrade to watch. “He’s about to get hit.”
