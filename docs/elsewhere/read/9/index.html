The POV Crowd

4,090 words · 18 min read

The clip went up on Saturday night, while he was asleep.

He found out the way he found out everything now, lying in bed at half past six on Sunday with his phone over his face. There were three notifications he didn’t understand and a number on his channel page that had to be wrong.

He’d had eleven hundred and three followers on Monday morning. Four years of streaming five or six times a month to ten people had got him eleven hundred, most of them from raids: people who’d followed once on their way through somebody else’s evening and never come back. That was fine. He’d never needed more.

It said one thousand, six hundred and eighty-eight.

He sat up.

The clip was forty seconds long. Someone called dl_learnsforms had cut it from Thursday’s stream and posted it on the ELSEWHERE forum with the title This tank streams POV and his form grades are 60%+. Watch his BACK FOOT. It was just him, from inside his own eyes. A leech coming up out of the black water, the slope of the shield coming up into frame, the turn, and then the cut. You couldn’t see his foot, of course, because it was POV. But you could see the world tilt, very slightly, half a beat before the blade came down, as his weight went back and round. And then the little window in the corner. Grade 62%. Whoever had made the clip had slowed it down, put a circle round the tilt, and written HERE in red.

Under it there were four hundred comments.

He didn’t really know what to do with sixty people. It wasn’t many. The big streams had ten thousand, twenty, more. But it was six times what he was used to, and they all wanted to know things, and he could feel himself wanting to tell them.

So, at dawn on the end of Sedge’s porch, before Deston and Xav sat up out of nothing beside him, he did something he’d never done on stream in four years. He taught.

He stood up with the sword and did a Falling Cut, slowly, at nothing. He talked them through it as he went. Sit back. Back foot starts first, inside the movement, not before it, or the game docks you for the pause. Look through the target, not at it. Let your hips turn you, not your arm. Then he did it fast, the real way.

He saw the red sashes a little before noon, Fernwick time.

He’d been selling on his crate by Marta’s door. It was a thing now. Ice’s crate. People in Fernwick said it like that, he’d heard them. Fifty draughts a day, sometimes sixty, Fair at forty copper and Poor at twenty. He still never sold the Good ones. There was a queue every morning. There was also a boy called Rafi, the boy with the hatchet from Tuesday, who now picked fenwort on the north walk all day for two copper a plant and brought it to Ice in a sack. It was the best deal anybody in Fernwick had ever made, according to Rafi, who was nineteen and from Surabaya and apparently had never been so happy.

“You hired a guy,” Xav had said on Friday, watching Rafi trot away with his empty sack.

“I hired a guy.”

“In game.”

“In game. He picks, I brew. I can’t do both and fight and sleep.”

Xav had looked at him with an expression that was almost respect. “You’ve played EVE too long.”

“There’s no such thing.”

Now there were three people in red sashes standing at the front of the queue, and they weren’t buying.

They didn’t look like the people of Fernwick. Everybody in Fernwick by now looked a bit damp and a bit bitten and a bit pleased with themselves. These three were clean. They had matching sashes, crimson, tied at the hip, and the same small tag under each of their names.

CL_Procurement_07. CL_Procurement_11. CL_Field_03.

He looked at the number for a while.

It was a lot of money. It was more than the silverleaf. It was the kind of number that, every day for a month, would be most of what he’d need to pay a friend in Singapore five thousand ringgit to come and live in Malacca. He felt it pull at him, the way a good trade always did.

And he thought about why.

Why would a company with people in four hundred beginner towns want potions from one man in a swamp? Not to drink them. Not two dollars apiece. You bought every unit of something because you wanted to be the only one who had it. You wanted to set the price. And after you’d bought all of it, you’d want to know where it came from, so you could stop paying the man and go and get it yourself.

We don’t sell the flower, Xav had said. If you sell the flower, someone asks where it grows.

A Good draught wasn’t a flower. But it was close enough.

“No, thank you,” said Ice.

07’s smile didn’t move. That was the most interesting thing about it. “Can I ask why?”

“I sell to people who drink them.” He nodded at the queue behind her, which was very interested in all of this. “They’ll still be here next week.”

“So will we,” said 07, pleasantly. “We’re in four hundred towns, Ice. We’ll be in the intermediate towns next week, and the cities after that. Everyone sells to us eventually.” She closed the window. “That’s not a threat. It’s just how markets work. You know that. You played EVE.”

He blinked.

“It was in your stream title in 2023,” she said, and smiled again, and turned to go.

“Then you should’ve done more research,” said Ice.

She stopped.

He hadn’t raised his voice. He didn’t, much. He said it the way he’d have told her the time, standing by his crate with a draught in his hand and ninety people watching through his eyes.

“If you had,” he said, “you’d know that doesn’t work on me. Threats. Or not-threats.” He shrugged. “World War Bee. 2016. And the second one, 2020 into ‘21. I had the most recorded kills in both. I flew solo. Merc.”

07 looked at him. For the first time, the smile didn’t seem to know what to do.

She hadn’t known. He could see it. Somebody at Crimson Ledger had typed his name into a search bar, found an old stream title with EVE in it, and written it on a card: played EVE, understands markets. She’d read the card and called it a day. Nobody had told her what he’d actually done there.

Or anywhere else. Nobody had told her that in almost every MMO he’d ever played, he’d ended up a grinder and a trader, a man with spreadsheets and patience. But only after he’d made his name the other way first.

In EVE, in a spaceship, alone, for hire, in two of the biggest wars the game had ever had.

In SimpleMMO, he’d made a fortune playing the market. Before that he’d been the most feared player-killer on the server. He’d started a guild on his own and declared war on every other guild in the game at once, and he’d killed so many people that the ones who loved a fight started turning up to join him. Twenty-odd players had ended up in the top fifty of five thousand guilds, most of them a hundred strong.

In Elder Scrolls Online he’d mostly farmed herbs. Except on the nights he went into the war zone. Then he’d stun and root and lock down twenty people at once, on his own, over and over, until they gave up and went home.

He didn’t say any of that. He didn’t need to.

“Good to know,” said 07, after a moment. The smile came back, a little thinner. Then the three of them walked away down the boardwalk together, sashes swinging, slightly faster than they’d come.

It took him a second to see it, and when he saw it he felt stupid.

The delay. He’d set it to sixty seconds so that nobody could see what he saw until a minute after he’d seen it. That was fine when the danger was somebody seeing a door. But a man following you didn’t need to see where you were now. He only needed to see where you’d been a minute ago, and walk there, and then watch again.

They’d been watching his POV all afternoon, a minute behind, and walking where he’d walked.

“Chat,” he said aloud. “I’m being followed.”

He took them north. Not toward the ring. Away from it and round, the way he’d come the very first morning, past the ninth heron’s line but never near the heron itself, toward the deep black water where the reeds stood in clumps and the mist lay low.

He went slowly. He wanted the red sashes to keep up. He went through the reeds and along the edge of the deep water. He stopped, as if he’d found something, and crouched, and pretended to pick a plant. Then he got up and went on, toward the place where the water was deepest, where a week ago a thing with a mouth like a door had come up out of the dark at him.

Then he turned left and went back the way he’d come, fast and quiet, Deston and Xav beside him. He went through the reeds on the far side, where the sashes, a minute behind on the stream, wouldn’t see him go until they’d already walked past.

“Chat,” he said, “I’m going dark now. Sorry. Something’s about to happen and I don’t want them to see where I go after.”

He ended the stream.

They heard it from a long way off, back at the edge of the tussocks.

A long, slow push of water. A sound like something very large deciding something. A shout, cut short. Then another shout, and somebody running in water, which is slow, and a splash, and another. Then quiet.

Xav was laughing. Not out loud; Xav almost never laughed out loud. But his shoulders were shaking.

“You stream sniped the stream snipers,” he said.

They had a meeting about it that night, the three of them, sitting in a row on Sedge’s porch with the stream off and their boots off. It was the first time they’d ever called anything a meeting.

“The delay’s not enough,” said Xav. “Sixty seconds is fine for a door. It’s useless for being followed. They’ll do it again, and next time they’ll bring more.”

“I know.”

“And if they bring swords next time,” said Deston, “instead of notebooks?”

“Then we use ours,” said Ice.

He said it the way he’d have said the time. Xav didn’t even look up. Deston was quiet for a moment, and then nodded, slowly. They’d all done it before. Not like Ice; nobody did it like Ice. But Deston had spent years in the war zones of another game, and Xav had followed Ice into every fight he’d ever started since poly and come out of most of them.

“We don’t start it,” said Ice. “We just don’t run from it.”

“Go dark before you leave town,” said Deston. “Every time. Before the north walk. Then nobody sees which way you go.”

“Then nobody sees the fights, either.”

“Then stream the fights near town,” said Xav. “And go dark for the fen. Your chat will live. They like it. They’ve got a meme about it.”

Ice looked out over the dark channel. It was night here, the fen’s sixth night since launch, and the frogs had started. Somewhere back in town, under a lantern, someone was laughing.

“They looked up my old stream titles,” he said.

“Yeah,” said Xav. “They’re good. That’s what good looks like.” He was quiet for a moment. “They’re going to be in the intermediate towns next week. And the cities. They’ve got money and people and time, and they’re not wrong, Dec. Most people will sell to them, eventually.”

“Not us.”

“Not us,” Xav agreed. “But we’ll need to be somewhere they can’t follow. And fast.”

Deston had his book open on his knee, reading the two lines in the dark, as he did every night now.

“We’ll need five,” he said. “For the door. And they’ll need to be people we trust.”

Nobody said anything for a while.

Ice thought about a name in his chat that had never typed a word. Suzuyunn. He’d seen it there every single day since Monday, in the viewer list, quiet, always there.

“The keys are on Tuesday,” he said.
