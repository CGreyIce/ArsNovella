Chapter 115

So the Rest May Sleep

2,176 words · 9 min read

They came down off the heron road into the old fen on Sunday, in the rain, the five of them. The fen looked exactly the way it had in November, which Ice found he’d been afraid it wouldn’t.

Black water. Reeds. The long narrow north walk on its posts, five hundred paces of old planking out to where the herons began. The narrows, where he’d stood all night on the longest night with the cold going into his arm like a river. The ring of nine stones off to the east in the reeds. He didn’t look at it. Not yet. Nobody else knew it was there, except the people who did.

And then the Landing. The causeway. The lamps. Fernwick.

“Oh,” said Kotori, behind him, very softly, as if she hadn’t let herself think about it until she could see it.

It was busier than it had been. It had been busier every time they came back. Door-hunters, and people who’d watched the longest night and wanted to stand where it had happened, and people who just wanted to see the swamp guy’s swamp. They looked up as the five of them came along the north walk in the rain. Some of them waved. Some of them took pictures. One girl on the Landing burst into tears for no reason she could have explained.

Ice nodded at all of them and kept walking.

Sedge was on her steps.

She was always on her steps, at the end of the north walk, at the top of the ladder down to the channel, where her raft gardens floated. An old woman with a pipe and a shawl and a face like a walnut. She’d been sitting there in November when a man came out of the reeds covered in mud with a sack of silverleaf. She’d been sitting there on the longest night, singing. She was sitting there now, in the rain, with her pipe going and her eyes on the north walk. Ice thought she’d probably been sitting there since Friday, since the window.

“Swamp guy,” said Sedge.

“Sedge.”

She looked at him for a long time. At the five of them. At Suzu, last, with his scythe on his back and his pack hanging slack and dark.

“Took your time,” said Sedge.

“Yes.”

“Heard about your window.” She took her pipe out of her mouth and looked at it, as if it had done something to her. “Friday night every traveller on the Landing stopped dead and stared at nothing for a minute. Hollis thought it was a plague. He hasn’t stopped telling people.”

Behind him, Deston laughed.

“Come in out of the wet,” said Sedge, and got up, slowly, the way she always got up, and went in.

Her house was the same. One room on stilts over the black water, a hearth, a table with grinding stones on it, bunches of herbs hanging from every beam so thick you had to duck. Ice ducked. He’d been ducking in this room since November. He sat at her table, in the chair that was his, and the others found places. Kotori on the bench by the hearth. Deston on the step. Sheng Yang standing in the doorway, because he always stood. Suzu on the floor with his back against the wall, the way he’d sat in the Long Watch.

Sedge went to the shelf over the hearth. There was a lamp on it, old clay, her mother’s, the one that had burned all night on the longest night. Beside the lamp there was a piece of paper, folded twice, held down with a pebble.

She took it down and put it on the table in front of him.

“Boy wrote,” she said. “Came by carrier on a Tuesday. It’s been on that shelf near a month.” She sat down across from him and put her pipe back in her mouth. “You said you’d read it. The swamp guy’ll read it out. I said that. So.” She nodded at it. “Read it out.”

Ice looked at the paper.

He knew what it was. He’d known for a month, since a message from Kei the night it happened: rafi’s here. somebody asked who sent him. he said nobody. he said he walked. Then Ice had gone and sat on a pier at the top of the world with his legs over a gorge, and thought about it, and not said anything to anyone.

He unfolded it. The handwriting was big and careful and went slightly uphill.

He read it out.

“Sedge,” he said. “I walked. They said yes.”

Sedge took her pipe out of her mouth.

“The gate is a girl with two different eyes and a man who used to be a bird.”

“Hm,” said Sedge.

“Bram said a word.”

“Bram,” said Sedge, and very nearly smiled. “That’s a first.”

“I’m in the book.” Ice stopped for a second. Then he read the last line. “Tell the swamp guy I didn’t say. Rafi.”

He put the paper down.

The rain went on, on the roof. Down below, the black water knocked against the stilts. Kotori had her hand over her mouth. Deston, on the step, was looking at the floor.

“Didn’t say what?” said Sedge.

Ice looked at the letter on the table.

“Who sent him,” he said.

Sedge looked at him across the table for a long moment, through the smoke.

“And who sent him?”

“Nobody,” said Ice. “He walked.”

Sedge put her pipe back in her mouth. Then she laughed. A short dry laugh, like a stick breaking, the only kind she had. She shook her head and laughed again, and wiped her eye with the back of her hand. She said it was the smoke.

“Eleven years,” she said. “Eleven years, nobody on those steps. Then a boy. Then you. Now the boy’s in a book in the round town and you’re reading me his letters.” She took the paper and folded it again, carefully, twice, the way it had come. She put it back on the shelf by her mother’s lamp, under the pebble. “Write back to him,” she said. “Tell him the steps are kept. Tell him I said good.”

“I will.”

“And tell him didn’t say is the cleverest thing he’s ever done,” said Sedge. “He won’t believe it from you. Tell him it’s from me.”

Suzu hadn’t said anything. He was sitting against the wall with his pack in his lap.

Sedge looked at him. She’d been looking at him since they came in, Ice realised. Every few seconds, sideways, the way she looked at a pot she didn’t trust.

“Boy,” she said. “The jar.”

Suzu opened the pack and took it out.

It was a sweet jar. That was all it was now. A plain glass sweet jar with a lid, the kind you’d get at a market, empty. In the firelight it looked like nothing. Sedge looked at it for a long time.

She’d held it up to her lamp on these steps a month and a half ago, with eighty-one small blue lights in it pressing against the glass toward the north. They’re going home. She’d said that, and my mother said the lights that don’t finish go north, and you’re carrying them home.

“They finished?” said Sedge.

“All of them,” said Suzu.

“Where?”

“North. Past the herons. There’s a door.” He turned the empty jar in his hands. “There were eighty-one lanterns. I gave them one each. The last one went in a big one. It went gold.”

Sedge didn’t say anything for a long time.

Then she reached across and took the jar out of his hands. Suzu let her. She held it up to the firelight, the way she’d held it up to the lamp. She looked through the empty glass at the fire behind it, with her head on one side.

“It’s not empty,” she said.

Suzu looked up.

“Feels empty,” said Sedge. “Isn’t.” She put it back in his hands. “A jar’s for filling. You emptied it right. Now it’s ready.” She sat back. “My mother’d have liked to see that. All of them home. She worried about them, the ones that didn’t finish. Every winter.”

Suzu held the jar in his lap and didn’t say anything at all. Ice watched him hold it differently than he had in the Long Watch. Not tight. Just held.

It was later, with the rain easing and the others drifting out onto the Landing, that Ice asked her.

He hadn’t planned to. He’d been carrying it since a message from Kei on the north moor three weeks ago. One line out of an old guild’s book, half of it washed away. He’d known exactly what it was the moment he read it. He’d said thank you and nothing else, and carried it all the way to the top of the world and back.

“Sedge,” he said. “The reed song.”

She looked at him.

“Somebody found a book,” he said. “Kei’s lot. Up north, in a ruin. An old guild, from before. They wrote down things they’d given to towns. Customs. One line for every town.” He looked at her across the table. “Fernwick’s said, cut the reeds in song, to keep the count.”

“That’s right,” said Sedge. “That’s what it’s for. Fell used to say. If I can hear you, you’re there.”

“There was more,” said Ice. “After that. Set one to watch—” He stopped. “And then the rain had the rest.”

Sedge looked at him for a long time across the table. She took her pipe out of her mouth.

“—so the rest may sleep,” she said.

Ice sat very still.

“My mother said it,” said Sedge. “When she put me to bed. Every night, all the years I was small. Set one to watch, so the rest may sleep. Then she’d blow the lamp out.” She looked at the old clay lamp on the shelf, by the folded letter. “I thought it was just a thing mothers said. Like mind the step.” She put the pipe back. “Is it not?”

“No,” said Ice.

He thought about a ring of nine stones in the reeds east of the north walk, where nothing came. Eight faced inward. One faced out, north, toward a door it couldn’t see, at the end of a road of herons. Someone had to watch. He’d stood behind that stone in March, the night before they went north, and looked where it looked. He’d known, without knowing how he knew, that it had been looking that way for a very long time. So that a town in a fen could sleep.

And a door in a hill at the top of the world, facing back south. Watching too.

“No,” he said again. “I don’t think it is.”

Sedge looked at him. She didn’t ask what he meant. She’d never once asked him what he meant, in five months. It was one of the things he liked best about her.

“Well,” she said. “Now you know the whole of it.”

He sent it to Kei from the Landing in the rain, at the end of the evening.
