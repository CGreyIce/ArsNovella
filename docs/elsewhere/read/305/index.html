Live from Elsewhere · Volume 12: Even

Chapter 305

A Tenth

3,234 words · 14 min read

Suzu had been asked to fight eight times in three months, and he’d liked every one of them more than the last.

He hadn’t expected that. He’d thought it would be effort. Being woken up, being looked at, being asked. And it was. But the fights weren’t. The fights were the best thing he’d done since a ladder a long time ago, out there, near the top. Somebody across from him trying everything they had. And him not being anywhere they’d decided he’d be.

Xav, three times, in a round pale room. Six at the settling ring in June, on Sundays. And Teo of the Ninefold on Sunday night in a cellar that breathed. Who’d decided everything for nine minutes, and then stopped. And for eleven minutes been better than anybody Suzu had fought except Xav.

He’d gone to sleep afterwards against the wall, smiling. Kotori said so. She’d put a blanket on him.

He woke up on the Monday at the second dusk because somebody was at the door.

He knew who it was. Nobody else in Masmark came to the netloft door the way Teo did. Seven people in sea-blue, two by two and one in front. They stopped in the lane, very still, as if they’d decided exactly where to stand.

They hadn’t, today. Suzu could tell from the cellar stair, where he’d gone back to sleep after Kotori’s soup. He could hear their feet. Not two by two. Bunched. Uneven. Like people who’d come down a lane not sure where to stop.

Rafi was in the door.

“Who’d stand next to you?” said Rafi.

There was a long silence. Suzu, on the cellar stair with his eyes shut, listened to it.

“Eight,” said Teo. “And I’d like nine.”

Rafi didn’t say anything. He was a good door. He’d learned that you waited.

“The one with the scythe,” said Teo. “I’d like to ask him something. Properly. At the door.” He was quiet. “We’ve never asked anybody. In two and a half years. People ask us.” His voice did something. “I don’t know how to do it.”

“You just did,” said Rafi.

Suzu got up off the stair. It was effort. He did it anyway.

He came to the door and stood beside Rafi in it. The netloft door was one shield wide; they stood in it shoulder to shoulder, Rafi with his big black sword and Suzu with his scythe. In the lane, seven people in sea-blue, and Teo at the front with his two short spears and his hands at his sides.

Teo looked at him.

“The Ninefold,” said Teo. “We’ve been nine since the first morning. On the last island. Nine, never a tenth. Nobody’s ever been a tenth.” He swallowed. “I’m asking. Will you be.”

Behind Teo, the six didn’t move. Suzu looked at them. Six faces that had heard this said for the first time ever, and hadn’t said we’re nine. That told him something. He wasn’t sure what yet.

He thought about it. Like a cat. It took a while. Nobody in the lane moved.

“No,” said Suzu.

Teo didn’t move either.

“I’ve got a guild,” said Suzu.

He watched Teo hear it. It wasn’t what Suzu expected. He’d thought it would be a shut door, the face Kestrel had seen on Teo at Saltreach, coming up the cliff stair seven.

It wasn’t. It was a man who’d asked something for the first time in his life, and been told no, and found out it didn’t kill you.

“I know,” said Teo. “Constellation.” He was quiet. “I didn’t think you’d say yes. I asked anyway.”

“Why?”

Teo thought about it. Properly. Suzu watched him do it. He’d learned that in a cellar, Suzu thought, in eleven minutes of not deciding anything.

“Because I couldn’t decide what you’d say,” said Teo. “And I wanted to find out.”

Suzu looked at him for a long time.

“That’s good,” he said.

“I lost the fight,” said Teo. “And you said no. That’s two.”

“You lost the fight,” agreed Suzu. “And then you came to a door and asked something you didn’t know the answer to.” He shifted the scythe on his shoulder. “That’s two good things.”

“Then who’d stand next to you?” said Teo.

Rafi, beside Suzu in the door, made a small sound, like a man who’d waited a year for somebody to ask the door’s question back to somebody else.

Suzu thought about it.

He’d been asked a lot of things in three months. He’d never once been asked this. Not at the gate at home; Tobi had asked him, going out, and he’d said Kotori, with tea. That had been true. It wasn’t all of it.

“Kotori,” said Suzu. “With tea.” He looked at the lane. “Ice. Since before the wind game. Out there. In a guest room.” He was quiet. “Xav. Across. In a round room. Every time he asks.” He thought. “Isa, on the north steps. Who knows when I’m asleep and when I’m not, because she’s blind.” He thought some more. “Everybody at a long table at a quarter to eight.”

“That’s a lot of answers,” said Teo.

“I’ve got a lot,” said Suzu.

Teo stood in the lane and looked at him.

“Nine,” said Teo, slowly. “I’ve only ever had nine.”

“Take a tenth,” said Suzu.

Teo stared at him.

“Not me,” said Suzu. “Somebody you can’t decide.” He looked at the six behind Teo, who’d gone very still. “You stopped deciding for eleven minutes and you were better than you’ve ever been. You said.” He leaned on the doorframe. “A tenth is somebody you can’t decide. That’s what you’re frightened of. That’s what you need.”

“Who?”

“I don’t know,” said Suzu. “Ask at doors.” He tipped his head at Rafi beside him. “He’ll help. He asks everybody.”

Rafi went red to the ears.

Teo stood in the lane for a long time after that.

Then he sat down. On the step, a sword’s length off from the door, the way he had on the first Monday. The six sat down in a row in the lane behind him. They didn’t say anything. Suzu didn’t either. He sat down on the threshold of the netloft, in the door, beside Rafi’s feet. And for a while the eight of them sat there in the dusk, on a quay in Masmark, not saying anything, with the lake making small noises against the wall.

“Is this the bit people forget?” said Teo, eventually.

“Yes,” said Rafi.

“I’m going to remember it,” said Teo.

He got up. The six got up. They went back up the lane toward the east, not two by two. Bunched, uneven, talking. Suzu heard one of them, a woman, say who, though and Teo say I don’t know. And the woman laugh, out loud, in the lane. As if I don’t know were the best thing she’d heard him say in two and a half years.

Lucan came to the netloft on the Monday night. On his own, without Ves, which Suzu was told was very rare.

He stood in the lane and Rafi asked him who’d stand next to you. He said Ves. She counts. Then he asked for the one with the scythe. Rafi came and found Suzu on the cellar stair. Suzu came to the door, because it was Lucan, and Ice had once said Lucan was the best he’d ever seen at being early.

They’d never met. Lucan looked at him across the lane, at where he was, the way he looked at everything now.

“You taught Sketch to be on time,” said Lucan.

“I sat down,” said Suzu.

“I watched it. Forty times. On the south coast, a friend of mine watched it forty times too.” Lucan almost smiled. “Everybody watched you sit down. And then I stopped falling over.”

Suzu thought about that.

“That was you,” he said. “Not me. You fell over four hundred and fourteen times. I just sat.”

Lucan looked at him for a long time.

“Will you fight me?” he said.

Suzu thought about it. He did want to. He could feel how much he wanted to, the way you can feel a ladder you haven’t climbed. A man who’d been early his whole life and was on time now. Who’d stood still with Ice for four minutes and a half. Who’d held the slow one back at him.

“When you’ve learned his tik,” said Suzu.

Lucan stared at him.

“That’s a year,” he said.

“Good,” said Suzu. “I’ll have slept.”

Lucan laughed. Out loud, in the lane, the second time anybody in Masmark had heard him do it in two days. He went back up the lane toward the north rim still laughing, and at the top of it he stopped and looked back. Not up. Back. At the door.

“Next year,” he called.

“Next year,” said Suzu.

Kotori found him on the step afterwards with two cups.

“You’ve been asked twice tonight,” she said, sitting down beside him. “To fight, and to be a tenth.”

“Three times,” said Suzu. “Lucan. When he’s learned the tik.”

Kotori gave him a cup and looked at him over hers.

“You’re going to be famous,” she said.

“That’s effort.”

“You’re going to be famous anyway,” said Kotori. “Eleven million people watched you sit down. Teo walked from the south coast. Lucan came to the door on his own.” She drank. “Who else?”

Suzu thought about it. Properly. He’d never once let himself think about this. Not in here. Out there, a long time ago, on ladders, he’d thought about it all the time.

“Kestrel,” he said. “She doesn’t decide anything either. I’d like to see what happens when two people who don’t decide anything fight.” He thought. “Teo again, when he’s got a tenth. Xav in September. Lucan next year.” He looked at the lake. “The girl who looks up, at VANTA. In a few years. When she’s ready.”

“And him?” said Kotori.

She didn’t say who. She didn’t need to.

Suzu was quiet for a long time.

“Not yet,” he said.

“Why not?”

“Because if I fight him, one of us will know something,” said Suzu. “Who’s better. And then that’ll be true for always.” He drank his tea. “I like not knowing. I’ve liked it the whole time I’ve known him.” He was quiet. “It’s the best thing we’ve got.”

Kotori didn’t say anything. She put her head on his shoulder for a moment, the way she did at the back of fires, and then took it off again.

“Good,” she said. Which was his.

Hyv found him in the cellar on the Tuesday night, when her voice had come back.

He was lying on the floor in the middle of it, the way he’d lain in the Seven Below the night before the best of three. She came down the steps and stood at the bottom with her bow.

“I said I could call you now,” said Hyvred. “In May. After Xav.”

“You said maybe.”

“I want to try,” said Hyvred.

Suzu thought about it. Then he got up. He stood in the middle of the breathing cellar with his scythe, and looked at her. About two thirds awake.

“Call,” he said.

Hyv looked at him. Edge to edge, the way she looked at everything she was about to call. Not where he’d be. Where he was. Standing in the middle of a cellar, doing nothing.

“Left,” said Hyvred.

Suzu went right.

Hyv stared at him. Then her face did something. Not anger. She started laughing.

“I said left on purpose,” she said. “So you’d go right. Because you never go where somebody says.” She was laughing harder. “And you went right. I called it. I called you by calling the wrong way.”

Suzu stood on the right side of the cellar for a long time, looking at her.

“That’s very good,” he said.

“I know,” said Hyvred. “I’ve been thinking about it since May.” She wiped her eyes. “I’m not going to tell anybody. It’s ours.”

“It only works once,” said Suzu.

“I know,” said Hyvred. “That’s why it’s ours.”

Xav found him on the quay at the third dawn.

Suzu was asleep. Of course he was. On his back on the warm stone of the quay, with the scythe beside him and Kotori’s blanket over him, under the stars. Xav sat down a sword’s length off and didn’t wake him.

Suzu woke up anyway. He always knew.

“You said no,” said Xav.

“Yes.”

“To the Ninefold. Nobody’s ever been asked to the Ninefold.”

“I’ve got a guild,” said Suzu.

Xav looked at the lake.

“I’m staying,” he said. “Riverbank. Here. In Masmark.” He was quiet. “You’re going home on Thursday. With everybody. Fourteen days.”

“Yes.”

“So I won’t get you,” said Xav. “For a while.”

Suzu looked at the stars.

“September,” he said.

Xav turned his head.

“Walk,” said Suzu. “Like you always do. To the round town. Fourteen days.” He shut his eyes. “Sunday. The settling ring.”

Xav was quiet for a long time.

“Best of three?”

“Best of three.”

“And if I beat you,” said Xav, “I get him.” He tipped his head toward the netloft, where Ice was. “That’s the deal.”

“That’s his deal,” said Suzu. “Not mine.” He thought about it. “Mine’s just: next time.”

“Next time,” said Xav.

“Next time,” said Suzu.

Xav lay down on the quay beside him, on his back, a sword’s length off, and looked at the stars too. They didn’t say anything else. After a while Suzu heard Xav’s pencil, very quietly, on a sketchbook in the dark. Drawing something.

He didn’t ask what. It was effort.

“You were in the corner,” said Suzu. On the Wednesday morning, out there, in the back room at Melaka Raya, coming out of the egg.

Ice was on the edge of his, with his feet on the floor.

“Yes,” said Ice.

“At the top of the cellar steps. In the dark. Where the lamp didn’t reach.” Suzu sat up. “On Sunday. With Teo.”

“Yes.”

“That’s my corner,” said Suzu.

“I borrowed it,” said Ice.

Suzu looked at him for a long time.

“Teo didn’t see who was up there,” he said. “He said. Afterwards. Somebody at the top of the steps, in the dark.”

“Nobody looks at the corner.”

“Hyv does.”

“She was asleep,” said Ice. “On the quay. With her voice gone.” He almost smiled. “She’s been asleep a lot since Saturday.”

Suzu looked at him.

He’d known Ice a long time. Since before the wind game, out there. Longer than anybody. Long enough to know that Ice could read everybody in a fight except him, and that he could read Ice everywhere except in one. He’d never once been able to tell what Ice would do in a fight. He could always tell what Ice would do everywhere else.

He could tell now.

He’d seen it on the quay on the Tuesday night, late, before the egg. Ice standing in the netloft door, on his own, with Rafi asleep against the doorframe. He was holding something small in his hand and looking at it. Black. With a point of gold in it that caught the light from the lamp in the lane.

Ice hadn’t seen him see it. Suzu had been where nobody looked.

“You’re going to the notch,” said Suzu.

Ice went very still on the edge of the egg.

“Kei’s board,” said Suzu. “The notch. The second dawn out. Early.” He got up. “I can read.”

“You haven’t said anything.”

“No,” said Suzu. “It’s not mine to say.” He went to the door of the back room. He stopped there, and looked back, for once. Not at where Ice would be. At where he was. On the edge of the egg in the grey light, with his hand on his chest.

“Good,” said Suzu.

Then he went to the kitchen and took Ice’s tea, the way he always did. Before Ice had even made it. He stood at the counter waiting for the kettle with his eyes about a third open.

Gabe, on the counter with his kopi, looked at him.

“You’re awake,” said Gabe.

“Yes,” said Suzu.

“Why?”

Suzu thought about it. Like a cat.

“Because something good’s going to happen,” he said. “And I want to have slept enough.”
