Live from Elsewhere · Volume 6: There's a Chair

Chapter 165

There's a Chair

4,414 words · 19 min read

On Sunday morning, three weeks after the duel, Gabe was still talking about it.

“Forty-one times,” said Gabe. He was standing in the doorway of the back room at Melaka Raya with a cup of kopi in each hand and his hair still flat on one side from the pod. “I’ve watched it forty-one times, Dec. I watched it twice last night before I went in. I found a new thing.”

“You find a new thing every time.”

“Because there’s a new thing every time.” Gabe handed him a cup. “Last night it was the bit at the start. Before she even does the three arrows. You move first. Before she’s touched the string. I slowed it down. You’re already going six frames before her hand moves.” He pointed at Ice with his own cup. “You went first the whole fight, Dec. She never went first once.”

“She decides before she draws,” said Ice. “She’s very good. You can see her decide. So I went before she decided.”

“I can’t see her decide.”

“You weren’t looking at her shoulder.”

“Nobody looks at her shoulder, Dec. Normal people look at the arrow.”

Ice drank his kopi. It was too sweet, the way Gabe always made it. He didn’t say anything about it. He’d stopped saying anything about it in about the second week.

The back room was bright with the morning. The white tiles, the ceiling fan going round, the two pods side by side with their lids up. His and Suzu’s. Outside, Melaka Raya was waking up on a Sunday. Motorbikes, a shutter going up across the road, somebody’s radio. The shoplot smelled of kopi and the pods’ faint warm electric smell. And of the roti somebody had brought back from the mamak at seven and left on the desk in the front office.

Suzu was sitting on the edge of his pod with his phone, reading. He read it every morning before he went in. Not the stream, not the forums. His messages.

“Mum says the bus was a nine today,” said Suzu, without looking up. “She read it from the stop. Before it got there.”

Gabe stopped talking.

Ice put his cup down.

“Before it got there,” said Gabe.

“From the stop,” said Suzu. He was still looking at his phone. His face was doing what it always did, which was nothing. But his thumb had stopped scrolling, and was resting on the screen, very still. “She says it was a nine and she knew it was a nine and she didn’t have to ask anybody.”

Nobody said anything for a while. The fan went round.

“Okay,” said Suzu, to his phone, very quietly.

Then he put it face down on his knee and sat there looking at the white tiles. And Gabe, who never stopped talking, didn’t say a word. He went over and sat down on the edge of the pod beside Suzu, close, with his kopi. Ice stayed where he was and drank his own. That was what you did. You let Suzu have it.

Vivian had sent a message at eight. She always sent them at eight, on the dot, Sundays included. She said it was because the auntie downstairs from her office opened at eight, and it was the first thing she did with her coffee.

He laughed, out loud, in the back room. Gabe looked over.

“Vivian,” said Ice.

“The auntie?”

“The auntie.”

“The auntie’s my favourite person in Malacca,” said Gabe, with deep feeling, and went to get the roti.

He went in at nine.

He’d keep the room. He’d said so to Kei, on the stair. Kei and Higu and Gigi and Thea and Xola and Hana and Curse and Tiger and Drew and Rafi were all four days north at the edge of the world, looking at stones. And somebody had to keep the room.

He came out of the little dark of the egg into the banner room at Carrow Cross, into his chair, the first of the nine. The room was quiet. Sunday. Most of the guild were down the hatch, or out on the roads, or asleep in the gallery, or at the Hart. The long table was empty, end to end. Sixty chairs, most of them pushed in neatly. Ama Corrie liked them pushed in.

And Ama Corrie was at the far end, by the window, with her broom.

The fen-reed broom, with KEPT in blue ink on a tag on its handle. She was doing the chairs. Sunday. Under, not round. Slow. Humming the tune that wasn’t a tune. She was at Thea’s six, by the window, the ones that had been empty for so long. She was sweeping under them as carefully as she’d swept under them when they were empty. More carefully, maybe.

Ice got up and went down the long table to her.

“Can I help?” he said.

Ama Corrie stopped. She looked at him over her spectacles.

“Three times,” she said. “Forty years nobody asks. Then three times in a month.” She sniffed. “It’s catching.”

But she held out the broom.

He took it. He’d done it last Sunday too, with her watching him the way she watched everything. He did Thea’s six and then Hana’s five. They had a lot under them; Leaka’s especially. Then the nine, Moonlight Harvest’s. Pencil shavings under Dennis’s, mud under Calvin’s, fen grit under his own, still, after all this time. He didn’t know how. He hadn’t been to the fen in two months. Maybe fen grit just stayed with you.

He kept going up the table. He swept under every chair, slowly, under not round, the way Thea had told him Ama Corrie had told her. He didn’t look at whose they were, mostly. He just swept. And it was only when he got to the middle, to the first twenty, that he stopped looking at the floor and started looking at the chairs.

Kei’s, at the head. A ring on the table in front of it, from a cup.

Higu’s, empty, with an oilskin case hung over the back.

Gigi’s. Chair twenty-one. Halfway down the north side. He swept under it. There was hawthorn under it. Not petals this time, a little twig. It had come in on somebody’s coat from the south-west road. He swept it out into the light and looked at it for a moment.

Then he went on.

Thirty-six of them, in this room. He’d counted on the Friday the five signed. He’d told Kei on the stair, because he had to tell somebody. And Kei had said do you want me to mark them and he’d said no. They’re dots like everybody’s.

He didn’t count them again now. He just swept under their chairs. And under all the others too, the ones that weren’t the thirty-six. Rafi’s and Bram’s and Joy’s, Kite’s and Tobi’s. Riverbank’s three. Kotori’s. Tif’s and Xyro’s and Slep’s. The ones he hadn’t known a year ago and wouldn’t now have known how to do without.

Sixty chairs. Under, not round.

When he’d finished, Ama Corrie was sitting on the window seat with her hands in her lap, watching him.

“Hm,” she said.

He handed her the broom back.

“You missed one,” said Ama Corrie.

He looked down the table. He hadn’t missed one. He’d done all sixty. He’d counted.

Ama Corrie didn’t say anything. She just tipped her head, very slightly, toward the north door.

He looked.

There was a chair there.

It wasn’t at the table. It stood on its own at the north end of the banner room, by the door. Wooden, a bit wobbly, the same as all the others. It was set a little apart, facing north, toward the Hall’s north door beyond. As if it were waiting for somebody to come in that way.

Nobody sat in it. Nobody’s name went with it. He’d have known; he’d have swept under it.

He looked at Ama Corrie.

“Who’s that for?”

Ama Corrie looked at the chair for a long time over her spectacles.

“I set it out on Thursday night,” she said. “After the boy with the map messaged you from the edge. You read it out, at the long table. There’s a light in there. It didn’t go out.” She folded her hands. “I’ve been keeping this Hall forty years. My mother kept it before me, and hers before her. And in all that time, nobody ever came home through the north door. Things have come in through it. Travellers have gone out of it, north, now and then. Nobody’s ever come home that way. It sticks. It’s always stuck.” She nodded at it. “But a light that didn’t go out. Three hundred years in the dark. Somebody’s keeping that. And whoever it is, if they ever come home, they’ll come in that way.”

Ice didn’t say anything.

“So I set out a chair,” said Ama Corrie. “That’s all. Nobody’s ever going to sit in it, probably. It’s just a chair.” She picked up the broom and stood up. “But there’s a chair.”

She went over and swept under it. Slowly. Under, not round. There was nothing under it at all. She swept anyway.

They came home on Monday at the second dusk.

Hyvred saw them first. Of course she did. Ice was in the Hall, at the counter, writing something in the back pages of the Roll for Ama Corrie, whose hand was tired. And Hyvred’s voice came down the stair from the eye, the whole height of the dome, flat and carrying, and the pigeons went up.

“North road. Two miles. Ten.”

A pause.

“They’re coming round to the south.”

Of course they were. Everybody came through the gate.

He went out onto the south steps. So did everybody else in the Hall, it turned out, and half the market. There were more people on the steps than there’d been for the six, or the five. Everybody had heard. There’d been a light in the black and it hadn’t gone out. Kei had said so on his stream, and Hana had said so on hers to eleven thousand people, crying. And the whole game was talking about it.

He stood at the side, at the top of the steps, near Wendy. Not in the gate. Out of the way.

They came round the curve of the south road into the round market in the evening light. Ten of them, dusty, slow. Kei in front with his map under his arm. Higu beside him. Tiger with an empty sack. Rafi with his big sword. Drew at the edge. Hana with her hand in her pocket. Curse with his inkwell. Thea and Xola together. And Gigi, a pace off from Thea, on her other side. Not close. Near.

They came to the bottom of the steps and stopped.

“Hi,” said Wendy. “I’m the gate.”

“I know,” said Kei. “We live here.”

“Doesn’t matter,” said Wendy. “Everybody gets asked. Every time.” She took her hands out of her pockets. “Who’d stand next to you?”

Kei looked up the steps.

Past Wendy. Past Kite and Rafi’s empty place and Bram and Joy and Tobi. Past the crowd. To the side, near the top, where Ice was standing out of the way.

“Him,” said Kei. “He kept the room.”

Ice didn’t move. He didn’t know what to do with his face. So he didn’t do anything with it, and stood there, and let Kei have it.

“Fair,” said Wendy.

And she went along the ten. Higu said Kei. Tiger said Drew, and Drew said the one who stays, which made Tiger go red. Rafi said Boss, and looked at Ice, and grinned. Hana said Milli, who was on the steps already, crying. Curse said her, and looked at Leaka, who was also crying, very loudly.

Xola said Thea.

And Thea, at the bottom of the steps, with her tower shield on her back and the dust of the north moor on her boots, looked up at Wendy.

She didn’t look at Gigi. Gigi was standing a pace off on her left, looking at the fountain. She didn’t look at anybody. She just looked at Wendy, steadily, the way you look at a gate.

“Xola,” said Thea. “And Chi, at the back. And Slep. And my six.” She paused. “And the person who held the left at the last stone. When I set the lantern. She was there.”

The steps went very quiet.

Gigi didn’t look round. Her face didn’t do anything. But Ice was at the top of the steps, and he could see her hands. They were behind her back, holding her staff, and they had gone perfectly still.

“Fair,” said Wendy, after a moment. Very quietly. “Gigi?”

Gigi looked at the fountain for a long time.

“Gu,” she said. “And—“ She stopped. “And whoever calls the Turn.”

“Gate’s open,” said Wendy.

He didn’t go down the steps to them. It wasn’t his. He went back into the Hall, and through to the banner room, and sat down in his chair, the first of the nine. And the room filled up round him.

It filled up the way it had on the night the five signed. People came in from the steps and the market and the stair and the gallery and the cellar. Gabe telling somebody about the second heart, again. Leaka and Xola meeting in the doorway and shouting ICEY BOI at each other, which was apparently how they said hello now. Kotori and Tif with a pot. PenguChi in her chair, mending something, with Patience asleep on her feet. Deston counting nothing. Suzu doing nothing. Hana and Milli by the window, in their chairs, with their hands on the arm between them. Curse beside them, with his HOME tag. Jared standing behind them with a beam, for no reason, because he liked having one.

Kei at the head of the table with his map unrolled, telling it. The rings, and the stones, and the soot in the hollow. LAMP, in blue. Twenty-two lanterns. And a light, a long way in, that didn’t go out. He told it plainly, the way Kei told things, and the whole long table went quiet to hear it.

When he’d finished, Ama Corrie came round the table with the old Roll in her arms. She opened it at the back, at the page that wasn’t blank. Beside the line she’d written in on the Friday the five signed, in her own small hand, she wrote one more.

A light seen, north, past the last stone. It did not go out. Twenty-two lamps set out behind it. By ten, for the rest.

“There,” said Ama Corrie. “Kept.”

And at the north end of the banner room, by itself, facing north, the chair stood empty. Nobody said anything about it. Everybody in the room had seen it. Nobody needed to.

Halfway down the table, Gigi passed Thea a cup of tea.

She did it the way they did it, now. Not directly. Down the table, through two other people’s hands. Maigo’s and Hako’s. It arrived in front of Thea. Thea picked it up, and drank, and said “Hot,” down the table, to nobody in particular.

“I can do hot,” said Gigi, to her own cup.

That was all. Nobody else at the table seemed to notice. Ice noticed. Xola, beside Thea, noticed, and her gills flared. Then, very slowly, they went flat again. She didn’t say anything. She just reached over and took a biscuit off Thea’s plate, the way she’d been doing for years, and ate it.

And on the stair outside the banner room door, on the first step, where she could see in and nobody could see her, Hyvred was sitting with her bow across her knees.

He saw her when he got up to go to the roof. He’d decided he’d go to the roof. He’d never been up the whole two hundred and twelve; everybody said you should, once, at moonrise. He came out of the banner room door, and she was there, on the first step, and he stopped.

“Hi, Hyv,” he said.

She looked up at him. She didn’t hit him. She’d hit him every time he came back from anywhere for five months, and he’d stopped expecting anything else. She didn’t hit him.

“Hi,” said Hyvred.

He waited. She looked at her bow.

“I called the left heart,” she said. “At Elmcross. The fifth try.”

“I know,” said Ice. “I went.”

“You didn’t turn your head.”

“I didn’t need to,” said Ice. “You’d called it.”

Hyvred went red. All the way up, from the collar to the gold horns, and her tail did a thing that he’d started to notice it doing lately and didn’t understand. She looked at him for a long, long moment. With her eyes that could see everything, edge to edge, all of it at once.

“No,” said Hyvred.

He didn’t know what she was saying no to. He thought it might not be to him.

“Okay,” said Ice.

And she got up and went into the banner room, past him, without touching him, very fast, in a straight line. She sat down next to Bee. Bee, without looking up from her drawing, moved one wing a little, so there was room behind it if anybody needed it.

He looked at the two of them for a while. Then at Xola, who was looking at him from across the room with an expression of the most enormous delight. Then at Thea, beside her, who had turned to face the window.

Something’s happened, he thought, the way he’d thought it in the Fell Country. Probably Thea.

He went up the stairs.

Two hundred and twelve. A hundred and six to the first landing, where the little window looked out over the east road. Forty to the second, where you started to feel the wind. Sixty-six to the top.

He counted them. Everybody counted, the first time.

Higu was on the ledge, in her place, with the lamp beside her and two cups. Gigi wasn’t with her. Gigi was in the banner room, at chair twenty-one, with a cup of tea that had gone through two other people’s hands.

“You came up,” said Higu.

“Everybody says you should. Once.”

“Everybody’s right,” said Higu. She moved over on the ledge, and gave him her place, and the second cup. “Sit. It’s nearly time.”

He sat.

The eye of the dome was a round hole in the top of the world, full of sky. Below him, two hundred feet down, the floor of the Hall of Roads. The gold dots and silver lines Bee had painted on the old stone, sixty of them, all solid. And one gold dot near the north door that wasn’t anybody yet. People were crossing the floor, small, going to the banner room or the stair or the gallery. Ama Corrie was at her counter with her pen. The night was coming down blue through the eye.

And then the moon came up over the rim.

He’d seen it on Higu’s stream, in June. Everybody had. He’d never seen it from here.

The moonlight came down through the eye and touched the ceiling of the dome all round him. And the lines came out. One by one, slowly, silver on grey stone, all round him at once. Roads and rivers and towns, the whole world. The round town in the middle, and the seven roads going out of it. The fen, far to the west, where a ring of nine stones stood in the dark with one heron facing out. Quarrymouth on its coast. Merrowgate on its river. Elmcross with its seven elms. Millrace with its wheels. Every one of them, every town anybody he loved had ever woken up in. Every line running north. All of them. North across the moor and past the old hall and up the line of stones every fifty paces, to the great gold star above the north door, off every map.

He sat on the ledge with Higu’s cup in his hands and looked at it for a long time.

“Kei says you counted,” said Higu, after a while. Quietly. Not looking at him.

“I told him.”

“He didn’t tell me how many,” said Higu. “He said it wasn’t his.”

“It wasn’t.”

“Is it mine?”

Ice thought about it.

“Thirty-six,” he said. “Counting me.”

Higu didn’t say anything for a long time. She’d known him a long time too. Not since school. From chats. From three in the morning.

“I said bring everyone,” said Higu. “To Thea. In April. I never thought it’d be everyone.”

“Nor did I.”

“You were in everyone’s chat,” said Higu. “For years. I used to see your name in mine at four in the morning and think, he must never sleep.” She smiled at the ceiling. “And now we’re all in yours.”

“It’s Kei’s,” said Ice.

“It’s Kei’s,” agreed Higu. “But you’re at the end of the table. Facing everybody.” She turned her head and looked at him in the moonlight. “Not the far end of the room any more, Ice. The end of the table. That’s different.”

He looked down through the eye at the floor of the Hall. At the banner room door, warm with lamplight and noise. At sixty chairs, all sat in. At one more, by the north door, by itself, facing north. Swept, though there was nothing under it.

“Set one to watch,” he said.

“So the rest may sleep,” said Higu. “Who’s watching tonight?”

He thought about it. About a ring of nine stones in a fen, with one heron facing out. He’d sat with his back to that stone more nights than he could count. About a man at the bottom of a north door, saying go on, then. About twenty-two lanterns on a moor, set out behind. And a light, a long way in, that somebody had kept for three hundred years so whoever came after could see it.

“Somebody,” he said. “A long way north.”

Higu laughed, very softly, and the laugh went up through the eye and out into the night.

“Then you’re the rest,” she said.

“Yes,” said Ice.

He was surprised to find he meant it.

He went down at midnight, two hundred and twelve steps, and the banner room was still full, and still loud. Gabe was asleep in his chair with his mouth open. Leaka and Xola were asleep on each other on the window seat. Kotori and Tif were asleep against the pot. PenguChi was awake, at the back, watching all of it, with the sheep on her feet.

Ama Corrie was going round the chairs with the broom, though it wasn’t Sunday any more. Under, not round. Under the sat ones and the empty ones. Under all sixty. And under the one by the north door.

He went down the long table, past all of them, to the first of the nine. He sat down and put his feet up on the rung of the next one. He looked down the table at everybody he’d ever known for years, and everybody he’d known for a few months, at one table, in one room, on one map. Then at the chair by the north door, empty, facing north.

He didn’t know who it was for. Nobody did. It didn’t matter.

There was a chair.

He fell asleep in his own, after a while, sitting up, with his hands flat on the long table. Nobody woke him. Ama Corrie came by with the broom and swept under it anyway. Slowly, under not round, with him asleep in it.

PenguChi, at the back, watched her do it. Then she watched the room for a long time after that, all of it, the whole line. And when she was sure everybody was asleep, and nobody needed her to be the back of anything, she went to sleep too.
