Live from Elsewhere · Volume 4: The Ninth Heron

Chapter 102

Owed

2,167 words · 9 min read

She didn’t go down the stair on Wednesday night.

She’d meant to. She’d stood on the step and said Bee into the quiet, and then she’d found that she couldn’t make her feet go down. If she went down, she’d say it. And if she said it, it would be true. Her best friend had gone behind her back, after she’d said I, after she’d said I don’t like owing people things, it sits on me. Bee had listened. Bee had said then I’ll be annoying about it. And then Bee had gone and—

So she logged out on the stair, in a safe place, and lay in the egg in her room in Singapore for a long time with the lid up, not sleeping.

At two in the morning she got her phone out and looked at the note.

3,821.

She didn’t write 0 under it. She couldn’t. Her thumb wouldn’t do it.

Thursday evening the expedition came home.

She heard them before she saw them. She was on the stair again, not watching the roads, just sitting. Then there was a noise from the north door. Not the stick it used to make. The new sound, the smooth one Gabe had put in, and then voices. Maigo first, obviously, at full volume, and Tiger laughing, and somebody falling over, also obviously Tiger. Then Hako, narrating. Then a lot of people talking at once, all of them tired, all of them happy, the way people sounded when they’d walked a long way and come home.

She looked down the stair into the Hall.

From the stair you could see everything. That was the point of it. In here she could see everything from everywhere, and she still hadn’t got used to it after four and a half months. She saw nine people come in through the north door with mud to the knees. She saw Gigi sit straight down on the floor. She saw Kei, with his map under his arm, looking older than he had eight days ago. Not tired. Older. As if he’d read something.

And Salty, last, with something in his arms. Something big and square, wrapped in a cloak and a coat and an oilskin. He carried it the way you’d carry somebody asleep.

He walked across the Hall to the counter. Ama Corrie was behind it, as she always was, with her spectacles on their chain. She watched him come. Salty put the bundle down on the counter in front of her, very gently. Then he stepped back.

Ama Corrie unwrapped it.

Hyvred saw her face when the last of the oilskin came off. She’d never seen Ama Corrie’s face do anything much. It did something now. Ama Corrie looked down at the black book on her counter. Swollen, green at the bottom, older than anything, the twin of the one she’d kept for forty years. And she put her hand flat on the cover, and didn’t say anything for a very long time.

Then she said, “Well.” She cleared her throat. “Somebody had better keep it.”

Hyvred watched Kei say something to her across the counter, very quietly. She couldn’t hear it from the stair. She saw Ama Corrie take her spectacles off and wipe them on her sleeve, which she’d never seen her do, and put them back on.

Then Hyvred stopped watching, because she’d seen Bee.

Bee was in the banner room.

She’d been there since the afternoon, drawing. She drew in there most evenings, sitting on the floor under the banner with her back to the wall and her book on her knees. She said the light was good. Hyvred thought she just liked being near the banner. She’d painted it with an eyelash brush over two nights on a bridge, and she looked at it the way Hyvred looked at the seven roads.

Hyvred came down the stair and across the Hall, past the noise and the mud and Maigo telling somebody about a tree. She went into the banner room and stood in the doorway.

Bee looked up.

“You turned your stream off,” said Bee.

“Yes.”

“In the middle. You never turn it off in the middle.” Bee put her pencil down. “I watched the alert. Hyv. That’s—“

“Don’t,” said Hyvred.

Bee stopped.

“Don’t that’s me,” said Hyvred. “Don’t you dare that’s me. I said I. In February. On the phone. You said we save, and I said I save, and I said I don’t want to owe anybody. Not you. Especially not you. And you said fine, you said then I’ll be annoying about it, and you were. You were annoying about it every single night. And I thought that was it. I thought that was what you were doing instead.” Her voice was going up. It always went up. “And then you went and— to the dollar, Bee. To the dollar. You’ve been asking me the number every night for forty-seven nights so you’d know exactly—“

“I don’t have three thousand eight hundred dollars,” said Bee.

“Twenty-one.”

“I don’t have three thousand eight hundred and twenty-one dollars,” said Bee. “I have a pencil.”

Hyvred stood in the doorway.

“I’m a person with a pencil, Hyv.” Bee hadn’t moved. She was still sitting on the floor under the banner, with her book on her knees and her wings folded flat. She said it in her ordinary voice, blunt and flat, the way she said everything. “I streamed to thirty people in November.” She looked up at Hyvred. “If I had four thousand dollars I’d have thrown it at your head in February. And you’d have thrown it back. So I didn’t. Because you said I.”

The banner room was very quiet. Out in the Hall, Maigo was still talking about the tree.

“You asked me the number,” said Hyvred. Her voice had gone small. “Every night.”

“Because you liked saying it,” said Bee. “You liked it, Hyv. Every night it went down and you said it like you’d won something. So I asked.” She picked her pencil up again, and then put it down again. “I listened. In February. You said I. So I listened.”

Hyvred looked at her best friend for a long time.

Then she came into the banner room and sat down on the floor next to Bee, with her back against the wall, under the banner. She put her face in her hands.

“Sorry,” she said, through her hands.

“I know.”

“I’m sorry, Bee.”

“I know. Stop.” Bee’s wing moved, a little, and settled against Hyvred’s shoulder. The way it did when Bee wanted to hold onto somebody and wouldn’t say so. “It wasn’t me. That’s all. You can be sorry later. Right now somebody gave you four thousand dollars and you don’t know who, and that’s worse.”

“It’s so much worse,” said Hyvred, into her hands.

“How much?”

They both looked up.

Kei was in the doorway. He had mud to the knees and his map under his arm and his green hair tie coming loose. He looked as if he’d come in to see the banner and found them under it. He must have been standing there for a while.

“Sorry,” said Kei. “I didn’t mean to— I came to look at it. I’ve been looking at the other one.” He glanced up at the banner, and then back down at Hyvred. “How much?”

“Three thousand eight hundred and twenty-one,” said Bee, before Hyvred could decide whether to say anything. “On her stream. Last night. Anonymous. No message.”

“To the dollar,” said Hyvred. “Exactly what was left. Not a dollar more.”

Kei didn’t even think about it.

“Ice,” he said.

Hyvred stared at him.

“What? No. He doesn’t— Kei, he doesn’t even know. I never told him. I never told anyone except Bee, and Bee never—“

“Was Suzu there?” said Kei. “At the clinic?”

Hyvred went very still.

“Monday,” she said.

“Then Ice knows.” Kei came into the banner room and sat down on the floor across from them, with his back to the opposite wall, as if his legs had decided to. He looked very tired. “Suzu wouldn’t have told him. Suzu doesn’t tell things. He’d have just come home and said Hyvred was there. Nothing else. And Ice would’ve watched your stream that night for the first time in a month, and seen the bar, and done the sum.”

“You don’t know that.”

“I know Ice,” said Kei. He said it gently. “He’s got more money than he knows what to do with, Hyv. Real money, from before. From the old game. And I think it bothers him, having it sit there.” He turned his map over in his hands. “In January he tried to pay for this whole guild. Five hundred gold. I said no before he’d finished the sentence. And he said I know. I had to say it.” He looked up at her. “He didn’t get to say it to you. So he didn’t say it.”

“To the dollar,” said Bee slowly.

“So you can’t send any back,” said Kei. “If it’s a dollar over, you send the dollar back, and then you send the rest back. He knows you. If it’s exactly what’s left, there’s nothing to send back. There’s just—“ He made a small shape with his hand. “Done.”

Hyvred sat on the floor of the banner room under Bee’s banner and didn’t say anything.

She was thinking about a rope over a gorge. About Suzu saying two per cent and Ice laughing. About a waiting room on the eleventh floor, and a small woman sitting very straight, who’d said both of us as if it were already true. About Suzu at the end of a corridor, saying nothing at all, when he always said something.

“He paid for Suzu’s mum,” she said.

Kei didn’t say anything. Which was a yes.

“Two per cent,” said Hyvred. “On the gorge. That’s what that was. He lent it him, and Suzu made him take two per cent, and they laughed about it on stream in front of thirty thousand people, and nobody knew what it meant—“ She stopped. “And now he’s done it to me. Without asking. Without lending. He just—“

“Yes,” said Kei.

“I don’t want it.”

“I know.”

“I don’t want to owe him.” She could hear her voice going up again. “I don’t want to owe anybody. I told you. I told Bee. I don’t like owing people things. It sits on me. It sits on me like a—“ She didn’t have a word for what it sat on her like. “And I don’t even know him that well. He made a joke about my eyes once and apologised for ten minutes. He sent me a text on my first night saying no flashlight now. That’s— that’s it. That’s all we’ve got. And now he’s—“

“Yeah,” said Kei.

She stopped.

The banner room was quiet. Out in the Hall, somebody had started a song. Newish on the drum, probably, and Maigo singing, badly. Under it, very faint, Ama Corrie’s voice, reading something out loud at the counter. A list.

Hyvred got her phone out.

She did it in the game, the way you could now the egg talked to your phone, a small window in the corner of her eye. She opened the note. 3,821, at the top of the long column that only went down. She looked at it for a long time.

Then she scrolled to the very top of the note, above 10,000, above all of it. On a new line, she wrote a name. And beside it, a number.

ICE — 3,821.

She looked at it. A name. She had a name now, in her head, that she owed.

“What are you doing?” said Bee.

“Starting again,” said Hyvred. “A piece at a time.”

Bee looked at the note over her shoulder. Then she laughed. A short, surprised, flat laugh, like a door shutting, the only kind Bee had.

“He’s going to hate that,” said Bee.

“Good,” said Hyvred.

She shut the note and opened her messages. She found his name, the one she’d turned on notifications for in November, the first person she’d ever done that for. She typed four letters, in capitals, and sent them before she could stop.

[ Messages · Ice
Hyvred: ICE.
]

Then she sat on the floor under the banner, between her best friend and Kei, and watched the screen.

He didn’t answer.
