Chapter 185

Somewhere at the Front

3,511 words · 15 min read

“Forty,” called John, from the eye. “Forty left on the road.”

Salty heard it the way he’d heard every number since half past eight. Through the roar of the fire behind him, the way you hear somebody calling down a mine shaft. He didn’t look up. You didn’t look up at the front. You looked at the road.

The north road went out of the north door into the dark. For a quarter of a mile you could see it, lit gold by the great iron basket roaring in the door behind him, the way you could see a path by a lighthouse. The heather either side, the first stones of the moor, the line of the road going away north. Then the light ran out, and there was nothing. Just black. The black where the road went on, four days, ninety-four miles, ninety-four lamps. Forty of them still lit, out there, facing the other way, a long way off.

And something coming down it, putting them out.

“Thirty-eight,” called John.

He stood in the middle of the north door with Right Size in both hands, point down on the stone in front of him. Two shields wide. Thea’s three on his left, Azac’s three on his right. Wendy beside him with her bow. Kite on her other side with his. The fire roaring at their backs so hot he could feel it through his armour. And the cold coming at their faces off the moor.

He was on fifty. He’d been on fifty since the first week, since a shaft mouth in Grimmet, because Ice was on fifty. He could feel everything. The heat on his back, the cold on his face, the weight of the sword in his hands, which was exactly the right weight. And underneath all of it, a sort of hum. Like standing on a railway line and feeling a train coming a long way off.

“Okay?” said Wendy.

“Okay,” said Salty.

She’d asked it every twenty minutes since twenty past eight. She’d asked it every twenty minutes all night last year, too, on a call, from a gate in a wood eleven days away, while he stood in a shaft mouth. Eighteen inches away in the next egg, in the real world. He’d answered every time. Okay. It was the only word either of them needed.

At ten past two, the road ran out of lamps.

“Five,” called John. “Four. Three.” A pause, a long one. “Two.” His voice changed. “Tiger. It’s coming. One left. The first mile. It’s you.”

Salty looked up the road.

A mile out, in the dark, past the end of the fire’s light, where he couldn’t see, was the first waystone. The last lamp on the road of lights. Tiger, Maigo and Hako were standing round it. They’d been standing round it since eight, in the cold, in the open, at the end of everything. Keeping the one lamp that the dark would see last. Because when it went, the next nearest light was the round town.

“Tiger,” called Kei, from the eye. Not John; Kei. Salty had never heard Kei shout before. “When it goes, you come in. Don’t stay. You come in.”

There was a long pause.

Then, very faint, a mile off in the dark, a voice. Tiger’s. Thin with distance and cold, but you could hear it.

“Not yet.”

The wall went very quiet.

“Oh, Tiger,” said Thea, on the left, under her breath.

“He’ll come,” said Gigi.

“He’d better,” said Xola.

Salty stood in the door with his sword and stared up the road into the black. He couldn’t see anything. He could hear things, a long way off. A shout, Maigo’s. The crack of Hako’s spear on something. And under it, getting louder, the hum. The train. The thing coming down the road a hundred miles long.

“First mile’s holding,” called John. “It’s holding. Tiger’s on it. Maigo’s healing. Hako’s— oh, Hako—“

A minute. Two. Three. Salty counted them with his hands on the hilt.

“First mile’s out,” called John. “It’s out. Tiger, COME IN.”

They came out of the dark at a run.

Three of them, down the north road, into the edge of the fire’s light. Tiger in front, going like he went, low and fast and never quite touching the ground, Skim. Maigo behind him with his staff and his antlers and his sunglasses, stupidly, on his face. Hako at the back with his spear, turning every ten paces to look behind him.

And behind them, the dark.

Salty had seen Unlit before. Everyone had. He’d killed them by the hundred in a shaft mouth in Grimmet a year ago, coming up out of the mine at him in the dark. He’d seen the eleven Hako had drawn on the map in November, tall and thin and swaying like reeds.

This was not that.

This was the dark itself, coming down the road. All of it. Not shapes. A wall. A wave. A black so black that the fire’s gold didn’t go into it, it just stopped at the edge, the way light stops at the edge of deep water. It came down the road behind the three of them at a walk, filling the road and the heather either side, edge to edge, as far as he could see. It leaned. All of it, at once, the whole wave, toward the fire in the north door.

Toward him.

“Open,” said Azac, on the right. “Let them in. Open.”

The wall had to open. Two shields wide, and three people running into it with the dark behind them. If it opened too early, the dark came through with them. If it opened too late, they died against the wall. Salty saw the sum, the way Milli would have. A heartbeat. Maybe less.

Thea saw it too.

“Turn,” said Thea.

Her tower shield came round, the whole left side of the wall swinging like a gate. And Gigi, beside her, on the word, said hers. Lie. A line of living thorn came up out of the stone of the road, low, across the gap between Thea’s shield and the open air, and grew. And Xola, on Thea’s other side, was already moving into where Thea had been. And Tiger came through the gap at a skim, and Maigo, and Hako, past the thorn and the shield and into the door. And the wave hit the thorn a second behind them.

“Turn,” said Thea again, and the shield came back.

“Good thorn,” said Thea.

“Good turn,” said Gigi.

And the wall was closed.

Tiger fell over just inside the door, at Salty’s feet, on his back on the stone, with his chest going up and down. He was at eleven per cent. He was laughing.

“Not yet,” said Tiger, to the dome. “I said not yet. I meant it.”

“You’re an idiot,” said Maigo, already kneeling beside him with his hands on Tiger’s chest. Hold. A green light.

“I kept it,” said Tiger. “Kei. Tell Kei. I kept it twenty minutes.”

“We heard,” called Kei, from the eye. His voice was very odd. “Tiger. We heard. Thank you.”

Salty looked back at the road.

The dark was at the wall.

He’d tell it to nobody. He wasn’t good at telling things. He’d tell Wendy a bit, in the café, afterward, eighteen inches away. It was very dark. I hit it a lot. That was all he’d ever say. But he’d remember all of it, the whole night, his whole life. The way you remember the first time you ever stood somewhere that was exactly the right size.

It came at the wall all at once. The whole wave. It leaned in at the north door, at the fire, and the front of it, where it touched the wall, came apart into shapes. Tall ones and low ones and ones with no shape at all, coming at the fire. And between them and the fire were nine people standing two shields wide.

Salty swung.

He’d swung the greatsword for ten months. A size too small. It had ended before he did. Ice had said it, in Joss’s smithy. Like it ended before you did. Every swing he’d ever made, he’d had to stop a little short. Pull it in. Not finish.

Right Size didn’t end before he did.

It went round. The whole of him, the way Drew had told him at the anvil: hips, not shoulders, turning, the weight coming up out of the stone through his legs and his back and his arms and out to the end of the blade. And the blade was exactly where his swing ended. Not before. Not after. Exactly. Black, dull, the length of a man, it went through the front of the dark like a scythe through standing grain.

And the Unlit didn’t see it.

He understood that later. On the third swing, maybe, or the fourth. They saw light. That was all they saw. They leaned to light, and away from nothing. And Right Size had been forged from the dark’s own iron, quenched in the dark where it came from. It was never meant to see the light. It didn’t shine. In the gold of the fire it was a black line moving through black, and the Unlit didn’t see it coming, any more than you’d see a shadow coming in the night.

It took them before they knew it was there.

“Salty,” said Wendy, beside him, in a voice he’d never heard her use. “Salty, your grades—“

“I know.”

He swung again.

They held.

All night, they held. He lost track of time. There was no time at the front; there was the next one and the next. There was Wendy’s bow beside him, let go, four arrows, four, four, the way Hester had taught her in Ashcombe. There was Kite on her other side, quieter, every arrow where it was needed. There was Thea’s shield on the left, turning and turning back, and Gigi’s thorn laying itself across every gap the shield left. Thorn, left. Lie. Turn, right. Good thorn. Good turn. Over and over, like a song. Xola between them, on the outside, glaring at the dark as if it had personally offended her.

On the right, Azac.

He didn’t swing. He didn’t move. He stood on the right of the north door with the biggest shield in the guild planted on the stone and let the dark hit it. And the dark hit it and hit it and didn’t get past. RX was on his left with two knives, and Ryan on his right, and they cut whatever came round the edges. And every so often Azac said, to nobody, through his teeth, “I’m a wall.” Not loudly. As if he were reminding himself.

Behind them, on the north steps, the fire. Gabe’s three, feeding it. Gabe with a sack of coal on each shoulder, talking the whole time, shouting the whole time, SimpleMMO and the duel and the second heart and come ON then at the dark. Rachael with her hands up, Mending whoever needed it most. Salty had felt her twice already, a warmth going up his back from behind like a hand. And Calvin running up and down the line telling Rachael who was lowest. Salty, Rae. Salty. Then Azac. Then Hako.

“Okay?” said Wendy.

“Okay.”

At five o’clock, the big ones came.

Ice had said they would. On the first Long Night, at Fernwick, they’d got bigger as the night went on, and the biggest had come near the real dawn. That’s when it’s hardest. Just before. Salty had heard him say it in the banner room. And Hollis had said it, a year ago, on Ice’s stream: it’s not a long night till the dawn doesn’t come.

They were the height of the door. Taller. Tall and thin and swaying, the heron-shapes Ice had fought in the fen, but bigger, much bigger. They came out of the wave one at a time and stood up over the wall and leaned down at the fire. And their hits were cold. Not cold like the moor. Cold like something being taken out of you.

The first one hit Thea’s shield, and Thea went back a pace. Salty had never seen Thea go back.

“Thea,” said Xola.

“I’m fine,” said Thea. “I’m fine. Gigi—“

“Lie,” said Gigi. Thorn across the gap. Good thorn.

The second one hit Azac’s shield, and Azac didn’t move, and the shield went white with frost from rim to rim.

“I’m a wall,” said Azac. Louder.

The third one stood up over the middle of the door. Over Salty.

He looked up at it.

It was the biggest thing he’d ever seen that wasn’t a building. It stood up over him in the fire’s light, black, swaying, with no face, the shape of a heron made of the whole night. It leaned down. Not at him. Past him. At the fire. It didn’t care about him. It was going to fold itself over the great iron basket in the north door like a hand over a candle, and put out the biggest light in the round town. And then the next nearest light would be the windows. Every window. The baker’s, with three faces behind it. Old Joss’s, with his candle in both hands. The cottage at the end of the north road where an old woman had lit her own.

Salty stepped out of the wall.

He didn’t think about it. He’d tell Wendy later he hadn’t thought about it, and it would be true. He stepped out, one pace forward, in front of the door. In front of everybody, two shields wide, Thea’s three on his left and Azac’s on his right. Somewhere at the front, with nobody in front of him, the way he’d wanted to stand since the first morning in Grimmet with a sword a size too small.

“Salty,” said Wendy.

He swung.

He swung with everything. With the hips, the way Drew said. With the weight coming up out of the stone. With ten months of a sword a size too small and four hundred and eighty folds. With nineteen black pieces on Joss’s windowsill and a stream in the dark of the fourth tunnel singing like a bell under water. And the blade went round, the whole length of it, and ended exactly where he ended.

It went through the thing’s leg.

It didn’t see it coming. It was looking at the fire. A black line in the black, the dark’s own iron coming round at the dark, and it never saw.

The heron of night came down.

It came down slowly, the way big things do. It folded in the middle, the way it had meant to fold over the fire. But it folded sideways, away from the door, off its cut leg, onto the road. And it came apart as it fell, into shapes and then smoke and then nothing. It poured back into the wave behind it like ink into ink.

And the wave, for a moment, all of it, edge to edge, leaned back.

Not far. A pace. Less. But the whole of it, at once, as if the dark itself had flinched.

Salty stood in front of the north door with Right Size in his hands and the frost on his armour and the fire roaring at his back. And he found he couldn’t lift the sword again. Not straight away. His arms had nothing left in them.

Wendy’s hand caught the back of his belt and pulled.

He went back into the wall. Into his place, in the middle, between her and Thea. Thea’s shield came round in front of him like a door closing. Turn. Gigi’s thorn came up across the gap. Lie. And Rachael’s warmth went up his back from behind like a hand, and kept going, and didn’t stop.

“You idiot,” said Wendy, in his ear. “You absolute idiot. You stepped OUT.”

“I know.”

“You don’t step out. Nobody alone. That’s the rule.”

“I wasn’t alone,” said Salty. “You had my belt.”

Wendy made a noise that wasn’t a word.

He couldn’t read it. He was in the egg. Tif told him afterward. He didn’t need to read it.

“Okay?” said Wendy, at twenty past five, because it was twenty minutes.

He thought about it. He was at thirty-one, coming up. His arms were shaking. The dark was at the wall, all of it, still, and the big ones were still coming, one at a time, out of the wave. Thea was at forty and Azac at thirty-five, and Tiger, beside Hako on the right now, was at nineteen and grinning. Gabe was hoarse from shouting. Rachael was swearing at everyone individually.

Behind him, the fire was roaring. In front of him, the dark.

In the middle, the wall. Two shields wide. Him in it, with a sword the right size.

“Okay,” said Salty.

He meant it more than he’d ever meant it.

At half past six, John called from the eye.

His voice had changed again. Not frightened, like at two. Not quick, like a scout. Something else. Salty didn’t know what it was.

“The road,” called John. “The far end. The edge. Something’s—” A pause. “The lamps. They’re coming back on.”

The whole wall heard it. Salty felt it go through them, all nine, like Deston’s song.

“The twenty-two,” called Higu. “One. Two. Three. Somebody’s lighting them. Somebody’s lighting them one at a time. From the north.”

“Coming south,” called John. “Down the road. Behind the dark.”

Salty stood in the north door with Right Size in his hands and looked up the road. He couldn’t see anything. Just black, and the wave, and the heron-shapes still coming out of it, fewer now. He didn’t look up at the eye. You didn’t look up at the front.

But he thought about a lamp, a long way north, set down in the black past the last stone. For three hundred years, somebody carrying it. Sitting beside it, Hako had said, looking back.

Waiting for somebody to light the road.

“Okay,” said Salty, very quietly, to the road. To whoever was on it. “Okay. Come on, then.”

Beside him, Wendy let go. Four arrows.
