Live from Elsewhere · Volume 7: Keep the Lamps

Chapter 179

Chernn

3,519 words · 15 min read

She’d said later for a year, and on Wednesday she’d said now, and Deston hadn’t asked why.

He’d learned that a long time ago. Not about her; about people. When somebody said yes to something after a year of saying later, you didn’t ask why. You said okay, and you booked the booth before they could change their mind. If they wanted to tell you why, they’d tell you. Mostly they didn’t need to. Mostly it was just that it was time.

So on Saturday morning he was standing at the top of the steps in the old cinema on the fourth floor of a mall in town. The Cradle Commons, Singapore. The same place it had been a year ago, when Maigo had turned up at nine minutes to seven on the first day it opened. Forty eggs in curtained booths on the old cinema steps, where the seats used to be. A sign on the wall that said Your account’s on your thumb, not on the egg. Please don’t eat in the eggs. Somebody had added, in marker, underneath: we mean it.

Cherning was standing beside him, looking at the sign.

“Do people eat in the eggs?” she said.

“Apparently.”

“Why would you eat in an egg?”

“I don’t know,” said Deston. “Twelve hours is a long time.”

She looked at the sign a while longer. Then she looked down the steps at the forty booths, the curtains, the little lights on the lids. She looked at them the way she looked at most new things, carefully, as if she was going to have to describe them to somebody later.

“Booth twenty-two,” said Deston. “I’m in twenty-three. Next to you.” He held up his phone with the booking on it. “I’ll be at the round town. You’ll be wherever it puts you. It’s random. Nobody gets to choose.”

“How far?”

“Could be anywhere.”

“How long to walk?”

“Could be a day,” said Deston. “Could be two weeks. Gabe’s was a week and he slept some of it.”

She nodded slowly. She didn’t seem worried. She seemed, if anything, interested.

“And then you’ll walk to me,” she said.

“I’ll walk to you,” said Deston.

The name took her four minutes.

He sat on the step outside booth twenty-two with the curtain half open and watched her lie in the egg with the lid up. The screen above her face was asking for a name. The first thing it asked anybody. He remembered it. Who are you, here?

She typed Chern.

[ That name is taken. ]

She looked at it.

“Somebody’s got Chern,” she said.

“Somebody’s got everything,” said Deston. “Bee only got Bee because somebody gave it back two days before.”

She thought about it. She typed something else, and deleted it. Typed something, deleted it. Then she typed Chern again, and added an n.

Chernn.

[ That name is yours. ]

“Two n’s,” said Deston.

“Because Chern was taken,” said Cherning, as if that explained it completely. And it did, really.

He leaned in and kissed her on the forehead through the open lid, which you probably weren’t supposed to do either. Then the lid came down, and the little light went on. He sat on the step outside booth twenty-two for a minute longer, with his hand on the curtain, not going in.

Then he went next door and lay down in twenty-three.

He came out of the dark into the banner room at Carrow Cross, in his chair, halfway down the long table, beside Gabe’s.

It was the first dawn. The room was nearly empty. Ama Corrie was at the far end. Dec was at the first of the nine, the way he always was at the first dawn, with a cup of something. He looked up as Deston came in.

“She’s in,” said Deston.

Dec put his cup down.

“Where?”

“Don’t know yet. She hasn’t said.” Deston opened the window for his letters. “She’ll write when she’s out of the first bit. The tutorial room. Whatever they do now.” He looked at the window. Nothing yet. “It’s random.”

“I know.”

“It could be anywhere.”

“It won’t be anywhere,” said Dec. “It’ll be somewhere.” He picked up his cup again. “Somewhere’s always walkable.”

That was very Dec. Deston had known him since they were thirteen, in a school choir, standing next to each other in the back row because they were the two who couldn’t stop growing. Thirteen years, all of them good. Somewhere’s always walkable. It was the kind of thing he said that sounded like nothing and turned out, if you thought about it later, to be the whole of something.

The letter came at twenty past.

[ Letter · from Chernn
Tallow Green. It smells of candles.
Is that bad? ]

He showed Dec.

Dec read it. Then he got up and went down the long table to Kei’s chair at the head, where Kei’s map was left rolled on the table overnight. He unrolled it. He looked for a long time. Then he put his finger on a place.

“Five days,” said Dec. “South-west. Off the Millrace road, past the White Hills.” He looked up. “It’s a candle village. Kei marked it in March. Chandlers. Small. Kind.” He let the map roll back up. “Higu read its line out, on the roof. In June. From the old book.”

“What was it?”

Dec thought about it. He’d been on the floor of the Hall that night, listening. Everybody had.

“Tallow Green,” he said. “On the longest night, a candle in every window. And one more for the road.”

Deston looked at the letter on his window. It smells of candles. Is that bad?

He wrote back.

[ Letter · to Chernn
Not bad. Good. Five days. I’m coming. ]

He walked it in five days, and he sang the whole way.

Not out loud, mostly. Under his breath. He was Kai in here, and Kai had the book, Brother Amon’s old chapel book with the one-line skill in it: Accord. Let the many move as one. He’d learned it in Fernwick on the second day in the world, in a chapel nobody had opened for a long time. It was a song, really. A count-in. You sang it, and the people round you fell into time with each other, the way a choir does. Xav had called him a conductor once.

There was nobody round him on the Millrace road. He sang it anyway. It kept his feet in time.

She wrote every night.

[ Letter · from Chernn
There’s a woman called Mother Dipp who makes the candles.
She says I hold the wick like a fishing line.
She says it’s wrong. ]

[ Letter · from Chernn
I’m level 2. I hit a goose with a stick.
The goose was very rude about it. ]

[ Letter · from Chernn
I made a candle.
It’s not straight. Mother Dipp says it’s honest.
41%. Is 41% good? ]

He read them on the road, walking. He read the one about the goose four times and laughed so much on the White Hills at dusk that a Chalkwalker stopped walking in its straight line and looked at him.

41% is very good, he wrote back. Better than my first anything.

[ Letter · from Chernn
Tell Declan I beat him. ]

He told Dec. Dec, at the round town, four days away, wrote back to him in about a minute.

[ Letter · from Ice
tell her congratulations
tell her candles are harder than swords ]

He passed it on. She didn’t reply for a long time. Then:

[ Letter · from Chernn
He would say that. ]

He came over the last hill into Tallow Green on Thursday at the second dusk.

It was small. Smaller than Fernwick. A village green, very round and very flat, with a pond on it, and a ring of low cottages with thatched roofs all the way round. A chalk hill behind, white in the dusk. And every cottage had a window lit. Not lamps. Candles. Little yellow flames in every window, dozens of them, all round the green, steady in the still evening. The whole place smelled of warm wax. It was the gentlest smell he’d ever smelled.

She was on the green.

She was standing by the pond in the plain grey clothes everybody started in, with a stick in one hand. A goose was standing about four feet away from her, looking at her with deep suspicion. She was looking at the goose. Neither of them had noticed him.

He stood at the top of the green and watched for a while.

The goose took a step. She took a step back. The goose took another step. She held up the stick, not threateningly, just to show it was there. The goose considered the stick. Then it turned round with enormous dignity and walked off into the pond. She stood there with the stick in her hand, looking after it, as if she’d won something.

“You’re slow,” said Deston.

She turned round.

He’d thought about what he’d say for five days. He’d thought of a lot of things. That was what came out. It was a thing you said to somebody you were very glad to see, when you couldn’t say the thing itself.

She looked at him for a long moment. In the dusk, on the green, with candles in every window.

“I’m not slow,” she said. “I’ve been fishing.” She held up the stick. “Mother Dipp says it’s a fishing line.”

“It’s a stick.”

“It’s a wick,” said Cherning. “Apparently. It’s how I hold things.” She lowered it. “You walked five days.”

“Yes.”

“Is it far? Five days?”

“Gabe’s was a week.”

“And he slept some of it,” said Cherning. “You said.” She looked at him for a moment more. Then she reached into the pocket of her grey starter clothes and took something out and held it out to him.

It was a candle.

It wasn’t straight. It leaned, a bit, about a third of the way up, like somebody who’d been told a joke halfway through standing up. The wax was uneven. The wick was slightly off centre. It was the most honest thing he’d ever seen.

“Forty-one per cent,” said Cherning. “I made it the first day. I kept it for you.”

He took it.

He stood on the green at Tallow Green, four days and five from the round town, holding a crooked candle. And he found he didn’t have anything to say at all. Which never happened to him. He liked talking to people. He was good at it. He could talk to anybody, anywhere, about anything; Dec said he could network with a lamp-post.

He couldn’t think of a single word.

“Thank you,” he said, at last.

“It’s crooked,” said Cherning.

“It’s honest,” said Deston.

He stayed a week.

He hadn’t meant to. He’d meant to stay a day or two and then walk her up to the round town, slowly, levelling on the way. But Tallow Green didn’t want to be left. It was small and kind and smelled of wax. Mother Dipp turned out to be an enormous old woman with forearms like a blacksmith’s and a voice like a pigeon. She took one look at Deston, all six foot whatever of him in the game and in life, and gave him a job.

“You,” said Mother Dipp. “Big one. Hold this.”

He held it. It was a frame with forty wicks hanging from it, for dipping. He held it for three days.

Cherning dipped. She got better. Her candles stopped leaning by Saturday and started being straight by Monday. On Tuesday she made one so straight that Mother Dipp held it up to the light and looked down it, the way Malcolm looked down his stick, and said “Hm,” in a way that sounded exactly like Ama Corrie.

“Is sixty-six good?” said Cherning.

“Dec’s never got sixty-six on anything,” said Deston. That wasn’t true, but it was the right thing to say.

They levelled in the afternoons. Not fast. She didn’t want fast. She wanted to look at things. They went up the chalk hill behind the village and fought the little white hares that lived in the turf, which were angry and bit ankles. She hit them with her stick, and he stood behind her and sang.

Accord. Let the many move as one.

He’d never used it with just one other person before. It was meant for a party, for a line, for twenty people on a bridge. With one other person it was different. It was like humming along with somebody in a kitchen. He felt where she was in the beat, and she felt where he was. And when the hare came at her ankle she moved before it got there, without knowing why, on the count.

She stopped, after the third one. She stood on the chalk hill with her stick and looked at him.

“Was that you?” she said.

“Was what me?”

“I knew where it was going to be.” She frowned. “I’ve never known where anything was going to be. In my life. I just knew.”

“That’s Accord,” said Deston. “It’s a song. It puts you in time with whoever’s singing.” He felt himself going red, which he hadn’t done in years. “With me. It puts you in time with me.”

She looked at him for a long moment, on the hill, in the wind, with the village below them and its candles not lit yet because it was day.

“Do it again,” she said.

He did it again.

On Wednesday night, at the second dusk, she asked about the round town.

They were sitting on the bench outside Mother Dipp’s cottage, on the green, with a candle each in their hands and the pond in front of them. The goose was asleep on the far bank. She’d reached Lv 6 that afternoon; he’d felt it through the song.

“What’s it like?” she said. “The round town.”

He told her. The seven roads and the market and the fountain, and the ring of nine dark stones Ama Corrie had found in the paving. The Hall of Roads, with its dome and the eye at the top and the two hundred and twelve steps. The banner room and sixty chairs and the long table. Ama Corrie, sweeping under, not round. Wendy at the gate, asking everyone who’d stand next to them. Dec at the first of the nine with his feet on the rung, asleep sitting up.

The chair by the north door, by itself, facing north. For nobody yet.

“Who’s it for?” said Cherning.

“Nobody knows,” said Deston. “Somebody who’s coming home. From the north. There’s a light.” He looked at the candle in his hands. “And there’s a road. Ninety-four lamps. All facing the wrong way, so only the dark can see them. They light the way for it. To the round town. So it doesn’t go anywhere else.”

She was quiet for a while.

“On the longest night,” she said.

“On the longest night.”

“Tallow Green’s got a line,” said Cherning. “Mother Dipp told me. The first day, when I asked why every window’s got a candle.” She held hers up. “On the longest night, a candle in every window. And one more for the road.”

“Dec told me. The first morning.”

“Which road?”

Deston thought about it. He thought about a line of ninety-four black cages on the backs of grey stones, all facing north. About Kei’s arrow on the map, drawn on the south steps in the cold, pointing down the north road to the north door. About a lamp, a long way out past the last stone, being carried.

“I think,” he said slowly, “it might be ours.”

Cherning looked at her candle for a long time.

“How many people are going?” she said. “To the round town. For the longest night.”

“All of us. Sixty. Kei’s making threes.” He hesitated. “Nobody’s allowed to be alone. Not this time. Last time people were alone. Salty was alone in a mine. Gigi was alone at a gate. Hako was alone on a riverbank all night.” He turned his candle round. “Kei says not this time. Twenty threes.”

“Sixty-one,” said Cherning.

He looked at her.

“If I come,” she said. “Sixty-one. That’s twenty threes and a spare.” She looked at the pond. “Is there room in yours?”

Deston didn’t say anything for a moment. He couldn’t. He hadn’t been able to say anything on the green on Thursday either. She was making a habit of it.

“I’m level six,” said Cherning. “I know. I’d be useless. I’d hold a stick. But I could hold a candle.” She held hers up again. “I’m quite good at candles now. Sixty-six. Mother Dipp says honest.” She paused. “And I’d be in time with you. That counts for something. Doesn’t it?”

“It counts for a lot,” said Deston.

He wrote to Dec that night, from the bench, while she was inside with Mother Dipp learning to dip a candle as thick as her wrist.

[ Letter · to Ice
Coming back. Slowly. She’s 6.
She wants to come for the longest night. She asked if there’s room in my three. ]

[ Letter · from Ice
tell kei
then tell her yes ]

[ Letter · from Ice
then tell her to bring candles ]

He read it twice. Then he went inside and told her, and Mother Dipp, overhearing, put her enormous floury hands on her hips and said that if anybody from Tallow Green was going to the round town on the longest night, they weren’t going without a sack. And she started filling one.

They left on Saturday at the first dawn, by the road north-east toward the White Hills and the Millrace road and the round town. Two weeks of walking, if they did it slowly. She wanted to do it slowly.

She carried the stick. He carried the sack. It was enormous. Mother Dipp had filled it with candles, two hundred of them, every size, from little ones for windows to great thick ones as fat as your arm that would burn for days. “And one more for the road,” Mother Dipp said at the edge of the green. She put one last candle in Cherning’s hands, a tall straight white one, plain, the best she’d ever made.

“Which road?” said Cherning.

“Whichever one needs it,” said Mother Dipp. “That’s always been the way.” She looked at Deston. “Big one. Mind her.”

“I will.”

“Not like that,” said Mother Dipp. “Mind her like a wick. Steady. Don’t pull.”

He didn’t know what that meant. Cherning did; he could tell. She went pink and looked at the ground.

They walked up the chalk hill and over the top, and Tallow Green went out of sight behind them. Its windows were all dark in the morning light, waiting for evening.

He sang. Under his breath. Accord.

And beside him on the road north-east, holding a stick like a fishing line, Chernn walked in time.
