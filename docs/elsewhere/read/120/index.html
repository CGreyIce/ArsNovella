Live from Elsewhere · Volume 5: Ask Me Again

Chapter 120

Like a Wheel

1,827 words · 8 min read

The first time something hit Thea’s shield, she fell over.

It was a crayfish. That was the humiliating part. A big one, admittedly, grey-green and the size of a dog, that came up out of the silt at the bottom of a mill channel on Thursday morning with its claws open.

[ Silt Crawler · Lv 7 ]

She’d seen it coming. She’d had the shield up. She’d had her feet planted the way she’d watched a man plant his feet for five months, side on, weight low. She’d thought, very clearly, this is the front. I’m the front. Then the crayfish’s claw hit the iron rim of the tower shield like a door being kicked in, and she went straight over backwards into the channel.

She came up spluttering, with the shield on top of her and Xola laughing so hard she had to hold onto a waterwheel.

“Don’t,” said Thea.

“I’m not—“ Xola was crying. “I’m not laughing—“

“You’re laughing—“

Slep had already gone round the crayfish’s other side with the glaive. Its claw dropped, the tell he’d learned to watch for, and he put the blade into the joint, and it was over. He looked down at Thea sitting in the channel under her shield.

“You were too still,” said Slep. Not unkindly. Slep was never unkind. “You stood like a wall. Walls fall over.”

“What am I supposed to stand like?”

Slep thought about it. He was always thinking about it.

“Not a wall,” he said.

The millwright told her the rest.

Her name was Jessamy, and she was old and short and made of knuckles, and she kept every wheel in Millrace turning. There were a hundred and twelve of them, and she knew every one by its sound. Thea had gone to her on Sunday for Artificing, because Artificing was making things that moved, and nothing in Millrace moved without Jessamy. She’d been in Jessamy’s workshop under the big wheel every morning since. Taking a broken wheel apart and putting it back together, cog by cog, until it turned.

Jessamy had watched her come in on Thursday afternoon, soaking wet, with a dent in her shield the shape of a claw.

“Fell over,” said Jessamy.

“How did you—“

“You walk like somebody who fell over.” Jessamy put down the cog she was filing. “What were you trying to be? When it hit.”

Thea thought about it.

“Something that doesn’t move,” she said.

“Hm.” Jessamy got up and went to the window and pointed out at the big wheel, turning in the race. “Look at that.”

Thea looked.

“Water comes down the race,” said Jessamy. “Hundreds of tons of it. All the time. Pushing. If that wheel tried not to move, the water would rip it off the wall in a day.” She watched it turn. “It doesn’t try not to move. It lets the water push it. It turns. And when it turns, it drives the hammer and the loom and the bellows and the saw.” She looked at Thea. “It takes the push, and it turns it into work. That’s what a wheel is. That’s all a wheel is.”

Thea looked at the big wheel, turning, turning, in the water.

“Don’t be a wall,” said Jessamy. “Walls fall over. Be a wheel.”

She went back to the channel on Friday.

The others came with her, because they always came with her now. Xola in the water with her trident and her gills going. Slep on her other side, always on her other side. PenguChi on the bank with her long spear, watching everything with her head on one side. Xyro with his bow. Tif behind all of them with her staff and her ears turned forward.

The crayfish came up out of the silt with its claws open.

Thea didn’t plant her feet. She stood loose, the way Jessamy stood when she was listening to a wheel. She let the claw come. When it hit the shield she didn’t try to stop it. She let it push her, turned with it, a quarter turn, the way the big wheel turned with the water, and the claw slid off the iron rim and went past her into nothing. The crayfish, which had put all its weight into a push that didn’t land, stumbled forward.

Right onto PenguChi’s spear.

“Thea,” said Xola, from the water.

Thea stood on the bank with her shield up and her heart going very fast. Behind her, Tif’s staff rang once, and something warm went through her.

“Again,” said Thea.

They did it again. And again, all afternoon, crayfish after crayfish up out of the silt. By evening she wasn’t falling over. By evening she’d worked out that if she turned the blow to the left, Slep was there, and if she turned it to the right, PenguChi’s spear was. She stopped thinking about the front at all, and started thinking about where she was sending things. A system. Water coming down the race, and the wheel turning it into work.

She thought about a man in a fen with a shield, turning blows off a slope for five months. Turned Block, he’d called it, on the first day, in front of twenty-one people. She’d been one of them. She’d thought then that it was just a thing he did.

She understood now that it was the whole of him.

Higu rang on Friday night.

Not a message. A call. Thea’s phone lit up on the shelf by the egg, and she looked at the name for a long time before she picked it up.

“Hi,” said Higu.

“Hi.”

Higu was in Australia too. In the game she was in a round town eight days east, at the top of a stair inside a dome, where she said the moon came in. Thea could hear wind behind her voice.

“I’m on the roof,” said Higu. “Don’t tell Kei. Ama Corrie says it’s not safe. It’s very safe. I’ve drawn it.”

Thea laughed. Higu always made her laugh. It was one of the reasons she’d said yes to bring everyone before she’d finished reading it.

They talked about nothing for a while. Millrace, the wheels, Xola’s gills, Slep’s sheep. Higu laughed at the sheep for a full minute. Then she was quiet.

Thea waited. She knew what the quiet was. She’d known since she picked up the phone.

“She knows you’re in,” said Higu.

Thea didn’t say anything.

“I didn’t tell her. Everybody told her. It was on Xola’s stream.” Higu’s voice was very careful. Higu was always careful, the way you’re careful carrying something full to the brim. “She didn’t say anything. She went very quiet. For a long time. And then she asked if you were all right.”

Thea sat on the edge of the egg in her room in Australia with the light off and the phone against her ear.

She asked if you were all right.

She didn’t know what to do with that. She’d spent a long time in a place where nothing she did was right, where everything was somehow her fault. If she did well it was her fault and if she did badly it was her fault. In all that time she couldn’t remember being asked if she was all right. Not once. Not by her.

“I’m not asking you anything,” said Higu. “I’m not asking her anything either. I won’t. I’m not in the middle. I’m just—“ She stopped. “I love you both. I’m telling you what’s true. That’s all.”

“I know,” said Thea. Her voice didn’t sound like hers.

“No hurry,” said Higu. “On anything. Ever.”

Thea was quiet for a long time. The wind went on behind Higu’s voice, on a roof inside a dome eight days east.

“Is she okay?” said Thea, eventually. It came out very small.

“She’s building a guild,” said Higu. “She’s good at it. She lays hedges. She says everything’s going to fall down, and then she builds it so it doesn’t.” A pause. “She’s okay. She’s better than she was. I think she’s—“ Higu stopped. “I think she’s trying to be better than she was.”

Thea closed her eyes.

“Me too,” she said.

“I know,” said Higu. “That’s why I rang.”

On Saturday she got the map out again, on the step of her room, with the wheels going in the dark.

Not the round town. She didn’t look at the round town. She looked the other way. South and east, along the river road, four days. To a fen, and a causeway, and a long wooden walk on posts out into the reeds, and a town on stilts over black water that she’d watched every night for five months on a screen.

Xola came and sat beside her on the step. She looked at where Thea was looking.

“Fernwick,” said Xola.

“When we’re ten,” said Thea. “Four days. River road.”

Xola looked at her sideways. Her gills did a small flutter.

“We’re going to surprise Icey.”

“We’re going to surprise Ice.”

“Tif says he doesn’t like surprises.”

“He’ll like this one,” said Thea.

She didn’t say why she was sure. She didn’t say a lot of things, about Ice, even to Xola, who knew most of them anyway. That they’d been best friends once, the three of them, a long time ago. That he’d liked Xola, and then he’d liked her, and nothing had ever come of either. That he’d stepped back, gently, the way he did everything, right about when Thea’s life went dark. And that she’d been so far down in her own mess she hadn’t even asked why.

She’d worked it out much later. He hadn’t been able to stand there and watch. So he’d stepped away, to keep himself in one piece, and never said a word about it to anyone. She’d been glad, later, that one of them had been sensible.

He’d been in every chat she’d ever had anyway. Raiding, gifting, saying hi at three in the morning. Never once asking for anything back. She’d found that out too, later.

“He’ll like this one,” said Thea again.

Xola leaned her head on Thea’s shoulder, gills and all, and didn’t say anything. She didn’t have to. She’d been on the phone for all of it.
