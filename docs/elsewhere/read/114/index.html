Live from Elsewhere · Volume 4: The Ninth Heron

Chapter 114

After His Door

1,993 words · 9 min read

The eggs came on Thursday. All six, more or less, in three countries, within about a day of each other. Thea had ordered them on the same afternoon on purpose, because Thea did things like that.

Tif’s came at four. She’d been at her desk with Ice’s stream on the second screen, the way it always was, watching him walk out of a stone door at the top of the world for the second night in a row and sit down in the grass. Then the doorbell went. Two men carried a box into her room and stood it in the corner, by the window. It was very white. When they’d gone she sat on the floor beside it for a long time with her knees up and didn’t open it.

She’d said after his door. She’d meant it.

On Friday she sat at her desk until one in the morning and was in his chat for every minute of it. She’d made a new command at three on Thursday morning, for Suzu, because Suzu was going to have to say a lot of numbers out loud and somebody should keep count for him. !home. She typed it eighty-one times on Friday night. Every time Suzu said a number, she typed it, and Nightbot said it back to forty thousand people. Lights home: 8 of 81. 32 of 81. 61 of 81.

At 81 of 81 she’d had to put her hands over her face for a while.

Then the window. WORLD FIRST. And forty thousand people typing it at once, and Thea and Xola and Xyro typing it too. And a man sitting on the floor of a blue hall with his arm round his friend, not saying anything, until he turned the stream off.

She’d typed one last thing before he did. She hadn’t planned it. It just came out.

that was his door. tomorrow’s mine.

On Saturday afternoon she opened the box.

It took her an hour, because she did it slowly, properly, reading everything. The safety leaflet. The setup card. The little booklet about the fingerprint and the pain slider and the Call. She’d watched five months of a man explaining all of it on stream, so she knew everything in the booklet already. She read it anyway. It seemed polite.

Then she sat at her desk and did the commands.

Not new ones. The old ones, getting them ready to be without her. She’d started on the night Leaka went in, three weeks ago, and she’d been finishing them a bit at a time ever since, like packing for a trip.

!dark, open to anybody, once a minute and no faster. Thirty thousand people could count for him. She’d seen, three weeks ago, that they would.

!stone, the same.

!leaka, which said Leaka has been in the egg for 14 days. She says eat something green. It would go on counting on its own.

!home, which said Lights home: 81 of 81, and always would now. She left it like that. Somebody would type it, every so often, for no reason, to see it.

Then the pinned message. She’d written the first one on the first night, for twenty-one people. She’d rewritten it twice since, as the twenty-one became two thousand and then thirty thousand. She rewrote it now, carefully, at her desk, the way you’d leave a note on a fridge.

hello new people :) rules are pinned. be nice. if he says trust, it’s over. if he goes dark, type !dark. he will not tell you what he found. don’t ask. tif’s in the egg now. she’ll be back. behave.

And then, last, the one she’d written at two in the morning on the night Leaka went in, and saved, and never turned on.

She looked at it for a long time.

!tif

Tif’s in the egg. Behave. She’ll know if you don’t.

She turned it on.

Thea rang at half past seven.

Not just Thea. All of them. Thea had made the call, because Thea made the calls. One by one the little squares came up on Tif’s phone. Thea, from Australia, two hours ahead, in the voice she used when she meant business. Xola, from across Singapore, who’d been crying already and was annoyed about it. PenguChi, from the Philippines, eating something. Slep, calm, from somewhere quiet, who said “hey” in his slow way and then didn’t say anything else, and didn’t need to. And Xyro, from the other side of Singapore, grinning so hard his square was mostly teeth.

“Everyone’s egg is plugged in?” said Thea.

Everyone’s was.

“Everyone’s read the booklet?”

“I’ve watched him explain the booklet for five months,” said Xola. “I could write the booklet.”

“Tif’s read the booklet,” said Xyro proudly. “Twice.”

“Of course Tif’s read the booklet,” said PenguChi, through her food.

“Eight o’clock,” said Thea. “Same minute. Higu says whoever wakes first does the Call. Four each, she says. She says don’t panic if it’s a weird town. She says all the towns are weird.” Thea looked at her square on the call for a moment, at the five of them. Her voice went a bit different. “She says welcome home.”

Nobody said anything for a second.

“Okay,” said Xola, wiping her eyes. “Okay.”

At a quarter to eight, Tif went back to her desk.

His stream was on. Of course it was. He was in a fen, at the bottom of a long road of stone herons, walking south toward home with the other four, in the dusk. He’d been walking all afternoon. He hadn’t said much. He didn’t need to. Forty thousand people were watching him walk anyway, the way they’d watched him stand on a plank all night in December, because that was what they did now. They watched him.

She typed it.

For a moment nothing happened.

Then somebody she’d never seen before, a lurker with no badge, typed TIF. In capitals. On its own. And then somebody else did. And then fifty people did. And then the chat was full of it, three letters going past faster than anyone could read. TIF. TIF. TIF. Forty thousand people, most of whom had never said a word to her in their lives. The same way they’d done it for Leaka three weeks ago, with CGI. Filling the hole before she’d even made it.

Tif sat at her desk and watched it go past.

She’d told Xyro, three weeks ago, on the phone, that Nightbot didn’t mean it.

She’d been right. Nightbot didn’t. But forty thousand people did. She sat there and watched them mean it, and found she couldn’t see the screen very well.

In the fen, on the other screen, Ice had stopped walking.

He was standing on the old causeway between two stone herons, in the dusk, with the others a little way ahead. She watched him glance at the edge of his eye, the way he did when he was reading chat. He stood there for a long moment and read it. Thirty-eight thousand people typing three letters that were her name.

Then he smiled. On stream. Not the small one. The other one, the one he hardly ever did.

“Tif,” said Ice.

That was all, for a moment. He looked out over the fen in the dusk, toward the south, toward home.

“Go on, then,” he said.

She knew where that came from. She’d been there for all of it. A set of steps in a fen, an old woman with a pipe, a boy with a black sword. Go on, boy. And last night, in a blue hall at the top of the world, a boy with an empty jar. Go on.

She put her hand over her mouth.

Then she typed one more thing, the last thing, with her hands not quite steady.

And she turned the screen off.

The egg was in the corner by the window, very white, with its lid up. Outside, it was nearly dark. Singapore at eight o’clock on a Saturday, lit up, going on without her.

She lay down in it. It was more comfortable than she’d expected. On the phone on the little shelf by her head, the call was still open. Five squares. Five faces she’d known for years, in rooms in three countries, all lying down in eggs at the same minute.

“Ready?” said Thea.

“Ready,” said Xyro, at once.

“Ready,” said Xola, and then, very small, “I’m so nervous.”

“Ready,” said PenguChi.

“Mm,” said Slep. Which meant ready. And then, to Xola only, quietly, in his calm voice, “I’m right here.” Which meant something else.

“Tif?” said Thea.

Tif lay in the egg and looked up at the ceiling of her room. Five months. Every minute. A man in a swamp, and a shield, and twenty-one people. A drowned church. A plank. A bell girl, and a ford, and a dark ring, and a round town where seven roads met. A girl on a wheel. A boy with a jar. A door at the top of the world, facing south.

She’d watched all of it. Every minute. On a screen.

“Ready,” said Tif.

She put her thumb on the plate.

[ Welcome to ELSEWHERE.
Choose your name. ]

It was the little room. Just like his. Small and wooden, with rain on the window. She’d seen it on the first night, through his eyes, on a screen, with twenty other people. She’d typed !dark into a chat that didn’t have a !dark yet, because she’d wanted to count something.

She stood in it now.

She could feel the floor. She could hear the rain. She lifted her hand and looked at it. There were her own fingers, and the rain on the window behind them, and it was all real. It was all so much more real than a screen.

Tif.

[ That name is taken. ]

Of course it was. It was always taken. It had been taken on every single thing she’d ever signed up for in her life.

She smiled, and typed the one she always used.

Tif2.

[ Welcome, Tif2. ]

[ Your likeness. ]

There was a mirror. She stepped in front of it.

A girl looked back at her. Her own face, more or less. And above it, two soft black cat’s ears that turned toward the sound of the rain on their own. And eyes. Green. Bright green, the green of the little black cat she’d been drawn as for years, on a screen that only ever showed her from the shoulders up.

The ears moved again. She hadn’t told them to.

Tif stood in front of the mirror in a little wooden room with rain on the window, and laughed out loud, alone. Somewhere a long way off, in a fen at the bottom of a road of stone herons, a man who never said much had just said go on, then. Forty thousand people were typing her name.

She was in.
