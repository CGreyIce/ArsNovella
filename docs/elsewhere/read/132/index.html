Live from Elsewhere · Volume 5: Ask Me Again

Chapter 132

In Between

1,702 words · 7 min read

Higu had told Thea, on a Friday in April, I’m not in the middle.

She’d meant it. She’d meant it every day since. She loved them both, and she’d decided a long time ago that loving them both meant not standing between them. Standing between two people was a way of choosing. You faced one and had your back to the other. She’d watched other people do it, years ago, when it all went dark. She’d watched Xola do it, out of love, with her back to Gigi and her arms round Thea. Higu didn’t blame her. She just wasn’t going to.

So she wasn’t in the middle.

But on Friday morning she had a message on her phone from Gigi that said tell her i’m glad she’s at the front. And she sat on the edge of her bed in Australia, before the café opened, and understood that she wasn’t going to be in the middle. She was going to be the road.

A road wasn’t in the middle of anything. A road just went from one place to another, and carried what people put on it. It didn’t decide. It didn’t face either way. It just went.

She could do that.

She rang Thea at seven in the evening, Thea’s time, which was the same as hers.

Thea picked up on the second ring.

“Hi, Gu.”

“Hi.”

“You’re not on the roof.”

“I’m on my bed,” said Higu. “In real life. Like a normal person.”

“That’s worse,” said Thea. “That means it’s serious.”

Higu laughed. It came out a bit wrong. Thea heard it. Thea always heard things.

“Is it her?” said Thea.

Higu didn’t say anything for a moment. On the other end of the phone she heard Thea go very quiet. Not the ordinary quiet. The other kind. The one Higu had heard a lot of, a year ago, on calls at three in the morning when Thea couldn’t sleep and Xola had finally fallen asleep.

“I’ve got a message,” said Higu. “From her. For you. She said I could give it to you if I talked to you. She said that’s all it is.” She took a breath. “You don’t have to hear it. You can say no, and I’ll never say it, and that’s fine. That’s completely fine.”

Thea didn’t say anything for a long time.

“What is it,” she said.

Higu read it to her. Exactly. Every word, the way it had been written, no more.

“Tell her I’m glad she’s at the front.”

Silence.

“Don’t tell her anything else. Not yet.”

The silence went on for a long time. Long enough that Higu looked at her phone to check the call was still there. It was. Somewhere on the other end of the line, Thea was sitting with that.

“At the front,” said Thea, eventually. Her voice was very small.

“The shield,” said Higu. “She saw it. On Xola’s stream. Everybody saw it.”

“I know.” Thea was quiet again. “I was always behind. With her. I was always— I was always the one behind. Holding it.” Her voice went somewhere. “And it was always wrong. Whatever I held. It was always the wrong way.”

“I know,” said Higu.

“And she’s glad.”

“She said glad,” said Higu. “She didn’t say anything else. She told me not to.”

Thea breathed out. A long, shaky breath, down the phone.

“It’s not sorry,” said Thea.

“No.”

“It’s not anything. It’s not—“ She stopped. “It’s not nothing, either.”

“No,” said Higu. “It’s not nothing.”

They sat with that, on two phones, for a while.

Thea didn’t say anything about it for a long time. They talked about other things. About Millrace, and the river road, and Fernwick, and the sheep called Patience who’d now walked further than most players. About Brackenford, where the six of them were levelling. About Thea’s stream, which had been over two thousand every night since the creator email. About Xola, who was fine. And Slep, who was always fine. And Tif, whose ears had gone pink in front of Kotori four times.

Then, right at the end, when Higu had already said I should go, Thea said, “Gu.”

“Yeah.”

“Can you take one back?”

Higu sat very still on the edge of her bed.

“Yes,” she said. “If you want.”

“Just one,” said Thea. “Like hers. Nothing else.”

“Okay.”

There was a long pause. Higu could hear Thea thinking. Thea thought out loud, usually, in lists, at two in the morning. Not this time. This time she was very quiet, for a long time, putting something together carefully, one piece at a time, the way you’d fit a cog into a wheel.

“In March,” said Thea. “At the founding. When the bird came through the eye. She put thorn across the counter, in front of the book. In front of the old woman. I watched it.” A breath. “Tell her I saw her hold the counter. Tell her it was brave.”

Higu closed her eyes.

“And don’t tell her anything else,” said Thea. “Not yet.”

“Not yet,” said Higu.

“And Gu.” Thea’s voice changed. “Don’t tell Xo. Not yet. She’s—“ She stopped. “She was on the phone for all of it. Every night. For a year. She’s tired. I don’t want her to have to pick it all up again. Let her have a rest. I’ll tell her. When there’s something to tell.”

“Okay,” said Higu.

“Thank you,” said Thea. “For being the road.”

Higu hadn’t told her she’d decided to be the road. She hadn’t told anyone. She sat on her bed with the phone and laughed, a little, and didn’t ask how Thea knew. Thea always knew things. It was one of the reasons Higu loved her.

She took it back that night.

Gigi was on the roof of the dome when Higu came up, with two cups, because Gigi always had two cups. The moon was coming up over the rim of the eye. Gigi didn’t look at her when she sat down. She looked at the moon.

“You talked to her,” said Gigi.

“Yes.”

“Did you—“

“I told her. Exactly what you said. Nothing else.”

Gigi nodded. She held her cup in both hands and didn’t drink it.

“She sent one back,” said Higu.

Gigi went completely still.

“You don’t have to hear it,” said Higu. “Same as her. You can say no.”

Gigi didn’t say no.

Higu told her. Exactly. Every word, the way Thea had said it, no more.

“Tell her I saw her hold the counter. Tell her it was brave.”

Gigi didn’t move.

“Don’t tell her anything else. Not yet.”

Gigi sat on the stone ledge inside the dome with the cup in her hands and the moon coming up through the eye. Then she put the cup down very carefully on the stone beside her, and put both her hands over her face.

Higu didn’t touch her. She didn’t say anything. She sat beside her and looked at the moon, the way you’d sit beside somebody at a milestone, and waited.

After a long time, Gigi said, through her hands, “It’ll fall down.”

“Probably,” said Higu.

“It always falls down.”

“I know.”

Gigi took her hands away from her face. Her eyes were wet. She looked up at the ceiling of the dome, where the moon was starting to touch the silver lines. One at a time, a lake, a hedge, a staircase.

“So I’ll build it like it’s going to,” said Gigi. “Slowly. A bit at a time. Like a hedge.”

“Like a hedge,” said Higu.

“Lie,” said Gigi, very quietly, to nothing. It was the word she used to lay thorn across a gap. Nothing happened, because there was nothing there. She said it anyway.

At two in the morning their time, which was a dawn in the game, a notice went up in every Hall in Elsewhere.

It came the same way the Banner Writ had come in January, the same way the Long Night had come in December. In fresh ink, on every wall, all at once. Higu saw it from the eye of the dome, looking down into the Hall of Roads. Ama Corrie was standing at her counter reading it with her spectacles on their chain. Then she looked up at the eye, at the two of them on the ledge, with an expression Higu couldn’t read.

[ From the first dawn of Monday, the Halls of Ten are open.
Ten travellers. One banner.
A hall for ten answers only to a banner. ]

Higu read it twice. Beside her, Gigi read it too.

“One banner,” said Gigi.

“One banner,” said Higu.

She looked at the gold star at the top of the ceiling, above the north door, off every map. She thought about a company of nine in a fen, with a shield and a scythe and an empty jar. And a company of eight on a riverbank, with a founder who’d said ask me when it’s big in January, in an inn by a bridge.

She thought about Kei, asleep in Australia, who was going to wake up and read this. And go very quiet. And then say hm.

“It’s going to be big,” said Gigi. She said it the way she said everything, as if it were bad news. Higu could hear, under it, that it wasn’t. “Isn’t it.”

“It’s already big,” said Higu. “Three thousand on the steps.”

“Then he’s going to have to ask.”

“Yes,” said Higu.
