Piece Two

1,765 words · 8 min read

Hyvred had known her room by heart for as long as she could remember.

She’d had to. You learned a room by heart when you saw it through a tube. Where everything was, how far, which corner your knee would find before your eyes did. She knew it all the way you’d know a song. She could have walked it with her eyes shut, and often, without meaning to, more or less had.

On Friday afternoon her family brought her home from the eleventh floor. They fussed, the way they’d always fussed, and she let them, more than usual. Then, finally, she went to her room, and opened the door, and stood in it.

The room was there. All of it. Not all at once. The middle was what it had always been, sharp and small and clear. But round it, for the first time, the edges. Blurry, faint, wobbling slightly like the air over a road in the sun. The doctor had said they’d wobble for weeks. But they were there. The things at the sides of the room weren’t things she knew were there because her knee had found them a thousand times. They were things she could see, at the side of her eye, without turning her head.

She stood in the doorway for a long time.

Then she lay down on the bed, on her back, and looked up.

The ceiling.

She’d seen a ceiling like this before. Once. On the twelfth day of a game, in a little wooden room by the sea, lying on a bed. She’d looked up and seen all four corners at once, and her own hand at the edge, and cried, and Bee had said that counts. You win.

She hadn’t thought she’d ever see it out here.

She looked at her own ceiling, in her own room, in Singapore. White. Very plain. She’d never known how plain it was. And the four corners. All four. Faint, blurry, wobbling. There.

She held her hand up. At the edge, the very edge, she could see it. Five fingers, blurred, against the white.

She didn’t cry this time. She’d cried enough today for about a year. She just lay there with her hand up, looking at it at the edge of the world, until her arm got tired.

At nine o’clock she opened his stream.

She hadn’t meant to. She’d meant to sleep. The doctor had said rest, and Bee had said rest, and Kei had sent her a message that said only rest. hm. But at nine o’clock she was lying on her bed with her phone, and her thumb did it without asking her, the way it had done the note in the taxi.

She held the phone at arm’s length. She’d never done that in her life before today.

And there it was. All of it.

She’d watched his stream since November. A man in a swamp, with a shield, with a hundred and forty-three people watching, on her second night. She’d turned on notifications for him that night, the first person she’d ever done that for. She’d watched it every night since, through the tube, the way she watched everything. The middle. His face. His hands. Whatever was right in front of him.

She’d never seen the rest of it.

He was on the Landing in Fernwick, with the others. There were nine of them now, she knew that; Kei had told her. And she could see all of them. Not one at a time, swinging the phone round to find them. All at once, in the picture, at the edges. A tall man on a barrel. A girl in pale blue with a bell. A big man with a hammer. A short woman with her arms folded who must be the healer, Rachael. A boy with a spear running somewhere and coming back. The black water under the platform. The lamps. The reeds going away behind, grey-green, to the edge of the picture.

And on the right, the chat. Going past, faster than she could read. She’d never been able to read it and watch him at the same time; she’d had to choose. Now she could see both. Him in the middle, the chat at the edge.

And him.

All of him. Not just his face. She’d only ever really seen his face, and his hands when they did something. Now he was sitting on a bench at the end of the Landing, and she could see the bench, and his shield leaning on it, and his knees, and—

His feet.

She’d never seen his feet.

He had boots on. Dark leather, worn, with mud on them. And on the laces of the boots, a thin cord, teal, a bright small colour against all the charcoal and brown. She’d never known it was there. In six months of watching, through a tube, she’d never once seen the bottom of the picture.

She lay on her bed and looked at a teal cord on a man’s bootlaces on a phone at arm’s length, and something happened in her chest that she was absolutely not going to think about.

She opened the donation box.

She watched him read it. She could see him read it. That was the thing. She could see his whole face, and his hands, and the bench, and his feet, all at the same time, while he read it.

He went still.

Not the ordinary still. The other one. The one he’d done on the terrace in a city with no name, when she’d walked across the square toward him with her fists at her sides. He sat on the bench on the Landing in the lamplight and looked at the edge of his own eye for a long time, and didn’t say anything at all.

She lay on her bed and didn’t type anything for a while.

Then he said something else. He said it to the chat, the way he said everything on stream, without looking at the orb. But she knew it wasn’t for the chat.

“Go and look at your ceiling, Hyv,” said Ice. “All four corners.”

She stopped breathing.

He’d watched. On her second night. Her first stream, the one that went round the world. Forty-three seconds of a girl in a little room by the sea, crying because she could see a ceiling. He’d watched it. He’d texted her at midnight that night, no flashlight now, and she’d thought it was just him apologising again, for a joke he’d made years ago. She’d never thought he’d remembered. The corners. Six months later.

She turned the stream off.

She lay on her back in the dark, in her room, and looked up at the ceiling she’d already looked at.

It was too dark to see the corners now. She knew they were there. She’d seen them. That was the thing about seeing something: once you’d seen it, you knew it was there even in the dark.

She got her phone out, one last time, and opened the note.

ICE — 3,779.50 + one lesson.

She took thirty-nine fifty off.

ICE — 3,740 + one lesson.

She looked at it. And then, because there was nobody in the room and nobody would ever know, she put her other hand up to her head. To the top of it, where on a terrace in a city with no name a hand had been, between horns she didn’t have out here. Just for a second.

Then she took it away very fast, and said “No,” out loud, to the dark.

And then, because she was furious, and because she could see the edges of her own room for the first time in her life, and because she had absolutely no idea what to do with any of it, she laughed. Out loud, like a door banging, alone in the dark. She laughed until she fell asleep.
