Live from Elsewhere · Volume 5: Ask Me Again

Chapter 140

Share the Weight

2,980 words · 13 min read

Deston had sung in the back row of a school choir with Declan and Gabe when they were thirteen.

None of them had been any good. That was the thing he remembered most. Three boys in the back row, mouthing the high bits, holding on to the low bits for dear life. And somewhere in those years, between the three of them, they’d worked out the only thing about singing Deston had ever really kept. They’d said it to each other ever since, about singing and then about everything else.

Nobody sings the whole song. You don’t have to. You hold your part. You hold it so well that you can hear everybody else’s round it. And then it doesn’t matter who’s loud.

He’d been thinking about that since Thursday. Since the stair, and the seven, and Declan going down on one knee in the middle of the ring at twenty-two per cent, and Hyvred making a sound he’d never heard anyone make.

He’d gone quiet. Declan had noticed. Declan always noticed when he went quiet. He’d said so to Hyvred, on the stair, Deston found out later. He goes quiet when he’s working something out.

He’d been working it out.

On Friday night, a window came up in front of every traveller in the world. Deston was in the banner room with the book in his lap.

He read it. Everyone at the long table read it. Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Calvin said “Aw,” loudly, from the far end, and Rachael said “Cal,” and Calvin said “I’m allowed to say aw.”

“Good for them,” said Declan, from his chair by the wall.

Everybody looked at him.

“They’ve been building for it since January,” said Declan. “Eighteen rooms. Three tens. Two wiped and they went back in.” He shrugged slightly. “That’s a lot of going back in. Good for them.”

Kei, at the head of the table, nodded slowly.

“So it’s not a race,” said Kei.

“It was never a race,” said Suzu, quietly, from beside Declan. The empty jar was on the table in front of him. “Not if it’s ours.”

Deston looked at Suzu. Then at Declan. Then down at the book in his lap.

He’d taken the book on the first day of the game, in a white room, because it was the thing on the rack that nobody else would. A one-line skill, from a toothless old man in a chapel nobody had opened in thirty years. Accord. Let the many move as one. Then, in a drowned church, a page. The Fourth Verse. Then, in January, the thing that went with it. He’d never really understood that one until Gabe stepped in for Kotori with a hammer and Deston felt the weight of it come apart across the six of them. Share the Weight. When one of them was hit, he could spread it. A little. Across the ones he’d linked.

He’d only ever linked five. Six, with Gabe. He’d never tried ten.

“I’ve got it,” said Deston.

The banner room went quiet.

He explained it standing up. He wasn’t good at standing up and explaining. Kei was good at it. Declan was, when he bothered. Deston was better at the middle of things than the front of them. But they were all looking at him, so he stood up.

“There’s seven of it,” said Deston. “One’s real. Hyv can’t see which, nobody can. There’s no tell.” He looked at Hyvred. She was at the long table with her chin on her hand, glaring at the wall. She’d been glaring at the wall since Thursday. “That’s not your fault. There’s nothing to see. That’s the point of it.”

Hyvred glared at him instead. It was an improvement.

“So we don’t try to see which,” said Deston. “Declan said that. On the stair. You stand so it doesn’t matter which.”

Declan, by the wall, didn’t move. But Deston saw him listen with his whole back, the way he did.

“Accord,” said Deston. He held up the book. “Let the many move as one. I’ve done it with six. I can do it with ten. I think. If I count it right.” He looked round the room. “If I link all ten of us, and the real one hits somebody, Share the Weight goes across all ten. A tenth each. Nobody goes to twenty-two. Nobody goes down. It just hits all of us a bit.”

“That’s still getting hit,” said Rachael.

“It’s getting hit a tenth,” said Deston. “You can heal a tenth. Kotori’s Vigil heals a tenth every eighth beat. That’s what Vigil’s for.”

Kotori, at the table, went very still. Then she nodded, slowly.

“And then,” said Deston, and stopped.

He looked at Declan. And at Suzu, beside him. The only two in the ten who were on fifty.

“When it hits somebody,” said Deston, “and it goes across all ten. Everybody feels a tenth of it. At twenty, a tenth is nothing. You’d barely notice.” He paused. “At fifty you’d notice.”

Declan looked up.

“You’d feel where,” said Deston. “Not that you got hit. Where it came from. Which side. Which way. You’d feel it come in, a tenth of it, from the direction the real one swung. Because it’s real. The other six don’t hit anybody. Only the real one does.” He swallowed. “Pain tells you where you got hit. You said that. On the first night. To twenty-one people.”

Nobody said anything.

“Even when it’s somebody else,” said Declan slowly.

“Especially then,” said Deston.

Declan looked at him across the banner room for a long moment. Thirteen years. A back row in a school hall. Two boys mouthing the high bits.

Then he smiled. The real one. The one he hardly ever did.

“And then I call it,” said Declan.

“And then you call it. To her.” Deston looked at Hyvred. “You can’t see which one out of seven. Nobody could. But if somebody tells you that way, that one—“

Hyvred had taken her chin off her hand.

“Then I’m only looking at one,” she said slowly. “Not seven. One.” Her crosshair eye was doing something. Deston could see it from across the table, swinging, locking, letting go. “I could see one. I could see anything if it was only one. I could see dust come up under its foot.”

“He feels where,” said Deston. “You see what. Together.” He sat down, because his legs had decided to. “That’s all. That’s the whole idea. Nobody sings the whole song.”

Gabe, at the table, laughed. Out loud, suddenly, the big laugh. He looked at Deston, and then at Declan, and Deston knew he’d recognised it.

“You hold your part,” said Gabe.

“So well you can hear everybody else’s round it,” said Declan.

“And then it doesn’t matter who’s loud,” said Deston.

Nobody else at the table had any idea what they were talking about. Kei looked from one to the other and wrote something on his map. Hyvred looked at Declan with an expression Deston couldn’t read. Calvin said what, very loudly, and Rachael said Cal.

They went down on Saturday at the second dawn.

Bee hung the banner on the hooks. The seven lines in the iron lit, silver-white, and the hatch sank into the floor and became a stair. The ten went down. The whole guild crowded into the space under the stair to the eye above them, and the Hall beyond that, and the steps outside. Three thousand people in the round market, who’d heard by now, all very quiet.

At the bottom, in the round room with the seven tunnels and the ring of stones, Deston opened the book.

[ Accord · Let the many move as one. ]

He’d done it with six. He reached for ten.

He’d thought it would feel like carrying something heavy. It didn’t. It felt like the back row of a choir, the moment before the conductor’s hand came down. Ten people holding their breath. He could feel all of them. Declan at the front, steady as stone. Gabe, warm, two paces from Rachael. Rachael, sharp, watching everyone’s health. Kotori, small and bright, her bell in her hand. Suzu, very still. Sheng Yang, coiled. Xav, quick and quiet. Drew, loose and easy. And Hyvred, in the middle of the ring of stones, wound so tight he could feel her thinking.

Ten. Linked. Moving as one.

[ Accord · ten ]

[ Share the Weight · ten ]

“One,” counted Deston. “Two. Three. Four.”

The first waves came out of the six tunnels, the way they had on Thursday. Hyvred called them. The front moved. It went the way Thursday had gone, well, and steady, and it wasn’t the hard part.

Then the north-west tunnel. Hyvred saw it first. She always saw it first.

“North-west. Standing. At the end.”

“Here it comes,” said Declan, quietly.

It came out of all seven at once.

Seven tall still shapes, seven dark lanterns, coming down seven tunnels toward the ring of stones from seven directions, exactly the same. Deston felt the ten go tight through Accord, all at once, like a choir taking the breath before the hardest bar.

“Hold your part,” said Deston. “Don’t look for it. Hold your part. Five. Six. Seven.”

On the eighth beat Kotori rang. Vigil went through all ten of them, warm.

They came into the ring.

Declan took one on the shield. It came apart. Not that one. Gabe took one. Not that one either. Suzu went wide with the scythe, and Sheng Yang, and Xav. Not that one, not that one, not that one.

And somewhere, from somewhere, the real one swung.

It hit Kotori.

Deston felt it land. Through Accord, all of it, the whole weight of a lantern the size of a bell coming down on a small girl in a pale blue robe from behind. And then he felt Share the Weight take it and break it into ten. A tenth to Kotori, a tenth to Gabe, a tenth to him, a tenth to everyone. The way you’d break a loaf to feed a table.

[ Kotori · 90% ]

[ Party · −1 tenth each ]

Ninety. Not twenty-two. Ninety. Kotori stood in the ring with her bell still in her hand, staggered, and didn’t go down.

And at the front of the ring, with his back to her, Declan said, before anyone else could say anything, quite calmly:

“South-east. Low. Behind Kotori. Left shoulder.”

He hadn’t turned round. He’d felt it. A tenth of a blow, at fifty, through somebody else’s shoulder, coming in from the south-east. He knew exactly where.

And Hyvred, in the middle of the ring, stopped looking at seven, and looked at one.

Deston felt her do it through Accord. Felt her whole attention go from seven tunnels to one patch of floor, south-east, behind Kotori, low. Narrow, and sharp, and certain, like a crosshair locking.

“THERE,” said Hyvred. “That one. The dust. It lifts. THAT ONE.”

Her arrow was in it before she’d finished saying it.

After that it was the song.

Deston would never be able to explain it better than that, afterwards. He tried, on calls, for weeks. It was the song. The thing the teacher had said in a school hall when they were thirteen. Nobody sang the whole of it. Everybody held their part.

Undertow, said Declan. The pull went out from the shield into the real one and dragged it off its six, out of the crowd of shapes, into the middle of the ring. And the other six stopped, and stood, and held up their dark lanterns, and watched.

The real one turned on Hyvred. Of course it did. She was the one who’d seen it. It swung its lantern at the girl in the middle of the ring with the bow.

And Gabe was there.

Two paces. Deston hadn’t seen him move. Nobody ever saw Gabe move. He was just there, between the lantern and Hyvred, with the shield up.

“Stand In,” said Gabe.

The lantern came down on the shield and Share the Weight broke it into ten. Gabe went back half a step and grunted and didn’t go back any further. The hammer in his other hand went white-hot.

Rachael, at two paces from Gabe, put a Mend on him before it landed. Of course she did.

Drew slipped the next swing, slip and answer, and was inside its reach before it knew he was there, iron knuckles up under its arm. Xav went in on its other side, two notes, quick. Suzu took it across the legs with the long wide stroke of the scythe. The wide row.

And Sheng Yang, who’d come in alone in the blue hall in April and gone to six per cent for it, came in at two paces from Drew this time. He brought the long sword round on the back of its knee, where he’d cracked the Last Watcher’s, and put everything into it.

[ Ender’s Cut · 99% ]

The crack went all the way up.

It didn’t close. There was no dark down here to close it. There was a ring of ten people, holding their parts, and a choir of lanterns watching, and Kotori ringing on the eighth beat, and Deston counting under all of it.

“Six. Seven. Eight.”

Declan went in last. Not with the shield. With the sword. The Falling Cut, the first thing a man called Hollis had taught him in a yard in a fen in November. He brought it down on the cracked stone where Sheng Yang had opened it.

The Warden of Seven Ways stood very still in the middle of the ring of stones, with its lantern raised.

Then it knelt.

The way the Waking Heron had knelt on its cairn, and the Last Watcher in its blue hall. It knelt in the ring of seven stones, at the crossroads under the round town. It lowered its dark lantern to the floor and set it down on the flat stone in the middle, on the star cut into it. All round the ring, at the mouths of the seven tunnels, the other six did the same. Seven dark lanterns, set down at once, on the stone.

And the seven stones round the ring began to glow. Silver-white. Like the lines on the banner. Like the lines on the ceiling by moonlight. Seven lines of light went out from the ring, down the seven tunnels, under the seven roads, away into the dark in seven directions. As far as Deston could see. Further.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

They stood in the ring of glowing stones, ten of them, breathing hard. Deston could still feel them all through Accord, every one, the way you could still feel a chord in the air after the last note had stopped. He didn’t let go. He didn’t want to, yet.

Then, above them, up the stair, through the hatch, through the space under the stair to the eye, through the whole Hall and out of the south door, the noise came.

Three thousand people. And forty more. They’d heard. The dome carried.

Declan sat down on the edge of the flat stone in the middle of the ring with his sword across his knees. Deston sat down beside him. Then Gabe, heavily, on the other side, with the hammer still smoking.

The three of them, in a row. The back row.

“Thirteen years,” said Gabe.

“Thirteen,” said Deston.

“Nobody sings the whole song,” said Declan, very quietly, to the floor.

Deston let go of Accord, finally. All ten of them came apart into ten again, gently, the way a choir does when the last note’s gone. He felt them go. Each one of them, separately, back into themselves.

Except one.

At the very end, the last thing, as the link went, he felt Hyvred. She was standing in the middle of the ring with her bow, looking at Declan sitting on the stone. And for one half-second, through Accord, before it went, Deston felt exactly what she was feeling.

He didn’t say anything. He never would. It wasn’t his.

But he looked at Hyvred, once, across the ring. She looked back at him, and went pink to the roots of her horns, and knew that he knew. She turned round very fast and walked straight up the stair without a word.

Beside him, Declan watched her go. He didn’t say anything either.

Deston opened the book in his lap, and looked at the one line written in it, and closed it again. He decided, sitting on a stone at the bottom of the world with his two oldest friends, that some things you held as your part and didn’t sing out loud.
