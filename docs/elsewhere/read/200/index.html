Live from Elsewhere · Volume 8: Somewhere Else

Chapter 200

What She Saw

3,914 words · 17 min read

Aurelie said come with me on Wednesday morning, and Harlan went, because when the guild master of ten thousand people said come with me, you went.

“Where?” he said, on the way down the castle stairs.

“Wherever he goes.”

Harlan stopped on the stair.

“You’re going to follow the GM?”

“I’m going to watch him,” said Aurelie. She didn’t stop. “For a week. Vane told him. He knows.” She went on down the stairs, quick and quiet, in plain blue with no gold on her at all. The guild master of Meridian wore less gold than anybody in it. She said gold was for people who needed to be found. “You’re coming because you know him. You’ll tell me what I’m looking at.”

“I’ll tell you what you’re looking at,” said Harlan. “You’re looking at the GM.”

“I’ve heard about the GM for six years,” said Aurelie. “From Vane. Every time somebody said he’d quit.” She reached the bottom of the stairs and looked back up at him. “I’d like to see what the GM is when nobody’s asked him to be anything.”

He was at the fish man’s cart at the first dawn, eating a fish.

They watched him from a harbour wall a hundred paces off, Aurelie sitting, Harlan standing because he couldn’t sit still. He was standing by the cart in the morning light with his shield on his back and the stick in his hand. Looking at the lake. Not talking. The fish man was talking at him, waving a stick, telling him something long. The GM was nodding.

“He’s listening,” said Aurelie.

“He always listens,” said Harlan. “He listened to everything. Even in the war. He’d listen to you for twenty minutes and then say one word and it was the right word.”

Aurelie didn’t say anything. She watched.

The GM finished his fish. He paid for it. He said something to the fish man that made the old man laugh, a big cracked laugh that came across the harbour. And then he turned round and looked straight at the harbour wall a hundred paces off. At Aurelie. At Harlan behind her.

He nodded. Once.

Then he went up the north-west road toward the caves.

“He saw us,” said Harlan.

“I know,” said Aurelie. “He saw us from the bottom of the road. Before he got to the cart.” She stood up. “Vane said he’d notice.”

“Vane said he’d notice. Vane didn’t say he’d nod.”

“No,” said Aurelie, and something happened very briefly at the corner of her mouth. “Vane didn’t say that.”

On the first day he went into the Undercliffs and killed things for eleven hours.

The Undercliffs were the caves under the west terrace, where the crater wall went down into the lake. Dark and wet and full of things that came up out of the water at Lv 50 and 51 and 52. Meridian ran parties of ten into them every day. Sable Court’s outriders had been in there since they came down from Dawnmere. Everybody went in in tens.

He went in alone.

Aurelie and Harlan followed him at a distance, which was easy. He didn’t hurry and he didn’t hide. Harlan had watched him do this before. A long time ago, in a little game on a phone, where it had been a word and a number. Here it was a man with a shield in a wet cave, going first.

He went first at everything. Harlan had told Aurelie that. Everybody had seen the duel. But it was different watching it in a cave, against things that weren’t people. Against things that had no idea who he was and had never seen the clip.

A thing like an eel the size of a horse came up out of a black pool on his left. He’d already turned. Three things like crabs with too many legs came off the ceiling. He’d already stepped. A great slow thing came out of the dark at the end of a tunnel, the kind you’d take in tens. He walked toward it at a walk, and it came at him, and he wasn’t there. And then he was behind it, and then it was down.

He didn’t stop. He didn’t look back. He didn’t seem to be enjoying himself, particularly, or not enjoying himself. He just went first, again and again, for eleven hours, in a wet cave, alone.

At the end of it, Aurelie sat on a rock at the cave mouth for a long time and didn’t say anything.

“Well?” said Harlan.

“He’s very good,” said Aurelie.

“I told you he was very good.”

“You told me he was the best PvP player you’d ever seen,” said Aurelie. “That’s not what I saw.” She was looking into the cave, where he’d gone back in for one more. “I saw a man who’s done this every day for a year. On his own. Because he likes it. Not because anybody’s watching.” She paused. “He doesn’t need it to be a fight. He just needs it to be quiet.”

Harlan didn’t say anything. He didn’t know what to say. He’d never thought about it like that.

On the second day he went to the Rim Hall.

Masmark didn’t have one Hall. It had a Hall of Halls: a great round building on the north terrace, older than anything else in the city. Inside, under a dome bigger than Meridian’s whole castle, there were forty counters. Forty clerks, forty ledgers, forty queues. It was where you went to register a guild or rent a Hall or find out which of the city’s three hundred Halls was free. Meridian had six. Sable Court had four. The dark red and black lot had eleven, and one of them was the size of a hill.

He queued.

Harlan couldn’t believe it. They stood at the back of the Rim Hall by a pillar and watched him queue, in the longest queue, at the oldest counter, behind about forty people. With his shield on his back and his hands at his sides. For an hour.

“He could just go to the front,” said Harlan. “Everyone knows who he is. They’d let him.”

“He doesn’t want to go to the front,” said Aurelie.

“He always goes to the front!”

“In a fight,” said Aurelie. “Not in a queue.” She was watching. “Watch.”

At the front of the queue, at last, he stood in front of the oldest clerk in the Rim Hall. A woman with spectacles on a chain and a pen behind each ear. Harlan, who’d been to the round town, thought she looked a bit like Ama Corrie, if Ama Corrie had been shut in a ledger for fifty years. He said something. The clerk looked at him over her spectacles. She opened a ledger, a very old one, and ran her finger down it. Then she said something back.

He nodded.

She wrote something. He paid. She gave him a key.

That was all. He took the key and went out of the Rim Hall by the north door, and they followed him. Down the north terrace, past the fish man’s harbour, to the very edge of the city on the north-west. The oldest quarter, where the terraces were narrow and steep and nobody had painted anything in a long time. To a little stone building at the end of a lane, on the water, with a sagging roof and a door that stuck.

A Hall for ten. The smallest one in Masmark, the clerk told Aurelie afterward, when she went and asked. The cheapest. Nobody had rented it in a year. It had been a netloft once, where the old fishermen mended their nets. It smelled of salt and old rope.

He opened it. He went in. Through the door, which stuck, Harlan saw him stand in the middle of the bare stone room with the key in his hand and look round it. At the two old iron hooks on the wall at the end, for a banner. At the window onto the lake.

Then he found a broom in the corner, an old one. And swept it.

Under the one old bench. Under the hooks. In the corners. Slowly. Harlan couldn’t see from the lane, but he was nearly sure: under, not round.

“Who’s it for?” said Harlan.

Aurelie didn’t answer for a long time.

“Not him,” she said at last. “He’s got a room over a boatbuilder’s.” She was watching through the door. “It’s for when they come. The ten. And the rest.” She paused. “He’s keeping a room.”

“He always did,” said Harlan.

Aurelie turned and looked at him.

“In the war,” said Harlan. He didn’t know why he was telling her. “In the twenty. When one of us got knocked out for the day, he’d keep our place. Whatever we’d been holding, he’d hold it till we were back. So we’d know where to come back to.” He looked at the netloft, the GM inside with an old broom. “I never thought about it till now. He kept a place for every one of us.”

Aurelie looked at him for a long moment. Then back at the netloft.

“Hm,” she said.

On the third day, three guilds tried to buy him.

Harlan had known they would. Everybody in Masmark had seen the duel, and the seven, and the clip of the gate, which had been watched by more people than anything else in the game that month. And now the man himself was in the city, alone, with no guild at his back. Everybody knew he was Constellation. Nobody in Masmark had ever heard of Constellation.

The first was a silver guild from the south, polite, in a café on the north terrace. They offered him a seat at their top table and a share of their Halls. He said no, thank you and finished his tea.

The second was a company of eight from the far east, elite, very fast, who found him in the Undercliffs. They offered him a place in their eight for the race everybody knew was coming. He said no, thank you, I’ve got one, and went on.

The third was the dark red and black.

They came for him on the north-west harbour in the evening, in front of the fish man’s cart. Twenty of them, in step, under their banner, which was the biggest Harlan had seen in Masmark after Meridian’s. Dark red with a black hand on it, open, fingers spread. The Open Hand. Eleven thousand members, the biggest guild in the world since a week ago, when they’d passed Meridian by about four hundred. Their leader was a big man with a bigger voice. He stood in front of the GM on the harbour and offered him, in front of everybody, a thing Harlan had never heard anybody offered in any game.

“Name your price,” said the leader of the Open Hand.

The harbour went quiet. The fish man stopped turning his sticks.

The GM looked at him.

“No, thank you,” he said.

“You haven’t heard it.”

“I don’t need to.”

“Every man has a price,” said the leader of the Open Hand. He said it loudly, for the crowd. “Gold. Halls. Your own company under our banner, any twenty you choose. A seat at my table. Name it.”

The GM thought about it. Harlan saw him actually think about it, the way he thought about everything, with his face doing nothing.

“I’ve got a seat,” he said. “At a table.” He paused. “Sixty chairs. The first of nine. Somebody sweeps under it on Sundays.”

The leader of the Open Hand stared at him.

“Sixty,” he said. “You’d rather sixty than eleven thousand?”

“I’d rather mine,” said the GM.

And he turned round and bought a fish from the fish man and ate it, with his back to the Open Hand. After a while, the Open Hand went away.

The fish man gave him the next one free.

“He didn’t say Constellation,” said Aurelie, that night, on the castle wall.

Harlan looked at her.

“In front of the Open Hand. In front of the whole harbour. He could have said Constellation. He could have said I’m Constellation, the world first of the second rank, the road of lights, the longest night. Everyone would have heard. Half of Masmark would have known their name by morning.” She was looking out over the lake, at the lights going round. “He said sixty chairs. And somebody sweeps under it on Sundays.”

“That’s the GM,” said Harlan.

“Is it?”

“He never said MotU either,” said Harlan. “Not once. In two years. Other guilds would shout their names. He just put up the notice and started. And we’d come, and he’d fight, and afterwards he’d log off and go to bed.” He laughed. “He named us something stupid and then never said it out loud.”

Aurelie didn’t say anything for a long time.

“Vane said that,” she said at last. “Six years ago. The first time I asked. He named it something stupid and then never said it.” She turned her head. “I thought Vane was exaggerating.”

“Vane doesn’t exaggerate.”

“No,” said Aurelie. “I’m beginning to understand that.”

On the fifth day, in the Undercliffs, a party died.

Not all of them. Four of a five. Harlan and Aurelie were a long way back in the dark, following, when they heard it. A party from somewhere in the south, five of them in green, at fifty, who’d gone too deep and met something they shouldn’t have. A thing like a lake standing up, small, but enough. Four windows going grey, one after another, in the dark ahead.

Locked out. Twenty-four hours.

The fifth was a boy with a spear, backed into a corner of a wet cave with the thing coming at him. His last friend’s window going grey beside him.

The GM was there.

He hadn’t been there a second before. He’d been forty paces off, in another tunnel, killing crabs. And then he was in front of the boy with the spear, shield up, between the boy and the thing. Harlan didn’t see him move. Nobody ever saw him move.

The thing hit the shield.

The GM didn’t go back. Harlan watched him not go back. He watched him take it, square, the whole weight, and not move. And the thing hit him again, and he took that. And again. And Harlan saw his window from forty paces off, because the GM had let it show, the way he did.

He didn’t fight it. That was the thing Harlan would remember. He could have. Harlan had seen him take a Brann off his feet with the Undertow, and a lake. He didn’t. He just stood in front of the boy with the spear and held. And without turning round, he said something.

The boy didn’t move.

He said it again. Louder. Harlan heard it this time, across the cave.

“Go.”

The boy went. Back up the tunnel, toward the light, toward the cave mouth, past where Harlan and Aurelie were standing in the dark. Running, crying, with his spear. He didn’t see them.

The GM backed up the tunnel after him. Slowly. One step at a time, shield up, with the thing coming at him and him taking every blow. Not answering. Walking the boy home, backwards, a hundred paces up a tunnel to the cave mouth.

At the cave mouth, in the light, the thing stopped. It didn’t like the light. It went back down into the dark.

The GM stood in the cave mouth at thirty-one per cent and drank something out of a little bottle. Bitter, by the face he didn’t make.

The boy was sitting on a rock outside, crying.

The GM looked at him. Then he sat down on the next rock, a few paces off, the way you’d sit beside somebody you didn’t know. And he didn’t say anything. He just sat there, while the boy cried, until the boy stopped.

“They’ll be back tomorrow,” said the GM. “Your four. Twenty-four hours.”

“I know,” said the boy. “I know. I just—“ He wiped his face. “I was the only one left.”

“I know,” said the GM. “That’s the worst bit.”

The boy looked at him.

“You’re him,” he said. “Aren’t you. From the duel. From the gate.”

“Yes.”

“Why did you—“ The boy stopped. “You don’t know us.”

The GM thought about it.

“You were on your own,” he said.

That was all. He got up. He went back into the Undercliffs, alone, to the crabs. The boy sat on the rock outside in the light and watched him go for a long time.

Aurelie, in the dark beside Harlan, hadn’t moved the whole time.

On the sixth day, Harlan saw him read a letter.

He was on the harbour wall by the netloft at the second dusk, sitting with his back to the lake, with a letter open in the corner of his window. Harlan was two harbours away with Aurelie, eating a fish. He couldn’t read it from there, of course. But he could see the GM’s face.

It went pink.

Harlan dropped his fish.

He stared. He’d known the GM for two years in a war, and a week in a round town, and a week in Masmark. He’d seen him win and lose and log off and go to bed. He’d seen him on a top step being dapped up, with his ears going red. He’d never once, not ever, seen his face do that.

“Who,” said Harlan, “is that.”

Aurelie had seen it too. She was looking at the GM on the harbour wall, two harbours off, reading a letter with his face gone pink.

“I don’t know,” she said.

“I’ve never— in two years—“

“I know,” said Aurelie. “Vane told me. He never did.”

She watched for a while. The GM read the letter twice. Then he wrote back, slowly, sitting on the wall. Then he took an arrow out of the strap of his pack, an arrow with one white feather, and held it up flat on his palm over the harbour, the way you’d hold up a compass. He looked at it for a long time. Then he put it back.

“Good,” said Aurelie, very quietly.

Harlan looked at her.

“Six years,” said Aurelie. “Vane telling me about a man who held everybody’s spot and named his guild something stupid and never said it out loud. Who went first at everything and logged off and left the numbers.” She was still watching. “Vane never once said he had anybody.”

“He didn’t,” said Harlan. “Not in the war.”

“He has now,” said Aurelie. “Good.”

On the seventh day she didn’t follow him.

She stood on the castle wall in the morning with Harlan and Vane and looked out over the lake. The GM was somewhere down there, at the fish man’s cart, or the netloft, or the Undercliffs. She didn’t go and look.

“Well?” said Vane.

He said it the way Vane said things, very quietly. As if he already knew the answer and was only asking so it would be said.

“I’ve seen enough,” said Aurelie.

“And?”

Aurelie was quiet for a long time.

“You told me the truth,” she said. “For six years. All of it. I thought half of it was you being fond of him. It wasn’t.” She looked out over the water. “He’s the best I’ve ever seen. And he doesn’t want any of it. The Open Hand offered him anything. He said sixty chairs.” She paused. “And he kept a room.”

Vane didn’t say anything.

“I’m going to ask him something,” said Aurelie.

“What?”

“Why he isn’t running it.” She turned from the wall. “Not now. When his guild comes. When the ten walk down the north-west road with that banner. I want to see him with them first.” She paused at the top of the stair. “I want to see where he stands.”

She went down.

Harlan and Vane stood on the castle wall together. Two of the twenty. Looking out over Masmark, at the lake and the lights, and the little netloft on the north-west harbour with its sagging roof. Somebody had swept it.

“She likes him,” said Harlan.

“She does,” said Vane.

“She doesn’t like anybody.”

“No,” said Vane. “That’s how you know.” He looked at the netloft for a long time. “He kept a place for every one of us. Did you know that? While we were out.”

“I told her,” said Harlan. “On Thursday. I only just worked it out.”

“I worked it out the first week,” said Vane. “I just never said.”

Harlan looked at him. At Vane, who didn’t ask things, and didn’t say things, and had done the numbers for ten thousand people for six years. And who’d said to all of them, every time, the GM doesn’t quit. He goes somewhere else.

“You missed him,” said Harlan.

Vane didn’t answer for a long time.

“I liked the twenty better,” he said.
