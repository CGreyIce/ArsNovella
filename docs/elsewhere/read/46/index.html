He went back in on Tuesday evening for one reason.

He’d slept most of Tuesday. He’d slept on the floor of the back room at first, between the eggs, with Suzu’s pillow under his head because Suzu had put it there. Then in his own bed at the flat, in the afternoon, with the curtains shut and the fan going round. At six he’d woken up with the shape of Steadfast still in his left arm, and lain there looking at the ceiling. Then he’d got up and driven to Melaka Raya and lain down in the egg for an hour. Not to play. Just to do one thing.

The windows were waiting for him when he came out of the heron-stones. The XP from the Deep, held over until he came back. He read them standing in the warm water, in the fen’s evening, with the moonbells still shut and a heron watching him from the reeds. Then he closed them and walked into town.

Fernwick looked like a town that had been up all night. Lanterns still hanging on every post, burnt out, their glass black. The boardwalks were scuffed and wet. Two of the outer walks were half under water where the Unlit had come over them. The pale new plank at the narrows had a long crack down it, and one of the four tall posts beside it was split. People were sitting on steps, NPCs and travellers both, not doing anything. They looked at him as he went past, and nobody said anything, and that was right.

Hollis was on the bridge.

He was sitting on the parapet in his old leather coat with the militia’s spear across his knees. He was looking out at the fields going blue in the dusk, the way he’d been looking at them at sunset on Monday. Ice walked out onto the bridge and stopped beside him.

Hollis didn’t look round.

Ice didn’t say anything. He stood there for a moment. Then, when Hollis did look round, he nodded. Once.

Hollis looked at him for a long time. At his face, and at the shield on his arm, at the old iron boss in the middle of it with its long bright scar.

Then he nodded back.

That was all. Ice turned round and walked back along the bridge, and down the Landing, and out along the north walk to the stones, and logged out. He’d been in the game for fifty-one minutes. Chat had nine thousand people in it and none of them said a word the whole time, because Tif had told them not to.

On Wednesday at noon, there were four hundred people at the old ford.

He’d known there would be a lot. Tif’s counter had stopped at one thousand two hundred and four on Sunday night, when she said she couldn’t watch any more. But knowing it was different from walking down the bank through the alders at a quarter to twelve and seeing them.

They were everywhere. On both banks of the river below Brackenford, sitting on the grass, on the rocks, in the trees. On the old stone bridge upstream, three deep along the parapet. A few had brought food. A lot had swords. Some had their little floating lights up, streaming it themselves. MOONLIGHT HARVEST SWORD TRIALS LIVE, he saw on one, in the corner of his eye. The ones with swords were mostly down at the water’s edge, practising. Falling Cut, badly, at nothing. Back feet everywhere.

In the middle of it all, the river ran brown and quick over the gravel, the way it always had. Three flat stepping-stones across the middle. The deep pool below them. Kotori’s rock, downstream.

“Hi,” said Kotori, from her rock, and waved, and the bell rang.

Salty had the stream up on his phone, wherever he was. So had everybody else, it felt like. Twenty thousand people, to watch him stand on a stone.

Xav had a table.

He’d borrowed it from somewhere: a small wooden folding table, set up on the near bank at the top of the shingle, with his notebook on it and a pen. He was sitting behind it like the clerk at the Adventurers’ Hall, with the river in front of him. He looked like a man who’d slept four hours since Monday, which he had.

“Rules,” he said, when Ice got there. Not to Ice. To everyone. He stood up, and the whole of the near bank went quiet, and he said it in the plan voice, calmly, the way he’d said everything for five weeks.

“One at a time. You walk out to the second stone. Ice will be on it already. You stand next to him. Whatever comes up out of the pool comes up, and you fight it, with him. Not instead of him. With him.” He paused. “If you knock him off the stone, you’re done. If he has to move for you, you’re done. If you get knocked off, you’re done. If you’re unkind to anyone, including the thing in the pool, you’re done.” He sat down. “That’s it. Kotori’s on the rock. Deston’s counting. Suzu’s watching. I’m writing it down.”

There was a short silence on the bank.

“That’s it?” said someone.

“That’s it,” said Xav. “Go on. Second stone. Whoever’s first.”

Ice walked out to the second stone.

He hadn’t stood on it since the ford rematch, three weeks ago. Since the night before that, really, when it had been under water in the dark and his back foot had gone out from under him and the river had taken him. It was under water now, a little, in the noon light. Brown water ran over the top of it, an inch deep, quick. He put his back foot on it and stood.

It felt different. He felt different. Not just the level, though it was that too: twenty, now, and two levels he hadn’t had on Monday. Something else. Something under the surface of him, when he stood still on a wet stone in moving water with a shield on his arm. He felt it the way you feel your own pulse when you’re very quiet.

Still water on top. A current underneath.

Behind him, on the bank, Deston started to hum. Finding the note.

The first one came out.

They came out all afternoon, and almost none of them could do it.

That surprised the bank. It didn’t surprise Xav, and it didn’t really surprise Ice. He’d known it was harder than it looked. He’d fallen off this stone himself.

It wasn’t that they were bad. Most of them were good. They were level fifteen or sixteen or seventeen, with good weapons and good grades and good forms that they’d learned off his stream. A lot of them did the back foot. They walked out across the first stone and onto the second, and stood beside him in the quick brown water, and the river sent something up out of the pool. Mudwolves, mostly, sleek and brown and snapping. Once an hour, on the hour, the Ford Warden itself, grey and huge, with river stones grown into its back.

And almost all of them got in the way.

They didn’t mean to. They were brave, and they wanted it. That was the trouble. They wanted it so much they went forward. Toward the thing in the pool, past the line of his shield, into the space where he was going to turn the blow. He’d have to shift his feet to keep from hitting them. He’d feel the stone go slick under his boot. If he has to move for you, you’re done. Xav would make a small mark in the notebook and say, quite kindly, “Thanks. That’s it. Next.”

Or they hung back. They were so careful of him that they never hit anything, and stood on the stone with their swords up like people waiting for a bus.

Or they slipped.

The second stone was under water. It was always under water. If you’d never stood on a wet stone in a river with your weight forward, it put you in the river. A big cheerful man from Johannesburg with a two-hander went in at the first mudwolf, laughing. A very serious girl from Kraków with a rapier went in on the Warden, and didn’t laugh, and came out and shook Xav’s hand anyway. The man with the halberd went in without anything coming out of the pool at all. He just stepped onto the stone and kept going, in one long smooth motion, all the way under.

“Sorry,” said Kotori, to every one of them, from her rock. She healed them as they came out of the water, before they’d finished coughing. “Sorry. The stone’s very slippery. It’s not you. It’s the stone.”

At half past one, a woman came out who was very good.

She was level seventeen, with a longsword, and she walked out across the first stone onto the second like she’d been doing it all her life. She stood exactly where she should: half a pace behind his shield and to the open side, out of his way, where she could reach anything that came round him. A mudwolf came up out of the pool and went for his shield. She waited until it hit, and then cut it once, on the beat, on Deston’s three, very clean. It came apart.

She smiled at him. It was a nice smile.

The Warden came up on the hour. She did it again. Waited. Cut on the beat. Never once in his way. Never once in the water. When it went down, she stepped back off the stone onto the first one, and turned round, and helped a small boy who’d been watching on the shingle get his ball out of the shallows, where it had rolled. She was smiling at him too.

By three o’clock, it was sixty-one attempts and nobody.

The bank had thinned a bit. People had come to watch a man stand on a stone and somebody walk out to join him, and nobody had, and it was hot. Hyvred and Bee were still there, up on the old bridge. Hyvred had her glasses pushed up in her hair and was watching every single attempt, all of it at once, the way she watched everything. Bee had her book on the parapet and was drawing it all. Rafi was there, too, down on the shingle with the black sword across his knees, looking at the second stone with total longing.

“Next year, boss,” he’d said, when Ice saw him. “Next year I’ll be fifteen.”

Xav’s notebook was full of small marks. He’d stopped saying next with any hope in it.

“You know what’s funny,” he said quietly, when Ice came back to the table for water. “I can tell you exactly what’s wrong with every single one of them. I could draw it. I could draw what they should do.” He looked at the second stone. “I can’t find anyone who does it.”

“Maybe there isn’t anyone.”

“There’s always someone.” Xav rubbed his eyes. “I just don’t know where.”

Ice walked back out to the stone.

He stood on it with his back foot in the quick brown water and his shield on his arm. Behind him, on the bank, Deston was still humming, softer now. In the pool, something was coming up. Mudwolves again, by the ripple. The next one would be along in a minute. Whoever was next.

“Declan.”

He went still.

It came from behind him, from the far bank, from the green shade under the alders. One word. A man’s voice, low, unhurried. Every letter of it pronounced properly, all the way to the end, the way almost nobody bothered to. Calm. Relaxed. The kind of voice that made you trust it before it had said anything else.

Nobody in the game called him Declan.

Hardly anyone anywhere did. Vivian, with a Mr in front of it. And a handful of people from a polytechnic classroom in Singapore, nine years ago, who’d never once shortened it. He knew all their voices.

He knew the voice before he turned round.

“Sheng Yang?” he said.

He came down the far bank out of the alders.

Tall. That was the first thing anyone saw. Taller than anyone on the shingle, taller than Xav, taller than Ice. Not big, not built like Salty. Long and easy and strong in the way that came from using it, not from showing it. He came down the bank the way he did everything Ice had ever seen him do: unhurried, as if he had all the time in the world and knew exactly what to do with it. He had a long two-handed sword across his back. A plain one, steel, with a long grip wrapped in dark leather.

Up on the bank, Suzu sat up.

He’d been lying back on the grass all afternoon with his arms behind his head and his eyes half shut, watching. Now he sat up, slowly, and looked at the tall man coming down the far bank. The corner of his mouth went up, about a millimetre.

“Leveti,” he said, to nobody in particular.

Suzu had known him two years. Since a winter in a game with wind in its name, when Ice had dragged the both of them into the same guild and they’d turned out to like each other. Leveti. He’d used the name everywhere, for years, in everything. They’d have known it on sight. They’d have known it the first day, if they’d ever thought to look.

Nobody had thought to look. Nobody had thought he’d play. He spent his money on trips. China. Japan. He’d come back with photos of mountains and noodles and say you should go, and nobody ever did.

At the table, Xav had put his pen down.

Sheng Yang crossed the river on the stepping-stones. He didn’t slip. He came across the first stone and stopped on the edge of it, a long step from the second, and looked at Ice standing there in the water.

“Declan,” he said again. He sounded pleased.

“You’ve been playing,” said Ice.

“Since the first morning.”

“Since launch?”

“Since about ten past eight.” Sheng Yang shrugged. It was a long, easy shrug, all shoulders. “I ordered it in October. I thought I’d see what it was like.”

He’d got up from the table. He was standing at the edge of the shingle with the notebook in one hand, looking at Sheng Yang across the water. He had an expression on his face that Ice had seen maybe three times in nine years.

Sheng Yang considered this. He looked, for a moment, faintly apologetic. It wasn’t very convincing.

“It slipped my mind,” he said.

Xav made a small mark in the notebook. Ice couldn’t see what it was.

“Second stone,” he said.

Sheng Yang stepped across onto the second stone, and Ice didn’t have to move.

That was the first thing. He felt it the moment the tall man’s boot came down in the water beside him. Not in front of him. Not behind him where he’d be useless. Beside him, half a pace back, on the off-side. Exactly in the space that the shield made and didn’t use. He’d stepped into it like a man stepping into his own doorway in the dark, without looking, because he knew where the door was.

They’d played together. Years ago. A month in a winter guild, a dozen nights of something else, a hundred rounds of something else again. Long enough, it turned out.

Sheng Yang had always said he liked monsters better than people. The best nights he’d ever had in any game were the hardest raids, the ones with forty wipes and one clear, the ones most people never saw the end of. He’d cleared the worst of them in two different games. But Ice had stood next to him in fights with people too, more than once, and he’d been just as good. He just didn’t enjoy it as much.
