Live from Elsewhere · Volume 11: Three Hundred and One

Chapter 275

Three Hundred and One

3,716 words · 16 min read

The first stone was sunk a hand deep, and Salty felt for it with his front foot the way Ice had told him, and found it.

“One,” said Suzu, at the front.

“One,” said Salty.

He said it out loud. They all did. Kotori behind Suzu, then Gabe, then Salty with the stone on his back, then PenguChi at the back, then, in her own way, the sheep. Five people and a sheep standing on the first of three hundred stones at the edge of the still water, saying one. So the stones would know they were somebody’s.

The still water was a mirror.

He hadn’t expected that. Everybody who’d been had told him what it was like in the dark. Black, still, like a floor you’d fall through. Nobody had told him what it was like now. Now there was a light on the hill.

It lay across the water like a road. From the gold point on the hill, three hundred stones away, all the way to his feet. A long thin line of gold on black glass, perfectly straight, perfectly still. And the stones went along it. Dark and flat, one after another, each one with a little gold on its wet top where the light caught it. As if somebody had laid a path of gold coins across a black floor and said walk on these.

“Two,” said Suzu.

Salty stepped.

He’d never carried anything this far.

He’d carried a lot of things. Ore, mostly. Out of the mine at Grimmet, on the black cliffs over a grey sea, in the first weeks, in a sack, until his back hurt. Iron bars to Joss’s smithy. A greatsword the length of a man, out of a dark stream in the fourth tunnel, quenched with the lamp out. He carried Right Size on his back every day and had for a year. It weighed about as much as a small person.

The stone weighed about as much as three.

He’d carried it seventeen days. Up the north road. Through the old hall. Past the twenty-two, past the edge, nine days across the dark country with the heather coming up green under his boots. He hadn’t put it down except at night. On the eighth night Suzu had said you can rest, and he’d said no. And meant it.

Now he was carrying it across three hundred stepping stones in the still water. Every one a little different. Some wide and flat, some narrow. Some tilted, a bit, under his foot. And every time one tilted, the stone on his back tilted with it, and he had to find the middle again. The way you’d find the middle of a swing.

“Forty-one,” said Suzu.

“Forty-one,” said Salty.

The forty-first and forty-second were wide, close together. Kotori said the nine had slept on them in January, on the way home, with an ordinary candle. There was still a little wax on the forty-first. Salty stood on it for a moment and breathed.

“Okay?” said Gabe, in front of him, not turning round.

“Okay,” said Salty.

That was Salty’s. He’d said it every twenty minutes on the longest night, two years ago, at the front of the north road with Tiger. Okay? Gabe had borrowed it. Everybody had borrowed everything, now.

At a hundred and fifty, nothing rose.

He’d known about a hundred and fifty. Everybody had. The first time, in November, something had come up out of the still water there. A shape, at the edge of the light, rising. Kotori had said keep counting. And all nine had said Suzu’s number, his number, together, louder, until it went down at a hundred and eighty.

In January, going home, at a hundred and fifty, nothing.

He stood on the hundred and fiftieth stone with three people in front of him and one behind and looked at the still water. The black glass, with the gold road across it. Perfectly still.

“Nothing,” said Gabe. He sounded as though he didn’t believe it.

“It’s not looking,” said Kotori. “Not for anything. There’s nothing on our backs.”

Salty thought about that. There’s nothing on our backs. In November there’d been the jar. In the black case Ina made, so the dark couldn’t see the light going north. The dark had felt it anyway and come up to look.

Now the jar was on the hill, and the case on Suzu’s back was empty. And on Salty’s back there was only a stone out of a dark stream. The dark’s own. Something that had never once shone.

“It knows what this is,” said Salty.

Suzu looked round at him, from the front, along the gold road.

“The stone,” said Salty. “It’s from the fourth tunnel. Same as the iron. The dark’s own.” He shifted the harness. “It’s not a light. It’s not anything the dark wants. It’s just a stone going home.”

Suzu looked at him for a long time.

“Good,” said Suzu. And turned back. “A hundred and fifty-one.”

At two hundred and sixteen, the gap.

Salty knew about that too. A space in the stones, a stride and a half of black water, where there should have been one and wasn’t. The spring that wasn’t, Isa called it. One year, three hundred years ago, the keeper hadn’t walked out. Nobody knew why. And there’d been a gap in the way ever since. A space you’d have to jump, in the dark, over water you’d fall through.

There wasn’t a gap now.

There was a hawthorn.

It came up out of the still water between the two hundred and sixteenth stone and the two hundred and eighteenth. A low thick tangle of black stems, flat on top, wide enough to stand on. Gigi had grown it out of the water in November, with her hands, when Suzu said no. Her thorn, a hedge, a thing she’d only ever grown to keep people out. And in December it had flowered. One white blossom in the dark. And in January it had set fruit. A haw, small and hard and green, on the dark country’s water.

Now it was in leaf.

Small, new, bright green leaves, all over the black stems. In the gold light off the hill they were almost too bright to look at. Like the shoots in the heather, but more. A whole small tree’s worth of spring coming out of the still water, at two hundred and seventeen, where there’d been nothing for three hundred years.

“Oh,” said Kotori.

Suzu stood on the two hundred and sixteenth stone and looked at it. Then he stepped onto it.

It held. Of course it held. It had held nine.

“Two hundred and seventeen,” said Suzu.

Kotori stepped onto it, and Gabe, who had to hold on to Kotori’s shoulder because he didn’t trust it. Then it was Salty’s turn.

He stood on the two hundred and sixteenth with the stone on his back. He looked at the hawthorn. At its new leaves, very green, very small. He thought about how much he weighed with three people’s worth of stone on top of him.

“It held nine,” said Suzu, from the far side.

“Nine people,” said Salty. “Not nine and a stone.”

“It holds what it’s given,” said Suzu.

Salty didn’t know how Suzu knew that. Then he did. It was Gigi’s. Gigi had said it, at the long table, in December, when she came home from the hill. A hedge holds what it’s given. I didn’t know you could give it anything but out.

He stepped onto the hawthorn.

It went down an inch under him. The black stems creaked. The new leaves shook. He felt it in his knees, the whole of it taking his weight and the stone’s, and bending. Like Jared’s green pole in the wind at New Year.

And it came back up.

[ a step, grown · It holds what it’s given. ]

“Two hundred and seventeen,” said Salty. His voice came out strange.

Suzu was already reaching down. He took one thorn off the edge of the hawthorn, a long black one, carefully, between two fingers, the way you’d pick a flower. He put it in his coat.

“For the notch,” said Suzu.

The last one was crooked.

He’d heard about that one most of all. Isa’s. The last one’s mine. I set it the spring before I came home. It’s crooked. I was old. A new flat stone, tilted left, so you had to step onto the high side and let it settle. The three hundredth. The last stone before the hill.

They came to it in the afternoon of the second day, in the gold light, with the hill close. Low and dark, with the ring of twelve stones round its top and the flame standing straight up over the middle. Salty could see the flat stone with the worn crown, and the jar on it, and the flame. He could see something grey folded beside it. Isa’s blanket. Where it had been left.

Suzu stepped onto the crooked stone. It tilted under him, and held.

“Three hundred,” said Suzu. Very quietly.

And then he didn’t step off.

Salty, two stones back, saw why.

There was water between the crooked stone and the hill. Not much. A stride. A stride of black still water between the last stone and the heather on the hill’s shore. With the gold light lying across it.

In November there hadn’t been. Ice had told them. He stepped off the crooked stone onto the hill. Nine people had stepped off the crooked stone onto the hill. In January nine people had stepped from the hill onto it.

Now there was a stride of water.

“It’s come in,” said Kotori softly. “The water. Over the winter.”

“It always comes in,” said Suzu. He was looking at the stride of black water, at the hill beyond it. “Every year. A step. That’s why there’s a stone every spring.” He was quiet. “So the way would always be there.”

Salty understood it then, standing on the two hundred and ninety-eighth stone with three people’s worth of rock on his back.

The hill was getting smaller.

It had always been getting smaller. For three hundred years. The still water coming in round it, a step every year. And every spring one of Isa’s mothers had walked out from the hill with a stone and set it in the water, a step further than the last. So the way would reach. Three hundred stones, three hundred springs, three hundred steps of water. One a year. Always just enough.

And one spring, three hundred years ago, nobody had. And there’d been a gap.

“Three hundred and one,” said Salty.

He hadn’t meant to say it. It came out of him the way okay did, on the north road.

Suzu turned round on the crooked stone and looked at him.

“Not yet,” said Suzu. “The equinox. Tomorrow.” He looked at the stride of water. “Isa’s mothers set it at dusk, on the day the light and the dark are the same. So neither of them minds.”

He jumped the stride. Easily. He’d jumped wider. He landed on the heather on the hill’s shore and turned round and held out his hand.

Kotori jumped, holding it. Gabe jumped, not holding anything, and landed on his knees and said ow and laughed. Patience the sheep stood on the two hundred and ninety-ninth stone and looked at the stride of water with an expression of enormous disapproval, until PenguChi picked her up and handed her across to Gabe.

Salty didn’t jump.

He couldn’t, with the stone. He stood on the crooked stone, the three hundredth, with the still water a stride wide in front of him and the hill on the other side. And he didn’t mind at all.

“I’ll wait here,” he said.

Suzu looked at him from the shore.

“Till tomorrow?”

“It’s one night,” said Salty. “I’ve carried it seventeen days.” He sat down on the crooked stone, carefully, with the stone still on his back. The crooked stone tilted, and held. “I want to be the one who puts it down.”

He sat on the three hundredth stone all night.

The others went up the hill. He watched them from the water. The ring of twelve stones round the top, and the flame, and Suzu kneeling by the flat stone, and Kotori making tea. Gabe standing at the flame for a long time on his own, very still, which Salty didn’t watch, because it wasn’t his. PenguChi at the back, of course, by the eastern stone, looking out.

At a quarter to eight, out there, he heard them say it. Up on the hill, round the flat stone, faint across the water.

Set one to watch.

So the rest may sleep.

He said it too, on the crooked stone, on his own, in the still water. And then, a long way south, so faint he thought he’d imagined it, sixty people at a long table said it at the same time. He hadn’t imagined it.

Wendy messaged him out there in the morning, at the end of the night.

They set it at the second dusk on the equinox.

The twentieth of March. The day the light and the dark were the same length, out there and in here. The sun going down in the south-west over the dark country, gold, flat across the still water. And at the same moment, on the hill, the flame standing straight up, gold too. The two lights the same colour, from opposite sides, with the still water between them like a floor.

Salty stood up on the crooked stone.

Everybody had come down to the shore. Suzu at the front, at the edge of the heather, with the thorn in his hand. Kotori beside him with a cup. Gabe, red-eyed, holding the sheep. PenguChi at the back.

Salty took the harness off.

He’d done it every night for seventeen days. It had never felt like this. The weight came off his shoulders and he nearly fell over, standing up. The stone hung in the harness ropes in his hands in front of him, over the stride of black water. Flat and grey and rough-bottomed. The dark’s own. Out of the stream in the fourth tunnel, where it had lain in the dark since before the round town.

“How deep is it?” said Gabe. Very quietly.

“I don’t know,” said Salty.

Nobody knew. Nobody had ever known. You couldn’t see the bottom of the still water. You couldn’t see anything in it, not even your hand. Isa’s mothers had set three hundred stones in it, and every one of them had sat right, a hand above the water, flat, a step. Except the last, which was crooked, because the woman who set it was old.

Salty had chosen the stone in an hour, in the dark stream, with Wendy holding the lamp.

He’d chosen it by how it felt. By its weight in his hands. By its thickness, which was the length of his hand from wrist to fingertip. He’d thought, at the time, in the fourth tunnel, that’s right. He hadn’t known why. He didn’t know now. He was a miner from a town on black cliffs over a grey sea. He’d always just known what size things were.

He lowered it.

Hand over hand on the harness ropes, into the stride of black water between the crooked stone and the hill. The bottom edge went in without a sound. No splash. The still water just closed round it, black, as though it had been expecting it. Then the stone was going down. Slowly, out of sight, a hand, two, three. The ropes running through his hands. Salty on one knee on the crooked stone, leaning out.

It stopped.

He felt it stop. Through the ropes, through his hands, through his arms. The bottom of it finding the bottom of the still water, wherever that was, and settling.

He let the ropes go slack.

The stone stood in the stride of black water between the crooked stone and the hill. Flat. Level. Its top a hand above the water, dry, grey, with the gold light from the hill on one side of it and the gold light from the sunset on the other.

Not crooked. Not sunk. A step.

Right size.

“Three hundred and one,” said Suzu.

“Three hundred and one,” said Kotori.

Gabe said it, crying. PenguChi said it, at the back. The sheep said something.

And Salty, on one knee on the three hundredth stone, said it last. Very quietly. To the stone.

“Three hundred and one.”

[ The way · three hundred and one ]

[ Somebody came. ]

He stepped onto it.

It didn’t move. Not a hair. He stood on it with his whole weight and it was as if it had been there three hundred years. Then he stepped off it, onto the heather of the hill. First, before anybody else. Because he’d carried it.

Suzu was waiting at the top, at the flat stone, with the thorn.

Salty watched him kneel by the rim. Three hundred notches, two lines of them round the edge, old, worn smooth, every one cut by somebody who’d set a stone. A cross, small, where the spring that wasn’t would have been. And beside the cross, newer, sharper, Suzu’s own, from December. A thorn notch for the missing year.

And at the end of the second line, past Isa’s crooked one, a space.

Suzu put the thorn from two hundred and seventeen to the stone and cut.

It took a long time. A thorn is not a chisel. But Suzu didn’t hurry, and nobody said anything. The sun went down behind them, and the only light on the hill was the flame, standing straight up over the jar. And Suzu, kneeling at the rim of the flat stone, cutting a notch with a thorn.

When he’d finished, he sat back on his heels and looked at it.

Three hundred and one.

“Hm,” said Suzu. Which was Isa’s.

Kotori put a cup down beside the notch. Not tea for anybody to drink. For the stone. For whoever came next spring. She’d made it from the green shoots, the ones in the heather. PenguChi had seen them first and Patience had eaten most of them.

[ Brewing · a tea of the first green · 88% ]

[ For whoever comes next. ]

Salty sat down on the heather at the top of the hill, with no stone on his back for the first time in eighteen days. He felt as though he might float up off the ground. He lay down instead, on his back, and looked at the sky over the dark country. It was black. With edges.

Somewhere a long way south, at the bottom of it, on a roof by an eye, he thought there might be an old woman in a blanket. Looking north at the same light, at the same dusk.

He thought he could almost see her.

He couldn’t. It was too far. But he thought it anyway.
