Kestrel had been second three times, and she had decided, somewhere on the second day of the march to Elmcross, that there would not be a fourth.

She didn’t say it out loud. She didn’t need to. Her ten knew. They’d been with her for the drowned church in December, when a party nobody had heard of walked down their own green steps and out again eleven hours ahead of them. They’d been there for the Saltwell in April, when Brann looked up for one second at a window that said somebody else had cleared something. They’d been there on Kei’s stream in July, when a guild that hadn’t even been racing kept the first-rank hall under its own stair, and VANTA wasn’t a guild yet and couldn’t go in at all.

Three times. Three different ways. Every one of them, in the end, the same man at the front.

She didn’t hate him. That was the strange part, and she’d stopped pretending it wasn’t. She’d watched every one of his VODs since December. Every one, from the first drowned church to the walk home from the Long Watch. She’d watched him sit in a chair at the end of a long table and say not always me. She knew how he held a shield. She knew where he stood in a room. She knew he never flinched, and why. She’d heard him tell a woman with two curved knives on a moor, in February. Because if the front flinches, everybody flinches.

She’d thought about that sentence more than she’d thought about most of the people she’d met in her life.

She didn’t hate him. She just wanted, once, to be first through a door he was also walking toward.

They marched for three days. Ten of VANTA, and the other ten behind them, and a crowd behind them. Streamers, and people who watched streamers, and people who’d heard there was going to be a race. By Saturday it was nearly two hundred, strung out down the south-west road like a carnival that had lost its tent.

Kestrel had picked the ten herself. Brann, with his tower shield, the best she’d ever had. Ottie, who healed like breathing. Mags with the spear. Ren with the fire. And five of the fifteen who’d signed VANTA’s writ by hand in a Hall in July, one at a time, over sixteen horrible days. She’d chosen the five who were thirty-eight, and good, and didn’t look up when a window came.

She streamed all of it. That was the job. Ninety-six thousand watching by Saturday night, which was more than she’d ever had for walking.

“I’m going to walk to Elmcross,” said Kestrel. “And then I’m going to open a door. That’s all I know.”

She didn’t say win. She’d said win before the drowned church.

They came into Elmcross on Sunday at the second dusk, out of the east. She saw Constellation come in out of the north at almost the same time.

She’d known they would. Three days, both of them. Kei didn’t do things carelessly, and she didn’t either. But it still went through her like cold water to see them on the north road. A long line coming over a low hill in the evening light, ten in front and dozens behind. And at the very front, small at that distance, a shield.

Elmcross was a crossroads town. Seven roads came in to a square, the way they did at the round town. But Elmcross wasn’t round, and it didn’t have a dome. It had seven elm trees, enormous and old, one at the mouth of each road. And it had a Hall in the middle of the square, low and long and built of grey stone, with a slate roof sagging like a tired horse’s back. Smaller than the Hall of Roads by a long way. Older, maybe.

The doors were shut. A notice was nailed to them in a careful old hand.

Closed till the first dawn. No running. — J. Rook, Keeper.

Both crowds filled the square. VANTA’s two hundred from the east, Constellation’s however many from the north. Kestrel guessed three hundred, maybe more. It turned out half their guild had walked down to watch. She saw a girl with gold horns and a bow. She saw a woman with gills and a trident, beside a tall one with a tower shield nearly as big as Brann’s. She saw a sheep. She decided not to think about the sheep.

And in the middle of the square, in front of the shut doors, the two tens stood and looked at each other.

He was at the front of his, of course. Mud to the knees from three days of road. Shield on his back. Helm under his arm, the way he always carried it when he wasn’t using it. Black hair with the teal streak. She’d only seen his face once before, in a city with no name, at the bottom of some steps. On his stream you never saw it. You saw what he saw.

He looked at her across the square.

“Next door,” said Kestrel.

“Next door,” said Ice.

That was all either of them said. It was enough. It had been enough since December.

But she noticed his hands. She noticed everything; it was why she was good. His hands hung loose at his sides, the left one near the shield-strap, the right one near the sword. They were perfectly still. Not calm-still, the way she’d seen them in a hundred VODs, holding a door or a stream. A different still. A very particular one. She’d seen it on two or three people in her whole life, in the moment before something started. She’d seen it on her own hands once or twice, in a mirror, before a final.

She filed it away, and didn’t know yet what it meant.

The first dawn came at midnight, the way the first dawn always did.

The sky over the seven elms went grey, and then white, and the whole square went quiet. Six hundred people, maybe, in the square and on the roofs and up the elms. And the bar came off the inside of the Hall doors with a heavy wooden clunk, and the doors swung open.

An old man stood in them. Very small, very upright, with a long white beard tucked into his belt and a lantern in his hand. He looked at the six hundred people the way you’d look at weather.

“I’m Rook,” he said. “This is my Hall. There’s a hole in the floor at the back of it since Thursday night, with two hooks over it. I’m told it wants a banner.” He held up a finger. “You may come in. You may go to the hooks. You will not run. I have had this floor since before your grandmothers were born. It is oak. It is three hundred years old. Nobody runs on it.”

There was a silence in the square.

Kestrel looked at the doors, and the floor beyond them. Long and dark and polished, running maybe sixty paces from the doors to the far end, where she could see a ring of iron in the floor and two hooks on the wall above it.

Sixty paces. She could do sixty paces in about six seconds if she ran.

She looked at Ice.

He was looking at the floor too. Then he looked at the old man, for a moment, and something happened in his face that she’d seen on his stream when he talked to the woman in the fen. Sedge. A sort of quiet respect, for somebody old who’d decided a thing a long time ago and wasn’t going to undecide it.

A girl with big pale wings came up beside him from Constellation’s ten. She was carrying something rolled. A banner. She held it out to him.

“You’re at the front,” said the girl with wings. “You take it.”

He took it.

And Mags, beside Kestrel, put VANTA’s banner into her hands. Black, plain, with a single white line across it, like the horizon. Kestrel had chosen it. She’d chosen it because it didn’t waste anything.

“Go,” said Rook, from the doorway. “Walking.”

Ice walked.

Kestrel walked too.

She’d decided in the half-second before her first step. She’d decided she wouldn’t run. Not because of the old man, though that was part of it. But because Ice was walking, and she wasn’t going to win a race against a man who was walking by running past him in an old man’s Hall. She wasn’t going to win it like that. If she was going to be first, she was going to be first the same way he was. Or she wasn’t going to be first at all.

So she walked.

She found, after about three steps, that she was walking at exactly his pace.

Not on purpose, at first. It just happened. His stride and hers, side by side on the dark oak floor, three paces apart. Him with the rolled banner under his arm, her with hers. Six hundred people pouring in through the doors behind them, and stopping, and going silent, and watching two people walk the length of a Hall.

Ten paces. Twenty.

She sped up, a little. Just a little. She couldn’t help it.

He sped up exactly the same amount.

She slowed down. So did he.

She looked sideways at him. He was looking straight ahead at the hooks on the far wall, with his face perfectly calm. Except at the corner of his mouth, which wasn’t calm at all.

He was matching her. On purpose. The same way she’d matched him.

Thirty paces. Forty.

Something rose up in Kestrel’s chest that she didn’t have a name for. It wasn’t anger. It was nearer the opposite. It was the feeling she’d had, very young, the first time she’d played anybody who was actually as good as her. Oh. There you are.

Fifty paces. The iron ring in the floor. The two hooks on the wall above it, side by side, a hand’s width apart.

They reached them at the same moment. They unrolled their banners at the same moment. Hers black with its white line. His night-blue, edge to edge, with gold dots and silver lines and, at the very top, small and faint, a gold star. They reached up together and put them on the hooks.

In the same second.

She heard the iron click under both of them at once.

The seven lines round the iron ring lit. Half of them. Then flickered. Then went dark again. The iron didn’t sink.

Six hundred people let their breath out.

Rook came down the Hall with his lantern, slowly, the whole sixty paces. He stopped in front of the two banners and looked up at them for a long time.

He couldn’t see the windows. Kestrel had learned that about NPCs, months ago. They didn’t see windows. They saw travellers go still. But he could see the iron in his floor not opening, and two banners on two hooks, and two people standing side by side under them. And he knew what it meant. Of course he did. It was his Hall.

He looked at Ice. Then at Kestrel. Then at the crowd, filling his Hall from wall to wall.

“There’s an old custom,” he said. “Older than me. When two come to one door at the same time, the door doesn’t choose. It’s settled on the floor.” He tapped the oak with his foot. “One for each banner. In the middle. Until one yields or falls.”

He turned and looked at the crowd.

“Make room,” said Rook.

And six hundred people, in an old man’s Hall, made room.

They made a ring. Kestrel had never seen a crowd do anything so quickly or so quietly. They just moved back, all of them, to the walls and the doors and the edges, and left the middle of the floor empty. Thirty paces across, maybe. Dark oak under the lantern Rook hung on a hook in the roof-beam. It was a fighting ring, the way rings must have been before anybody had ever heard of streams.

“One for each banner,” said Rook again, standing at the edge of it.

“Me,” said Kestrel.

She said it before anybody on her side could open their mouth. She heard Brann start to say Kes— and stop. She didn’t look at him. She looked across the ring.

Constellation’s ten had gone very still. She saw Kei, at the front of them, turn his head and look at Ice. She saw a few others look at Ice. The quiet one with the scythe. The one who counted. The little healer with the white staff, who’d gone pale. A huge man with a hammer, who was grinning.

The girl with gold horns had her bow in a fist gone white at the knuckles. The tall woman with the tower shield had stepped forward half a pace, as if she might stop it. They thought he was going to lose. His own guild. They’d watched him hold doors and streams and halls for eight months. They knew what he was. A tank. And a tank couldn’t catch a bow.

Only two of them didn’t look worried. The man with the hammer, grinning. And one man in the ten with two short blades crossed on his back. He’d sat down cross-legged on the oak at the very edge of the ring, as if he were settling in for a play, with a sketchbook open on his knee. He was drawing.

Ice didn’t look at any of them. He was looking at Kestrel.

“Okay,” said Ice.

He handed his helm to the man with the hammer. He walked out into the middle of the ring.

“You’d have lost a day,” he said.

“You’d have lost nothing.”

“That’s not the point,” said Ice.

She stood there. Behind her, her ten were coming across the floor. Brann at the front with his face white, Ottie already ringing a heal on her that she didn’t need.

And across the ring, his guild.

It wasn’t only VANTA who looked like that. She’d expected his people to be cheering. Some were. The man with the hammer was shouting something she couldn’t hear, with both arms in the air. But most of them looked exactly like her own ten. The girl with gold horns had let her bow hang down by her side and was staring at him with her mouth open. The tall woman with the tower shield had both hands over her mouth. The man with platinum-and-red hair had stopped looking at feet and was just looking. The loud boy in Riverbank’s colours was still silent. Kei had dropped his map; it had rolled away across the oak and nobody had picked it up. The healer with the white staff was crying. The quiet one with the scythe was looking at Ice with no expression at all. Somehow that was the most expression of anyone.

They hadn’t known either. Eight months in a guild with him, and they hadn’t known.

Except the man with two blades, at the edge of the ring. He was still sitting cross-legged on the oak with his sketchbook. He finished a line, unhurried. Then he looked up at Ice across the ring and lifted two fingers, lazily, in something that wasn’t quite a salute.

Ice lifted his chin at him, very slightly. As if they’d talked about this before. As if they’d talked about it for years.

Kestrel looked from one to the other.

“Is there anyone?” she said. Her voice still wasn’t level. “Who can do that to you?”

Ice didn’t answer straight away. He glanced across the ring at the man with the sketchbook.

“One,” he said.

“Who?”

“Sketch,” said Ice. “Riverbank. Xav.”

Kestrel looked at the man on the floor. Two blades, a sketchbook, and a face that looked as if it found most things mildly funny. She knew him. Riverbank’s leader. She’d offered him a seat in VANTA once, in the winter, no strings, and he’d said no, for his friends. She’d thought he was good. She’d never once thought he was that.

“The one who draws?”

“He’d give me a run for my money,” said Ice.

On the floor, without looking up from his sketchbook, the man with two blades said, “He’s being nice. He’d win.”

“Eventually,” said Ice.

“Eventually,” agreed Xav, and went back to drawing.

“Your draw was very good,” said Ice. “At the end. The held one. On one knee.”

Kestrel stared at him.

“I’d have breathed out,” said Ice. “Most days. Most people would. That was a very good draw.”

He’d said that in April. Your arrow in its eye at 7%. That was very good. She’d carried it about for a week.

“Thank you,” said Kestrel. She didn’t know what else to say.

“Next door,” said Ice.

She looked at the stair of light in the floor, going down under his banner. At the black banner beside it, with its white line, still on its hook, dark.

“Next door,” said Kestrel.

And this time, for the first time, she wasn’t sure she meant it as a threat at all.
