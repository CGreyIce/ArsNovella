Live from Elsewhere · Volume 7: Keep the Lamps

Chapter 181

It'll Be March

3,484 words · 15 min read

The wick went out at nine hours and fourteen minutes, and Malcolm wrote it down.

He’d been writing them down all week. He had a little notebook from the market, the paper kind the game sold for people who liked to write things that didn’t go in windows. On the first page, in his small careful hand: WICKS. Under it, a list. Every wick he’d burned, how long it lasted, and what it died of.

Market wick, Bee’s oil. 8h 02m. Charred to the dish.

Market wick, doubled. 8h 51m. Charred.

Linen, from Aleaka. 7h 40m. Charred, smoky.

Hemp, from Tiger. 9h 14m. Charred.

He looked at the last one. Nine hours and fourteen minutes was the best yet. In the game, on the longest night, the dark would last a day and a half. Thirty-five hours. Ina had counted it. Bee had counted it. Everyone had counted it. You’d need four wicks, one after another, in every lamp. Ninety-four lamps on a road four days long. Twenty-two more past the old hall. And somebody walking up and down that road in the dark, all night, on the longest night, changing them.

With the Unlit coming down it.

“It’ll be March,” said Malcolm, to the notebook.

He said it the way he always said it. Calmly. He wasn’t being dramatic. He said it because it was true, usually. Things took longer than people thought. People always thought things would be done by Christmas, and they were never done by Christmas. They were done in March. He’d been saying it about everything for years.

He was right, mostly. He was a pessimist; it was his job. Somebody in every group had to be the one who said it’ll be March, so the others could prove him wrong.

He didn’t think they were going to prove him wrong about this.

He went to see Ina on the Tuesday, because Ina knew the cages.

“The old ones,” said Malcolm. “On the rings. The ones from before. How long did they burn?”

Ina was at Joss’s anvil doing a star. He didn’t stop. He’d done three hundred stars by now, and he could talk and do one at the same time. “Don’t know. Nobody knows. They’ve been out three hundred years.” He bent the five bars in the fire. “Why?”

“Because ours go out in nine hours.”

Ina stopped.

He put the tongs down. He looked at Malcolm for a long moment, with his oni mask at his shoulder and the forge-light on his face.

“Nine hours,” he said.

“Nine hours fourteen. At best. Hemp.” Malcolm showed him the notebook. “The longest night’s thirty-five.”

Ina read the list. All of it, slowly. Then he went to the end of Joss’s bench, where the very first cage still sat in its sacking, the one off the first ring. He’d been keeping it like a grandmother at the end of a row. He unwrapped it and turned it over. Then he tipped the little iron oil dish at the bottom toward the forge-light.

There was soot in it. Old soot, hard as stone, black. The same as in the hollows on the backs of the waystones.

“There,” said Ina. “That’s what’s left of theirs.”

Malcolm looked at it.

He was an essence harvester. That was his gathering skill. Everyone had one. Most people’s were things you could put in a sack, ore and herbs and wood. His was the other kind. You went to a thing and you took what it was out of it, a little. The way you’d take a breath of something. And you held it in a little glass phial and it was yours. He’d harvested essence from moonbells and from the wind on the high moor. And once, in March, from a very old dog asleep in front of a fire in a farmhouse, which had been the gentlest thing he’d ever held.

He’d never harvested soot.

He took a phial out of his pack. He put his hand over the oil dish of the old cage. He closed his eyes, the way you did, and breathed.

[ Essence Harvesting · Old soot ]

[ It remembers a flame that did not go out. For a year. Then another year. Then nobody came. ]

He opened his eyes.

Ina was looking at the window over the dish. He’d gone completely still.

“A year,” said Ina.

“A year,” said Malcolm. “Then another year.” He looked at the window for a long time. Then nobody came. “Their wicks didn’t go out. Not for years. Not until nobody came to fill the oil.”

“How?”

Malcolm didn’t answer. He didn’t know. But he was already writing it down in his little notebook, under the list of wicks. Old soot: a flame that did not go out for a year. And under that, in smaller letters: so it’s possible.

He looked at that for a while. He wasn’t used to writing possible.

He spent the Wednesday and the Thursday finding out how.

He was an enchanter. That was his production skill. You took the essence you’d harvested and you put it into something, the way you’d put salt into soup. You held it over a thing and told it what to be. A boot that remembered the wind ran a bit faster. A cloak that remembered a fire was a bit warmer. Nothing big. The game was very strict about enchanting. You could only put into a thing what it could hold. You could only tell it to be something it already wanted to be, a little.

He tried telling a wick to remember a flame that didn’t go out.

He took the essence of the old soot, a little black breath in its phial, and held it over a hemp wick on Joss’s bench. He told it.

[ Enchanting · Remember · Failed ]

[ It doesn’t know what that is. ]

He tried a linen one.

[ Enchanting · Remember · Failed ]

He tried one of Tiger’s, and one of Bee’s, and a strand of wool from the Hart’s cat, which had been sitting on the bench watching him and which didn’t mind. He tried telling them softer, and louder, and slower. He tried holding the phial for a whole minute instead of a breath.

[ Enchanting · Remember · Failed ]

[ It doesn’t know what that is. ]

Twenty-three times, by Thursday night. He wrote them all down.

“It’s because they’ve never been lit,” said Ina, who’d watched most of them from the anvil. “Your wicks. They’ve never burned. You can’t make a thing remember something it’s never done.”

“I know.”

“So burn them first.”

He’d burned them first. He’d burned them all first, for an hour each, and then put them out, and then tried. They remembered burning for an hour. That was all they remembered. You couldn’t tell a wick that had burned for an hour to remember burning for a year. It would just look at you.

“It’ll be March,” said Malcolm.

Ina didn’t argue. He went back to his star.

Dec came to the Hart on Thursday night and sat at the corner table, and Malcolm didn’t look up from the notebook.

“Twenty-three,” said Dec.

“Ina told you.”

“Ina told everyone.” Dec had a cup of Kotori’s tea. He put it down on the table between them. “He said you wrote possible.”

Malcolm stopped writing.

He looked up at last. Dec was sitting across from him in the corner of the Hart, in the lamplight, with his shield propped against the wall. The same as he’d sat across from him at seventeen, in the canteen at poly. With the group chat open on both their phones, which they’d made in the first week. Malcolm and Dec. Nobody else had been in it for the first ten minutes. Then they’d added the whole class, one at a time, and it had become what it became.

They’d drifted, a bit, after Dec moved. That was nobody’s fault. People moved; you drifted. Malcolm had always thought it would be March before they drifted back.

“I wrote possible,” said Malcolm. “Then I failed twenty-three times. So.”

“So it’s possible.”

“It’s possible for them. Three hundred years ago.” Malcolm turned the notebook round on the table and pushed it across. “They had something we haven’t got. A light that didn’t go out. They put it in their wicks somehow. I can’t find it.”

Dec read the list. All twenty-three. He didn’t say anything kind about it. That was one of the good things about Dec. He never said kind things about things that weren’t working yet.

“What would you need?” he said instead.

Malcolm thought about it. He’d been thinking about it for three days.

“Something that’s kept a light,” he said slowly. “Not a light. Something that’s held one. For a long time. And didn’t let it go out.” He turned his cup round. “A wick doesn’t know what that is. It’s never done it. But if I could find something that had, and take what it remembers—“

He stopped.

Dec was looking at him.

He wasn’t looking at him the way he usually looked at things, flat, his face doing nothing. He was looking at him the way he’d looked at the black iron in Joss’s smithy, Ina said. The way he looked at a door.

“Suzu,” said Dec.

The jar sat on the table in the Hart’s corner at the second dusk, and the three of them looked at it.

It was a plain clay jar with a lid. About the size of two hands cupped together. It had come from a woman called Tamsin in Larkhollow, Suzu’s town, a long time ago, in the first weeks. A chandler. She’d said it was for keeping old lights in. And Suzu had kept eighty-one in it, from a barrow on the downs, all through the spring, without knowing why. Until April, in the Long Watch. One by one, he’d let them go, into eighty-one empty lanterns in eighty-one dark alcoves, saying each number out loud. Until there were none left.

Kept: none. Let go: eighty-one.

It was on the wall now, in the Hall of First Clears, though the jar wasn’t. Malcolm had seen Suzu read that bit, at Year One, at the Hart. He’d read it the way he read his mother’s messages. His face doing nothing. His thumb gone still on the screen.

“It’s empty,” said Suzu.

“I know,” said Malcolm.

“I let them all go.”

“I know.” Malcolm looked at it. “Dec says you said it wasn’t empty. After. In the hall at the end. He says you said it was ready. For something.”

Suzu was quiet for a long time.

“It felt like that,” he said. “When the last one went. It didn’t feel empty. It felt like a room after people have gone home from a party. Like it was waiting for the next ones.” He put his hand flat on the lid. “I’ve kept it in my pack since. I don’t know why. It’s just a jar.”

“Can I?” said Malcolm.

He didn’t reach for it. He held out his hand, palm up, beside it, and waited. You didn’t take a thing like that. You asked.

Suzu looked at him. Then at Dec, beside him. Dec didn’t say anything. He was very careful not to say anything. It was Suzu’s.

Suzu took his hand off the lid.

“Go on,” he said.

Malcolm took out an empty phial. He put his hand over the jar, not on it. He closed his eyes and breathed.

[ Essence Harvesting · Tamsin’s jar ]

[ It remembers keeping. Eighty-one. For a long time. In the dark. It did not let one go out. Not one. Until they were home. ]

Nobody said anything.

Suzu had gone very white. He was looking at the window over the jar the way he’d looked at the window over the eighty-first lantern in April, Dec said afterward. As if somebody had said his name in a room where he’d thought he was alone.

“Okay,” said Suzu, very quietly, to the jar.

Malcolm held the phial up to the lamp. There was something in it. Not black, like the soot. Not anything you could see, really. A sort of warmth in the glass, like the glass had been in somebody’s hand.

He took a hemp wick out of his pack. He held the phial over it. He told it.

[ Enchanting · Remember · Failed ]

[ It doesn’t know what that is. ]

He sat back.

Twenty-four.

“You can’t take it out,” said Suzu.

They were still at the table. It was past ten. Dec had gone to sleep in the chair beside them, sitting up, with his feet on the rung. Malcolm had tried six more times with the phial and written each one down, and each one said It doesn’t know what that is. Thirty.

“What?”

“You took it out of the jar,” said Suzu. “The remembering. In the little bottle. And then you tried to put it in the wick.” He was looking at the jar, not at Malcolm. “That’s not how it went in. The lights. They didn’t go in because somebody put them. I didn’t put them. They went in on their own. One at a time. And I said the number.” He touched the lid. “You can’t take it out of the jar. It’s not a thing. It’s what the jar is. You have to put the wick in.”

Malcolm looked at him.

“And wait,” said Suzu. “And say the number.”

They did it that night. Nobody else was left in the Hart’s corner but Dec, asleep. And the Hart’s cat, which had come and sat on the table next to the jar and was watching, the way it watched everything Malcolm did.

Suzu took the lid off.

Malcolm took a hemp wick, a plain one from Tiger’s sack that had never been lit, and held it over the open mouth of the jar. He didn’t tell it anything. He didn’t do anything at all. He just held it there.

“One,” said Suzu.

Malcolm let go.

The wick fell into the jar, the way a light falls into a lantern. Suzu put the lid back on, gently, and put his hand flat on top of it. And they waited.

They waited a long time. Malcolm didn’t know how long you were supposed to wait. Suzu didn’t seem to either. He sat with his hand on the lid and his face doing nothing and waited. Malcolm sat across from him and waited too. It was the most patient thing he’d ever done, and he was a patient man. In the chair beside them Dec breathed, slowly, asleep. The Hart was quiet. The lamp on the table burned down a little.

At the third dawn, at midnight, the light coming grey through the Hart’s small windows, Suzu took his hand off the lid.

“I think it’s done,” he said.

Malcolm took the lid off. He reached in and took out the wick.

It looked the same. A plain hemp wick, never lit. Nothing on it. He held it in the grey light and his heart was going, which was ridiculous, over a wick.

He put it in the little iron dish of Ina’s new cage on the table, the one Ina had given him to test with, and poured in a little of Bee’s oil. He took out his stick, the one he cleaned every night, and touched the end to it. He didn’t have Kindle. He said the word every child in the game learned in the first week, the small one for lighting a candle.

It caught.

A small flame, yellow, steady. And a window, over the cage, on the corner table of the Hart, at the third dawn.

[ Enchanting · Kept wick · Grade 81% ]

[ It remembers being kept. It does not go out by itself. Something has to put it out. ]

Malcolm sat and looked at it.

He didn’t say anything. He didn’t write anything down. For the first time in thirty wicks, he didn’t reach for the notebook. He just sat at the corner table and looked at a little flame in a black cage that wasn’t going to go out by itself.

“Eighty-one,” said Suzu, softly, looking at the window. “It says eighty-one.”

“It’s the grade.”

“I know it’s the grade,” said Suzu. And something happened to his face. Not much. You’d only have seen it if you’d known him a long time. Malcolm hadn’t known him a long time. He saw it anyway.

In the chair beside them, Dec opened his eyes.

He looked at the flame in the cage. At the window. At the two of them, at the corner table, at the third dawn, with a jar between them and the cat.

“Not March,” said Dec.

Malcolm looked at him.

“It’s December,” said Dec. “It’s only December.” He sat up, slowly, and took his feet off the rung. “It’s not March.”

Malcolm opened his mouth to say it will be, for the rest of them. A hundred and sixteen wicks. Ninety-four and twenty-two. One at a time, in a jar, all night. And Suzu with his hand on the lid, saying the number. He’d done the sum already, the way he always did the sums.

He didn’t say it.

“It’s December,” he said instead.

They did a hundred and sixteen. It took eleven nights.

Every night from the second dusk they sat at the corner table in the Hart. Suzu with his hand on the lid, Malcolm with the wicks, the cat watching. Eleven wicks a night, sometimes twelve, one at a time. Suzu said the number as each one went in. He didn’t skip any. He said them all out loud, one, two, three, all the way to a hundred and sixteen, the way he’d said them in the Long Watch in April with his hands shaking. His hands didn’t shake now.

People came and sat with them. Not every night. Ina, after the forge, with his mask in his lap. Bee, drawing the jar. Ice, sometimes, falling asleep in the chair. Once, very late, Kotori with a pot of tea and three cups and one for the cat, which drank it.

On the eleventh night, at a hundred and sixteen, Suzu took his hand off the lid for the last time. He looked into the jar for a long time.

“It’s still not empty,” he said.

“No?”

“It’s ready,” said Suzu. “For something else. I don’t know what.” He put the lid on and put the jar in his pack. “I’ll know when it’s time.”

Malcolm wrote in his notebook, at the bottom of the list of thirty failures and a hundred and sixteen that weren’t.

Kept wicks: 116. Done 13 Dec.

He looked at it for a long time. Then, under it, in his small careful hand, because it was true and because somebody in every group had to be the one who said it:

It wasn’t March.
