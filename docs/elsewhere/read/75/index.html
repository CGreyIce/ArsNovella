Chapter 75

Two Front Doors

2,162 words · 9 min read

On the eve of Chinese New Year, Ice logged out of Fernwick at five in the afternoon, with the sun still high over the old fen, and told his chat he was going to dinner.

“Reunion dinner,” he said. He was standing on the Landing with the others, at the game’s second dawn, which felt wrong, because it was nearly evening on Earth. “I’m going. Gabe’s going. We’ll be back on Sunday. Probably.” He looked at the chat panel at the edge of his eye, scrolling faster than he could read. “Happy new year. Everybody. Gong xi fa cai.”

Sheng Yang and Kotori were staying on. Sheng Yang said he’d be with his family tomorrow and had nothing to do tonight, which wasn’t true, and everybody knew it. He just liked the fen when it was quiet. Kotori said she’d keep Sheng Yang company, and went pink, and said she meant she’d heal him, and went pinker.

Deston had gone home to Singapore on Wednesday. Suzu had gone on Thursday, on the morning bus, with a bag of mandarin oranges Ice’s stepmom had pressed on him at the door. He’d said I can’t three times, in the end said thank you, and taken the whole bag.

So it was Ice and Gabe in the flat, at half past five, with the sun going down orange over Malacca. And on the other side of the opening in the wall, his parents’ side, the smell of dinner.

The flat was two flats, really. Everybody who came said so. His, and his parents’ next door, on the same floor, with the wall between them opened up, so the two were one long home with two front doors. Most days the doors stayed shut and the opening stayed quiet, and Ice’s side was his and theirs was theirs. His parents were used to him being up at strange hours, and to strange things being delivered, and to a man in a swamp shouting about moonbells at three in the morning.

Tonight both front doors were open. The opening in the wall was just a room joining onto a room. And everything smelled of dinner.

His dad was at the table already, in a red shirt, with a newspaper open in front of him, not really reading it. His stepmom was in the kitchen, and had been since lunchtime, and wouldn’t let anybody in. Both of them had worked at Singapore Airlines before they retired.

Gabe stood in the opening in the wall in a clean shirt, holding a box of pineapple tarts he’d queued forty minutes for on Jonker Street. He looked more nervous than he’d looked at Vivian’s.

“Come in,” said Ice’s dad, without looking up from his paper. “Sit. You live here now.”

Gabe sat.

There were more dishes than people. There always were. That was the point.

Ice’s stepmom brought them out one at a time and put them down on the table and went back for more. She wouldn’t let either of them help. There was fish, a whole one, because there had to be. Ice knew without anybody saying it that it wouldn’t all be eaten tonight, because some of it had to be left for next year. There was chicken, and vegetables he didn’t know the names of in English, and a soup that had been on the stove since noon. And there was the big plate of yee sang in the middle. Thin strips of raw fish and shredded carrot and radish and pickled ginger and crackers and sauce and a hundred other things, piled up in colours, waiting.

“You know how to do this?” said Ice’s dad to Gabe, nodding at the yee sang.

“No, uncle,” said Gabe honestly.

“Chopsticks,” said Ice’s dad, handing him a pair. “Everybody. All together. You lift it up as high as you can and you say good things. The higher, the better the year.”

“How high?”

“High,” said Ice.

“How high, Dec?”

“As high as you want your year to be,” said Ice’s stepmom, coming in with the last dish and sitting down.

Gabe looked at the yee sang. Then at his chopsticks. Then at Ice, with an expression Ice had seen him wear at a squat rack.

“Okay,” said Gabe.

They stood up, all four of them, round the table, with their chopsticks in the yee sang. Ice’s dad said something in Mandarin, and his stepmom said something back, and they all lifted. Up, and up, the strips of fish and carrot and radish and cracker going into the air in a great colourful shower, everybody saying good things at once. Huat ah! Good health! Prosperity! Ice said good health.

Gabe said “EVERYBODY STAYS,” at the top of his voice, and lifted so high that half a carrot landed on the light fitting.

There was a short silence.

Ice’s dad looked up at the carrot on the light fitting. Then at Gabe. Then, slowly, he started to laugh, a laugh that went all the way down. His stepmom put her hand over her mouth. Gabe stood there with his chopsticks in the air and his face going redder and redder.

“Sorry, uncle,” said Gabe.

“No, no,” said Ice’s dad, wiping his eyes. “No. That’s a good year. That’s a very good year.” He looked up at the carrot again. “We’ll leave it there. For luck.”

They left it there.

They ate for two hours.

Ice’s stepmom kept putting things on Gabe’s plate. Every time he finished something there was something else on it, without his seeing it arrive. He started eating more slowly, out of self-defence, and she just put things on it more slowly. Ice’s dad asked Gabe about Melbourne, and about the gym, and about whether he really did hit things with a hammer all day. Gabe said yes, uncle, and Ice’s dad nodded seriously, as if that were a reasonable job.

Nobody asked about the game. Not directly. Ice’s parents knew what he did. They’d known since the first egg came up in the lift in November and took up the whole of the spare room, and they were used to him being up at strange hours, and to strange things being delivered.

But at about eight o’clock, when they’d moved on to oranges and pineapple tarts, his stepmom looked at Gabe across the table.

“The fish,” she said. “Last month. You stood in front of the small girl.”

Gabe stopped with a tart halfway to his mouth.

Ice stared at her.

“The auntie downstairs at Vivian’s told me,” said his stepmom calmly. “I see her at the market. She showed me on her phone.” She peeled an orange. “You stood in front of the small girl, and the fish bit you instead.”

“Yes, aunty,” said Gabe, very quietly.

His stepmom nodded, and put half the orange on his plate.

“Good,” she said. That was all.

Ice looked at his dad. His dad had gone back to his newspaper, the way he did when something had been said that didn’t need saying again. But he wasn’t reading it. Ice could tell from the angle.

Suzu rang at nine.

He rang on video, from Singapore, from his mum’s kitchen, with the phone propped against something on the table. Behind him the room was small and bright, and there was a plate of oranges on the counter that Ice was fairly sure were the ones from the bag. Suzu looked different at home. Softer round the edges. He sat at the table with his mum beside him, and held the phone so she could hear.

“Happy new year,” he said.

“Happy new year, Suzu,” said Ice’s stepmom, leaning into the camera before Ice could say anything. “Happy new year, Mrs—“ and she said Suzu’s mother’s name, carefully, the way she’d practised it at lunch. Ice hadn’t known she’d been practising.

“Happy new year,” said Suzu’s mum, turning her face toward the voice. “Thank you for the oranges. He told me there were a lot. He said the bag was very heavy.”

“It was very heavy,” said Suzu.

“He told me what’s on your table, too,” said Suzu’s mum. She smiled, in the direction of the phone. “He told me there’s a carrot on your light.”

Gabe put his face in his hands. Ice’s dad laughed again, the deep one.

Suzu didn’t say anything. But the corner of his mouth went up about a millimetre, the way it did. And Ice understood that Suzu had been telling her about it. The whole table, all of it, the way he’d told her about everything since he was small. The fish and the soup and the yee sang and the carrot, so that she could see it.

“You sound happy,” said Suzu’s mum, to the phone. To all of them.

“We are,” said Ice.

Deston rang at ten, from Singapore, over a lot of noise.

“Gong xi fa cai,” he said. “I’ve eaten four dinners. I’m going to die.” A pause. “Gabe. Did you lo hei? Did you do the yee sang?”

“I put a carrot on the light.”

“You what?”

Kotori texted at eleven, from Singapore, from a flat on the fourteenth floor. It wasn’t her new year, but she’d looked it up.

happy new year!!! (lunar!!) my dad says gong xi fa cai. he practised. it was not very good. but he practised!!!

And at midnight, in Malacca, the fireworks started.

They watched them from the balcony on Ice’s side, Ice and Gabe, with the doors open behind them into both flats. Ice’s dad and stepmom were on their own balcony next door, a few feet away round the corner of the building, watching the same sky.

The fireworks went up over the river and the old town and the sea, red and gold, one after another. They were the same colours as the lights in the church, Ice thought, going up the wrong way. Every boom came a second after the light. The whole of Malacca was outside, on balconies and in the streets, and you could hear it, under the bangs. Everybody saying the same thing at once.

Gabe was holding a red envelope.

He’d been holding it since the end of dinner, when Ice’s dad had stood up from the table. He’d given one to Ice, because Ice wasn’t married, and you gave them to people who weren’t married. Then, without saying anything, he’d held out a second one, to Gabe. Gabe had looked at it, and then at Ice’s dad, and hadn’t moved.

“You live here now,” Ice’s dad had said, and put it in his hand.

Gabe had been holding it ever since. He hadn’t opened it. He was turning it over and over in his hands on the balcony, in the light of the fireworks, red and gold.

“Your dad gave me an ang pao,” he said.

“He does that.”

“I’m not his.”

“You live here now,” said Ice.

Gabe looked at the envelope for a long time. A firework went up over the river, a big one, gold, and opened like a flower, and hung there, and came down slowly in sparks.

“Everybody stays,” said Gabe quietly. “That’s what I said. With the yee sang. I didn’t plan it. It just came out.” He turned the envelope over. “I meant it, though.”

“I know you did.”

“The back row,” said Gabe. “And Suzu. And the small girl. And the tall one. And Kei’s lot, and Xav’s lot, and everyone.” He looked out at the fireworks. “Everybody stays. That’s my year. That’s what I want.”

“It’s a good year,” said Ice.

“It’s a very good year,” said Gabe. “Your dad said. There’s a carrot on the light.”

Ice laughed. He leaned on the balcony rail next to one of his oldest friends, in the long home with two front doors, both open. The fireworks went up over Malacca, and round the corner, a few feet away, his parents watched the same sky. He thought he’d never been so happy in his life to be exactly where he was.
