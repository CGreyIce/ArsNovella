Chapter 201

Worth Gabe

3,352 words · 15 min read

Vivian’s figures came at eight on Monday, on the dot, and Deston forwarded them to Dennis at four minutes past, because Dennis read figures the way other people read novels.

[ Messages · Deston → Dennis
Deston: can you look at this
Deston: is it bad
Deston: be honest ]

Dennis looked at it on his phone at the bus stop, in Singapore, in the morning, with the bus late.

It was a very neat document. Vivian made very neat documents. Five pages. The purchase price at the top, five hundred thousand ringgit, the minimum, seventy thousand over the asking. Then everything underneath it, in a column, the way Dennis liked things. The lawyer’s fees. The state consent fee. A stamp duty that was higher than a Malaysian would pay, because Deston wasn’t one. A note in Vivian’s small hand at the bottom: The consent application has been lodged. The state advises three to five months. I have asked them to make it three. They have not replied. I will ask again.

Dennis read it twice. He did the sum in his head, all of it, the whole column. Then he did it again, to be sure.

He wrote back.

[ Messages · Dennis → Deston
Dennis: it’s the worst deal i’ve ever seen
Dennis: you’re paying seventy over asking for a unit worth less than asking, plus a foreigner’s stamp, plus consent, to live across a corridor
Dennis: in every way that matters it’s a terrible investment ]

There was a long pause. The bus came. Dennis got on it.

[ Messages · Deston → Dennis
Deston: so don’t do it? ]

Dennis looked out of the bus window at Singapore going past. At the shophouses and the trees and a woman on a bicycle with a basket full of oranges.

He’d known Deston since he was seventeen. Deston had brought him into the group. Deston was always the one who brought people in.

[ Messages · Dennis → Deston
Dennis: do it
Dennis: i said it was a terrible investment
Dennis: i didn’t say it was a terrible idea ]

He held the purse. That was what he did.

He’d held it in a screen MMO with Deston and Gabe, a long time ago, where he’d been the money one. And now in here. You can lead. I don’t want to lead. Leading is unpaid. I’ll hold the purse. He’d said it in Brackenford in April a year ago, when Moonlight Harvest chartered as a company of nine. He’d meant it. He’d been holding it ever since.

He liked money. He wasn’t ashamed of that. Some people pretended not to care about money, and those were the people who never had any. He liked knowing exactly how much there was, and where it was, and what it was doing. He liked making it do more.

What he’d never told anybody, not even Deston, was that he liked it most when he gave it away.

Not to anybody. He wasn’t stupid. To the right thing. To the thing that was worth it. Gold that sat in a purse was just gold. Gold that went into something that mattered turned into that thing. And then you could see it, every day, being what it had become.

He’d learned that from Dec, if he was honest. Dec, who’d had more money than all of them and never once spent it on anything you could see. And then put a quarter of a million ringgit into a company called Moonlight Harvest Workshop, for friends.

Worth it, Dennis thought, on the bus. That’s the column nobody writes down.

The Hollowgate was on Wednesday.

It was a hall for ten, three days east of the round town, in a hill full of hollow trees. Lv 48. Moonlight Harvest had been working up to it for a fortnight. They’d planned to do it the week Dec went to Masmark, with Dec at the front. Then Dec went to Masmark.

“Gabe can tank,” Dec had said, at the long table.

So Gabe was tanking.

Dennis watched him on the walk east. Three days of it. Gabe was enormous and nervous and talking the whole time. He’d tanked the Thornway on the night Dec left, the whole thing, forty minutes. He’d told the boss about SimpleMMO the entire time and then cried afterward on the steps, and told everybody he hadn’t. He was still glowing about it. He was also, Dennis could see, terrified.

“The Thornway was a forty-four,” said Gabe, on the second night, by the fire. “This is a forty-eight. It’s a hall. It’s got a heart, Kei said. Like the Elm Below. Dec did the Elm Below. With Hyv calling.” He poked the fire. “I haven’t got Hyv. Hyv’s on the road to Masmark. I’ve got Calvin.”

“I’m right here,” said Calvin.

“I know you’re right here, Cal, I’m just saying. You’re a very good Calvin. You’re not Hyv.”

“Nobody’s Hyv,” said Rachael, from the other side of the fire, with her arms folded. “Hyv’s not Hyv any more. Hyv’s in love.”

“Rae.”

“I’m just saying.”

Gabe put his face in his hands.

“I’m not Dec,” he said, through them.

Nobody said anything for a while. Kotori poured tea. Sheng Yang, who never said anything, sharpened his sword. Suzu sat at the edge of the firelight with a clay jar on his knee. It was glowing very faintly through the clay, the way it had glowed since Christmas Eve. His hand flat on the lid.

Deston, beside Dennis, didn’t say anything either. Dennis could see him thinking. Deston was always thinking, in a relaxed sort of way, like a man doing a crossword.

“No,” said Deston, at last. “You’re not Dec. You’re Gabe.”

“That’s worse.”

“It’s not worse,” said Deston. “It’s different.” He looked at the fire. “Dec doesn’t flinch. You flinch a bit. Dec doesn’t talk. You talk the whole time. Dec goes first. You—“ He thought about it. “You go loudly.”

“That’s not a strength, Dest.”

“It is,” said Dennis.

Everybody looked at him.

He hadn’t meant to say anything. He was the purse. He did the numbers and kept his mouth shut, mostly, unless somebody was about to overpay for something. But he’d been thinking about it for three days, watching Gabe on the road, and it had come out.

“It’s a strength,” said Dennis. “In a hall. Everything in a hall goes for the most important thing in the room. That’s how they work. The tank’s job is to be the most important thing in the room.” He shrugged. “Dec does it by being somewhere first. You do it by being loud. Both work. They just work differently.”

Gabe looked at him through his fingers.

“And anyway,” said Dennis, “I’m going to make you look expensive.”

He’d only done it once before, properly. On a road in April a year ago, with five Fen Wolves.

Gild. That was his class. Gilder: one who makes a thing look like more than it is. You pointed your wand at something and said the word, and it looked greater than it was. Shinier. More important. More worth. And everything that saw it looked at it, and only at it. And then the line on the class that he’d never told anybody he loved: Most of the time, it becomes so.

He’d Gilded Gabe on that road, as a joke, really. And five Fen Wolves had ignored everybody else and gone for Gabe. Gabe had said do that every time. And Dennis had said, without thinking, it’s worth Gabe, and then been embarrassed about it for a week.

He’d never done it in a hall. Dec didn’t need it. Dec was already the most important thing in any room he was in. You couldn’t make Dec look like more than he was. There wasn’t any more.

Gabe was different. Gabe looked like what he was, a big friendly man with a hammer and a shield who talked too much. Things in halls looked at Gabe and then looked past him at the healer.

“Gild,” said Dennis, at the door of the Hollowgate, on Wednesday night, with his wand pointed at Gabe’s back.

[ Gild · on: Baer · Grade 74% · He looks like more than he is. ]

And Gabe, who’d been nervous all day, looked down at himself. He didn’t look any different. Not really. His shield was the same and his hammer was the same and his armour had the same dent in the shoulder. But there was something about him. A shine, very faint, at the edges. As if somebody had turned a light on behind him.

“Oh,” said Gabe. “Oh, that’s nice.”

“You look expensive,” said Dennis.

“I feel expensive,” said Gabe. He squared his shoulders. “Okay. Okay okay okay. Okay.” He raised his shield. “Let’s go and be loud.”

The Hollowgate had a heart.

Kei had been right. It was a great hollow tree at the bottom of the hill, a tree so old it had died standing and gone hollow from the roots up, and in the hollow, something lived. A thing like an owl made of bark and dark, the size of a house, with two eyes like lamps that went out and came on again. When it opened them, the hollow lit up gold. When it shut them, it was dark, and it moved in the dark.

It went for Gabe.

Dennis watched it go for Gabe. It came out of the hollow with its wings open, the whole width of the chamber. And it looked at the ten of them standing in its hall, the healers and the scythe and the singer and the scout. And it looked straight past every one of them at the big man with the shining edges and the dented shield. It went for him.

“HELLO,” shouted Gabe, at it. “HELLO. Have you heard of SIMPLEMMO?”

It hit him.

He went back a pace. He’d always gone back a pace. Dec never went back; Gabe always did, a bit, and then came forward again. He went back a pace and came forward again and shouted THERE WAS THIS GUY. It hit him again.

“Rae,” said Calvin, beside Dennis, already moving. “Gabe, Rae. Gabe.”

“I know,” said Rachael. “Triage.” Her hands came up. One who decides who is helped first, and is not sorry. She decided. It was Gabe. It was always going to be Gabe tonight.

[ Baer · 61% → 79% ]

“And I didn’t KNOW,” shouted Gabe, “I didn’t know it was HIM for FIVE YEARS—“

The owl hit him again. He took it. He went back a pace, came forward again. Behind him, everybody else was where they were supposed to be. Because nothing was looking at them. Deston on the count, singing, low and steady. Kotori’s Vigil coming every eighth beat, a little for everyone, whether they needed it or not. Sheng Yang going round the back, two paces, silent, waiting. Suzu with his scythe and his jar at his belt, glowing. Tiger skimming. Maigo with his staff and his sunglasses, saying hold. Calvin calling.

And Dennis at the back, with his wand on Gabe, keeping him gilded.

That was all he did. The whole fight. He didn’t hit anything. He kept the Gild on Gabe. Every time it flickered, he said the word again. Gild. Gild. So that every time the owl opened its lamp eyes and looked round its hollow, the first thing it saw was Gabe, shining, shouting about a phone game.

Most of the time, it becomes so.

At thirty-one minutes, the owl went into the dark and didn’t come out.

Its eyes shut. The hollow went black. And it moved. Dennis could hear it, above them in the hollow tree. A dry rustling, going round. Going round, and round, and stopping.

“Where is it,” said Gabe. For the first time all night, he wasn’t shouting.

“Up,” said Calvin. “It’s gone up. I can’t— Gabe, I can’t see it.”

Nobody could see it. It was dark. Kei had said, about the Elm Below, Hyv called the left heart, and Ice went without turning his head. They didn’t have Hyv. Hyv was on the road to Masmark, four days out, with nine other people and a banner.

Gabe stood in the middle of the hollow in the dark with his shield up. He was shining. Dennis could see him. The Gild still on, a faint light at his edges in the black. The only thing in the whole hollow you could see.

Everything in a hall goes for the most important thing in the room.

“Gabe,” said Dennis.

Gabe didn’t turn round.

“It can see you,” said Dennis. “You’re the only thing it can see. You’re shining. It’s going to come at you. It has to.” He kept his wand up. “You don’t need to see it. It needs to see you.”

Gabe didn’t say anything for a moment. He stood in the dark, shining, with his shield up.

Then he did a thing Dennis had never seen him do. He stopped talking.

He just stood there. Quiet. In the middle of the hollow, in the dark, with the light at his edges. He set his feet. And waited.

The way Dec waited.

The owl came down.

It came out of the black above him with its eyes snapping open, gold, all at once, the whole hollow lit. Its wings out and its claws out, straight down at the shining thing in the middle of the room. And Gabe didn’t go back a pace. He put the shield up into it, square, and took it, the whole weight. And didn’t go back.

“NOW,” said Sheng Yang. The only word he’d said all night.

He was already behind it. Two paces. The way he’d been two paces from Kotori in the Long Watch because he’d promised Gabe. Ender’s Cut. And Suzu’s scythe from the other side, the wide row, and Tiger, and Calvin, and Deston’s song going up. And the owl, with its eyes open and its claws in Gabe’s shield, came apart in the gold light of its own eyes.

[ The Hollowgate · cleared · Moonlight Harvest ]

Not a world first. Nobody’d expected a world first. The Hollowgate had been cleared by forty guilds before them. It said cleared, plainly, in the corner of everybody’s window, the way it did for everybody.

Gabe sat down.

He sat down on the floor of the hollow tree, in the gold light, at nine per cent, with his shield across his knees. He sat there for a long time without saying anything. Then he started to laugh. And then he was crying, at the same time. Rachael was there with her hands up, Triage, deciding, saying you absolute idiot, you absolute idiot, you stood still. And Gabe was saying I stood still, Rae, I STOOD STILL.

Dennis put his wand away.

The Gild was still on Gabe. A faint shine at the edges, in the gold light of the hollow. Dennis let it stay. He didn’t need to keep it up any more. He watched it for a while, fading, slowly. And as it faded, he saw that Gabe didn’t look any less than he had with it on.

Most of the time, it becomes so.

He did the loot on the walk back. He always did the loot.

It was good. A hall for ten at Lv 48, cleared, the heart of an old tree. Gold, and owl-feather, and a lump of something called heartwood that Ina would want and that would sell for a great deal. Dennis did the sums on the first night by the fire, in his head, and then again on paper to be sure. He worked out each person’s share to the copper and wrote it down in the purse ledger in his small neat hand.

Then he looked at his own share for a long time.

It was a lot. More than he’d made from anything since the Elm Below.

He thought about Vivian’s five pages. A column of numbers. The lawyer and the consent and the stamp. Real money, ringgit, not gold. You couldn’t pay a Malaysian state government in owl-feather. But gold went through the Exchange, and the Exchange turned it into money. And money was money.

He didn’t tell anybody. He wasn’t stupid.

He just wrote a second line in the ledger, under his share, in pencil. Pencil, so he could rub it out if he changed his mind. He knew he wouldn’t change his mind. He’d known since the bus.

D’s share → the corridor.

He’d tell Deston when the consent came. Or he wouldn’t. He’d see. He’d just hand him an envelope one day, on the six o’clock bus, and say for the cactus, and refuse to explain.

That was the column nobody wrote down. Worth it. He’d written it down.

Dec’s letter came on the third night, on the walk back, by the fire.

[ Letter · from Ice
dennis
how’s gabe ]

Dennis looked across the fire at Gabe. He was asleep on his back in the heather with his mouth open and his shield across his chest like a blanket. He was snoring. Every so often, in his sleep, he said I stood still.

He wrote back.

[ Letter · from Dennis
expensive ]

He thought about it. Then he added one more line.

[ Letter · from Dennis
worth it ]

The reply came four seconds later, from a city on a great lake a long way south and east.

[ Letter · from Ice
i know
i always knew ]

Dennis looked at it for a long time. Then he put it away. He turned over in the heather with his back to the fire, so nobody would see his face.
