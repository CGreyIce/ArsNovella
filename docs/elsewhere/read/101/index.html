Live from Elsewhere · Volume 4: The Ninth Heron

Chapter 101

Exactly

2,319 words · 10 min read

The note on Hyvred’s phone had said 10,000 on the nineteenth of February. On the fifth of April it said 3,860.

She’d done every one of the sums herself. Arrows, mostly. Forty at a time, sold on the Exchange to people who wanted arrows made by the archer from the mill roof. Subs, which was her job, so that was fine. A little from the bank. Every night she took the number off the top of the note and wrote the new one underneath. The note was very long now. She liked scrolling it. It was the only thing on her phone that only ever went down.

She didn’t take donations for it. She’d been very clear about that.

Chat had found out in March. She’d been streaming in the Hall, selling arrows, and she’d said thirty-eight fifty out loud when the money came in, and then, without thinking, minus. And rinpa had asked minus what? and she’d sat there with her mouth open for a full five seconds. So she’d told them. Not all of it. Just that there was a thing, for her eyes, that cost money, and she was saving for it.

Chat had wanted a bar. Streamers had bars. You put the number up and people helped. She’d said no. Chat had said please. She’d said no again. Moocow_on_ice had said we just want to watch it go down. And that, it turned out, she couldn’t say no to. She knew exactly how they felt.

So there was a bar now, in the corner of her stream, and it said:

[ A PIECE AT A TIME
US$6,140 / 10,000
(subs and arrows only. don’t.) ]

Nobody did. Her chat was good like that. It was the best chat in the world, and she told it so most nights.

The clinic was on the eleventh floor of a hospital in Singapore, at nine in the morning, and she was early.

She was always early to places like this. She’d been to a lot of them. You got early, and you found a chair at the edge of the room where nobody would come at you from the side, and you sat. And you listened, because through a tube, listening was faster than looking.

In the tube: a reception desk. A nurse’s hands. A poster of an eye, cut in half, which she thought was a bit much for a waiting room. Her own shoes.

Everything else was sound. A child somewhere to the left. A man coughing. The squeak of somebody’s trolley. And a voice, very quiet, behind her and to the right, reading something out loud in Mandarin. The careful way you read to somebody who couldn’t.

Her phone rang. She knew who it was. She’d known it would ring at exactly nine, because it was eleven in Bee’s world and Bee had said I’ll ring at eleven and Bee always did what she said.

“I’M FINE,” said Hyvred, before Bee could say anything.

“I didn’t ask.”

“You were going to.”

“I was going to ask if you’d found a chair at the edge.”

“…I found a chair at the edge,” said Hyvred, and laughed. Somebody in the waiting room turned to look, probably. She was loud. She’d always been loud. Bee said she laughed like a door banging.

Behind her and to the right, the quiet voice reading Mandarin stopped.

“Hyv?” it said, in English.

She turned her head. That was the thing about the tube. You had to turn your whole head and point it, like a torch, until whatever you were looking for came into it. She swung it slowly round to the right, past a pillar, past a water cooler, past a pair of knees.

A young man with a long careful face, sitting beside an older woman, holding a letter. Looking at her as if he’d known her voice before he’d known anything else.

She’d never seen his face in her life. She knew him at once.

“Suzu?”

They sat together. Of course they did.

Suzu moved so she could sit on the end of the row, with the wall on her left, without her asking. She noticed. He didn’t say anything about it. He just did it, the way he did things in the game. Quietly, before anyone else saw they needed doing.

His mother was small and sat very straight. She held Hyvred’s hand when Suzu introduced them. She didn’t shake it. She held it in both of hers and turned her face toward Hyvred’s voice, and her eyes were looking somewhere just past Hyvred’s ear.

“You’re the one with the bow,” said Suzu’s mother, in English, carefully. “He told me. On the roof.”

“The mill roof,” said Hyvred. “That was a long time ago.”

“He says you see further than anybody.”

“In there,” said Hyvred. “In there I do.”

Suzu’s mother patted her hand. “Then you’ll see further out here too,” she said. “Soon. Both of us.”

Hyvred didn’t know what to say to that, so for once she didn’t say anything.

They called Suzu’s mother first. Suzu went in with her, holding her arm, with the letter. Hyvred sat on her own at the end of the row with the wall on her left, and looked at the half-eye poster through her tube. She didn’t wonder how Suzu was paying for it. She knew to the dollar what it cost, and she had a rough idea what a person earned working for Ice. She’d heard Suzu say two per cent on Ice’s stream once, at a rope over a gorge, and Ice had laughed, and neither of them had explained.

She didn’t think about that for very long. It was none of her business. Whatever it was, it was theirs.

Then they called her name.

The doctor was kind and quick. Hyvred liked her. She did tests Hyvred had done a hundred times, and some she’d never done, with a machine that hummed and a light that went somewhere behind her eyes. Then she sat back and said the word.

“Eligible.”

Hyvred sat very still.

“You’re a good candidate,” said the doctor. “Better than most. The pathway’s intact. That’s the part we need.” She explained it. Hyvred heard about half. Behind the ear. Glasses with a camera. It goes past the eyes. She knew all that. She’d known it since February. She’d known it since the first night in the game, really, when she’d lain down in an egg and seen the whole ceiling for the first time. The Cradle doesn’t use your eyes. It sends you the seeing.

“When can we schedule?” said the doctor. “There’s a list, but the list moves once the payment clears. A few weeks, usually.”

Hyvred looked at her through the tube.

“I’m doing it a piece at a time,” she said.

The doctor looked at her for a moment. Then she nodded, the way people did when they’d heard that answer before and respected it.

“Then come back when the last piece is in,” she said.

Suzu was waiting for her outside, by the lifts, on his own. His mother was in the bathroom.

“Eligible,” said Hyvred.

“Mum too.”

“Yeah?”

“Yeah.” He didn’t smile much, Suzu. But he did something with his face that was the same thing. “Surgery’s next week. Maybe.”

“Next week—“ Hyvred grabbed his arm, which she hadn’t meant to do, and then held on to it, because why not. “Suzu. Next week.”

“Maybe.”

“When will you know?”

“Thursday.” He looked at her. At her hand on his arm. At her, pointing her whole head at him like a torch so she could see his face. “When’s yours?”

“When the last piece is in,” said Hyvred.

Suzu didn’t say anything.

She thought afterwards that she should have noticed that. Suzu not saying anything. Suzu always said something. Good, or okay, or hm. He looked at her for a long moment, at the end of a corridor on the eleventh floor of a hospital. Then his mother came out of the bathroom and he went to get her, and he didn’t say anything at all.

She streamed that night from the Hall of Roads.

The expedition was still out on the north moor, Kei and Higu and Gigi and the rest. So the Hall was quiet, just her and Bee and the gate and Ama Corrie at the counter. She sat at the bottom of the eye’s stair with her bow across her knees and a heap of arrow shafts beside her, fletching. Forty. She’d sell them at the end.

“The thing went well,” said Hyvred. She held up a finished arrow and squinted down it, though she didn’t need to in here. Habit. “The thing said eligible.”

Chat went completely, utterly mad.

She let it. She sat and fletched and laughed while it went past, faster than anyone could read. She didn’t need to read it. She’d always read chat the way you’d listen to the sea. Bee, beside her, drawing something in her book, didn’t look up. But Bee’s wings buzzed once, very fast, which was what they did when Bee was happy and didn’t want to say so.

She sold the forty arrows at the end of the night. Thirty-nine fifty. The bar in the corner of her stream ticked over.

She got her phone out, out of habit, and took the number off the top of the note.

3,821.

On Wednesday night it happened.

She was on the stair to the eye, halfway up, sitting on a step with her back against the wall, watching the seven roads. She did it every evening now. Since the writ. Since Ama Corrie had given her the key and said you’ve got the eyes for it, and the knees. All seven roads at once, out of the seven doors, going away in seven directions to the edge of the world. She’d never in her life seen seven of anything at once until she came in here. She could have sat on that step forever.

She was talking to chat about nothing. About the Nightjar, who’d gone quiet. About the roof, which Chris said he could fix if somebody bought him enough lead. About the expedition, which had been gone eight days and which Kei said, in a message, would be home tomorrow with something for Ama Corrie that you won’t believe.

And the alert went off.

It had a sound. A little chime, two notes, the one every streamer’s alert made. She’d heard it ten thousand times. She didn’t even look at it, usually. Her chat said thank you for her when she missed one, because they were the best chat in the world.

The chime went, and her chat stopped.

That was what she noticed first. Not the alert. The chat. Six thousand people, and all at once, nobody was typing.

She swung her head round, slowly, the way she did on Earth, though in here she didn’t have to. In the corner of her stream, over the bar, the alert was sitting there.

No message.

Under it, the bar.

Hyvred sat on the step on the stair to the eye of the dome, with seven roads going away in front of her, and didn’t move.

To the dollar.

Three thousand eight hundred and twenty-one. Not three thousand eight hundred. Not four thousand, which was what a person would give if they were being generous. Three thousand eight hundred and twenty-one dollars. The number at the top of the note on her phone. Somebody had been watching. Somebody had read the bar and done the sum and given exactly that, not a dollar over. As if they’d known that if it was a dollar over, she’d send it back.

There was one person in the world who’d offered. On the nineteenth of February, on the phone, the morning the news came. Then we save, Bee had said. We. And Hyvred had said I.

And Bee had said then I’ll just be really annoying about it every day, and Bee always did what she said. Bee had asked what’s the number every night for forty-seven nights. Hyvred had told her, every time.

Bee was in the Hall below her. Bee had gone quiet an hour ago, drawing.

Hyvred stood up on the stair. Her hands were shaking. She noticed that from a long way off, the way you’d notice weather.

“I have to go,” she said to chat.

“I’m fine,” said Hyvred. She wasn’t. She didn’t know what she was. “I’m fine. I’ll be back. Don’t— I’m fine.”

She turned the stream off.

Then she stood on the step for a long time in the quiet. She looked down the stair at the Hall below, where the lamps were, and the banner on its old hooks, and her best friend somewhere under it with a pencil.

“Bee,” she said.
