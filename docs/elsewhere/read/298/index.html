Live from Elsewhere · Volume 12: Even

Chapter 298

The Netloft Door

3,336 words · 15 min read

Rafi had been a door for a year, and on Friday night fifty-nine people came through him at once.

He saw them coming from the top of the lane. The netloft was at the bottom of it, a tall narrow wooden building on the quay with nets hanging off every floor. It smelled of fish and tar and the lake. He’d been standing in its door since last August, since Wendy gave it to him with her hands out of her pockets. Everybody who comes to the door. That was the job. You stood in it and you asked them, and you sat on the step with them after. Kite had written to Wendy that that was the bit people forgot.

He hadn’t forgotten it once.

They came down the lane in the dusk, out of the crater road, in threes and fours and pairs. Kei at the front with his map. Gabe and a girl Rafi didn’t know at no paces. Leaka, loud, before Rafi could even see her. A sheep. Fifty-nine people who’d walked fourteen days across half the world to watch one man get hit on a bridge. Rafi’s heart did something he hadn’t felt since the first week, in a fen, with a sack.

He stood in the door with his big black sword on his back and his hands at his sides, where people could see them.

“Who’d stand next to you?” said Rafi.

He asked every one of them. Fifty-nine.

It took nearly two hours. Nobody minded. Kei said everybody behind me, and nine who stayed. Gabe said Rae and the girl said Gabe and Rafi said you’re Rae? and she said yes, and he said fair. Kite said Drew and Ash. Drew said Kite and Ash and somebody on a bridge. Ash, very calmly, said Kite, Drew, and everybody whose road I held, and him, if he says yes. And then, after a moment, and a crossroads.

“That’s five,” said Rafi.

“I’ve been practising,” said Ash.

Rafi looked at her. Calm and narrow, two blades, no bird on her collar. He knew who she was. Everybody did. He’d been on the beach at the bottom of the Crown Below, a year ago last March, when Nightjar was already four people and then one. He’d heard what she’d done. He’d heard what Kite had done, before.

He’d been a boy from Surabaya picking fenwort in a fen for two copper a plant when the man with the shield came past. He’d never once been anybody anyone was frightened of. He’d been afraid of people like her for most of the first year.

“Fair,” said Rafi.

Ash looked at him for a long time. Then she sat down on the step of the netloft. Not going in. Just sitting down, a sword’s length off, the way Kite sat.

“Is this the bit people forget?” she said.

“Yes,” said Rafi. And sat down beside her. Because it was.

Boss came last but one. PenguChi was last, with the sheep, at the back, the way she always was. And Boss was in front of her, with Hyvred holding his arm, coming down the lane in the dark with his shield on his back.

Rafi stood up.

He’d called the man boss since the first day. In a fen, two copper a plant, four hundred plants. I’ll show you which ones. It was the first money he’d ever made anywhere. And the first time anybody had looked at him and decided he was worth something before he’d done anything at all. The man had never once told him to stop calling him boss. Rafi didn’t think he ever would. He’d asked him once, at the long table, why he didn’t mind. And Boss had thought about it properly and said because you mean it.

“Boss,” said Rafi.

“Rafi,” said Boss.

“Who’d stand next to you?”

Boss stopped in the lane. He thought about it. Even now. Even after fourteen days and a gate with a boy in it who’d asked him the same thing.

“Everybody,” he said. “And her. And the ones who stayed.”

“You said that to Tobi,” said Rafi.

“How do you know?”

“Kei wrote,” said Rafi. “Kei writes me everything. So I know who’s coming to the door.” He grinned. “It’s still true?”

“It’s still true,” said Boss.

Hyvred, on his arm, looked at Rafi edge to edge with an expression he didn’t know.

“You’re a good door,” she said.

Nobody had ever told him that except Boss. Rafi went red right up to his ears, in the dark, in the door, with fifty-seven people inside behind him being loud.

The netloft was a hall for ten. Sixty is not a guild, young man, the Rim Hall clerk had said last year, when Boss went to register it. Sixty is a wedding. Fifty-nine wouldn’t fit in a hall for ten. They didn’t try.

They slept everywhere. The netloft’s three floors, among the hanging nets. The cellar, which smelled of fish and breathed, where Sketch had fought Boss in May last year and lost. Riverbank’s rooms at the top, which they gave up without being asked and slept on the quay instead. The fish man’s boats, tied up along the quay. He came down at the third dawn with his nets and found Leaka and Curse asleep in one of them. He stood looking at them for a long time, and then went and fished from another one without waking them.

And the quay itself. The long stone quay along the netloft’s front, by the water, under the stars. People in rows on the warm stone, wrapped in coats, with the lake making small noises against the wall below them.

Rafi stood in the door all night and watched them.

He didn’t sleep. A door didn’t sleep. That was another bit people forgot.

Kite came and stood with him at the third dawn of the Saturday. Not in the door. Beside it, against the wall of the netloft, with his bow unstrung across his shoulders and his hands at his sides.

Rafi had never met him. Not properly. He knew him from letters. Kite had written to Wendy, and Wendy had read them out on the netloft’s step. Ask. And then you sit on the steps with them after. That’s the bit people forget. Rafi had said it to himself every night for a year.

“You’re the door,” said Kite.

“You’re the gate,” said Rafi.

They stood there for a while. Two people who’d spent a year asking strangers the same question in two places a fortnight apart. Neither of them had ever asked it of the other.

“Who’d stand next to you?” said Rafi, at last. Because it would have been strange not to.

“Drew and Ash,” said Kite. “And a boy called Tobi, in my gate, while I’m here.” He looked up the lane. “And whoever comes up the steps.” He was quiet. “You?”

“Everybody who comes to the door,” said Rafi.

Kite looked at him sideways.

“That’s a job,” he said.

“Wendy said that too.”

“Wendy’s right,” said Kite. “It’s a good job. It’s the best one I’ve ever had.” He looked at his hands, at his sides. “But it’s not an answer. You have to have an answer for you too. Or you’re just a door.”

“I am a door.”

“I know,” said Kite. “So was I. For a year. In a gate.” He was quiet. “And then I sat with a woman at a milestone for ten nights. And on the longest night, at a crossroads, she asked me back. And I had to find one.” He pushed himself off the wall. “Somebody’ll ask you back. When they do, have one.”

He went in. Rafi stood in the door and thought about it all morning.

Riverbank went home on the Saturday morning. Up to their rooms at the top of the netloft, the four of them, for the first time since April. Xav stood in the door of his room for a long time looking at the wall, which was covered in drawings. A year and a half of them, pinned up one over another. Ice, mostly. Ice against a wall, Ice with a shield, Ice’s face. Never quite right.

Rafi watched him from the stair. Xav took one down, the oldest, from the corner. He looked at it for a long time. Then he pinned something new in the middle of the wall, over all of them. A man sitting cross-legged on a floor with a scythe across his knees, smiling. Drawn fast, and right.

“New wall,” said Xav. Not to anybody. And went to sleep.

The letter came on the Saturday, at the third dawn. By hand. Not folded small, like most of Sable Court’s. Folded once, black wax, silver thread. Carried down the lane by a tall pale woman in black with silver at the cuff and a slate under her arm.

“For the tank,” said Ves.

“Boss is asleep,” said Rafi.

“I know,” said Ves. “Lucan said he would be. After fourteen days.” She held it out. “That’s why it says what it says.”

Rafi took it. Ves didn’t go. She stood in the lane in front of the netloft door and looked at him, very patiently.

“Who’d stand next to you?” said Rafi. Because she’d come to the door.

Ves thought about it.

“Him,” she said. “Lucan. On the bridge.” She looked at the slate under her arm. “I count.”

“That’s an answer,” said Rafi.

“He gave it to me first,” said Ves. “Last month. I’m just giving it back.” And went back up the lane in the grey light toward the east rim, with the slate.

Boss read it on the quay at the second dusk. Rafi read it over his shoulder, because Boss let him. Hyvred read it over the other one with her chin on it.

Boss read it twice.

“Not today,” said Hyvred. “He said not today. That’s Ash’s.”

“Everybody’s got everybody’s,” said Boss.

He was smiling. Not the small one. Rafi knew all of Boss’s smiles; he’d been watching them for two and a half years. This was the one from the bridge, last July, on the stream, when Lucan stood still for the first time and Boss saw it. Tif had clipped it. LOOK. Four million.

“A week,” said Hyvred. “He’s giving you a week.”

“He’s being on time,” said Boss.

Rafi looked at him.

“In July last year he was early,” said Boss. “For everything. He’d have fought me tonight, at the end of fourteen days, and called it fair, because he was there first.” He folded the letter. “Now he’s waiting till I’m ready. So it’s properly.” He looked at the lake. “That’s new.”

“Is it good?” said Rafi.

Boss thought about it.

“It’s very good,” he said. “It means he’s worked out what he’s been learning.”

The Ninefold came to the netloft door on the Monday.

Rafi saw them come down the lane at the second dusk. He knew who they were before they got close. Everybody did, since Saltreach. Seven of them, in the sea-blue they wore, from the southern isles, with short spears. They came down the lane in a tight wedge, two by two and one in front. And the one in front was tall and quiet, and stood the way Kestrel had said he stood. Like a man who’d already decided everything.

Teo.

He stopped in front of the netloft door. The six stopped behind him. They looked at Rafi in the door, with his big black sword and his hands at his sides.

“Who’d stand next to you?” said Rafi.

Teo looked at him for a long time.

Rafi had asked the question perhaps four thousand times in a year. He knew all the ways people heard it. Some laughed. Some got angry. Some went very quiet and looked at the floor and said nobody, and those were the ones who came back. He’d never once seen anybody hear it the way Teo did. As if it were a word in a language he’d learned the alphabet of and never once spoken.

“Eight,” said Teo.

Rafi looked at the six behind him.

“There’s six,” said Rafi.

“Two fell at Saltreach,” said Teo. “At the turn. They came back. They’re on the south coast.” He was quiet. “They stand next to me.”

“That’s an answer,” said Rafi.

“We never take a tenth,” said Teo. “We never have. Nine. Since the start.” He looked past Rafi into the netloft, where fifty-nine people were eating the fish man’s fish. “I came to ask somebody something.”

“Who?”

“The one who sat down,” said Teo.

Rafi didn’t let him in.

Not because he wasn’t allowed. Because it wasn’t the netloft’s to decide. He went inside and found Suzu. Suzu was asleep, sitting up, on the bottom step of the stair to the cellar, with his scythe across his knees and Kotori’s empty cup in his lap. Rafi stood over him and didn’t know whether to wake him.

“I’m awake,” said Suzu, without opening his eyes.

“Somebody at the door,” said Rafi. “The Ninefold. Teo.”

Suzu opened his eyes about a third.

“Is he asking?”

“He says he came to ask somebody something,” said Rafi. “The one who sat down.”

Suzu thought about it. Rafi watched him do it. Like a cat deciding whether to get up.

“Sunday,” said Suzu. And shut his eyes.

Rafi went back to the door.

“Sunday,” he said to Teo.

Teo looked at him.

“It’s Monday,” said Teo.

“He fights on Sundays,” said Rafi. “It’s on Kei’s board. Suzu · the ring · Sundays.” He thought about it. “It says in Masmark too, if anybody asks.” He paused. “You’re asking.”

“Sunday,” said Teo slowly. “Six days.”

“After the bridge,” said Rafi. “The day after.”

Teo stood in the lane for a long time. Rafi could see him not liking it. Six days. The Ninefold had never once waited six days for anything in their lives. They’d cleared every door in the south before anybody else got there. Kestrel said they’d decided everything before they came in.

“Rest first,” said Rafi. He didn’t know why he said it. It came out. “Somebody said. Kestrel. Lucan’s doing it too.”

Teo looked at him.

Then, very slowly, as if it were costing him something, the tall quiet man from the southern isles sat down. On the step of the netloft. A sword’s length off from the door. Where Ash had sat on Friday, and Kite always did. With his short spear across his knees.

Behind him, after a moment, the six sat down too. In the lane. In a row.

“Is this the bit people forget?” said Teo.

“Yes,” said Rafi.

On the Tuesday night, late, Boss came and stood in the door with him.

Not to ask anything. Just to stand there. Rafi moved over, the small way Kite did, to make room. The netloft door was only one shield wide. They stood in it shoulder to shoulder, looking up the lane at nothing, the way Gabe and Rae stood in the north door at home on quiet nights.

“Rafi,” said Boss.

“Yes, Boss.”

“Who’d stand next to you?”

Rafi stared at him.

Nobody had ever asked him. In a year. Four thousand times he’d asked it, at this door, of everybody who came down the lane. Nobody had ever once turned it round. They answered, and went in, or sat on the step, or went away. He was the door. You didn’t ask the door.

“Everybody who comes to the door,” said Rafi. Because that was what he’d told Wendy.

“That’s a job,” said Boss. “Wendy said.”

Rafi looked at the lane.

He thought about it properly. The way Boss did. He thought about a fen and a sack and four hundred plants, and a man with a shield who’d decided he was worth something before he’d done anything. About the beach at the bottom of the Crown Below, and the Open Hand at a bread stall saying come and be somebody. About the Undercliffs, and a red ship, and Boss asked him properly. A year of a door.

“You,” said Rafi. Very quietly. “Boss. You.”

Boss didn’t say anything for a while.

“That’s an answer,” he said.

“It’s the only one I’ve got.”

“It’s a good one,” said Boss. He was quiet. “I’ve got a lot of answers. I’ve been practising.” He looked up the lane. “You’re in most of them.”

Rafi stood in the netloft door beside him in the dark and found he couldn’t see the lane properly. He didn’t mind. A door didn’t have to see. It just had to be there.

“Boss,” he said, after a long time.

“Yes.”

“Saturday. On the bridge.” He swallowed. “Can I stand at the front? On the terrace. Not to watch. Just to be at the front.”

Boss thought about it.

“You’re a door,” he said. “Doors are at the front.”
