Live from Elsewhere · Volume 12: Even

Chapter 296

o7

3,373 words · 15 min read

Ash had never been east of the White Hills, because Nightjar had never needed her to be.

Nightjar worked the west. The roads out of Fernwick and Millrace and the fen country, the crossroads on the moor, the drove roads. Places where travellers came on foot at dusk, tired, not looking. That was where the money was. Nobody in Nightjar had ever said let’s go east. East was cities. East was Masmark and the big guilds and four hundred Halls. East was people who looked.

So on the fifth day of the walk, when the road came down out of the White Hills onto the long flat plain and she saw the river, Ash stopped.

It was wide. Wider than anything she’d seen in the game. Brown and slow in the sun, going south. And on the near bank, a hut. A pole leaning on it. An old man sitting outside on a bench, with his feet up, watching fifty-nine people and a sheep come down the road toward him.

And a goose, which had walked behind them for three days, and nobody could make go home.

“The ferry,” said Kite, beside her.

Drew, on her other side, said nothing. He was watching the old man’s feet.

She’d heard about the ferry. Everybody had.

The ferryman’s door. Every traveller who’d crossed to the round town and come back had stopped on this bank and cut a mark into his door with his knife. A small one. o7. Two letters, a salute, the kind people did with their hands. Aurelie had told the long table about it. She’d cut one in September and another in January. Vane had cut his first one, next to hers, not under. The swamp guy had five.

Ash looked at the door as they came up to the hut.

It was covered. Every board. Little o7s cut into the grey wood in rows, hundreds of them, thousands, from the top of the door to the bottom. Some old and soft at the edges, some so new the wood was pale in the cut. Down the frame. Onto the wall of the hut beside it.

“There’s more since January,” said the swamp guy, from somewhere behind her. “A lot more.”

“Longest night,” said the ferryman. He’d taken his feet off the bench. “All the Halls from this side went across in December to stand on somebody’s road. They cut one each on the way home.” He looked at the fifty-nine. At the sheep, and the goose. His face didn’t change. “You’re going the wrong way for it.”

“We’re going to Masmark,” said Kei.

“I know where you’re going,” said the ferryman. “Everybody knows where you’re going. The bridge.” He spat in the river. “Half the east road’s going. I’ve been rowing since Tuesday.”

He got up. He took his pole. He looked at them again.

“Fifty-nine,” he said. “And livestock. Four trips.” He went down to the boat. “Don’t stand up in it.”

She went across on the third trip. With Kite and Drew, three, the way they went everywhere now. And the swamp guy, and Hyvred holding his arm, and PenguChi and the sheep. The goose had refused the first two trips and had to be lifted in by Gabe, who came back across specially, and got bitten.

The river went under them, wide and brown. The ferryman leaned on his pole and didn’t speak.

Ash sat in the bottom of the boat with her two blades across her knees and looked at the water.

She’d crossed rivers before. On bridges, on fords, on stepping stones in a fen. Never on a boat. You didn’t take boats, in Nightjar. Boats were slow and open and anybody on the bank could see who was in them. You crossed where nobody could see you cross. She’d never once in two and a half years crossed anything where people could see.

Everybody could see her now. Fifty-nine people, and a ferryman, and the east bank coming, with half the east road on it waiting their turn.

She didn’t mind.

That was the strange thing. She sat in the bottom of the boat in the middle of a river in the sun with everybody looking, and she didn’t mind at all.

On the east bank, she stopped at the door.

There was a door on this side too. She hadn’t known. A smaller hut, the ferryman’s other one, for the nights he stayed east. Its door had marks on it too. Fewer. The people who’d come from the east to the round town and gone home again.

The ferryman was tying up the boat. He looked at her standing in front of the door.

“You cut one?” he said.

“No.”

“Ever?”

“No,” said Ash.

He looked at her for a long time. At her calm narrow face, and her two blades, and the place on her collar where there’d been a bird and wasn’t now. Kite had cut the last four threads of it off with his skinning knife on the south steps, in December, on the dawn after the longest night.

“There’s no rule,” said the ferryman. “Some people cut one every time. Some people never do.” He held out his knife, handle first. “It’s for whoever’s been across and back. That’s all it means.”

“I haven’t been back,” said Ash. “I’m going.”

“You’ll come back,” said the ferryman. “Everybody comes back.” He was still holding out the knife. “And you’ve been across before. Just not on my boat.”

Ash looked at him.

“From the west to the round town,” said the ferryman. “Last spring. Across the moor at dusk, at crossroads, on your own.” He looked at the river. “That’s across. Even if nobody rowed you.”

She didn’t ask how he knew. Ferrymen knew things. It was the river.

She took the knife.

She’d held a lot of blades. Two of her own, every day, for two and a half years. She’d used them on people at crossroads at dusk. Fourteen people’s days, Kite had counted once, of his own. She’d never counted hers. She’d never wanted to know.

She’d never once used a blade to make a mark that wasn’t on somebody.

She cut it small. At the bottom of the door, in a corner, where nobody would look. o7. Her hand was steadier than she’d thought it would be. The wood was soft and grey and took the cut easily, as though it had been waiting for one there.

She gave the knife back.

“Thank you,” said Ash.

“It’s a door,” said the ferryman. “It doesn’t mind.”

Kite cut one beside hers. Then Drew. Three in the corner of the east door, small, in a row. Not under. Next to.

Ash looked at them for a long time.

“I didn’t know you’d never cut one,” said Kite.

“You never asked.”

“I’m asking now,” said Kite. “Why not?”

Ash thought about it.

“Because I never went anywhere and came back,” she said. “I went somewhere and stayed. Seven months at a milestone.” She looked at the three marks. “Nobody cuts one for that.”

“Somebody should,” said Drew.

He didn’t say things, mostly. He watched feet. But he’d said it, and Ash looked at him, and he looked back with his ordinary unbothered face.

“There isn’t a mark for staying,” said Ash.

“Then make one,” said Drew.

She thought about that for three days.

They walked east across the long plain. White road, green fields, villages with dogs that barked at the sheep and then at the goose and then gave up. Fifty-nine people strung out along a road in the sun, in threes and fours and pairs, talking or not. PenguChi at the back. Hyvred holding the swamp guy’s arm at the front, near Kei. Suzu asleep while walking, which Ash hadn’t known was possible.

She walked with Kite and Drew. Kite on her left. Drew on her right, a pace off, watching everybody’s feet. Three.

On the seventh day she asked Drew.

They were walking in the heat of the afternoon, out there two in the morning, in here the long white middle of a day on the plain. Drew was a pace off on her right, the way he always was, watching the road ahead of everybody’s feet. She’d never once heard him start a conversation.

“Who’d stand next to you?” said Ash.

She’d asked him on the longest night, at the corner, with the dark coming. He’d said you, and then I’m the one who stays. She asked it again now, on a white road in the sun, because things changed and you had to keep asking.

Drew thought about it.

“You,” he said. “And Kite.” He was quiet for a few paces. “And on the bridge, Lucan.”

Ash looked at him.

“He’s fighting the swamp guy,” she said. “Not you.”

“I know,” said Drew. “I’ll be on the terrace.” He watched the road. “But I’ve stood next to him before. On the shingle, all night, after the Crown Below. He’d lost. Second. To us.” He was quiet. “He sat on the shingle and didn’t move for six hours. First time in his life, he said. Not moving. I sat next to him.” He looked at his own feet. “Nobody’d ever sat next to him. They went round him. He’s a river.”

“And you sat.”

“I’m the one who stays,” said Drew. Simply. It was the truest thing about him, the way lazy was the truest thing about Suzu. “So on the bridge, I’ll be on the terrace. Not watching the swamp guy. Watching his feet.” He almost smiled. “If he falls over, somebody should see him get up.”

Ash didn’t say anything for a long time.

“Kestrel’s watching him too,” she said. “She wrote. Not the swamp guy. Him.”

“I know,” said Drew. “That’s two.” He looked at her. “Three, if you come and stand on the terrace with me.”

“I’ll come,” said Ash.

“Good,” said Drew. Which was Suzu’s. Everybody had it now.

On the eighth day, at dusk, they came to a crossroads.

She knew it before she saw it. She smelled it. The particular smell of a crossroads on a plain at dusk in July: dust and cut hay and four roads going away into the evening. A stone at the corner, not standing, lying on its side in the grass. A signpost with three arms and one broken off.

She stopped in the middle of it.

Kite stopped with her. Drew stopped a pace off. Behind them, the walk came up and slowed and began to go round them. Fifty-nine people and a sheep and a goose, going round three people standing in the middle of a crossroads at dusk. The way you’d go round a goose.

“Ash,” said Kite.

“I’ve been here,” said Ash.

Kite didn’t say anything.

“Not here,” said Ash. “Not this one. I’ve never been east.” She was looking at the four roads going away. “But one like it. A hundred like it. In the west.” She swallowed. “At dusk. Waiting.”

The swamp guy had stopped too. At the edge of the crossroads, with Hyvred on his arm. He didn’t come closer. He just stood there, the way he’d stood at the corner of the drove road on the longest night, a long way off, on the stream. Ash had seen it afterwards.

“I took a day at a crossroads like this,” said Ash. “The first winter. A girl. On her own, walking. To the round town, she said. I asked her where she was going and she told me.” She was quiet. “I never asked anybody where they were going again. I didn’t want to know.”

Kite didn’t say anything. He’d taken fourteen. He knew what it was.

“I never chose a road,” said Ash. “At a crossroads. Not once. I just waited till somebody came, and then the money told me.” She looked at the four roads. “I liked them because you had to choose. And I never once did.”

“You did,” said Kite. “On the longest night. At the corner. You chose the drove road both ways.”

“That was holding,” said Ash. “Not choosing. I stood still and held it.”

“That’s choosing,” said Kite.

She looked at the four roads for a long time.

East, to Masmark. The way they were going. Fifty-nine people, a bridge, a fight.

West, back. The ferry, the White Hills, the goose’s bend, the round town. A gate with a boy in it, asking. A long table. A Roll with three letters on it under Kite’s empty line. Ash.

North and south, she didn’t know. She’d never been east. She didn’t know where they went.

That was the thing, she thought. In the west she’d always known where every road went. That was what Nightjar had been for: knowing the roads, and who’d be on them, and when. She’d stood at a hundred crossroads and never chosen, because she always knew where every road went. So there was nothing to choose. Just waiting.

Here, she didn’t know.

She looked at Kite on her left, and Drew a pace off on her right. At the walk going round them, fifty-nine people who’d chosen to walk fourteen days to watch one of their own get hit on a bridge. At the swamp guy at the edge of the crossroads, with Hyvred on his arm, waiting. Not deciding anything for her. Just there.

“East,” said Ash.

Out loud. In the middle of the crossroads. To nobody. To the four roads.

She hadn’t known she was going to say it until she did. It was the first road she’d ever chosen, at a crossroads, at dusk, out loud. She didn’t know where it went, except Masmark. She didn’t know what was on it. She’d chosen it anyway.

“East,” agreed Kite.

“East,” said Drew.

She cut a mark before she left.

Not on the signpost. On the stone at the corner, lying on its side in the grass. She knelt down beside it with one of her own blades. The first time she’d ever used one of them for anything but a person. She cut it small, low on the stone, on the side facing the road east.

Not o7. That was for across and back.

She’d thought about it for three days. Since the ferry, since Drew said then make one. A mark for staying. For choosing a road and going down it, and not waiting for anybody to tell you. She didn’t know what it should look like. Nobody had ever made one.

She cut a short line. Then another, beside it, the same length. Two lines, side by side, standing up. Next to each other.

She sat back on her heels and looked at it.

“What is it?” said Kite.

“I don’t know yet,” said Ash. “It’s a road. And somebody on it.” She looked at the two lines. “Or two people. Next to each other.” She stood up. “It’s for staying. And choosing. At the same time.”

The swamp guy had come across the crossroads, at last, with Hyvred. He stood over the stone and looked down at the two lines for a long time.

“That’s the one,” he said.

Ash looked at him.

“Not today,” said the swamp guy. “You said it on the longest night, in front of the dark. And then you said it meant stay.” He was looking at the mark. “That’s what it looks like.”

Ash didn’t say anything for a while.

“It doesn’t mean stay,” she said, at last. “Not any more.” She put her blade away. “It means stay, and then go. Choose a road. Go down it. Come back.” She looked at the four roads, and then at the walk, which had stopped going round them and was waiting. “That’s what you do. Isn’t it. All of you. You go somewhere. And you come back. And then you go again.”

“Yes,” said the swamp guy.

“I never did that,” said Ash. “I only ever did one.”

“You’re doing both now,” said Hyvred. Beside him, with her chin on his shoulder, looking at Ash edge to edge. “I can see it.”

They camped two fields east of the crossroads that night. Dennis counted them in. Sixty: fifty-nine and the goose. He ticked himself last and the goose after that.

Ash sat at the edge of the firelight with Kite and Drew and didn’t sleep for a long time.

She thought about the girl. On her own, walking, at a crossroads in the west, in the first winter. To the round town, she’d said. Ash had taken her day. She’d come back the next night, the way everybody did, twenty-four hours later. Ash had never seen her again. She didn’t know if the girl had ever got to the round town. She didn’t know her name. She’d never asked.

She’d never once, in two and a half years, wanted to know.

She wanted to know now.

“Kite,” said Ash.

He was half asleep. He opened his eyes.

“When we get home,” said Ash. “Will you look in the Roll? With me. For a girl who came to the round town the first winter. On her own. From the west. Who’d have lost a day at a crossroads on the way.” She swallowed. “I don’t know her name.”

Kite looked at her for a long time in the firelight.

“Ama Corrie will know,” he said. “She knows everybody who ever came through a door.”

“Will she tell me?”

“If you ask,” said Kite. “Properly.”

Ash lay down by the fire, between Kite and Drew, with her blades beside her. Three. She looked up at the sky over the long plain, going dark, with edges.

She’d ask. When they got home. Properly.

It was the second road she’d ever chosen. She chose it lying down.
