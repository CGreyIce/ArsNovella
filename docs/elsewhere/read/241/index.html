Live from Elsewhere · Volume 10: The Middle

Chapter 241

All Three

4,160 words · 18 min read

Xav had three of them now, and a page, and a man who said practising the river on him was like being haunted.

“It’s like being haunted,” said RX, on the stair down to the Seven Below, for the fourth time that week. “You’re always there. Before I’ve moved. Wherever I’m going to be, you’re already there, and then you’re not, and then you’re somewhere else. For six weeks. On the road. At every inn.” He sat down on the step above the loose one. “I dream about it.”

“It’s Lucan’s,” said Xav.

“I know it’s Lucan’s. It’s on Tif’s clip. Four million people know it’s Lucan’s.” RX put his face in his hands. “It’s Lucan’s and Dec borrowed it and now you’ve borrowed it and you’re doing it at me.”

“Somebody has to be the river,” said Malcolm, on the step below, with his stick across his knees. “Or he can’t practise being the rock.”

“I’m not the rock. I’m the riverbed.”

Malcolm thought about it.

“That’s fair,” he said.

This was his fourth fight with Dec. October a year ago and January, here; May in the netloft’s cellar in Masmark, which smelled of fish and breathed. And now here again. The Seven Below didn’t breathe. It was round and pale and very still, with seven silver lines in the floor meeting in a ring in the middle. Seven tunnels going out of it into the dark under the round town. And in one of them, the north-east, there was still a mark on the wall where his right blade had gone in January, under a Falling Cut, a long way.

He stood in the ring with his blades out and down and waited.

He’d brought three things.

Val’s page, inside his coat, flat against his chest. Dashes and dots from the cellar. Then a second page, from the bridge, three kinds of mark. She’d sent it from Masmark in August with a note that said keep it, I’ve got another one. He’d read it every night since. He couldn’t draw it. But he could read it, now. Like a script.

A sketchbook. A new one, mostly empty, because there was nothing in the last six weeks he’d been able to draw.

And RX, haunted.

He didn’t need anything else.

The step was fuller than he’d thought it would be.

RX and Malcolm. Gabe, much too big for the step, because Gabe had said in January that he was never missing one of these and meant it. Hyvred, on the bottom step, with her knees up and her tail round her feet, not calling. He’d asked her not to, this time. I want to hear it, he’d said. Not have it said. And she’d looked at him and said fair in a way that meant she’d been told what fair meant by a girl with two different eyes.

And Vane.

Xav hadn’t asked him. He’d come to the hatch at the third dawn, tall and still, and stood behind Ama Corrie’s counter looking down the stair. Not asking. Xav had looked at him for a long time.

“You were there,” he’d said. “In the war.”

“Two years.”

“You saw the rain.”

“Every day,” said Vane.

“Sit at the top,” said Xav. “Don’t come down.”

Vane sat at the top.

Dec came down at twenty past eight with his shield on his back and the lamp in his hand. Ama Corrie said again and Dec said again, and she said mind the third step, and he minded it.

At the bottom, he put his hand up to the light at his shoulder.

“Not for them,” said RX, under his breath, from the riverbed.

Dec walked out and stopped ten paces off. Lamp down by the wall. Shield up, sword down.

“Whenever,” said Xav.

“Whenever,” said Dec.

And Xav didn’t move.

He’d decided that on the road. Somewhere past the second ferry, with RX asleep at the inn and the goose sitting in the road outside in the dark. He’d sat on the inn step with Val’s page and read it again, and understood a thing he’d been circling for a year.

Every time, Dec went first.

In October, Dec went first and Xav answered. In January, Dec went first and Xav went with him. In the cellar, Dec went first and Xav was in the rests, before. But still after Dec. Always after. Even when he was early, he was early for something Dec had started.

Lucan had been early his whole life. And on the bridge, Dec had gone first anyway, and made him answer for twenty-seven minutes.

So Xav wasn’t going to be early. Or with. Or before.

He was just going to stand there.

It went on a long time. Longer than January’s minute and forty. Two minutes. Three. The two of them in the silver light in the ring, ten paces apart, not moving. The room so quiet Xav could hear the stream in the north tunnel a quarter of a mile off, running.

He didn’t decide anything. He’d learned that from himself, teaching Kestrel, in a ring in a market with a pigeon. He held the whole room in his head and didn’t pick.

At four minutes and some seconds, Dec’s weight went forward.

The step began. The back foot waited.

And Xav didn’t go with it.

He let the step come. He let the back foot come round, left. He let Dec come all the way in, inside the distance, close enough that the shield rim was a hand from his shoulder. And still didn’t move.

The shield rim stopped.

Dec stopped. A pace away. Looking at him.

Xav had never seen that. In nine years. Dec, inside the distance, with the step finished and nothing to answer it. A man who’d gone first his whole life, standing in front of somebody who hadn’t done anything at all.

“Oh,” said Dec. Very quietly.

Xav cut him.

Not fast. Not clever. Just there, the left blade, under the rim, along the side, where Dec had come in to and stopped. Because Dec had come all the way in to meet something, and there’d been nothing there to meet, and for half a breath he was standing in front of Xav with his weight forward and nowhere to put it.

“One,” said RX.

Dec stepped back.

He looked at his side. Then at Xav. And something happened to his face that Xav had seen twice. Once in October, at three minutes forty, there you are. Once in January, at fourteen minutes.

This was different. Xav didn’t know what it was. It looked, for a moment, like a man who’d opened a door he’d opened every day for years and found a different room behind it.

And then the river came.

He’d known it would. He’d watched it on Tif’s clip a hundred times. The bridge, the first seven minutes, Dec moving like Lucan, a breath ahead. Round and round. Taps, not hits. He’s telling me.

It came now. All at once. The rock that had stood in the middle of every fight for a year and a half, moving. Weight left, weight right, head turning, hands loose, never in the same place twice. Round him. A breath ahead.

Six weeks of RX.

Xav went with it.

Not ahead. Not behind. With. He’d spent six weeks on the road being the river at RX, every inn, every evening, until RX dreamt about it. He knew what it felt like from inside. And when Dec went round him, a breath ahead, Xav went round too. The same way. The same breath. Two rivers, on a floor with seven silver lines on it, going round each other in the same direction at the same speed, so that nobody was ahead at all.

Nobody’s ever done it back, Lucan had said, Dec had told him. On a bridge, in July, with his sword just given back to him. Nobody’s ever learned anything off me.

Xav had learned it off Dec, who’d learned it off Lucan.

He did it back.

“Two,” said RX. A tap, on Xav’s shoulder, from the rim. “Three.” Xav’s, on Dec’s thigh. “Four.” Dec’s. “Five, six—“

They were trading. That was new too. In every fight before, Dec had made Xav pay for everything, and given nothing away. Now they were going round each other, a breath ahead of nobody, and the hits came both ways. Little ones. Taps and touches and lines. Like two people talking very fast in a language only they spoke.

“Eleven,” said RX, at nineteen minutes. His voice had gone up. “That’s— that’s eleven, that’s more than January already, that’s—“

“Count,” said Malcolm.

“I’m counting.”

On the bottom step, Hyvred had both hands round her knees and wasn’t breathing.

At the top of the stair, Vane hadn’t moved at all.

The rain came at twenty-two minutes.

Xav felt it before it came. That was what six weeks and two pages bought him. Not being ready for it; nobody was ready for it. Knowing it was coming. The river under Dec went tight, the way water goes tight at the lip of a weir. And then it wasn’t a river. It was the cellar.

Every beat. Sword, rim, step, sword. No rests. Rain on a tin roof.

He’d lasted fifty-one seconds in the cellar.

He didn’t try to answer it this time. He’d answered it in May; answering was how you went backward, shff-shff-shff, against the wall. And he couldn’t live in the spaces between, the way Lucan had for eight minutes. Lucan had had twenty people for a month. Xav had had RX at an inn.

So he did the only thing left. The thing Val’s page said, at the bottom, under the place where the pencil had gone through. In her small careful hand.

He reads the line fast. Don’t read it with him. Read the one after.

He didn’t go to the beat that was coming. He went to the one after it.

It cost him. He’d known it would.

Every beat he skipped, the one he wasn’t in, landed. The rim on his shoulder. The sword along his arm. He let them. Because while they landed he was already a beat further on. In the place Dec would be next, not now. And when Dec got there, Xav was waiting.

He was trading two for one. Then three for one. It was the worst trade he’d ever made in his life and he was laughing. He could hear himself, in the middle of the rain, laughing. Because he was in it. Not answering it. Not going backward. In it, a beat ahead of it, getting hit, and hitting back.

“Fifteen,” said RX. Something in his voice cracked right across. “Sixteen. Xav, that’s— sixteen—“

And then the rain stopped.

He didn’t go into the next beat.

That was the thing. That was what he’d watched on Tif’s clip a hundred times, the bridge at eighteen minutes, Lucan in the air. Mid-turn, round a sword that wasn’t there, early for a beat that didn’t come. And the slow sword from the floor, visible the whole way. And Lucan couldn’t do anything.

Xav had been reading a beat ahead. Which meant that when the rain stopped, he was already where the next one would have been.

He stopped there too.

Dead. Planted. In the middle of the Seven Below, in the silver light, a pace from Dec, with his blades low. The way Dec was standing. Wide. Heavy. Down into the stone.

The two of them. Still.

Nobody on the step made a sound.

Xav could see Dec’s feet. He’d drawn them two hundred times. He’d never seen them like this, set wide, the weight gone all the way down, so still the dust on the floor of the Seven Below round them didn’t move. Salty’s feet. He’d watched Salty stand like that on the longest night, and step out one pace, and the whole dark had leaned back.

He waited for the sword to come round from the floor.

It didn’t.

Dec was looking at him.

He’s waiting for me to go, thought Xav.

That was what the weight was. Not a blow. A blow was just the end of it. The weight was the waiting. Heavy, down, all the way. So long that the other person couldn’t bear it, and went. And the moment they went, the sword was already coming round from the floor to where they were going. Slow, obvious. And there was nothing they could do.

So he didn’t go.

He stood there. Heavy. Down. Not deciding anything. Holding the whole room in his head, the way he’d taught Kestrel, the way he’d been taught by a pigeon.

A minute.

Two.

He could hear the stream in the north tunnel. He could hear RX not breathing. He could hear his own heart, very loud, in the pod, out there, in Japan, at an hour of the night he’d stopped noticing.

Three minutes.

Dec didn’t move.

Four.

He can do this all night, thought Xav, and knew it was true. He did it for a year and a half on a stone in a fen with things coming up out of the water at him. He did it at the bottom of a lake for forty minutes with nineteen people in his lee. He did it on the floor here, last Christmas, with a lake standing up. He can wait longer than anybody alive.

That’s the first one, he thought. That’s the fen. Under all of it. Under the river and the rain and the weight. That’s what was always there.

He’d forced all three out of him. The river, the rain, the weight. And under the weight, at the bottom of everything, there was the first one. The waiting.

Nobody could out-wait him. Nobody ever had.

Xav didn’t try.

He went.

The sword came round from the floor.

Slow. He saw it the whole way, from the moment Dec’s hips moved. It came round to where Xav was going, and Xav was going there anyway. He’d decided, for once, the one time in the fight he decided anything. He went in to meet it, both blades crossed, and it hit them.

BOOM.

He went across the Seven Below on his back.

The way he’d gone in October, a long time ago, on an Undertow. Across the floor, over the silver lines, all the way to the wall between the north and north-east tunnels. And lay there, looking at the plain stone ceiling.

He lay there and laughed.

Dec came across the floor. Not fast. He always went slowly, after. He stood over Xav at the wall and looked down at him. Breathing very hard.

Nine, thought Xav. He’s at nine. Nobody had ever had Dec at nine. Not Lucan. Not a crown at the bottom of a lake.

“Yield?” said Dec.

“How many?” said Xav, to the step. To RX.

RX’s voice came from a long way off.

“Nineteen,” said RX. “Nineteen. In fifty-two minutes. Nineteen.” He was crying. Xav could hear it. “October was three. January nine. May fourteen. Nineteen.”

Xav lay on his back by the wall and looked up at Dec.

“Yield,” he said.

Dec didn’t take the sword away, because there wasn’t one at Xav’s neck. He’d never put it there. He just stood over him, breathing.

And then he sat down. On the floor of the Seven Below, beside Xav, with his back against the wall. At nine per cent, with his shield flat beside him, the way he’d sat beside Hyvred at the bottom of a lake.

They sat there for a long time. Xav at three. Dec at nine. Side by side against the wall between two tunnels, in the silver light.

“You didn’t go first,” said Dec.

“No.”

“At the start. I came all the way in, and you weren’t there.” He was quiet. “I didn’t know what to do.”

“I know,” said Xav. “I saw your face.”

“And the river,” said Dec. “You did it back.”

“I learned it off you. You learned it off Lucan.” Xav was still laughing a bit, in his chest. “RX says it’s like being haunted.”

“And the rain. You read the one after.”

“Val’s page,” said Xav. He put his hand on his coat, where it was. “She wrote it at the bottom. Don’t read it with him. Read the one after.”

“And the weight,” said Dec. “You stopped. You didn’t go into the next beat. Lucan went into the next beat.”

“I watched the bridge a hundred times.”

Dec didn’t say anything for a while.

“And then I waited,” he said. “And you waited. Four minutes.”

“And then I went,” said Xav.

“Why?”

Xav thought about it. He looked at the ceiling.

“Because under all three of them,” he said, “there’s the first one. The fen. The waiting. That’s the bottom of you. Everything else is on top of it.” He turned his head and looked at Dec. “Nobody can out-wait you. Nobody ever will. I knew that, sitting there. So I didn’t try.” He shrugged, lying down. “I went. Because I wanted to see it come round. From close.”

Dec looked at him for a very long time.

Xav had known him nine years. Since a poly classroom, since seventeen. He’d chased him the whole time. Not like RX, half a breath behind on a rhythm game. Differently. Xav had never wanted to beat him, exactly. He’d wanted to be the one who made him work. I’d make him work, he’d said, a long time ago, to somebody who asked. And Dec had said, somewhere else, to somebody else, he’d give me a run for my money.

Xav had heard that. Second-hand. Through Gabe, who’d told everybody. He’d kept it for a long time.

Dec had never said anything to his face. Not about the fights. Closer, in January. Yes. That was the most he’d ever said. Dec didn’t say things. He said okay and good and fair and stood at the front.

“Xav,” said Dec.

“Mm.”

“You’re the best I’ve ever fought.”

Xav didn’t say anything.

He couldn’t. He lay on his back on the floor of the Seven Below at three per cent and looked at the ceiling. And didn’t say anything at all, for long enough that RX on the step said Xav? in a small voice.

“Not the most,” said Dec. He was looking at the floor in front of him, not at Xav. Saying it plainly. Out loud. The way he’d said I like you too at a gate. “Lucan was faster. The crown was bigger. The war had more of them.” He was quiet. “You’re the best. Nobody’s ever made me use all three. Nobody’s ever made me go back to the first one to finish.” He turned his head and looked at Xav. “Nobody’s ever made me sit down after.”

Xav put his arm over his eyes.

“Dec,” he said, from under it.

“I should have said it in October,” said Dec. “I should have said it at poly. I didn’t know I was allowed.” He was quiet. “I’m saying it now. You’re the best I’ve ever fought. And you’re getting better every time. And I’ve never once minded you chasing me.” He paused. “I like it. I’ve always liked it. It’s the best part.”

Xav lay there with his arm over his eyes and didn’t move for a long time.

Then he laughed. Under his arm, on the floor. The laugh from October, from January, from the cellar. Bigger than all of them. It went round the Seven Below and down all seven tunnels and came back. And it set off RX, who was already crying, and Gabe, who was crying louder. And Malcolm, who said oh, for God’s sake and put his face in his hands. And Hyvred, on the bottom step, who had her knees up and her face hidden in them and was making the kettle noise.

At the top of the stair, Vane said nothing at all. But when Xav took his arm off his eyes and looked up at the hatch, Vane was looking down at the two of them against the wall. And his face was doing something.

“Again,” said Xav, eventually. Still lying down.

“January,” said Dec.

“January.” Xav looked at the ceiling. “And Dec.”

“Mm.”

“Don’t learn a fifth one.”

“There were three,” said Dec. “And the first one. That’s four.”

“Don’t learn a fifth one,” said Xav. “You’ll learn one by accident. Off a goose.”

“The goose sits down now,” said Dec. “For two.”

Xav put his arm back over his eyes and laughed until his three per cent felt like one.

He drew, that night. At the Hart, at the corner table, with the new sketchbook open and Val’s page beside it.

He didn’t draw feet. He’d stopped being able to draw it in May. You couldn’t draw a tempo; it wasn’t where, it was when. He drew something else instead.

Two people sitting on a floor against a wall. Side by side. One with a shield flat beside him. One lying down with his arm over his eyes. Seven lines on the floor going out to seven dark mouths. And above them, at the top of a stair, very small, a tall still man looking down.

It was the first thing he’d drawn in six weeks.

RX came and looked at it over his shoulder. He didn’t say anything for a long time. Then he went to the bar and got two cups of the Hart’s terrible tea and put one down in front of Xav without a word.

“It’s what you do,” said RX. “When somebody’s chasing.”

“I know,” said Xav.

“You’ve stopped chasing,” said RX. “Haven’t you.”

Xav looked at the drawing for a long time.

“No,” he said. “But I know he likes it.” He drank the tea. It was terrible. “That’s different.”
