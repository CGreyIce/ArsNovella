Live from Elsewhere · Volume 12: Even

Chapter 301

Where He Is

3,435 words · 15 min read

Lucan didn’t look up, and he knew exactly where everybody was.

Ves on the parapet at the east end of the bridge with her slate. Drew on the north terrace at the rail, between Ash and Kite, a sword’s length off each. Kestrel at the end of the rail, a little apart. The boy from the netloft door at the very front of the north terrace, because he was a door. Sable Court on the south wall. Meridian on the west, with Aurelie in a carried chair. The Ninefold on a balcony, seven, very still. Half of Masmark on the terraces of both walls of the gorge, all the way up, and the stream going down between them to the lake.

And the tank’s girl on the north terrace beside the boy, at the front, holding the rail with both hands.

He didn’t look at any of them. He knew where they were because he’d been told, and because he could hear them. A sound like a crowd before a final. Drew had said that, under the bridge. I’ve heard it a hundred times. I’ve never once heard it for a friend.

He stood at the west end of the bridge over the third stream at the second dusk with his sword, and looked at the planks.

He’d slept in the Hall till the first dusk, the way Kestrel said you should. We rest first. Sable Court’s Hall on the north rim: tall, narrow, black stone with silver in the joints, a long table nobody sat at because Lucan had never once been still long enough to sit at it. He’d sat at it today. Ves had brought him bread and the fish man’s fish, which the fish man had sent up specially in a basket with a note that said eat it.

He’d eaten it.

At the second dusk he’d gone down through the city to the bridge. Not the quick way, over the roofs, the way he went everywhere. The slow way. Through the streets. Past people who’d come from every region of the map to sit on a terrace and watch him get hit. They moved out of his way and looked at him and didn’t say anything, mostly. Some of them did. Good luck. You’ll be early. Don’t fall over. A child on a doorstep said are you the one who falls over? and he said yes, and the child said good, and he didn’t know why.

He’d been famous in two games for being first at things. He’d never once been famous for falling over.

He found he liked it better.

He thought about the wind game, walking down. He didn’t usually.

A bridge over a gorge, there too. A board with four hundred names above his when he started, and then three. And a tank in the top twenty, the only tank on the first page, who stood in the same place every morning, not moving. Lucan had gone round him every morning for a year. Fast and early and somewhere else. And never once moved him.

He hadn’t known, then, that it was the same man. He’d found out on this bridge last July, when the tank stood still and the river went past him like water past a stone. A long time ago, he’d said, afterwards, in the netloft. I don’t remember how most of them ended. He’d lied. He remembered all of them. He’d lost every one.

He’d never once been on time for any of them.

It was the same bridge. It always would be.

Old dark wood, wide enough for two people to pass. Rails at waist height, the stream going under it fast and loud between the gorge walls. He’d fought the tank on it last July and lost. He’d stood still on it for the first time in his life, at the end, and watched the tank smile. He’d fallen over on it four hundred and fourteen times since March. Then not, once, in June. Then not for thirty-two days.

He’d never once fought on it on time.

He walked out onto it. To the middle. He didn’t hurry. He’d hurried for everything his whole life, and he’d learned, on this bridge, with Ves hitting him in the ear with a slate, that you couldn’t be on time and hurry. You could only be one.

At the middle, he stopped.

He looked at the planks. At his own feet on them. Flat. Pointing at the east end of the bridge, where the tank would come from.

Not turned. Not ready to be anywhere else.

The crowd on the terraces made a noise. He didn’t look up.

The tank came out onto the east end of the bridge.

Lucan didn’t look at the terraces, but he looked at the tank. That was the whole of it. You looked at where the other person was. Not where they’d be. Not a breath ahead. Where they were.

He was at the east end of the bridge. Walking. Not fast. The way he always walked, as if there were plenty of time and he’d decided to use all of it. Shield on his arm, sword low. Plain clothes, nothing silver, nothing that caught the light. He looked the same as he had last July. He looked the same as he had in the wind game, a long time ago, on a bridge over a gorge in another world. A tank in the top twenty who stood in the same place every morning, not moving, while Lucan went round him.

He came out to the middle and stopped three paces off.

Lucan looked at his feet.

Pointing at him. The way they’d pointed at him on the wet stones under the bridge last night, with Drew watching. Not at the far end of the room. Not at a door. At Lucan.

Lucan looked up from the tank’s feet to his face.

He was smiling. Not the small one. All of it. The one from last July, at the end, when Lucan stood still.

“You’re on time,” said the tank.

“I’m on time,” said Lucan.

“Properly?”

“Properly.”

Somewhere, Ves said begin. Lucan didn’t know who’d asked her to. He thought, afterwards, that probably nobody had. She’d just decided it was time.

The tank went first.

He always did. Lucan had known he would. He’d thought about it for eleven months, on this bridge, falling over. He moves before you’ve decided. He chooses every distance and every exchange. You only ever answer. Kestrel had said it. A boss. It always goes first.

He came in with the river.

Lucan’s river. That was the first thing. The thing Lucan had been his whole life, which the tank had learned off him from a terrace in a month. A breath ahead of where Lucan was going to be. Already there before Lucan moved, and then not, and then somewhere else.

It was the most beautiful thing Lucan had ever seen. He’d seen it last July, in this place, coming at him, and been too busy to look. He’d been early for it, trying to get round it, trying to be a breath ahead of a man who was a breath ahead of him. Like two rivers trying to get to the same place first.

He wasn’t early for it now.

He didn’t try to get round it. He didn’t move to where the tank would be. He looked at where the tank was. Three paces off. Two. And then, as the river came round on his left, close, already where Lucan should have been going to, there.

Lucan wasn’t going anywhere. He was standing where he’d been standing. He put his sword where the tank was.

Tock.

The tank’s sword met it. Stopped. For one breath the two blades rested against each other in the middle of the bridge, and neither of them had moved past the other.

The terraces made a noise like a wave coming in.

The tank’s face did something. The small sudden one, the one Lucan had heard about from a man with two blades who drew it in sketchbooks and never got it right. The face of somebody seeing something he hadn’t seen before.

“Oh,” said the tank. Very quietly. Pleased.

It went on like that for twenty minutes.

Lucan would remember all of it. Every exchange. He’d never been able to remember fights before; they’d been a blur of being early, of being somewhere else, of you’re slow. This was different. This was like reading a page slowly for the first time, a page he’d only ever skimmed.

The tank went first. Every time. Lucan let him.

That was the strangest part. He’d spent his whole life making sure nobody went first against him. Now he stood in the middle of the bridge and let the tank choose every distance, every exchange. The river at him from the left, from the right, close, far. And every time, he looked at where the tank was, and put his sword there. Tock.

Not every time. He wasn’t good enough for every time. The tank was the best he’d ever seen and he’d been on time for thirty-two days. Maybe one in three. The others came through.

He learned, in those twenty minutes, where it went wrong. Every time. It was never the tank. It was always him. Every cut that came through came through because, for one breath, he’d started looking at where the tank would be instead of where he was. The old way, coming back without asking, the way a hand goes back to a pocket. A breath ahead. And the tank was never where Lucan looked. He was always where Lucan had stopped looking.

That was what Drew had said, under the bridge, about his feet. In a fight they’ve never once told me what he’ll do. Only what he’s done.

So Lucan stopped trying to know what he’d do. He looked at what he’d done. Where he’d got to, now, this breath. And put his sword there.

From the parapet at the east end, very quietly, so that only Lucan could hear it over the stream, Ves was counting. Not falls. Hits. Four, him. One, you. Five. One. He didn’t look at her. He listened to the numbers the way Fenn listened to the bell before.

But one in three, he was there. And when he was there, tock, the tank’s blade stopped against his for one breath. And in that breath Lucan’s other hand was free, and the tank was close.

He’d never once had his free hand when the tank was close. Last July the tank had been close the whole fight. A breath ahead, and Lucan always just behind, never with anything left over.

He had something left over now.

He put the pommel of his sword into the tank’s shield arm. Short, from no distance, the way a man with a scythe had put the butt of the snath into Sketch’s ribs in a round pale room in the west.

The terraces went up.

Lucan didn’t hear most of it. He heard one voice over all the others, from the north terrace, at the front, high and loud and furious.

“THAT’S ONE,” screamed the tank’s girl. “THAT’S ONE. HE HIT YOU. GET HIM BACK.”

Lucan didn’t look up. But he heard the tank laugh, three paces off, in the middle of the bridge. Out loud, short, completely surprised. The way, Lucan had heard, the man with the scythe had laughed on the floor of the Seven Below with a boot on his snath.

At about six minutes, the tank went far.

Lucan felt it before he understood it, the way he felt everything now: by looking at where things were. The tank stepped back. Not away. Back, along the bridge, toward the east end. Five paces. Six. Until there was a long stretch of empty planks between them, and the stream loud under it, and nothing in it.

Everything in Lucan said close it.

That was what he did. That was what he’d always done. When somebody made distance, you were across it before they’d finished making it. A breath ahead. You arrived at the far end of the gap before the gap knew it was there. It was the thing he was best at in the world. It was why he’d been fourth in the wind game and first to the fiftieth level in this one.

He’d done it last July, on this bridge, when the tank stepped back. He’d gone across the gap like water. And arrived at the far end of it, a breath early, at a place where the tank was going to be, and the tank hadn’t been there. The tank had been standing still in the middle of the gap, planted, with his sword back, low and long.

BOOM.

He didn’t close it now.

He stood where he was, in the middle of the bridge, and looked at the tank. Six paces off. Not at where the tank would be if Lucan crossed the gap. At where he was. Standing at the far end of six paces of empty planks, with his shield up and his sword low. Waiting to see if Lucan would come.

Lucan didn’t come.

The tank looked at him across the six paces for a long time. Then he came back. Walking, the way he always walked, as if there were plenty of time. All the way back to three paces off. And stopped.

“You didn’t come,” said the tank.

“You wanted me to,” said Lucan. “Last July I came.”

“I know.” The tank’s face was doing the small sudden thing again. “I was going to do the same thing.”

“I know,” said Lucan. “That’s why I didn’t.”

From the north terrace, the tank’s girl screamed something. Lucan didn’t hear what. He heard the shape of it, loud and furious and delighted, and he felt his head start to turn toward it. Toward the north rail, toward the voice. The old way. To see what she’d seen, a breath ahead.

He didn’t let it turn. He looked at where the tank was.

From the parapet, under her breath, so only he could hear, Ves said: didn’t look up.

At about ten minutes, the tank stopped going first.

Lucan felt it before he understood it. The river stopped. The tank stood three paces off in the middle of the bridge with his shield up and his sword low, and didn’t move.

The fen.

Lucan had heard about it. Everybody had. The first thing the tank had ever had, before the river and the rain and the weight. Out-waiting. Standing still, in a fen, in the first week of the game, until a thing came at you because it couldn’t stand not to. Nobody can out-wait him, Sketch had said. Nobody’s ever out-waited him.

Last July, Lucan hadn’t been able to stand still at all. He’d have gone. Of course he’d have gone. The tank had stood still on the bridge and Lucan had been in the air before he’d thought about it, mid-turn into a beat that never came.

He didn’t go now.

He stood still too. In the middle of the bridge, three paces off, on time. For nothing.

It was the hardest thing he’d ever done. Harder than the slow one, harder than four hundred and fourteen falls. Everything in him said move, you’re late, he’s going to decide where you are. And he let it say it, and didn’t. He looked at where the tank was. Standing still, three paces off. He stood still too. The way he’d stood on this bridge alone in June for three minutes before he started the slow one and didn’t fall.

One minute.

Two.

The terraces had gone completely silent. Half of Masmark not breathing. He could hear the stream under the bridge, and the gulls over the gorge, and nothing else.

Three.

He didn’t look up. He knew where Kestrel was. At the end of the north rail, a little apart. Watching him. He knew without looking.

Four minutes.

He thought about Kestrel, at the end of the rail. I’ve never once seen you learn something. I want to see what that looks like. When it’s done. He thought that this was probably what it looked like. A man standing still on a bridge for no reason, with half of Masmark holding its breath, not looking up. It didn’t look like anything. That was the point.

Four minutes and a half.

The tank smiled.

Lucan saw it. All of it, the whole face, three paces off. And then the tank lowered his sword. Not into anything. Just a few inches, the way you’d put down something heavy you’d been holding out for somebody who hadn’t come to take it.

“Nobody’s done that,” said the tank. Quietly. Only for Lucan. “Stood still with me. In a fight.” He was quiet. “Xav did it once. In January. At one per cent. Not at the start.”

“I’m on time,” said Lucan.

“For nothing?”

“For nothing,” said Lucan. “I practised it. On this bridge. In June.”

The tank looked at him for a long time.

“Good,” he said.

And then he went again. First. Because he always would. He was never going to be the one who stood there. And this time it wasn’t the river.

It was the rain.
