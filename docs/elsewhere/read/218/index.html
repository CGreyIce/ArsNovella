Live from Elsewhere · Volume 9: Properly

Chapter 218

Honest

3,770 words · 16 min read

Chernn had heard about the corridor for a year and a half, and she’d never seen it.

She’d heard about it on calls, late, from a tall man who talked to everybody and could talk to anyone and went quiet when he talked about a corridor. It’s long. There’s a window at the end. His door’s the last one. And opposite, there’s a unit with a sticker on it. She’d heard about the sticker until she could have drawn it. Red and white, faded pink. DIJUAL. A phone number nobody rang.

She’d seen photos. A white empty room. A balcony door gone grey with dust. A pot by a door with a very small cactus in it.

She’d never been.

On Saturday the seventeenth of June she took the bus to Malacca with Deston, and saw it.

The bus was four hours. He’d done it so many times he knew where every bump in the road was. He told her about them before they came. There’s one after the toll. And a big one at the turn-off. She’d laughed at him and then the bus had gone over the one after the toll, exactly where he said, and she’d stopped laughing and looked at him.

“How many times have you done this?”

He thought about it, the way he thought about everything, properly.

“I stopped counting at forty,” he said.

He didn’t sleep on the bus. She’d thought he would. He looked tired; he’d been up late all week, though he wouldn’t say why. But he sat by the window with his hands on his knees and watched the road go past. Johor, the trees, the long straight bits. When she fell asleep against his shoulder somewhere after the turn-off, he didn’t move. She woke at Melaka Sentral with his arm round her and his face doing the thing it did when he thought she couldn’t see.

The corridor was long.

That was the first thing. He’d said so, but she hadn’t understood how long. You came out of the lift and it went away from you, under strip lights, door after door, all the way to a window at the far end with the afternoon behind it. Malacca, white and hot, and the sea somewhere past it.

She stood outside the lift and looked at it.

“Go on,” said Deston.

She walked. He walked beside her, not saying anything, which for him was a lot. Past the other doors, past somebody’s shoes on a rack and somebody’s plants and somebody’s bicycle. To the end. To the window. And on the left, the last door, with a mat outside it that said nothing at all. On the right, opposite, a grille, slid open. A door with two locks. And beside it, two cacti in two pots.

She stopped between them.

“His,” said Deston, nodding left. “And his parents’, past that. It’s all one home inside. They opened the wall.” He nodded right. “Mine.”

Mine. He said it very carefully, the way he’d said you can sign the Roll on a fell in the wind. As if it might break.

She looked at the door on the right for a long time.

The auntie found them before they’d got the door open.

Chernn heard her before she saw her. Slippers, coming fast down the long corridor from the lift. A woman in an apron with a plate under a cloth, who stopped two doors short and looked at Chernn over the top of her glasses. The same way, Chernn would think afterward, that Ama Corrie looked at people over her spectacles. As if she were deciding which page of the book to put you on.

“This one,” said the auntie, to Deston. “The girlfriend ah?”

“Yes, auntie.”

The auntie looked at Chernn for a long time.

“Too thin also,” she said. “Both of you too thin. Singapore food.” She held out the plate. “Eat.”

Chernn took it. Under the cloth: kueh, the green kind, still warm.

“Thank you, auntie,” she said.

The auntie looked at her for a moment longer. Then at Deston, red to the ears. Then at the unit with its grille slid open and its two locks.

“Last week, the other one,” said the auntie. “The girl with the bag. For the quiet one at the end.” She jerked her head at the last door. “Now you. Aiyo. Everybody coming.” She sniffed. “Good. Too quiet, this corridor. Twenty-two months, nobody.”

She went away down the corridor in her slippers, still talking, to nobody in particular.

Chernn looked at Deston.

“She knows about Hyvred,” she said.

“Everybody knows about Hyvred,” said Deston. “Gabe told everybody. Within an hour.” He was trying not to laugh. “He’d kept it twenty-two days, and then the lift doors closed and it came out of him like a cork. He rang me. I was in the room. He rang me anyway.”

He opened the door with the first key.

Inside, a room. White walls. Not empty now: a mattress against one wall, a folding chair, a kettle on the counter with two cups. A flat-pack bed, finished, in the bedroom, with one part left over that nobody could find a place for. A box from Singapore, still taped. And on the wall opposite the door, the first thing you saw coming in, a frame.

The sticker.

She’d heard about it so many times. She’d heard him say I’m going to put it in a frame on a call in April, with Gabe crying in the background. She hadn’t believed him. Not really. Nobody framed a sticker.

He’d framed the sticker.

DIJUAL. Faded pink, with a rip down one corner where his thumbnail had gone under it. Behind glass, in plain black. And at the bottom, a little brass plate: NINETEEN MONTHS. TWENTY-TWO.

She stood in the doorway and looked at it.

Then she laughed. Out loud, in the doorway of a unit in Malacca, with a plate of the auntie’s kueh in her hands. She couldn’t stop. It wasn’t that it was funny. It was funny. But it was more that it was exactly him. A man who’d count how long a sticker had been on a door, because it was how long nobody lived there, and want to remember. And put it on the wall where you’d see it every time you came home. So you’d always know how long the door had waited before somebody came.

“You’re ridiculous,” she said.

“Everybody says that.”

“It’s a sticker.”

“It’s not a sticker,” said Deston. He was looking at her, not at the frame. “Gabe said that too. It’s how long.” He paused. “I wanted you to see how long.”

The cacti were outside the door, where they’d always been.

He’d told her about them too. The very small one, from January. He’d put it in the empty pot by the door the week Vivian sent the consent form, when the dead plant went in the bin. Everybody had said it would die. It hadn’t. It was still very small. It had grown, he said, about the width of a fingernail in five months, and leaned a little toward the window at the end of the corridor.

And the bigger one, in the better pot. From a nursery in Singapore. Carried on his knee on the six o’clock bus for four hours, in April, the night he peeled the sticker off.

For her. When she comes. One’s hers. She’ll choose.

She crouched down in the corridor in front of them.

She looked at them for a long time. The big one was very handsome. Round, green, even, with neat white spines in neat rows, like something on a card. The kind of cactus you’d buy somebody because you wanted them to know you’d thought about it. The pot matched.

The small one leaned toward the window. It had one bent spine and a brown mark near the bottom, where something had once nearly gone wrong with it and then hadn’t. Its pot had a chip in the rim.

“This one,” said Chernn.

Deston looked down at her.

“The small one?”

“It’s honest,” said Chernn.

She heard herself say it. She knew where it came from. A green in a candle village, a long time ago in the game, and her first candle. Crooked, forty-one per cent, a lump near the top where she’d lifted it too fast. And a woman with floury arms holding it up to the window and saying it’s honest. And keeping it.

“It was here,” said Chernn. “The whole time. Since January. Everybody said it would die, and it didn’t. It just stayed by the door and waited.” She touched the pot, the chip in the rim. “The other one’s lovely. But this one waited.”

Deston didn’t say anything for a long time.

When she looked up, his face was doing the thing.

He gave her the second key in the kitchen, later, with the kettle on.

He didn’t make a speech. She’d thought he might; he made speeches, sometimes, when he was nervous, long relaxed ones that made people like him. He didn’t make one. He just took the steel ring out of his pocket, worked the second key off it with his thumbnail, the way he’d got under the corner of the sticker, and put it on the counter beside her cup.

“Not asking,” said Deston. “It’s just a key.”

She looked at it.

“Always two, the agent said,” said Deston. “Spare. For family.” He was looking very hard at the kettle. “I’m not asking you anything. I’m not— it’s not that. I said I wouldn’t ask because there’s a door. You said not because there’s a door. I’m not.” He swallowed. “It’s just so you’ve got one. So the door’s yours too. Whenever. Or never. Never’s allowed.”

She knew where that came from as well. Thea, on the steps of the Hall of Roads, with Gigi. Whenever you want. Or never. Never’s allowed. She’d watched it on a stream, before she ever had a pod.

She picked up the key.

It was warm from his pocket. Plain, steel, a little rough on one edge where it had been cut.

“Thank you,” she said.

She put it in her pocket, the left one, where she kept things she didn’t want to lose. She didn’t say anything else. She didn’t need to. And he didn’t ask anything. He’d said he wouldn’t, and he didn’t say things he wasn’t going to do.

The kettle boiled. He made tea, in the two cups, and they drank it standing up in the kitchen because there was only one chair.

They saw Ice in the corridor, on the way out, at six.

He was coming back from somewhere with a bag of oranges. He stopped when he saw them. Outside his own door, the last one.

She’d met him in the game a hundred times. The swamp guy. The first of the nine. The one at the front, who walked round geese and swept under chairs and said good for them. She’d watched his stream before she ever had a pod, because Deston watched it.

She’d never met him out here.

He looked at her. He looked at Deston beside her. He looked at the small cactus by the door opposite, the one with the chip in its pot. She’d moved it, a little, before they left. Not far. Just to the left of the big one, nearer the door, where you’d see it first.

“You chose,” said Ice.

“Yes.”

He looked at it for a long moment.

“Good,” he said.

It was the way he said it in the game. Exactly. As if it settled something. Then he nodded, once, to her, and once to Deston, and went in through his door with his oranges, left hand first. The door closed.

“That’s it?” said Chernn.

“That’s a lot,” said Deston. “For him. That’s about a speech.”

She went back into the game on Sunday, from Singapore, and the first thing she smelled in the Hart’s kitchen was wax that wasn’t hers.

She knew her own. Bee’s beeswax and the butcher’s tallow from the east road, half and half. She’d been dipping in the corner of the Hart’s kitchen since January. She knew exactly what it smelled like warm, and cold, and on her hands at night.

This was different. Thinner. A little sweet. Like the wax in Tallow Green, in Mother Dipp’s long low room, where the vats were.

She stood in the doorway of the Hart’s kitchen with her pack on and looked at the back of the big black range, beside Kotori’s kettles, where her own pot sat. Her pot was cold. She hadn’t been in since Friday.

There was a ring of wax on the iron next to it. Fresh. Somebody else’s.

Kotori was at the kettles, with her back to the door. She’d gone very still.

Chernn didn’t say anything. She went across the kitchen to her corner, and put her pack down, and went to put her own pot on. And in the scrap bucket beside the range, where Kotori threw the ends of things, she saw a candle.

A bad one. Very bad. Crooked, lumpy, thick at the bottom and thin at the top, as if somebody had lifted it too fast and then too slow and then panicked. The kind of candle you made on your first morning, at forty-one per cent.

It had two wicks.

Two of them. Side by side, coming out of the top, very close, in the same candle.

Chernn looked at it in the scrap bucket for a long time.

Then she picked it up and looked at it properly. Turned it in her fingers. Held it up to the window, the way Mother Dipp did, the way Isa did. It was terrible. It was the worst candle she’d ever seen. Somebody had made it at night, in a kitchen that wasn’t theirs, with somebody’s permission and somebody’s stove, and hadn’t been able to get it right. And thrown it away, and started again.

She put it back in the scrap bucket. Exactly where it had been, at the same angle.

When she turned round, Kotori was looking at her.

“I’m not saying anything,” said Kotori. Very fast.

“Nor am I,” said Chernn.

“I’m very bad at secrets.”

“You’re doing fine,” said Chernn.

Kotori looked at her for a long moment, with a kettle in her hand. Then her whole face went pink and she turned back to the stove.

“Sorry,” said Kotori, to the kettle.

“Don’t,” said Chernn.

“Thank you,” said Kotori, to the kettle. And then, much more quietly: “He’s getting better. He’s done eleven.”

Isa Corrie was awake in her chair by the north door when Chernn brought the candles in at the first dawn.

She’d been bringing them since April. Forty a week, straight, for the old woman who didn’t sleep much and sat looking at the jar. Isa liked to have them lit along the table in front of her chair, a row of small white flames, beside the jar’s faint glow through the clay. More light. While she waits.

Isa looked up when she came in.

“Your tall one,” said Isa, “smells of Tallow Green.”

Chernn stopped in the middle of the banner room with the box.

“I know,” she said.

Isa looked at her with her bright black eyes for a long moment. Then she held out her root hands for a candle, the way she always did, to look down it. Chernn gave her one. Isa turned it over, and held it up, and looked down it.

“Honest,” she said. “And straight.”

“Still.”

“Hm.” Isa put it down. She was quiet for a while, with her hands folded. “My grandmother’s grandmother walked through Tallow Green,” she said. “Going north, with the lamp. She stayed a night. There was a wedding.”

Chernn didn’t say anything.

“In Tallow Green,” said Isa, “when somebody asks, they don’t use words first. They dip a candle. One candle, with two wicks. Side by side, close. It’s very hard. The wicks pull at each other in the wax and the candle goes crooked. You need steady hands and a lot of tries.” She looked at the jar. “And when it’s done, they give it to the one they’re asking. And light both wicks. And then they ask.”

The banner room was very quiet.

“And if both wicks burn all the way down to the bottom,” said Isa, “together, without one going out, then that’s the answer. That’s what my grandmother’s grandmother saw. She wrote it in the back of the old book. Two flames, one candle. Down to the bottom.” She looked at Chernn. “But they don’t wait for the candle. Nobody’s got that long. The one being asked just says it. Before it’s burned down. And then they sit and watch it burn the rest of the way, together, because it’s nice.”

Chernn stood with the box of candles in her hands.

“How many tries,” she said, “does it usually take?”

Isa thought about it.

“For somebody who’s never dipped?” she said. “With big hands?” Her bright black eyes went over Chernn’s shoulder, through the banner room door, across the Hall, to where a tall man was sitting at Moonlight Harvest’s end of the long table. He was writing in a book, with his sleeves pushed up. There was wax on his left forearm. “Forty. Fifty.”

“He’s done eleven,” said Chernn. “Kotori says.”

“Then he’s got a while yet,” said Isa. And closed her eyes, and folded her hands, and was asleep, or pretending.

She didn’t say anything to him. Not that day, or the next, or the one after.

She watched him not say anything back. It was very hard to watch. Deston, who could talk to anyone and liked talking to everybody, going quiet whenever she came into a room. Going red when she looked at his hands. Disappearing from the long table at the third dawn, every night, going to check something, and coming back an hour later smelling of thin sweet wax from a candle village five days away. With a new burn on his thumb that Rachael healed without asking about, because Rachael never asked about anything, she just decided who was helped first.

She could have told him. She thought about it. I know. Isa told me. You don’t have to. It’s fine. Just ask.

She didn’t.

She thought about a corridor in Malacca, and a man coming back with a bag of oranges. About a woman who’d knocked on a door instead of ringing the bell. About Gabe, keeping a secret for twenty-two days with his face red, and Ice looking at him and deciding not to ask. Because you were trying so hard. And I thought somebody should get to go first.

Deston was trying so hard.

So she left a gap. And let him.

A letter came on the Thursday, in a parcel, the way Mother Dipp’s letters came. A little bundle in brown paper with string round it, smelling of the long low room with the vats.

Inside, a note. And forty wicks, very fine, the good kind, bound with thread.

Chernn read it twice in the corner of the Hart’s kitchen. Then she folded it very small and put it in her pocket, the left one, in the game, where she kept things she didn’t want to lose. Next to nothing. In the other pocket, the real one, in a room in Singapore, there was a steel key with one rough edge.

She looked at the scrap bucket.

There was a new candle in it. From last night. Still crooked. Still lumpy. But less. Two wicks, side by side. One of them nearly straight.

She didn’t pick it up this time. She just looked at it. And then she put her own pot on the back of the range, next to the ring of thin sweet wax that wasn’t hers. She started to dip, down and up, like breathing. Steady. Not pulling.

Kotori, at the kettles, didn’t say anything at all.
