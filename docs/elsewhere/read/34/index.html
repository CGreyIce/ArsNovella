Chapter 34

Two Feet Apart

3,235 words · 14 min read

The trees in Ashcombe were too tall, and Wendy had decided not to like them.

It was a decision. She’d made it on Thursday morning, about forty seconds after she’d stepped out of the little wooden room with the rain on the window. She’d stood in the doorway of a long low timber hall at the bottom of a valley, and looked up, and up, and kept looking up. The trees had kept going. Grey trunks wide as houses, going straight up out of the ferns into a green roof so high and so far away it looked like weather. Light came down through it in long slanting shafts, full of dust and smoke, and hit the forest floor in pools. Everything else was shade. The air smelled of resin and woodsmoke and wet earth. Somewhere in the distance, very faintly, something went tock, tock, tock: an axe, in no hurry at all.

She’d stood there with her mouth open for about forty seconds. Then she’d closed it, and decided not to like it, because if she liked it straight away she’d have nowhere to go.

That was Thursday. It was Saturday now. She was still working on not liking it.

“You like it,” said Salty, on the call.

He said it at half past six on Saturday evening, in Singapore, which was the game’s morning. She’d come out of her wooden room in the timber hall and was standing in the first pool of sun with her bow over her shoulder, looking up again, because she couldn’t seem to stop. He couldn’t see her. He was in a mining town on a cliff on the other side of the world, eleven days’ walk away according to Kei. But he could hear her not saying anything, which was the same thing.

“I don’t like it.”

“You do.”

“It’s too tall. It’s ridiculous. Nobody needs a tree that tall.” She looked up at it. “It’s showing off.”

“Okay,” said Salty.

In the café, in Singapore, at that exact moment, he was eighteen inches away from her. That was the thing she kept thinking about. Booth sixteen and booth seventeen, side by side on the fourth step of an old cinema in a mall, with a grey curtain between them that he’d drawn back on Thursday so he could see her. She could have reached out of the gel and touched his arm. Instead he was on a black cliff over a grey sea somewhere in the north, she was at the bottom of a green valley under trees that were showing off, and between them was eleven days of road nobody had walked yet.

Two feet, she thought. And eleven days.

“How’s the mine?” she said.

“Cold,” said Salty, satisfied. “Good.”

She hadn’t wanted to come.

That wasn’t quite true. She’d wanted to come the way you want to go to something you know you’ll enjoy and have already decided to be cynical about, in advance, so nobody can say you were taken in. She’d watched Ice’s swamp for a month over Salty’s shoulder, saying it’s a game, it’s just a game, it’s a coffin with Wi-Fi. Salty had watched it the way he watched everything Ice did, with total and silent loyalty. And then Kei had sent a screenshot to the group chat of six bookings in four cities, in the first minute, with the words nobody has to come. but i got six.

She’d said she’d come for Salty. She’d said it so many times that Salty had started saying “I know, you’re coming for me,” before she could.

She’d come for herself. She knew that. She’d known it in the little wooden room, when the mirror had shown her her own face. The one she’d streamed in for years. Long dark brown hair, side-parted, with the green underneath where it caught the light. One eye amber-brown and one eye violet. She’d built it on her phone in the pre-launch creator over three nights, getting the green exactly right. And then it was there, in a mirror, in the rain, and it moved when she moved, and it was her, and she’d had to sit down on the little chair for a moment.

The mirror had shown her the headphones first. Her lavender ones, the ones she always wore. She’d looked at them for a while and said no. It was a stupid thing to keep. Headphones with nothing in them. What would they even be for, in here? She’d just sit in a forest with silent headphones on, like a crazy person.

So it showed her the necklace instead. The long chain with the small star on it and the small crescent moon, the one her model had always worn.

“Yes,” she’d said. “That one.”

It was round her neck now, under her beginner’s tunic. She could feel it against her skin when she moved, the star and the moon, cool and then warm.

She’d taken the bow because the rack had one, and because she’d taken Woodcutting and Fletching on Thursday afternoon, and arrows were the only thing she could think of that you’d make out of wood and then use.

It had been a mistake. She’d known it was a mistake by Thursday evening.

She couldn’t shoot.

She could hold the bow. She could put the arrow on the string. She could draw it back to her cheek, the way the little window said, and look down the shaft at the target. A straw boss on a frame at the end of the long clearing behind the bowyer’s, with a painted crow on it. She could hold it there. She could hold it there for a very, very long time, while her arm started to shake and the string bit her fingers and the crow went blurry. Then she’d let go. The arrow would go somewhere that wasn’t the crow, and the window would tell her about it.

“Nineteen,” she said to the bowyer, on Saturday morning.

“I can read,” said the bowyer.

The bowyer was a woman called Hester Lisle. She had grey hair cut short and forearms like rope, and she made every bow in Ashcombe in a shed full of shavings at the end of the clearing. She sat on her bench in the doorway with a stave across her knees and a drawknife in her hands, watching Wendy shoot with the expression of someone watching a pot that refused to boil. She’d watched Wendy shoot on Thursday, and on Friday. She’d said, on Friday, “Again,” about forty times, and nothing else.

Now she put the drawknife down.

“You’re waiting,” she said.

“I’m aiming.”

“You’re waiting.” Hester got up off the bench and walked down the clearing, through a shaft of sun, and stood beside her. She was shorter than Wendy. It didn’t seem to matter. “You draw, and you hold, and you look at the crow, and you wait. You’re waiting for it to be right. You think if you hold it long enough, it’ll go right, and then you’ll let go.” She looked at Wendy’s shaking arm. “It won’t.”

“It won’t what?”

“Go right. It doesn’t get righter. It gets worse. Every second you hold it, your arm’s tireder and your eye’s drier and the wind’s moved.” Hester took the bow out of her hands, quite gently, and drew it, and let it go, all in one breath, without seeming to look at anything. The arrow went into the crow’s painted eye with a sound like a slap. “You decide before you draw. Then you draw, and you let go. You don’t wait for it to be right. It’s never going to be right. You let go anyway.”

Wendy looked at the arrow in the crow’s eye.

“That’s the most depressing advice I’ve ever been given,” she said.

“It’s the only advice there is.” Hester handed her the bow back. “Again. And don’t hold it.”

She didn’t hold it.

That was the hard part. Not the drawing. Not the aiming. The not holding. She stood in the clearing with the bow and decided where it was going to go, and drew. Then everything in her wanted to stop there, at full draw, at her cheek, and wait. Look again. Make sure. Wait until her arm was steady and the wind was still and the crow was exactly where she wanted it. Wait until it was right.

She let go.

It went into the straw. Not the crow, but the straw, a hand’s width off the crow’s wing, which was closer than anything she’d done in three days.

She stood and looked at it. Hester didn’t say anything. Hester picked her drawknife up again.

She did it again. And again. Decide, draw, go. Every time her arm wanted to stop. Every time she let it go anyway, before it was right, before she was sure, before anything. Every time it went somewhere a bit closer to where she’d decided.

It was the most uncomfortable thing she’d ever done. It was like walking off the edge of something on purpose.

“Hm,” said Hester, from the bench, not looking up.

On the forty-first arrow, with the sun coming down through the green roof in long slanting gold and the smoke from the charcoal clamps drifting through it, she drew. She didn’t wait. She let go.

The arrow went into the crow’s painted eye, beside Hester’s, with a sound like a slap.

She stood very still in the clearing with the bow in her hand.

“Huh,” she said.

“There you are,” said Hester. She didn’t look up from her stave. “Took you long enough.”

“I hit the crow,” she told Salty, on the call, that afternoon.

“Good.”

“In the eye.”

“Good.”

“You have to let go before it’s right.” She was sitting on a log at the edge of the clearing, with a heap of ash shafts beside her and a knife and a bundle of grey goose feathers, making arrows. She’d found she liked making arrows. Fletching, the window called it. You split the shaft and straightened it and cut the nock, and glued the feathers on in a spiral, three to an arrow. It was fiddly and slow and quiet, and you could think about nothing while you did it. “That’s the whole thing. Hester says. You don’t wait for it to be right. You decide, and you let go anyway.”

Salty didn’t say anything for a moment. She could hear the wind wherever he was, a big empty grey wind off the sea, and a pick going somewhere below him in the cliff.

“That’s you,” he said.

“What?”

“That’s you. You wait for it to be right.” He said it the way he said everything, plainly, as if it was a fact about the weather. “Everything. Streaming. Anything you make. You never put it out because it’s not right yet. You wait till it’s perfect, and then it’s been four months.”

She stopped with a feather halfway to the shaft.

“That’s not the same.”

“It’s the same.”

“It’s not,” she said, defensively. Then she sat there on the log, holding the feather, and didn’t say anything for a long time. Salty didn’t either. That was the thing about Salty. He’d say the true thing once, bluntly, and then he’d leave it on the ground in front of you and not pick it up again.

She glued the feather on.

“I’m still not streaming,” she said.

“Okay,” said Salty.

Ashcombe at night was something else.

She hadn’t seen it properly on Thursday or Friday. On Thursday the game’s night had come round at lunchtime, and she’d spent it inside the timber hall, splitting ash shafts by lamplight and refusing to be impressed. On Friday her six hours had run out before the dark came. On Saturday she stayed.

The game’s sun went down a little after eight, Singapore time, and the Greatwood went dark in a way she’d never seen anything go dark. Not black. Deep. The shafts of light went out one by one, from the top down, as if somebody was walking through the roof turning them off. The ferns went grey and then blue. The charcoal burners’ clamps out in the clearings glowed red through their turf jackets, a dozen low red mounds in the dark, smoking. The burners sat by them all night on stools, keeping them, because a clamp left alone in the dark would either go out or catch.

And on the trunks of the great trees, low down, near the roots, something started to glow.

Pale green. Faint, at first, and then not faint. Little shelves of fungus, all over the bark, glowing like the hands of an old clock. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Running up the grey trunks in lines and patches into the dark. Lighting the forest floor a soft, cold, underwater green, so that the whole wood looked like the bottom of the sea.

Foxfire, said the little window, when she looked at it. It lights the way home.

She stood in the middle of the clearing with her bow and her quiver of forty-one ugly arrows and her star-and-moon necklace, and turned slowly round. She turned all the way round, twice, looking at it.

“Wendy?” said Salty, on the call. “You there?”

She didn’t answer.

“Wen?”

“It’s—“ She stopped. She didn’t have a word. She was supposed to be the one with words, the one who noticed how things looked. She stood in a glowing forest and didn’t have a single one. “Hang on,” she said. “Hang on.”

She opened her stream.

She didn’t decide to. That was the thing. She didn’t stand there and think about it and wait until it was right. She hadn’t streamed in four months, not since work had got heavy and everything else had got quiet. There was no title, no schedule, no overlay. Her hair was a mess and her arrows were ugly and she didn’t know how to do anything. Every bit of her wanted to wait. Not tonight. Next week. When she’d figured out the settings. When she was better. When it was right.

She opened it anyway, and the little light came up off her shoulder and floated behind her, and she let it go.

They looked at it.

She didn’t do anything for twenty minutes except walk through the Greatwood at night with forty-one people behind her. She showed them the foxfire on the trunks, going up into the dark. She showed them the charcoal clamps, glowing red, and the burners on their stools, one of whom nodded at her. She showed them Hester’s shed, with the light on and the shavings on the floor, and the painted crow at the end of the clearing with two arrows in its eye. She told them about let go. She told them she still didn’t like the trees. They didn’t believe her.

At the edge of the clearing, she looked up.

There was a gap in the green roof there, where one of the great trees had come down a long time ago. Through it, very high and very far away, there were stars. A whole lot of them. More than you ever saw in Singapore, so many they looked like dust. And low down at the edge of the gap, a thin bright crescent of moon.

She put her hand on her chest without thinking, where the necklace was. The star and the moon.

“Okay,” she said, out loud, to forty-one people and the sky. “Okay. It’s a nice coffin.”

“I don’t like it,” said Wendy. “It’s showing off.”

She streamed for twenty-six minutes. Then her booking ended, and the egg told her so, politely.

She lay in the gel in booth seventeen, on the fourth step of the old cinema, with the lid lifting over her and the low blue light along the floor. For a moment she didn’t move. Her arm still felt the bow. The back of her eyes still felt green.

The curtain was drawn back. Salty was already sitting up in sixteen, eighteen inches away, looking at her.

He had his phone in his hand. He’d been watching. Of course he had. He’d come out of his mine on his cliff and logged off the moment she went live, so he could lie in his egg in a cinema in Singapore and watch her walk through a forest on his phone. He’d been eighteen inches away the whole time.

“You streamed,” he said.

“I streamed.”

“You didn’t wait.”

“I didn’t wait.” She sat up in the gel and looked at him across the gap. Her eyes were doing something annoying. “Don’t say anything.”

“Okay,” said Salty.

He reached across the eighteen inches and took her hand, and didn’t say anything.

On Sunday night, the night before the year turned, the charcoal burners didn’t sleep at all.

Hester told her why. It was an old thing in Ashcombe, older than Hester’s grandmother. On the longest night you kept every clamp burning till the sun came back. All of them, in every clearing, all night, however long the night went on. Every burner sat up with his clamp. Every house in the timber town kept a lamp in the window. Because the Greatwood was very old, and very big, and the longest night was very dark. And things lived in the deep of it that only came to the edges when there was nothing burning to keep them back.

“Keep your lights,” said Hester, sitting on her bench in the red glow of the nearest clamp, with a new stave across her knees. “That’s all it is. That’s all it ever was. You keep your lights, and you don’t let them go out, and in the morning you’re still here.”

Wendy sat on the log beside her with a quiver of forty-one arrows, which she’d sorted, the straight ones at the front.

“How long is the longest night?” she said.

Hester looked out at the dark beyond the glow.

“Depends,” she said, “on the year.”
