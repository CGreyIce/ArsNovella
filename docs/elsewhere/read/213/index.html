Live from Elsewhere · Volume 8: Somewhere Else

Chapter 213

Seven

3,403 words · 15 min read

Deston peeled the sticker off on Saturday night, at a quarter to ten, in the corridor, with Ice and Gabe watching.

He’d come up on the six o’clock bus. Of course he had. With a pink box of kueh for the auntie downstairs, who’d say it was too sweet and eat three. And an envelope from Vivian, which she’d handed him at the bus station in Melaka herself, in her grey suit, with her umbrella, because she’d driven down to meet the bus on purpose. Ice had never known Vivian to drive anywhere on purpose. And a cactus. A bigger one, in a better pot, from a nursery in Singapore, which he’d carried on his knee for four hours.

The envelope said, on the front, in Vivian’s small neat hand: Consent. Granted. Three months, as requested.

Inside was a letter on thick paper with a state crest at the top. Deston had read it on the bus, he said, eleven times. Then he’d stopped reading it and just held it.

Now he stood in the corridor outside the unit opposite, under the strip light, with the letter in one hand. The grille was still padlocked; the keys would be June, the lawyer said, after the rest of the papers. The pot by the door had the very small cactus in it from January, which had not died, to everybody’s surprise. And on the grille at eye height, faded pink by the sun through the corridor window, the sticker.

DIJUAL.

Nineteen months. Twenty-two, now.

Deston looked at it for a long time.

“Go on, then,” said Ice.

Deston looked at him.

“You stole that,” said Deston. “From Thea.”

“I borrow things,” said Ice. “I give them back.”

Deston laughed. Softly, in the corridor. Then he reached up and got his thumbnail under the corner of the sticker, and pulled.

It came off in one piece. Old, dry, faded, with a long ripping sound. And under it, on the paint of the grille, a clean rectangle. The colour the grille had been twenty-two months ago, before the sun got to it.

Deston held the sticker in his hand and looked at it.

“I’m going to keep this,” he said.

“Of course you are,” said Gabe. His eyes were wet. They usually were, now. Gabe had cried more in the last three months than in the eleven years Ice had known him, and none of it was sad. “You’re going to put it in a frame.”

“I’m going to put it in a frame,” agreed Deston.

He put the new cactus down by the door, beside the old one, in its better pot. Two cacti. A very small one that hadn’t died and a bigger one that had sat on his knee on a bus for four hours.

“For her,” said Deston. “When she comes. One’s hers.”

“Which one?” said Gabe.

“She’ll choose,” said Deston.

Ice went in at nine on Sunday morning, the way he always did.

He came out of the dark into the first of the nine at Carrow Cross. The room was quiet. Sunday. Most of the guild were down the hatch, or out on the roads, or asleep in the gallery, or in Masmark. Riverbank, and Salty and Wendy, keeping a netloft and a gate on the far side of the world. The long table was half full.

And Ama Corrie was at the far end, by the window, with her broom.

The fen-reed one, with KEPT on its tag in blue ink. She was doing the chairs. Sunday. Under, not round. Slow. Humming the tune that wasn’t a tune.

At the north end of the room, by the north door, Isa Corrie was awake in her chair. With the jar on the table in front of her, glowing very faintly through the clay. And above her, on its three-hundred-year-old hook, the empty black cage with the star on top. With five words on the rim of its base in Curse’s blue ink that only somebody looking up would see. Carried. And carried home.

And beside him, in the chair that had been Bee’s until she’d started sitting on the other side, Hyvred.

She’d come in a minute before him. She was always in a minute before him now. She said it was so she could see him come out of the dark. He didn’t know what that looked like from outside. Something, apparently. She looked at him now, the way she’d looked at him since January. Openly. Not looking away.

“Hi,” said Hyvred.

“Hi.”

She looked at Ama Corrie at the far end of the room with her broom. Then back at him.

“Can I ask her?” she said.

He knew what she meant.

“You can ask her,” he said.

Hyvred got up and went down the long table.

He watched her go. Past Thea’s six by the window, half of them there, Thea and Xola back from Masmark, Chi at the back with the sheep asleep on her feet. Past Hana’s five. Past the middle of the table, the first twenty, Kei’s chair at the head with a ring on the table in front of it from a cup. All the way to the far end, where Ama Corrie was sweeping under Gigi’s chair, chair twenty-one, halfway down the north side.

Ama Corrie stopped. She looked at Hyvred over her spectacles.

“Can I help?” said Hyvred.

Ama Corrie looked at her for a long time.

Ice knew what she was going to say. He’d heard her say it six times. Three times. Forty years nobody asks. Then three times in a month. It’s catching. Then Kestrel, at the bottom of the Seven Below, after twenty-six minutes. That’s four. Then Gigi, on the night Isa came home, sweeping under the old woman’s chair while sixty people sat very still. That’s five. Then him, on Christmas, with the chair by the north door not empty for the first time. Six.

“Seven,” said Ama Corrie.

But she held out the broom.

Hyvred took it.

She stood there with it in her hands for a moment, the fen-reed broom, Sedge’s broom head from the fen, KEPT on the tag. She looked down the long table at Ice. Her tail did a thing.

Then she started sweeping.

She didn’t know how. He could see that from the first of the nine. She’d watched Ama Corrie do it for a year and him do it for six months and Gigi and Thea and Kestrel. But she’d never done it. She swept round the first chair. Ama Corrie made a noise.

“Under,” said Ama Corrie. “Not round.”

“Sorry. Sorry.” Hyvred went back and did it again. Under. “Like that?”

“Like that,” said Ama Corrie, and sat down on the window seat with her hands in her lap to watch.

Ice got up.

He went down the long table to where Hyvred was. She was on Thea’s six, at the window, under Chi’s chair. There was wool under it, from the sheep. She looked up when he came, with the broom.

“I’m doing it wrong,” she said.

“You’re doing it fine.”

“I swept round.”

“Everybody sweeps round the first time,” said Ice. “I did.”

“You didn’t.”

“I did,” said Ice. “On the first Sunday. Ama Corrie made the same noise.” He held out his hand. Not for the broom. Palm up, the way he’d held it out by the fish cart at the bottom of the north-west road. “Can I help?”

At the window, Ama Corrie, on the window seat, looked at him over her spectacles.

“Hm,” she said. And then, after a moment, very slowly, as if she were writing it in a margin: “Eight.”

They swept all sixty-one together.

One broom. He held it with her. Not taking it; she was the one who’d asked. Just his hand on the handle below hers. They went down the long table chair by chair. Under, not round. Slowly. The way Ama Corrie did it, the way he’d learned on the first Sunday.

Thea’s six. Wool under Chi’s, from the sheep. A feather under Xyro’s, white, which he picked up and looked at and put in his pocket without saying anything. Hyvred saw and didn’t say anything either.

Hana’s five. A lot under Leaka’s, always. Blue ink under Curse’s, a drop. A shaving of wood under Jared’s.

The middle. The first twenty. Kei’s at the head, with the ring from the cup. Higu’s, with an oilskin case hung over the back. Gigi’s, chair twenty-one, with a hawthorn twig under it, the way there always was.

Riverbank’s eight, empty, pushed in, the eight of them in Masmark. Under them anyway.

Moonlight Harvest’s nine. Pencil shavings under Dennis’s. Mud under Calvin’s. Fen grit under his own, still. Fifteen months and five hundred miles from the fen, and he still didn’t know how.

And under Kai’s, beside the first of the nine, a little drift of something pale. Wax. From a woman who dipped candles in the corner of the Hart’s kitchen and sat next to Deston at the long table now. On Moonlight Harvest’s end, on a stool, until Ama Corrie brought a chair.

Sixty-one.

And then the chair by the north door.

Isa was awake in it. She watched them come, the two of them with one broom between them. Hyvred with her horns gold in the light from the window, and him with his hand below hers on the handle. She didn’t say anything. She just watched, with her bright black eyes, the way she watched the jar.

They stopped in front of her.

“Can we?” said Hyvred.

Isa looked at her. Then at him. Then at the broom, the fen-reed head with KEPT on its tag.

“Hm,” said Isa Corrie.

They swept under it. With her in it. Slowly. Under, not round.

There was road dust under it still. Grey, from the north road, from a walk of a hundred miles behind the dark on the longest night. Nobody had ever quite got it all out. A little heather, dried. And a few small black flakes, like soot. From a lamp three hundred years old that had been carried home.

Hyvred swept them out into the light, very gently, and looked at them.

Then she looked up at Isa.

“Should we keep them?” she said. “The— the soot. It’s from the lamp. From the north. From your mothers.”

Isa looked down at the little black flakes on the floor in front of her chair, in the light from the window.

“No,” she said. “Sweep them out. That’s what they’re for.” She looked at the jar on the table, glowing through the clay. “The light’s in there. That’s the bit you keep. The rest is just where it’s been.”

Hyvred looked at the soot for a long moment.

Then she swept it out, under not round, with his hand below hers on the handle. Across the floor of the banner room, out of the north door, onto the north steps, into the morning. Where the north road went out past Kite’s milestone over the moor. Ninety-four lamps on the backs of grey stones, facing the wrong way, all lit. And past them, a long way north, past the old hall and the twenty-two and the edge of the world, a hill in the dark with a ring of stones on it. Nobody in it now.

Not this year.

His stream had been on the whole time. He’d forgotten, the way he forgot. The Eye at his shoulder, looking out of him, at a man and a woman with one broom.

He looked at the chat when they sat down again. In the first of the nine, with Hyvred beside him, and Ama Corrie bringing two cups of tea herself, which she’d never done.

“Hi, Kopi,” he said.

Beside him, Hyvred made a small sound and put her face in her hands.

“Twenty-one,” said Hyvred, through them. “Tif’s going to have to change the command. Twenty-one still here.”

“She will,” said Ice.

“She’s going to cry.”

“She’s already crying,” said Ice. “She said.”

The letters came that evening, the way letters came now. More of them than he used to get in a month.

He read the last one several times.

We’re fine.

He didn’t know why that was the one. He’d known Vane two years in a war, a long time ago, and a month on a harbour wall. Vane didn’t say things. He’d never once said we’re fine. He’d just made sure they were.

He put the letters away.

At the north end of the room, Isa was asleep in her chair with the blanket round her and her hands folded. Under the empty cage that knew what it was. Suzu was on the stool beside her where Ama Corrie usually sat, with the jar on the table in front of them both. A tall white candle beside it, plain, straight, for a road he wasn’t walking yet.

He looked down the long table.

At everybody he’d known for years, and everybody he’d known for a few months. And one old woman he’d known since Christmas, and one woman with gold horns he’d known since a winter, through a stream that was ten people and a swamp. All in one room. On one map. At one table. Sixty-one chairs, and a chair by the north door that somebody was sitting in. And ten more, on the far side of the world, in a netloft that smelled of fish. With a banner on its hooks, and a gold dot that had turned from white by itself, keeping the room.

Somewhere else, he’d told Salty by the fountain, in October, with Meridian’s banner going away down the west road. And this is here.

He’d been wrong. Not about the fountain. About the rest.

It wasn’t somewhere else and here. It was both. The black sky full of ships, and a phone game with a stupid guild name, and a bridge over a gorge in the wind. A man saying o7 at a ferry. A woman in silver saying tank. Vane on a harbour wall saying you went somewhere else. All of it had come here, down a road of lights and down a crater wall, to a round town and a lake. And it was all the same place. It always had been. He’d just been standing at the front of it, facing outward, like the heron. Never once turning round to look at what was behind him.

He’d turned round.

Hyvred was asleep on his shoulder. She’d fallen asleep about four minutes after sitting down, with her cup still in her hand. He’d taken it off her before it spilled and put it on the table. Her tail lay across the toe of his boot, white, loose, very slightly curled.

He didn’t move his boot.

At the far end of the room, Ama Corrie got up off the window seat with the broom and started round the chairs again, though she’d done them all once already, twice now, with help. Under, not round. Slow. Under the sat ones and the empty ones. Under Riverbank’s eight, and Salty’s and Wendy’s, empty, a long way off. Under the chair by the north door, with Isa asleep in it.

And under the first of the nine, with him in it, and somebody asleep on his shoulder.

She swept under it very carefully. She didn’t wake either of them. She didn’t look at them. When she’d done it, she stood beside the first of the nine for a moment with the fen-reed broom in her hands. Looking down the long table, the whole of it, all sixty-one, in the evening light.

“Hm,” said Ama Corrie.

It sounded like kept.
