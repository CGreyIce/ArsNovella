Live from Elsewhere · Volume 12: Even

Chapter 297

The Back of Fifty-Nine

3,307 words · 14 min read

PenguChi had stood at the back of a lot of things, and this was the longest.

Fifty-nine people and a sheep and a goose, strung out along the east road for most of a mile. In the mornings they were close together. By the afternoon, in the heat, the front was over the next rise before the back had got to the bottom of this one. And PenguChi at the very end of it, with her long spear on her shoulder and Patience walking in front of her. And the goose, which had decided at the ferry that it belonged with the sheep, walking beside Patience in total silence.

She liked it. She’d always liked the back. You saw more.

You saw everybody’s backs, for a start. Fifty-nine backs. And she’d stood at the back of most of them before, at one time or another, so she knew what they looked like when they were all right and what they looked like when they weren’t.

They were all right. All of them. She’d never seen so many people all right at once.

She counted the pairs. Not the way Dennis counted. Dennis counted heads, twice, and himself last now, and the goose. PenguChi counted the way you counted from the back: who was walking next to who.

Gabe and Rae, near the front, at no paces. Gabe talking, mostly, and Rae listening with her staff across her shoulders. Every so often she said something short, and Gabe stopped talking for a whole minute, which PenguChi had never seen anybody else make him do.

Thea and Gigi, close. Not touching, mostly. Sometimes. Gigi naming things in the hedges as they went past. Thea saying them back, softly, as if she were putting them somewhere.

Xola and Slep. Xola talking, always. Slep on whichever side she wasn’t, saying nothing, and once a day saying something nobody had said yet.

Deston and Chernn. Deston with his book under his arm that he didn’t open any more. Chernn with a bag of wicks, because she couldn’t not.

Hana and Milli. Hana with her hands in her pockets. Milli carrying, for some reason, two iron hinges in his coat, which PenguChi could hear clinking.

Leaka and Curse. Leaka loud. Curse writing small labels on things and leaving them on fence posts. Fence, on a fence. Very good dog, on a gate where a very good dog had come out and sniffed the goose.

Kite and Drew and Ash. Three.

Suzu and Kotori. Suzu asleep while walking. Kotori steering him by the sleeve.

Xav and RX and Malcolm and Val, Riverbank, going home. Xav with his sketchbook out half the time, drawing Suzu walking asleep, getting it right.

Dennis and Calvin, with the ledger.

Kei and Higu at the front, with the map.

And Hyvred and Ice.

He wasn’t at the front.

That was the thing PenguChi noticed, about the third day. On every walk she’d ever been on with him, he’d been near the front. Not at it; Kei was at the front, with the map. But near it. Where the things came from. Where he’d be first to them. It was where he walked. He didn’t decide to. It was just where his feet went.

On this walk, he came to the back.

Not all the time. About half. He’d walk at the front with Hyvred for the morning, and then, in the afternoon, when the heat came and the walk stretched out, he’d drop back. Through everybody, one pair at a time, saying something small to each one, until he got to the end. Then he’d walk beside PenguChi, at the back, with Hyvred on his arm.

He didn’t say why. She didn’t ask.

On the eleventh day, he came back on his own.

“Penguin,” said Ice.

She kicked him in the shins. Not hard. She’d never once kicked him hard. He let it land, the way he always did now, with a small oof. Then he walked beside her at the back of fifty-nine people and a sheep and a goose. On the white road east, in the heat.

He didn’t say anything for a long time.

“Hyv’s with Thea,” he said, eventually. “At the front. Gigi’s naming the hedges.”

“I know.”

“I said I’d come and walk at the back.”

“You’ve walked at the back every afternoon for eleven days,” said PenguChi.

He looked at her.

“I’m at the back,” said PenguChi. “I see everything that comes to the back.” She looked at the road ahead, at fifty-nine backs. “You’re thinking.”

“Yes.”

“You’ve been thinking since the round town,” said PenguChi. “At the front in the mornings you look at the road. At the back in the afternoons you look at nothing.” She shifted her spear on her shoulder. “You only look at nothing when you’re carrying something.”

He didn’t say anything for a while.

“You saw the board,” he said.

“I saw the board,” said PenguChi. “The notch. The second dawn out. Early. In pencil, at the bottom, in Kei’s hand.” She didn’t look at him. “I didn’t read it. I just saw it.”

“That’s the same thing.”

“No,” said PenguChi. “It isn’t. Reading’s when you decide what it says.” She walked on. “I haven’t decided anything.”

She’d known him a long time. Out there, before. He’d raided her once, years ago, with eleven people. She’d lasted forty minutes of his stream afterwards and decided he was boring. In here, the first time he saw her, he’d said Penguin, and she’d kicked him.

She’d been at the back of him across half the world since. A fen and a moor and a stone cross. A north door on the longest night, at the back of Gabe and Rae. She’d seen him fight and wait and go dark and come back. She’d seen him at the far end of a hundred rooms, where he liked to be, where you could see the door.

She’d never once seen him carrying something he didn’t know how to set down.

She could see it now. In his back, at the back, on a white road in the sun. Not heavy. He wasn’t frightened. She knew what he looked like frightened; she’d seen it once or twice, on the stream, when he thought nobody could tell. It was something else. Like a man holding a very full cup, walking very carefully, who didn’t want anybody to bump him.

“Penguin,” said Ice again. Quieter.

She didn’t kick him.

“At the notch,” he said. “Going home. The second dawn out. Early.” He was looking at the road. “I’d like it if nobody came up the road behind us. For a bit.”

PenguChi walked on for a while, thinking about it. Slowly. She did things slowly. She’d built a whole life on it.

“I’m at the back,” she said.

“I know.”

“I’m always at the back,” said PenguChi. “Of everything. Nobody comes up the road behind the back.” She shifted her spear. “That’s what the back’s for.”

He looked at her. She didn’t look at him. She looked at the fifty-nine backs in front of her, and the sheep, and the goose.

“Thank you,” said Ice.

“I didn’t decide anything,” said PenguChi.

“I know,” he said. “That’s why.”

Then he went back up the walk, pair by pair, to the front, to Hyvred, who was with Thea and Gigi. PenguChi watched him go. She watched Hyvred see him coming from fifty paces off, the way she saw everything, and let go of Thea’s arm and hold out her hand. Not looking at him. Just holding it out, sideways, at the height his hand would be when he got there.

He got there. She took it without looking.

PenguChi had stood at the back of a lot of things. She thought that might be the best thing she’d ever seen from there.

On the twelfth night, Hyvred came to the back.

On her own. She’d never done that either. She came through the camp in the dark, past fifty-seven people round fires, to the edge of the field where PenguChi sat with her spear across her knees and Patience and the goose asleep beside her. She sat down. She put her knees up and her tail round her feet, the way she sat on the bottom step of the Seven Below, not calling.

“He’s carrying something,” said Hyvred.

“Yes,” said PenguChi.

“Since the round town. Since before. Since the board.” Hyvred was looking across the field at the fire where Ice was sitting with Suzu and Kotori, very still. “He comes to the back every afternoon. To you.”

“He likes the back.”

“He likes the far end,” said Hyvred. “That’s different. The far end’s where you can see the door. The back’s where nobody comes up behind you.” She was quiet. “He’s never once cared about anybody coming up behind him. He goes first.”

PenguChi didn’t say anything. She’d learned it at the back. Most things got said if you didn’t say anything first.

“I can’t see what it is,” said Hyvred.

PenguChi looked at her for a long time. Slowly.

“You can,” she said.

Hyvred went very still.

“You see everything,” said PenguChi. “You’ve always seen everything. You saw the board. The notch. Early.” She looked at the fire, at Ice. “You can see it. You’ve decided not to look.”

Hyvred didn’t say anything for a long time. Her tail had gone tight round her feet. In the firelight from across the field PenguChi could see her face. Red, to the horns. And something else under it that PenguChi had seen on her once before, a long time ago, on a stream. On a girl at a gate, about to say me.

“Is that allowed?” said Hyvred. Very quietly. “To see something. And not look at it. So somebody else can show you.”

PenguChi thought about it. She did things slowly.

“Thea said everything’s allowed,” she said. “In a group chat. A long time ago.”

“She said it’s allowed. One thing.”

“It’s the same,” said PenguChi.

Hyvred laughed, very softly, into her knees. Then she sat there at the back beside PenguChi for a long time, not looking at the fire across the field, very carefully. Until Ice got up from it and started across the camp toward them. She got up too, and went to meet him halfway, and took his hand without looking.

On the thirteenth day, the road started to climb.

Slowly at first. Then not slowly. The plain gave out into rock, black rock, and the road went up it in long switchbacks, back and forth. The walk bunched up again, the way walks do on a climb. The front stopped being over the next rise and became just above you, on the next turn, looking down.

“Is it much further,” said RX, from somewhere near the middle, for the ninth time.

“It’ll be—“ said Malcolm.

“Don’t you DARE say March—“

“—tomorrow,” said Malcolm. “I was going to say tomorrow. It’s a rim. It’s a crater. We’re climbing the outside of it.” He sounded pleased. “Tomorrow at dusk.”

On the fourteenth day, at dusk, they came up the last switchback.

PenguChi was last, of course. She came up the last turn of the road behind everybody, with Patience and the goose. The road had been climbing for a day and a half. It got steep at the end, suddenly, the way the last flight of the two hundred and twelve did. Sixty-six steps, the hardest ones, with the wind.

At the top, the road went through a gap in the rock. A notch. Narrow, two walls of black stone either side, open to the sky. Wide enough for about four people to stand in it, if they stood close.

The whole walk was stopped in it. And on the road below the notch, on the far side. And on the rocks either side, where people had climbed up to see over.

Nobody was saying anything.

PenguChi came up into the notch behind everybody and looked.

Below her, a long way below, was a lake.

She’d heard about it. Everybody had. A lake the size of a country. It was true. It went away east from the bottom of the crater wall, flat and gold in the last of the dusk. Further than she could see. So far that the far side of it was only a line of light on the edge of the world. In the middle of it, small, an island, with a tower on it. Round the edge of it, all the way round, as far as she could see in both directions: a city. Lights coming on. Thousands of them. A ring of lights round a lake the size of a country.

And down the inside of the crater wall, from notches like this one, from every direction: roads. Dozens. She could see them, lit, cut into the black rock all the way round. Every one coming down from a notch on the rim to the ring of lights at the bottom. From every region of the map. All of them coming down to the same place.

[ Masmark · A city of the fiftieth level. All roads that come this far come here. ]

Beside her, Ash had sat down on a rock. She was looking at it with her calm narrow face completely still, and her hands in her lap. She’d never been east.

Dennis had his ledger open and wasn’t writing anything. Then he was. PenguChi watched him count the roads coming down the crater wall, his lips moving, twice. Then he wrote: Masmark. Forty-one roads down. Fifty-nine and a sheep.

“Forty-one,” said Dennis. To Ash, on her rock. “Roads. Coming down to it. From everywhere.”

Ash looked at them for a long time. The lit roads, cut into the black rock, all the way round.

“Every road comes here,” she said.

“Every road that comes this far,” said Dennis. “The window says.”

“Then it doesn’t matter which one you choose,” said Ash. Slowly, as if she were testing it. “At a crossroads. If they all come here.”

Dennis thought about it.

“It matters which one you go home on, darling,” he said. And wrote that down too.

Patience, at PenguChi’s feet, looked at the lake for a long time. Then she lay down. Beside her, the goose folded its legs, with enormous dignity, and sat down in the notch.

It didn’t get up when the walk started down the far side. It sat there, in the notch, with its back to Masmark, facing west. The way they’d come.

“It’s not coming,” said Gabe, from the first switchback down. “Dec. The goose isn’t coming.”

“It’s waiting,” said Kei.

“For what?”

Kei didn’t answer. He was already going down with his map.

Ice was standing in the notch.

PenguChi saw him because she was at the back, and he’d stopped, and everybody else had gone past him. He was standing in the middle of the notch, where the road went through between the two black walls. Hyvred wasn’t on his arm. She’d gone a few steps down the far side with Thea. She’d stopped there, looking back up at him. Not saying anything.

He was looking at Masmark.

Not the way the others had. They’d looked at it the way you look at something enormous you’ve never seen. He’d seen it before. He’d stood in this notch alone, PenguChi knew, a year and a half ago, at the end of fourteen days on his own. After a gate where a girl said me. He’d held something on his palm here, Hyvred had told her once, and it had leaned back the way he’d come.

He wasn’t holding anything now. His hands were at his sides.

He was looking at the notch. At the two black walls of it, and the gap between them, and the lake below going gold. As if he were measuring it. As if it were a room he was going to come back to and wanted to know where the door was.

PenguChi stood at the back, at the top of the road, behind him. She didn’t say anything. She didn’t decide anything.

After a while he turned round and looked at her. Up the road, past the goose sitting in the notch facing west.

“Penguin,” he said. Very quietly.

She didn’t kick him. She was too far away.

“I’m at the back,” said PenguChi.

He looked at her for a long time. Then he nodded, once. He turned and went down the far side of the notch to Hyvred, who’d been waiting on the third step down. She held out her hand, sideways, without looking, at the height his hand would be. He took it.

They went down into Masmark together, in the dusk, down the inside of the crater wall. With fifty-seven other people in front of them, and a thousand lights coming on below.

PenguChi went last, with Patience.

At the bottom of the first switchback she looked back up at the notch. The goose was still there. Sitting in the middle of it, facing west, in the last of the light. A grey and white shape between two black walls, very still, with an expression of permanent enormous offence. Waiting.

She didn’t know what for.

She thought she did.
