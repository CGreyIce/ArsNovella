Live from Elsewhere · Volume 6: There's a Chair

Chapter 144

Not Once

3,609 words · 16 min read

On Friday night Xola did a thing she’d promised herself, a long time ago, that she would never do again.

She opened Gigi’s channel.

She was at home, on the other side of the egg, sitting cross-legged on her bed with the lid of the pod up behind her like a big white shell. It was late. The flat was quiet. She’d come out of the game at the second dusk and eaten something standing up. Then she’d sat down on the bed meaning to sleep, and got her phone out instead, and typed the name before she could stop herself.

gigichiii_

She hadn’t looked at it in a long time. She’d stopped following it in the bad year, very quietly, the way you take your hand off a stove. She’d never told anyone she’d stopped. Not even Thea. Thea would have said you didn’t have to do that, and Xola would have had to say I know, and it would have been a whole thing.

The channel came up. Bigger than she remembered. Gigi had been streaming the game since December, it turned out. Since a café in a town called Cradle Commons, with Higu in a different café across the square. There were hundreds of hours of it. VODs going back months.

Xola looked at the list for a long time.

I’m not saying anything nice about her, she’d said on the bridge. Not ever.

She hadn’t said anything about not looking.

She started at the beginning, because Thea would have. Then she gave up on that, because there were hundreds of hours, and skipped to the spring.

She didn’t know what she was looking for. That was the honest truth. She thought, at first, that she was looking for proof. That she’d find the Gigi she remembered from the other end of the phone, and be able to say there, see, and close the app and go to sleep feeling right. She was very good at being right. She’d had a lot of practice in the bad year.

What she found was a woman with a hawthorn staff, standing in a gate.

The founding night first. Everybody had seen that clip; it had gone round. The thing coming through the north door of the Hall of Roads, all wings and teeth. Gigi stepping out in front of the counter with her staff and saying one word, lie, and thorn coming up out of the floor in a long low hedge right across the counter, so the thing couldn’t get over. Gigi behind it, holding it, with her knuckles white on the staff.

[ Lay · holding ]

Xola had seen it before. Thea had seen it before; it was the thing Thea had sent back through Higu. Tell her I saw her hold the counter. Tell her it was brave. Xola had thought, on the bridge, that Thea was being too kind. That was Thea’s whole problem. She was always too kind.

Watching it again on her own, on her bed, with the sound up, Xola had to admit it was brave.

She didn’t like admitting it. She did it anyway.

She skipped on. June. The night Higu told the world about the old guild, from the eye of the dome. Two hundred and eighty-four thousand people had watched that. Gigi was on Higu’s stream for most of it, sitting beside her on a stone ledge, quiet, holding tea. Xola watched Gigi’s own stream of the same night, from the same ledge, from a slightly different angle. Gigi barely said anything on it. She let Higu have the whole night. Once, near the end, Higu said something that made her voice crack, and Gigi put her hand out on the ledge, flat, and Higu put hers on top of it, and Gigi’s camera just stayed on their two hands for a long time.

Xola stopped the video and sat there.

Then she went on, because she was going to find it. She was sure it was in there somewhere. The thing she was looking for.

It was the chat that did it, in the end.

Gigi’s chat was loud. Bigger than Xola’s, bigger than Thea’s. Thousands of people most nights, with names like thornbush_ and briarpatch and hedgehog_hq. And in April, in the week the last wave came in, Gigi’s chat had found out about Thea.

Of course it had. Thea had come back from a year away and streamed her first night in the game with her old face and a new tower shield. It had gone round. People remembered Thea. People remembered that Thea and Gigi had been something, once. Nobody quite knew what, but they’d been in each other’s streams for a while, and then they hadn’t. And chats were chats.

Xola watched the video of that night with her whole body tight. This was it. This was where it would be. Gigi had always been good at being the one who’d been wronged. She’d been good at it on the phone, through Thea; she’d been good at it in chats. Xola waited for the long pause and the sad little laugh. For it’s complicated and we don’t really talk any more and some people just change. She waited for the thing that would turn Thea into a story Gigi told her chat about herself.

On the screen, Gigi was crushing something in a mortar, for Alchemy. She looked at the chat for a second. Her face didn’t do anything.

“Not mine to say,” said Gigi. “Next question.”

And she went back to the mortar.

The chat tried again, the way chats did. But are you friends? Did something happen? Gigi spill. Gigi didn’t look up.

“Not mine,” she said. “Ask me about the potion. It’s going to be green.”

Xola rewound it and watched it again.

Then she went to the next night, and the next. She went through April, and May, and June. She skipped the fights and the long walks and the hours of Alchemy, and went looking for the places where the chat said Thea. There were lots of them. Thea was streaming every night now. Thea hit two thousand. Thea was at the front, chat said, have you seen her, gigi, she’s tanking now. Somebody clipped the Turn and posted it in Gigi’s chat, with THEA’S A WHEEL in capitals.

Every single time, Gigi said the same thing, or near it.

Not mine to say.

She’s doing well. Good for her. Next question.

Not mine.

Once, in June, very late, with only a few hundred people watching, somebody asked do you miss her. Gigi was sitting on the ledge of the eye with her knees up, on her own, looking at the moon come up through the hole in the roof. She didn’t answer that one at all. She just reached forward and turned the camera off. The stream ended there.

Xola looked at the time. It was nearly three in the morning. She’d been watching for eleven hours, on and off, since she came out of the egg. She’d skipped and rewound and skipped, and she hadn’t found it.

Gigi had never said Thea’s name. Not once. Her chat said it all the time, and she never said it.

Xola put the phone face down on the bed and lay back and looked at her ceiling.

That wasn’t proof of anything. She knew that. One person not saying a name on stream wasn’t proof that they’d changed. It wasn’t sorry. It wasn’t anything. It was just a thing someone hadn’t done.

But it was the thing Xola had been certain she’d find. And it wasn’t there.

She rang Higu at three in the morning, which was five in the morning in Australia, which was unforgivable. Higu answered on the second ring.

“You’re up,” said Xola.

“I’m on the roof,” said Higu. She was in the game, then. Xola could hear the wind. “The moon’s going down. It’s nice. What’s wrong?”

“Nothing’s wrong.”

“Xola,” said Higu. “It’s five in the morning.”

“I watched her streams,” said Xola. “Gigi’s. All of them. Well. Not all of them. Some of them. Eleven hours of them.”

There was a pause on the line.

“Oh,” said Higu.

“I’m not saying anything nice about her.”

“Okay.”

“I’m asking you something,” said Xola. “And I know you’re the road. Thea told me. I’m not in the middle, I’m the road, I carry what people put on me. I know. So I’m putting this on you.” She took a breath. “Is she different?”

Higu didn’t answer straight away. Xola had known she wouldn’t. Higu never answered straight away when it mattered.

“I don’t carry answers about people,” said Higu, eventually. “That’s not a thing a road can do. A road can only tell you what’s walked down it.”

“Then tell me that.”

“Okay.” Xola heard her shift on the stone, getting comfortable. “She brings tea. Most nights. She comes up two hundred and twelve steps with two cups and sits in my place, and she doesn’t say anything, and we watch the moon. Some nights she says it’ll fall down. That’s her thing. Everything she builds, she says it’ll fall down. And then she builds it anyway.” A pause. “She says my fault now. Sometimes. Not often. She didn’t use to. She used to say it was everybody else’s fault. You know that. You were there.”

“I was there,” said Xola.

“She says it now,” said Higu. “About small things, mostly. A potion she got wrong. A thorn she laid late. But she says it, out loud, and she doesn’t put it on anybody else. The first time she did it I nearly fell off the roof.” Higu laughed, very softly. “She’s trying to be better than she was, Xola. That’s all I can tell you. That’s what’s walked down the road. Some days she’s better at it than others.”

“Trying isn’t enough,” said Xola.

“No,” said Higu. “It’s not. It’s just what there is.”

Xola sat on her bed with her knees up and the phone against her ear and didn’t say anything.

“Can I put something on the road for you?” said Higu. “Not from her. From me.”

“Okay.”

“She knows about you,” said Higu. “About the phone. I didn’t tell her. She told me. In June, up here, the night after she’d sat on the north steps with Drew. She’d worked it out. She said, Xola was on the phone for all of it.” A pause. “She knows who held Thea up, Xola. She knows it wasn’t her.”

Xola had nothing to say to that. Nothing at all. She’d had something to say to everything else for eleven hours, and now she didn’t.

“I’m going to go,” said Higu gently. “The moon’s down. Go to sleep. No hurry, on anything.”

“Everybody keeps saying that,” said Xola. Her voice had gone thick.

“Because it keeps being true,” said Higu, and rang off.

She didn’t go to sleep. She lay there with the phone and thought about she knows who held Thea up. After a while, for no reason she could have explained, she opened her messages and found Ice.

He’d be asleep. It was half three. Ice went to bed at sensible times, because Suzu and Gabe made him; it was a thing in his chat, go to bed, you go to bed. She typed anyway.

She laughed, alone on her bed, with her face wet.

He hadn’t asked her anything. He hadn’t said is it about Thea or is everything okay or any of the things everyone else would have said. He’d just told her the chairs were wobbly and the tea was good. And then come when you come, like the door had been open the whole time and always would be. He’d always been like that. He’d been like that for as long as she’d known him, and she’d forgotten. You could forget a thing like that, in a bad year.

She put the phone down and went to sleep.

She found Thea on Saturday morning at the Sallows, at the edge of the willows, sitting on a fallen trunk with her shield beside her and her hammer across her knees. Waiting for the others, who were late, because Xyro had lost a boot.

Xola sat down on the trunk next to her.

“Okay,” said Xola. “Here’s what I think.”

Thea looked at her.

“I don’t trust her,” said Xola. “I’m not going to. Not for a long time. Maybe not ever. I’m not saying anything nice about her, and I’m not ever saying anything nice about her, and if she says one wrong thing to you, the trident thing still stands.”

“Okay,” said Thea.

“But I watched her streams.”

Thea went very still.

“Last night,” said Xola. “Eleven hours. I went looking for the thing. You know the thing. Where she makes you into a story about her. Where she does the sad laugh and says it’s complicated to six thousand people.” She looked at the willows. “Her chat says your name all the time, Thea. Every week. Thea’s back. Thea’s tanking. Gigi, are you going to play with Thea. All the time.”

“And?”

“And she’s never said it,” said Xola. “Not once. Not in four months. She says not mine to say. She says next question. One night somebody asked if she missed you and she turned the stream off.” Xola picked at the bark of the trunk. “She never used you. Not once. I looked for eleven hours and she never used you.”

Thea didn’t say anything for a long time. She put her hand flat on the hammer’s head and looked at it.

“I didn’t know that,” she said finally.

“Nor did I. Till last night.”

“Why did you look?”

Xola thought about it. She wanted to say to prove I was right, because that was what she’d told herself at the start. But that wasn’t why. Not really. Not by the end.

“Because if you’re going,” she said, “I wanted to know what you were going to. I didn’t want to be guessing. I’ve been guessing for nine weeks. I’m done guessing.” She turned to look at Thea properly. “And I rang Higu. At five in the morning. Her time.”

“Xola.”

“She was on the roof. She didn’t mind. She said—“ Xola stopped. She didn’t know if it was hers to pass on. Then she decided it was, because Higu had put it on the road for her, and roads went both ways. “She said Gigi knows. About the phone. About me. Gigi told her. She said, Xola was on the phone for all of it.”

Thea put her hand over her mouth.

They sat on the trunk. A doe came to the edge of the water fifty paces off and looked at them, and decided they weren’t worth it, and went back into the willows.

“So,” said Xola. “Here’s the thing. I’m not being nice to her. I’m being nice to you. I’m being nice to you, Thea. And you want to go.”

“I haven’t decided.”

“You decided on the bed. The second Higu said chair. You just didn’t know it yet.”

Thea opened her mouth to say something, and shut it, and opened it again.

“Yes,” said Thea. “I think I did.”

“So we go,” said Xola.

“You don’t have to—“

“Thea,” said Xola. “If you finish that sentence, I’m pushing you in the river.”

Thea didn’t finish it.

They told the others that evening in the common room of the lodging-house on Tanner’s Row, with the fire going and Patience in front of it.

Thea told it. She didn’t tell it in order this time. She told it short, which wasn’t like her at all. Xola watched her decide how much was hers to tell and how much wasn’t, and draw the line in a place Xola thought was exactly right.

“There’s somebody in the round town I used to know,” said Thea. “Before. It was a long time ago, and it ended badly. It was dark, for a while. Xola was there for it.” She looked at the fire. “She’s sent a message. Through Higu. She says there’s a chair. Six of them. The woman who keeps the Hall set them out, for us. And I want to go and see.”

The common room was quiet.

PenguChi put down her sewing. She’d been mending the corner of Thea’s cloak, where a buck had caught it.

“Okay,” said PenguChi. “We’re going slow.”

That was all she said about it. That was very PenguChi.

Tif’s ears had gone flat at dark. They came up again, slowly, one at a time.

“I’m bringing tea,” said Tif. “Lots. The calming kind. Kotori’s recipe. And I’m going to behave, and so is everybody.” She looked at Xyro.

“I always behave,” said Xyro.

“You fell in six channels.”

“That’s not behaviour. That’s physics.” Xyro looked at Thea. “Where’s the round town?”

“East.”

“How far?”

“Eight days.”

“Cool,” said Xyro. “I’ve never walked eight days. I’ve never walked eight hours. Does it hurt?”

“Yes,” said Xola.

“Cool,” said Xyro again, a bit less sure.

Everybody looked at Slep. Slep was sitting on the floor with his back against the wall and Patience’s head on his knee, and he hadn’t said anything. He didn’t, mostly. But they all looked at him anyway, because when Slep did say something it was usually the thing.

“Patience has,” said Slep.

“Has what?”

“Walked eight days,” said Slep. “More than that. She walked to Millrace with me from the cliffs. Three and a half. Then Fernwick. Then here. She’s walked further than most players.” He scratched the sheep between the ears. “She’ll show you how.”

Patience made a small, satisfied noise.

So that was the six of them. Thea didn’t ask for a vote. Nobody offered one. It just turned out, the way it turned out with the six of them most of the time, that everybody had already decided.

“Sunday,” said Thea. “First dawn. We’ll go through Fernwick. I want to see Sedge first. She’ll want to know.”

Later, upstairs, Xola went past Thea’s door and it was open a crack. Thea was sitting on the end of her bed with the little window up in the corner of her eye. She was typing something very slowly, and stopping, and typing again.

Xola stood in the corridor and watched through the crack.

A year ago she’d have gone in. She’d have sat on the bed and looked over Thea’s shoulder and said say this, or don’t say that, or let me. She’d have filled it in. She’d have been so sure she knew what Thea meant that she’d have said it for her, and Thea would have nodded. It would have been easier for both of them.

She didn’t go in.

Thea typed. Stopped. Typed. Read it over for a long time. Then she sent it, and put the window away, and sat there with her hands in her lap, looking at nothing.

Xola never saw what it said. She found out later, from Higu, on a roof eight days east. It was four short lines.

Xola went back to her own room and lay down, and found she was smiling at the ceiling. She hadn’t said a single word for Thea all evening. She’d let Thea say every word herself.

It had been the hardest thing she’d done in the game. Harder than the pike, or the river, or eleven hours of Gigi’s chat.

She thought it might also have been the most helpful.
