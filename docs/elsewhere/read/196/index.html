Fourteen Days

3,280 words · 14 min read

The goose was still there on the second morning, and Ice walked round it.

It was standing in the middle of the road a mile past the first milestone, exactly where it had been the night before. A big grey farm goose with an orange beak and the expression of something that had been told it owned the road by somebody important. When he came up the road at the first dawn it lowered its head and hissed at him.

He looked at it for a while. It looked at him.

He thought about Hyvred’s letter. The goose is probably a boss. Don’t fight the goose.

He walked round it, through the wet grass at the side of the road. The goose turned on the spot to watch him go, hissing the whole time, and didn’t follow.

He read Tif’s line and felt his face do the thing it had apparently been doing since Monday. He didn’t know what it looked like. He could feel it, though, now that somebody had told him. A warmth, up the sides of his neck.

He kept walking.

It was fourteen days to Masmark.

He’d walked a lot of roads in the game. Fernwick to Brackenford in the first week, over and over, on the raised causeway across the fen. Brackenford to the round town. The round town to Elmcross and back. Up and down the north tunnel every morning for months. He liked roads. You knew where you were on a road. You knew what came next: the next milestone, the next bend, the next town.

He didn’t know this one.

It went south and east out of the round town, off the edge of everything Kei had drawn. Past the White Hills on the first day, where the chalk giants were cut into the turf, a man with a staff and a horse with too many legs. Past the Millrace road on the second, where it forked. And then on, into country nobody in Constellation had been to.

He streamed all of it. That was the job. He streamed it the way he’d streamed everything for a year, POV, quiet. Not talking much. Looking at things.

His chat didn’t seem to mind. It was smaller than usual, sixty thousand, seventy, because a man walking on a road for fourteen days wasn’t the duel. But it was a good chat. The regulars were all there. Teh Peng and Frog and Mangoes and Kitty and Night Owl. And every so often somebody new would come in, see what was happening, and say wait is this the guy from the gate. And Tif would say yes. behave, and they would.

He hadn’t known the gate would be on anything. He’d gone dark. But he’d forgotten that the whole of Constellation had been standing round the fountain with their own Eyes on. Hana’s had been pointing straight at him, by accident, she said afterward, crying, from the very beginning. And Leaka’s. And Kei’s. And about fifteen others.

It was on the forum by noon on Monday. Somebody had cut it together. The south steps, the fountain, a woman with gold horns holding out an arrow. Then the man’s Eye going out, and the clip switching to someone else’s view, and the rest.

The title said: HE WENT DARK AND IT DIDN’T MATTER.

He hadn’t watched it. He knew what happened. He’d been there.

Her letters came every night.

They came at the second dusk, when she logged out, or at the third dawn when she logged back in, or sometimes both. Long ones. Short ones. Ones that started in the middle of a sentence because she’d had to write them in a hurry between Scree-Runners on the high fells. He read them on the road in the corner of his window, walking. He read them again at night on the edge of his pod in the back room at Melaka Raya, with the fan going round, on his phone.

He laughed, on the edge of his pod, at that one. Out loud. Gabe, in his own pod beside him, opened the lid and looked at him and shut it again.

That same night, the second night, it was Chinese New Year’s Eve.

He logged out at nine, the way he always did. He sat on the edge of his pod in the back room at Melaka Raya for a while, the way he always did. Then he went home through the town in the dark, past the shuttered shoplots with red paper on their doors. Past the kopitiam where the auntie had put up a string of plastic lanterns that flashed. Up in the lift to the long corridor on his floor, past the unit opposite with its padlocked grille and an empty pot by the door. He’d heard Deston had put a cactus in it on Sunday. It was very small.

There was reunion dinner on his parents’ side, through the opening in the wall. He’d be there. He was always there.

He stopped in the corridor before he went in, by the window, and read the letter that had come at nine.

The country changed on the fifth day.

He’d been walking through farmland since the White Hills. Hedges, fields, villages with names on boards, the ordinary green of the middle of the world. On the fifth day the road began to climb. Not steeply. A long slow rise, all day, so slow you didn’t notice it until you looked back and saw the land you’d come from spread out below you. Grey and green and small. The White Hills a pale line on the horizon. Beyond them, if you knew where to look, a grey thumb that might have been the dome of the round town, and might have been a cloud.

He looked back at it for a long time on the fifth evening. Then he took the arrow out of the strap of his pack and held it up on his palm.

It leaned. Back the way he’d come. North and west. Toward the grey thumb on the horizon, and the north tunnel under it, and the cold place.

He put it back.

The monsters changed too. In the farmland there’d been nothing worth stopping for, things he’d passed at Lv 30. On the long rise they started to come out of the hedges at night. Lv 48, 49, 50. Things like badgers the size of carts, with bone-white stripes. Long low things in the ditches that whistled. Once, on the sixth night, something very tall in a field that he didn’t fight, because it didn’t come any closer and he didn’t want it to.

He killed what came. He didn’t go looking. He wasn’t there to level.

He didn’t stop to look at that either.

On the ninth day, at a ferry, somebody recognised him.

It was a wide brown river at the bottom of a valley, too wide to bridge, with a flat wooden ferry on a rope and an old man to pull it. There were other travellers waiting. A party of five in green, all about fifty. Two women together with spears. A man on his own with a crossbow, sitting on a post, eating an apple.

The man with the crossbow looked at him when he came down the bank.

Ice didn’t look back much. He went and stood at the side of the landing, out of the way, the way he always stood. He waited for the ferry.

The man with the crossbow finished his apple. He threw the core in the river. Then he looked at Ice again, for a long time, and raised his hand. Not a wave. Two fingers, to the side of his eyebrow, like a salute. And said, quite quietly, so only Ice would hear:

“o7.”

Ice looked at him.

He hadn’t heard anybody say that out loud in a very long time. Not in this game. Not in years. It was a thing people had typed in a different game, a long time ago, in a black sky full of ships. It meant hello, or goodbye, or respect, or good fight, depending. It was a little man saluting. Everybody in that sky had known it. Nobody here would.

He raised two fingers to his own eyebrow.

“o7,” said Ice.

The man with the crossbow grinned. He didn’t say anything else. He didn’t ask anything, or say you’re the one from the duel, or you’re the one from the gate. He just nodded, once, as if that were all there was to say. And when the ferry came, he got on it, and sat at the far end, and didn’t look at Ice again the whole way across.

On the far bank, he got off first and went up the road ahead, fast, and was gone.

On the fourteenth day, at the first dusk, he came over the rim.

He hadn’t known it was a rim. The road had been climbing for nine days, slow and long. Then, on the fourteenth afternoon, it got steep, suddenly, the way the last flight of the two hundred and twelve got steep. Sixty-six steps, the hardest ones, with the wind. He climbed it in the dusk. Up a road cut into black rock, switchbacking. Past other travellers going up and coming down, more of them every hour, in every colour, with banners. And at the top, at the very top, the road went through a cutting in the rock, a great notch, like a gate.

He walked through it.

And the world fell away in front of him.

He stopped.

He stood in the notch at the top of the road in the evening light. Below him, a long way below, was a lake.

He’d known it was a lake. Harlan had said a lake the size of a country. He’d thought Harlan was exaggerating; Harlan always exaggerated. Harlan hadn’t been exaggerating. It was a great round lake inside a ring of black mountains, the inside of a crater so big that its far side was blue with distance. The water was dark and still and so wide it went silver at the edges in the dusk. There was an island in the middle, very small at this distance, with something on it that might have been a tower.

And round the lake, the whole way round, on the inside of the crater’s rim, a city.

It went all the way round. A ring of a city, on the slopes between the mountains and the water. Roofs and walls and towers, terraces going down to the lake in steps, harbours. Streets running down to the water like the spokes of a wheel. Bridges. Thousands of lights coming on in the dusk, all the way round, on every terrace and every street and every harbour. And between the lights, everywhere, banners. Hundreds of them. On towers and walls and the roofs of Halls. Every colour there was, and some he’d never seen. Black with one white line. Red with a ledger. Deep blue with a gold line top to bottom. Green and gold and silver and purple. A great one in dark red and black, which he didn’t know, on a Hall the size of a hill.

Roads came down into it from the rim. Not one road. Dozens. He could see them from here, cut into the black rock of the crater wall all the way round, every one coming down from a notch like the one he was standing in. From every direction. From every region of the map. All of them coming down to the water.

Where many roads meet, the window had said.

[ Masmark · Great Lake Masmark ]

[ A city of the fiftieth level. All roads that come this far come here. ]

He stood in the notch and looked at it for a long time.

Behind him, a party of six came up the last switchback and stopped beside him in the notch, breathing hard, and looked too. One of them said a word in a language the game didn’t bother to translate, because everybody knew what it meant. Then they went on down.

He didn’t. Not yet.

He took the arrow out of the strap of his pack and held it up on his palm, in the notch at the top of the road, with the whole of Masmark below him in the dusk.

It leaned. Back. North and west. Over his shoulder, the way he’d come. Fourteen days of road, the long rise and the ferry and the White Hills, to a grey thumb on the horizon he couldn’t see from here. To a cold place at the end of a tunnel.
