Live from Elsewhere · Volume 7: Keep the Lamps

Chapter 170

First Dawn

3,723 words · 16 min read

On the first morning Kestrel lost sixty-one times, and she counted every one.

The round market at first dawn was a different place. She’d only ever seen it full, the week of the seven, when it had been louder than any Hall. At first dawn there was nobody in it at all. The stalls were shuttered. The fountain ran on its own. The light came in low and grey down the east road and lay along the paving in long stripes, and the nine dark stones of the ring were just stones. A pigeon was asleep on one of them.

Xav was already there. He was standing in the middle of the ring with his two blades still in their sheaths and his hands in his pockets, looking at the pigeon.

“It won’t move,” he said, when she came across the market.

“Have you asked it?”

“Ama Corrie says it’s been sleeping on that stone since before her mother.” He nudged it, very gently, with his boot. The pigeon opened one eye, looked at him with deep contempt, and closed it again. “We’ll work round it.”

She stepped over a dark stone into the ring. It felt like nothing. Last week it had felt like walking onto a stage.

“What first?” said Kestrel.

“Draw,” said Xav. “Shoot me.”

She looked at him.

“I’m not going to do anything,” said Xav. “I’m going to stand here. Shoot me.”

So she did.

She drew, and he wasn’t there. Not far. A foot to the left, at a walk. Her arrow went past his shoulder and over the fountain and into the shutter of the chestnut stall with a thock. The pigeon didn’t wake up.

“Again,” said Xav.

She drew, and he wasn’t there.

“Again.”

She drew, and he wasn’t there.

By the twentieth she’d stopped being surprised. By the fortieth she’d stopped being angry. By the sixty-first she’d stopped feeling anything at all except a sort of grey, patient tiredness in her drawing arm and the back of her neck. The chestnut stall’s shutter looked like a hedgehog. He hadn’t drawn his blades. He hadn’t moved more than three feet in any direction. He’d stood in the middle of the ring with his hands in his pockets and stepped, a foot one way, a foot the other, at a walk. Sixty-one times. And she hadn’t touched him once.

“Stop,” said Xav.

She stopped. Her arm was shaking. It never shook. It had shaken on the oak at Elmcross, and now here.

“Four frames,” said Xav.

“What?”

“You decide four frames before your hand moves. I can see it. Here.” He touched his own shoulder, the right one, at the point where it met the neck. “It goes down. A tiny bit. When you’ve picked where you’re going to shoot. Then the hand. Then the string.” He shrugged. “I see it at four. Dec sees it at six.”

“Six,” said Kestrel.

“Gabe slowed it down. In the duel. Six frames before your hand.”

She stood in the grey light with the bow in her hand and her arm shaking, and thought about that. Six frames. She’d watched the duel a hundred times. She’d never once looked at her own shoulder.

“How do I stop it?” she said.

“Decide later,” said Xav.

He made her pull the arrows out of the chestnut shutter herself. Then he made her sweep the ring. Then he went up the Hall steps and in, without saying goodbye, and she was left alone in the round market at the second dawn with a broom and sixty-one holes in somebody else’s shutter.

The chestnut man came at nine. He looked at the shutter for a long time.

“VANTA,” he said.

“Sorry,” said Kestrel.

“Hm,” said the chestnut man. He looked at the holes again. Then he went inside and came out with a piece of chalk and wrote across the bottom of the shutter in large letters, under the holes: KESTREL WAS HERE (61). He put the chalk back in his pocket and opened his stall.

She stood and looked at it for a while.

Then she went to the Hart, where VANTA had taken the back rooms for a fortnight, and ate a very large breakfast, and didn’t tell Brann anything.

Decide later.

She thought about it all that day. Out on the Fell Country with her ten, killing Scree-Runners on the high ground in the wind, she thought about it between every pull. She watched her own shoulder in the corner of her eye. And there it was. She’d never seen it before. Now she couldn’t stop seeing it. Before every draw, a tiny drop, like a nod. That one. Then the hand. Then the string.

She tried to stop it. She tried to draw without picking. It was horrible. Her arrows went everywhere. Ottie asked if she was all right. Brann, who’d lain on his face in the middle of a market four days before and laughed about it, looked at her for a long time and said nothing at all.

That night on her stream, a hundred and nine thousand people watched her miss a Scree-Runner four times in a row from twelve paces.

“I’m busy at first dawn,” said Kestrel, to the eye of the camera, and missed again.

Brann found her in the Hart’s back room at the third dusk, sitting on the end of her bed with her bow across her knees, not doing anything.

He stood in the doorway. He didn’t come in. Brann never came into a room until he’d been asked; it was one of the things she’d picked him for, six years ago, in a different game.

“Kes,” he said.

“Mm.”

“Four in a row. From twelve paces.” He said it without any weight on it, the way he’d say rain later. “On stream.”

“I know.”

“You’ve never missed four in a row.”

“I know, Brann.”

He waited. He was very good at waiting. She thought, not for the first time, that he and Ice would have got on, if one of them hadn’t put the other on his face in a market.

“I’m losing sixty times a morning,” said Kestrel at last, to the bow. “To a man with a sketchbook. In an empty market. With a pigeon watching.” She laughed, a bit. “He hasn’t even drawn his blades. He just steps. A foot. And I can’t touch him. I’ve never been so bad at anything in my life.”

Brann considered this from the doorway.

“Are you all right?” he said.

She thought about it properly, because it was Brann, and he’d know if she didn’t.

“I think I’m happy,” she said. She sounded surprised. She was. “Is that stupid? I’ve been the best at this for six years. And for six years I knew exactly how good I was, every morning, to the frame. And now I don’t. I don’t know anything. I’m losing to a pigeon.” She looked up at him. “It’s the first time in six years I’ve learned something I didn’t already know.”

Brann nodded slowly. He looked, for a moment, the way he’d looked lying on his back in the middle of the ring at six per cent, laughing at the sky.

“Then lose to the pigeon,” he said. “I’ll hold the room.”

He went. She heard him on the stairs, and then the creak of the chair outside her door, where he’d sit until she slept. He’d done that on the bad nights, years ago, in the other game, and she’d never once asked him to.

She lay down with the bow beside her. She slept better than she had since August.

On the second morning she lost fifty-four times. On the third, forty-eight.

On the fourth he drew his blades.

“Now I’m going to come at you,” he said. “Slowly. And you’re going to shoot where I’ll be. Not where I am.”

“How do I know where you’ll be?”

“You don’t,” said Xav. “That’s the point. You have to decide late. When I’ve already decided. Then you’ll know.”

It was the hardest thing she’d ever done in a game. She’d spent six years learning to decide early. That was what a bow was. You decided, and you held the decision, and the held decision was a weapon. That was what the held draw was. The whole of her famous half-second at the top was a decision so big and so obvious that everybody froze and waited for it.

“Your held draw’s the best thing you’ve got,” said Xav, on the fifth morning, while she sat on a dark stone with her arm in her lap. “And it’s the worst thing you could possibly do against him.”

“It nearly worked.”

“It didn’t nearly work.” He was sitting on the next stone, cleaning one blade with a cloth. “He let you hold it. He stood there and let you. You were holding a decision, and he could see it from the moon. So he waited till you couldn’t hold it any more and walked in under it.” He turned the blade to the light. “You can’t hold a decision at him. He’ll just wait. He’s very good at waiting. It’s his favourite thing.”

“So what do I do?”

“Don’t decide,” said Xav. “Then there’s nothing to see.”

He had a sketchbook. She’d seen it at Elmcross, and at the ring all through the week of the seven. He always had it on his knee, and he never showed anybody. On the sixth morning he left it on the dark stone with the pigeon while he went to fetch water from the fountain. She didn’t mean to look. She told herself she didn’t. She looked.

It was full of feet.

Pages and pages of them. Two kinds, pencil and ink. The ink ones were always in the right place. Round the ring, in and out, a dot and a line and then the ink, already there. Seven pages, one for each night. She recognised the Brann one: a solid pencil square that never moved, and the ink going round and round it in a perfect circle, and then a long pencil smear going forward off the square. Onto its face.

She turned back to the front.

Elmcross. The oak floor. Her own feet, in pencil. And his, in ink, walking her down. Every step. Every one of them first. Xav had drawn it from the edge of the ring, sitting cross-legged on the floor, while she was losing. She hadn’t known. She’d thought he’d been drawing the hall.

At the very back there was a page with only two drawings on it, very small, in the margin, side by side. The same step, twice. A man going in under a big sword. In one of them the back foot came round late, a breath late. In the other it came round on time.

Under them, in Xav’s handwriting, a single word.

New.

“Don’t,” said Xav, behind her.

She jumped. She put the book down. He was standing with the water skin, not angry, looking at the sketchbook on the stone.

“Sorry,” she said.

“It’s all right.” He sat down and picked it up and closed it, without looking at what page it was open at. He didn’t need to. “I don’t show people because they ask what they’re for. And I don’t know what they’re for.”

“They’re him.”

“They’re where he was going to be,” said Xav. “Before he got there. That’s not the same as him.”

She thought about the margin. Two steps. New.

“Can you beat him?” said Kestrel.

Xav didn’t answer straight away. He drank from the water skin. He looked at the pigeon.

“Draw,” he said.

On the seventh morning, the whole ring was full of people.

Not a crowd. Twenty, perhaps. She came across the market at first dawn and stopped. VANTA was there, all ten of the Elm Below, sitting along the Hall steps. She hadn’t told them. She’d never told them. And Riverbank was there, the other seven, sitting on the fountain’s rim. A loud one with two knives was counting them, for no reason. A big one with an enormous shield was eating bread.

“I didn’t tell them,” said Xav, from the middle of the ring, before she could say anything.

“I didn’t tell mine.”

“Brann followed you,” said Xav. “On Thursday. He’s been sitting behind the chestnut stall every morning for three days.” He nodded at the stall. It still said KESTREL WAS HERE in chalk. The number had been rubbed out and written again every morning; it said 48 · 44 · 39 now. “He was very quiet. I don’t think he thought I’d see him.”

She looked at the steps. Brann looked back at her, enormously calm. He raised one hand.

“And mine followed me,” said Xav, “because Rui Xiang can’t keep anything to himself, and I told Malcolm on Tuesday.” He shrugged. “Nobody’s streaming. I asked. It’s still ours.”

She stood at the edge of the ring for a while.

“Fine,” she said. “Fine.” And she stepped over the dark stone. And she lost thirty-six times in front of twenty people, which was worse than a hundred thousand, somehow, and also better.

The light came slowly, the way it does.

On the ninth morning she noticed she’d stopped watching her shoulder. On the tenth she noticed she’d stopped watching his feet. She’d been watching them for a week, because that was what you’d think you should watch, and they told you nothing. They were always somewhere else. Xav’s feet went where Xav went after he’d gone. Watching them was like reading yesterday’s newspaper.

On the eleventh she stopped watching him at all.

She didn’t know how to say what she did instead. She tried, afterwards, on her stream, and to Brann, and to herself. She couldn’t say it right. It was a bit like the opposite of aiming. She drew, and she didn’t pick, and she held the bow at the top without holding anything at all. There was no decision in it. Just the string and the arrow and the ring and the man in it, all at once, the way you see a whole room when you come into it.

And then he moved, and she knew where he’d decided to go, because he’d gone. And she let go.

It went past his ear.

Not through him. Past him. An inch. But it was the first one in eleven mornings that hadn’t gone where he’d been. It went where he was.

Xav stopped.

He stood still in the middle of the ring, with the arrow standing in the chestnut shutter behind him and a very small expression on his face. On the fountain’s rim, the loud one with the knives stood up. Brann, on the steps, put his hand over his mouth.

“Again,” said Xav.

She drew. She didn’t decide.

On the twelfth morning, at the ninth draw, it opened a thin line on his left forearm.

The fountain went mad. Riverbank stood up on the rim and shouted. The loud one with the knives was jumping up and down. The big one with the shield had dropped his bread. VANTA, on the steps, didn’t shout. They were VANTA. But Ottie had both hands in the air, and Brann was standing up for the first time in a week. And Kestrel stood in the ring with the bow still up, staring at the window over Xav’s head as if it were the first one she’d ever seen.

Xav looked down at his forearm. At the line.

Then he looked up at her and grinned. It was the first time she’d seen him grin properly. It made him look about seventeen.

“Good,” he said. “That’s it. That’s the thing.” He lifted one of his blades and pointed it at her, not unkindly. “Now do it a thousand times.”

On the last morning, the fourteenth, there was nobody watching. She didn’t know how he’d done it. Perhaps he’d asked them. Perhaps they’d just known. The ring was empty again, the way it had been on the first day, except for the pigeon on its stone.

They didn’t fight much. A few draws. She touched him twice in twenty. It was still the best thing that had happened to her in a year.

Afterwards they sat on two dark stones next to each other, the pigeon between them. She swept. He watched her sweep. Then they sat.

“Can I ask you something?” said Kestrel.

“You will anyway.”

“I asked you on the sixth morning if you could beat him. You said draw.”

“I did.”

“I’m asking again.”

Xav was quiet for a long time. The market was waking up round them. The chestnut man raised his shutter and looked at the chalk and the holes and sighed with enormous satisfaction, the way a man sighs who now has the most famous shutter in the round town. Somewhere up the Hall steps, the south door opened, and Ama Corrie looked out, and went in again.

“No,” said Xav.

“Then why haven’t you fought him?” she said. “Here. In this game. I’ve watched every VOD. Ten months. You’ve stood behind him, and next to him, and on a bank waiting for him. You’ve never once fought him. Not even for fun.”

“No.”

“Why not?”

Xav looked at the ring. At the nine dark stones, and the scorch by the third one, and the crack where the Crimson Ledger sword had stood.

“Because he’d never hold back,” he said. “Not against me. He’d hold back against anybody else. He held back against you. He’d have to, or it’d be over in a minute.” He turned the sketchbook over in his hands. “He’d never do that to me. He’d think it was rude.” He paused. “And then I’d know.”

“Know what?”

“How far,” said Xav.

She thought about the oak floor at Elmcross. About going down on one knee with her back to a crowd at nine per cent. About the moment she understood she’d been answering him all along. About sitting in a back room in the Hart that night, unable to sleep. And then, after, sleeping better than she had in a year.

“I found out how far,” said Kestrel. “At Elmcross. On one knee.” She looked at him. “It’s better. Knowing. I’ve slept better since. I didn’t think I would.” She shrugged. “I thought it’d be the worst thing. It wasn’t. It was the first time in six years I knew exactly where I was.”

Xav didn’t say anything.

And then, across the market, the Hall’s south door opened again, and a man came out of it and down the steps. Sword on his hip, shield on his back, alone, at first dawn, heading for the north tunnel the way he did every morning.

He saw them. He lifted a hand. He kept walking.

Kestrel watched Xav watch him go.

She’d seen that look before. Not on Xav. On herself, in a window, in December, on the stair at the bottom of the drowned church. Eleven hours behind somebody she’d never heard of. She’d been looking at a door that somebody else had walked through first.

“Dec,” said Xav.

He didn’t shout it. He said it in an ordinary voice, across the round market. And the man at the bottom of the steps stopped.

He turned round.

“Wednesday,” said Xav. “Third dawn. Not the ring.” He nodded at the Hall. “Downstairs.”

The man with the shield looked at him across the empty market for a long moment. Kestrel couldn’t see his face properly from here. But she saw something happen to the way he stood. A tiny change, all through him, the way the light changes when a cloud moves.

“Okay,” said Ice.

Then he turned round and went on toward the north tunnel. And Kestrel, who’d watched every one of his VODs and knew how he walked better than she knew how she did, saw that he was walking very slightly faster than before.

She looked at Xav.

He was sitting on his dark stone, with the sketchbook on his knee, open at a blank page. He wasn’t drawing anything. He was just looking at the page as if something might come up out of it.

“You’ve got a face,” said Kestrel.

“Everyone keeps saying that,” said Xav.
