Live from Elsewhere · Volume 10: The Middle

Chapter 251

Three Hundred

3,807 words · 17 min read

Suzu lit the best candle at the edge of the still water, and for the first time in two years he went first.

He’d never gone first. Not at anything. He’d stood behind Ice in a wind game and behind Ice in a fen and behind Ice at the bottom of a lake. He’d been the one who said good when somebody else had done the thing. He’d said maybe four hundred words to Deston in all the years he’d known him, and every one of them had been an answer.

He’d gone first once. In the Long Watch, at the eighty-first lantern, with his hands shaking. Go on. He hadn’t decided. It had just come out.

He’d decided this.

“I count,” he’d said, at the ring, at the edge, at eight in the evening when they came back out of the egg. To all of them. To Ice. “Isa said. Say the number. So the stones know you. I’m the one carrying it.” He’d put his hand on the strap, on the cat’s-paw. “I go first.”

Ice had looked at him for a long time across the ring.

Then he’d untied himself from the front of the rope. And tied himself on again, behind Suzu. Second.

“Okay,” said Ice.

The best candle. Mother Dipp’s. Tall, white, plain, straight. The one she’d given Chernn when Chernn left Tallow Green, and Chernn had given him at the Hart in April. So you can see your feet. Without using the lamp. He’d carried it in his pack for seven months, wrapped in a bit of Mother Dipp’s apron. He hadn’t lit it. Not on the road, not past the edge. He’d used the others.

He lit it now. From the watch, in the middle of the ring, before Kotori put it out with her fingers and gave it back to the tin.

It caught. A small yellow flame, standing straight up. He held it low, down by his knees, the way Isa said her mother had. And looked at his feet.

He could see them.

Not well. Not much. But the best candle, held low, made a little circle of light on the ground the size of a cart wheel. And in the circle, his boots. The first thing he’d seen of himself in nineteen nights. And the ground under them, short dry heather. And at the edge of the circle, in front of his toes, where the heather stopped—

Black.

Not nothing. Black. Flat and perfectly smooth and so dark it seemed to drink the candle’s light. The still water. He could see the edge of it, where the heather met it, a line as sharp as a knife.

“Oh,” said Kotori, behind Ice, very quietly. She could see it too, in his light.

He looked at the black for a long time.

Then he looked for the first stone.

The first one’s very old, Isa had said. It’s sunk a bit. You’ll have to feel for it.

He couldn’t see it. He held the candle low over the black water and there was nothing. Just the flat dark, drinking the light. No stone. No anything.

He knelt down at the edge, on the heather, with the black case on his back, and put his hand in the water.

It was cold. Colder than anything. Colder than the stream in the fourth tunnel where Salty quenched the sword, which Wendy said made your teeth ache from a pace away. It went up his arm like a hand closing. And it didn’t move. He put his hand in and the water didn’t ripple. It just took his hand, and was still round it, as if it had always been there.

He felt forward. Down. Along.

Nothing. Water.

Further.

Nothing.

He leaned out over the black, with Ice’s hand on the back of his belt. He hadn’t asked; Ice had just put it there. And reached as far as he could, down into the cold. And his fingers touched stone.

Under the water. A hand’s depth down. Flat, and old, and worn very smooth. Smoother than anything he’d ever felt. As if a great many feet had stood on it, a long time ago. Before it sank.

He knelt at the edge of the still water with his hand on the first stone.

He’d thought about what he’d say for three weeks. Since a stool beside a chair by the north door. Since this winter. He’d thought he’d say something about Isa, or her mothers, or three hundred springs.

He didn’t.

“One,” said Suzu.

He stood on it.

It was under the water, a hand deep. He stepped out onto it from the heather, carefully, with the candle low. And the cold came up over his boot and round his ankle and held it. And the stone was there, under his foot, flat and smooth and old.

It held.

He stood on the first stone in the still water and didn’t move for a moment. Then he held the candle out, low, ahead. Looking for the next.

There. A step on. Higher. Its top just at the surface, a little round island of grey in the black. Worn smooth too, but less.

He stepped.

“Two,” said Suzu.

Behind him, he heard Ice step down onto the first stone. Into the cold. And stop. And then, very quietly, in a voice Suzu had heard maybe four times in all the years he’d known him:

“One.”

He’d thought he’d be the only one counting.

He wasn’t. Ice counted one when he stepped onto the first stone, and two when he stepped onto the second, a step behind Suzu. And then Kotori, behind Ice, one, and Hyv, one, in a voice that shook. All the way down the rope. Nine people, one after another, stepping down off the heather into the still water onto the first stone. Each saying one. And then two.

So that when Suzu said three, there were nine numbers going at once along the rope behind him. Three, two, two, one, one, one. Like a round. Like the bells in the tower at Masmark, on the clip from July, changes, every bell a step behind the one before.

He didn’t tell them to stop. He didn’t want them to.

“Four,” said Suzu.

Three, said Ice.

The stones went out across the still water in a line. Not straight. They curved, slowly, left and then right, the way a path curves when it’s been walked by a great many people over a long time, each one a little differently. A step apart. Some just under the surface, some just above. Some tilted. Some cracked. Some so smooth his foot slid on them and Ice’s hand caught his belt.

He found every one with the candle, held low. In the little circle of light the size of a cart wheel, on the black water. He saw it, and stepped, and said the number.

Twenty. Twenty-one.

Ten, Ice said, behind him. Then Kotori, nine. All the way back.

Forty.

The cold had come up past his knees. Not the water; the water stayed where it was, at his ankles, at the stones. The cold was in him now. Going up. He didn’t stop.

Sixty.

He’d stopped thinking about it at about thirty. He’d stopped thinking about anything. There was the candle, and the circle, and the next stone in it, and the number. And behind him the numbers going back down the rope, one after another. And on his back the black case, very heavy, with nothing coming out of it at all.

Eighty-one, he said.

He stopped.

He hadn’t meant to. It was just a number. But it was the number. Eighty-one lanterns in eighty-one alcoves in the Long Watch, a year and a half ago, and his hands shaking at every one. And Kotori behind him, healing on the beat, trying not to say sorry. And the last one, and go on, and the Last Watcher turning round in its great hall to face north again.

“Eighty-one,” said Kotori, from three places back. She’d stopped too. She knew.

He stood on the eighty-first stone in the still water and looked at the candle for a long moment.

“Better,” said Kotori.

He’d said that to her. At the end of the Long Watch, when she hadn’t said sorry once. Better. She’d remembered.

“Eighty-two,” said Suzu, and went on.

At a hundred and fifty, the water moved.

He felt it before he saw it. The cold changed. It had been going up into him for an hour, steady, the way cold does. And at a hundred and fifty it pulled. Not at him. At the case on his back. Like a hand reaching past him for something he was holding.

He stopped.

The candle, held low, made its circle on the water. And in the circle, for the first time, the water wasn’t flat.

It was rising.

Not a wave. Not a ripple. The whole surface of the still water, at the edge of his light, a few paces to his left, going up. Slowly. Smoothly. The way the lake under the round town had gone up on the longest night, Ice said, under the north mouth of the Seven Below. It comes up out of still water. Not out of running.

A shape. Black, on black, tall. Rising out of the still water with the water running off it, without a sound. Leaning.

Toward him. Toward his back. Toward the black case with nothing coming out of it.

He couldn’t move.

He stood on the hundred and fiftieth stone with the best candle in his hand and couldn’t move at all. And the shape leaned in, very slowly, out of the dark at the edge of his light. Feeling for something it couldn’t see.

It can feel it, he thought. Hyv said. The lamps on the north road. They can feel it go past. And so can this.

Behind him, nobody was saying anything. The numbers had stopped. All of them. Nine people standing on stones in the still water, not breathing.

“Keep counting,” said Kotori.

Her voice came from three places back. Very small, and level, and very clear. The voice she used on the longest night, healing on the beat.

“Keep counting, Suzu,” she said. “Isa said. Say the number. So the stones know you’re one of ours.” She swallowed; he could hear it. “If you stop counting, they don’t know. If you stop counting, you’re just somebody standing on a stone with something it wants.”

The shape leaned closer.

He could feel it on his face now. Cold. Colder than the water. Something in it that was like the nothing past the edge, but heavier. Like the nothing had a weight, here. Like it had gathered.

The dark’s own water, Isa had said, once. The place it comes from.

“One hundred and fifty-one,” said Suzu.

He didn’t step. He just said it. Standing on the hundred and fiftieth stone, with the shape leaning over him.

The shape stopped.

“One hundred and fifty-one,” said Ice, behind him. Firmly. Although he was on the hundred and forty-ninth.

“Hundred and fifty-one,” said Kotori. And Hyv. And Thea, and Gigi, and Xola and Slep and Hako. All of them, all along the rope, the same number. Not their own; his. All at once. Nine voices, in the still water, in the nothing, saying a hundred and fifty-one.

The shape didn’t move.

Suzu stepped onto the hundred and fifty-first stone.

“One hundred and fifty-two,” he said. And they said it with him.

The shape stayed there for a long time. Leaning, at the edge of the light. While he stepped, and counted, and nine people counted with him, the same number, every stone.

A hundred and sixty. A hundred and seventy.

And at a hundred and eighty, it went down.

Not fast. The way it had come up. Smoothly, without a sound, back into the still water. The water closing over it so flat you’d never have known. The cold stopped pulling.

Nobody said anything about it.

They just kept counting.

At two hundred and sixteen, there wasn’t a stone.

He held the candle out, low, over the water, ahead. Where the next one should have been. A step on, the way every one had been a step on for two hundred and sixteen. Black water. Flat. Nothing.

He moved the candle left. Right. Further.

There. Two steps on. Not one. Two. A stone, its top just above the surface. Too far to step. Over black water nobody knew the depth of.

He stood on the two hundred and sixteenth stone and looked at the gap.

The winter the lamp nearly went out, he thought. Isa had told the long table. Before her mother. Before her mother’s mother. The woman who kept it had been ill, and the oil had run low, and for nine nights the flame on the flat stone had been so small you could cover it with a thumb.

She’d been ill. In the winter.

And in the spring, she hadn’t walked out with a stone.

“There’s a gap,” said Suzu. “A spring that wasn’t.”

Behind him, Ice didn’t say anything. Suzu heard him come forward, onto the two hundred and fifteenth, the one behind. Close. And look past Suzu’s shoulder at the gap, in the light of the best candle.

“I’ll go first,” said Ice.

“It’s water.”

“I know.”

“You don’t know how deep.”

“I know,” said Ice. “I’ll feel.”

Suzu didn’t move.

“No,” he said.

He’d never said no to Ice. Not once. In all the years. In a wind game and a fen and a guest room in Malacca and a bus station on the eleventh day. I need him, Ice had told Vivian. He’d never said no to him.

He said it now. On the two hundred and sixteenth stone, in the still water, with a light on his back that wasn’t his.

“Isa said the stones know you if you say the number,” said Suzu. “There isn’t a stone. If you go into the water there’s no number. There’s nothing to know you by.” He looked at the gap. “You’d just be somebody in the still water. With something it wants on the rope behind you.”

Ice was quiet for a long time.

“Then what?” he said.

“Let me,” said somebody.

Not Suzu. From further back. Five places back, on the rope. Gigi.

She came forward along the stones. Carefully, past Hyv and Kotori and Thea, holding the rope. Until she was standing on the two hundred and fourteenth, behind Ice, with her staff in her hand.

“I grow thorns,” said Gigi. “Through floors. Through stone. On the longest night, round the old gate.” She looked at the gap in the light of Suzu’s candle. “I’ve never tried water.”

“It might not work,” said Ice.

“Then it won’t,” said Gigi. “And we’ll think of something else.” She looked at Suzu. “Can I?”

She was asking him. Not Ice. Him. Because he was the one carrying it, and he’d said I go first.

“Yes,” said Suzu.

Gigi knelt on the two hundred and fourteenth stone. She reached past Ice’s boots and put the end of her staff into the still water, in the gap, between the two hundred and sixteenth and the two hundred and eighteenth. Where a stone should have been. And said her word. Very quietly. The one she said on the longest night, round the old gate, when the thorns came up through the cobbles.

Nothing happened.

She said it again.

The still water moved.

Not rising, the way the shape had. Something under it. In the gap. Coming up from below, slowly, dark and twisting. Wood, wet, black, a knot of it, a tangle. Thorns. Hawthorn, Thea said afterward; Gigi’s was always hawthorn. Coming up out of the still water in a knot of black branches as thick as a man’s body, its top just breaking the surface between the two stones, where the gap had been.

It stopped.

A step on. Exactly a step.

Gigi stayed kneeling on the stone with her staff in the water for a long time, breathing very hard.

“Bramble,” she said. And laughed. Out of breath. “The candle. Chernn gave me Bramble.”

He stepped onto it.

It held. Wet and black and knotted under his boot, the thorns pressing up through the leather but not through. It held him, and the case on his back, in the still water, where a stone should have been.

He didn’t know what number to say.

He stood on Gigi’s thorn in the gap and thought about it. Two hundred and seventeen. That was the number. That was the one that would have been here, if a woman who kept a lamp hadn’t been ill in a winter a long time ago, and missed a spring.

It wasn’t a stone. It hadn’t been set by one of Isa’s mothers. Nobody had said the number over it, ever. The stones wouldn’t know it.

“Two hundred and seventeen,” said Suzu anyway.

Behind him, on the two hundred and sixteenth, Ice said it too. And then, all the way back down the rope, everybody. Two hundred and seventeen. Into the still water, over a knot of hawthorn that hadn’t been there an hour ago.

He didn’t know if the stones knew.

He thought maybe they’d learn.

The last one was crooked.

He knew it before he got to it. He’d been waiting for it for three hundred stones. The last one’s mine. I set it the spring before I came home. It’s crooked. I was old.

It was. Tilted, badly, to the left. Its top half out of the water, grey, very new compared to the others. Not worn at all. Nobody had stood on it but one old woman, once, setting it. And she’d stood on it and looked back south across three hundred stones toward an edge she’d walk to that autumn and see a road lit.

He stood on the two hundred and ninety-ninth and looked at it in the light of the best candle.

Beyond it, at the edge of the light, the water stopped. And there was ground again. Rising. Up out of the still water in front of him, steep, dark, the shape of a hill.

He stepped onto the crooked stone. It tilted under him, and held.

“Three hundred,” said Suzu.

He said it very quietly. He didn’t know why. It just came out quiet.

Behind him, all down the rope, nobody said anything for a long time. And then, one after another, as they came onto the stones behind him, they said their own numbers. Two hundred and ninety-nine. Two hundred and ninety-eight. All the way back. Nine people on three hundred stones, in the still water, past the edge.

And then Ice, behind him, on the two hundred and ninety-ninth.

“Three hundred,” said Ice.

He stepped off the crooked stone onto the hill.

Ground. Hard, and cold, and real, under his boots. Not heather. Stone, and grit, and something like moss. Going up.

He stood at the bottom of the hill in the middle of the still water. In the middle of the nothing, past the edge, where nobody had stood but Isa’s mothers for three hundred years. With the best candle in his hand and the black case on his back. And looked up.

At the top of the hill, against nothing, he couldn’t see anything.

But he could feel them. Up there. In a ring. Stones. Standing. Waiting.

And in the middle of them, a flat one. With a crown cut into it, worn nearly smooth. Where a lamp had been for three hundred years.

Nobody on it.

Ice stepped off the last stone behind him, onto the hill. Suzu felt him come. He stood beside him, at the bottom, not in front. Beside.

“You went first,” said Ice.

“I went first.”

“All three hundred.”

“Two hundred and ninety-nine,” said Suzu. “Gigi did one.”

Ice didn’t say anything for a while. Behind them, the rest came off the stones, one by one, onto the hill. Kotori, and Hyv, and Thea, and Gigi, very tired. Xola and Slep and Hako, last, with the end of the rope.

“Good,” said Ice.

Suzu looked at him.

It was his word. Good. It was the only word he’d ever needed, and he’d said it a thousand times, to Ice, about everything. He’d never once heard Ice say it to him.

He didn’t say anything. He looked up the hill, at the dark, where the ring was.

He put his hand up to the strap, on the cat’s-paw, where your hand fell when you reached up to check it was there.

It was there.
