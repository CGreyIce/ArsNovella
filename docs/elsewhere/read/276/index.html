Live from Elsewhere · Volume 11: Three Hundred and One

Chapter 276

What the Door Was For

3,623 words · 16 min read

Gabe had held the north door twice, and he’d never once seen what was at the other end of it.

He saw it on the Monday night, the nineteenth of March, at the top of a hill in the middle of the dark’s own water. He jumped a stride of black water off a crooked stone and landed on his knees on the heather and said ow. He got up laughing. Then he looked up the slope and stopped laughing.

The ring. Twelve stones round the top of the hill, standing, one of them a little newer at the base where it had been raised. Twelve of twelve. Nothing comes inside. In the middle, a flat stone with a worn crown cut into it. On the crown, a black jar. And over the jar, standing straight up, a flame with nothing under it.

Gold. Small. About the size of his hand.

Not on a wick. Not on anything. Just standing there, over the mouth of an empty jar, in the middle of a ring of stones, in the middle of a hill, in the middle of the still water. In the middle of the dark country, which had been the nothing, and now wasn’t.

The middle.

He walked up the slope to it. He didn’t decide to. His feet did it. Past the twelfth stone, into the ring. And stood at the flat stone with the Anvil hanging off his arm, looking at a flame the size of his hand.

He started crying.

He cried at most things. Everybody knew. He’d cried at the Thornway, twice. He’d cried at Dec’s first stream in the egg. At the dawn after the longest night, the first one and the second one and the third. He’d cried on the south steps when Ash signed. He’d cried into Rae’s shoulder at a draw in the Seven Below so loudly that people thought he was screaming, which he partly was.

He’d never cried like this.

It came up out of him from somewhere a long way down. Under the noise. He stood at the flat stone with both hands over his face and his shoulders going, and he couldn’t stop. He didn’t try.

Behind him, he heard the others come up the slope and stop. Kotori, and PenguChi, and the sheep. He heard them stop at the twelfth stone and not come any further. Somebody, PenguChi, said something very quietly to the sheep, and the sheep didn’t come any further either.

Suzu came.

He came up beside Gabe at the flat stone and stood there, not touching him, looking at the flame. He didn’t say anything. He waited. Suzu was good at that. He’d been good at it before he started saying things first, and he was still good at it now.

“I held the door,” said Gabe, eventually. Into his hands.

“I know.”

“Twice. Last year and this year. The north door. All night.” He took his hands off his face. They were wet. “Last year Dec was under the floor, and I held it, and I didn’t know what I was holding it for. Just the town. Just so the dark didn’t come in. And this year he was here.” He looked at the flame. “On this hill. With you. Everybody in a ring round this. And I was at the door. A long way south. In the dark. Holding.”

“Yes,” said Suzu.

“I never saw it,” said Gabe. “What I was holding the door for.” He was crying again. “I’ve been holding a door for him for eleven years, Suzu. Out there. In every game. At every gate. And I’ve never once seen the other side of it.”

Suzu didn’t say anything for a long time.

“This is the other side,” said Suzu.

Gabe looked at the flame. The size of his hand. Standing straight up, over the jar, in the middle of everything.

“It’s so small,” said Gabe.

“Yes,” said Suzu. “That’s why it needed a door.”

He sat down at the flat stone after that, on the heather, with his back against it. Suzu sat down beside him. Kotori came over, at last, with a cup, and gave it to him without saying anything. He drank it. It tasted of the backs of stones.

Isa’s things were there. Where they’d been left. The bowl, beside the jar. The stick, leaning on the flat stone. The grey blanket, folded. Kotori’s cup, from December, for her mothers, still full. Something had kept the tea from drying up in three months. Gabe didn’t know what. He didn’t want to know. He looked at it and thought it was the most beautiful cup of tea he’d ever seen.

“Where did he stand?” said Gabe.

Suzu didn’t ask who.

“Everywhere,” said Suzu. “All night. Round the inside of the ring. A breath ahead of every gap.” He pointed, with his chin, at the twelve stones. “Then at eleven o’clock all twelve gaps went at once, and he couldn’t be in twelve places. So he turned round.” Suzu was quiet. “Everybody. In. Now. And we all came in. Round this. Shoulder to shoulder. Facing out.” He put his hand flat on the flat stone behind them. “And he wasn’t at the front. There wasn’t a front. He was next to it.”

Gabe looked at the stone.

“Next to it,” he said.

“He says he was never the front,” said Suzu. “Not on the hill. Not anywhere. He says he was always next to it.” He looked at Gabe. “He’s wrong about that. Mostly. But not on the hill.”

Gabe thought about Dec. In the kitchen in Melaka Raya, at nine in the morning, with the kettle on. Coming out of the egg on the morning after the longest night, very quiet, and putting the kettle on, and not saying anything for an hour. And Gabe, who’d been at the door, sitting on the counter waiting for him to say something.

He’d said, in the end, you held.

That was all. You held. And then, you’ve been holding me up for eleven years, and I’m heavier than the dark.

Gabe had thought that was about him.

It wasn’t. He saw it now, sitting with his back against a flat stone on a hill, looking at a flame the size of his hand.

It was about this.

At a quarter to eight they stood round the flat stone. Four of them and a sheep, close, facing in. And down at the bottom of the hill, on the crooked stone in the water, Salty, with the stone still on his back, sitting up straight.

“Set one to watch,” said Suzu.

“So the rest may sleep,” said Gabe. He was crying. He didn’t care.

And a long way south, through the dark, at the bottom of everything, he thought he heard sixty people say it too.

Out there, he came out of the egg at eight in the back room at Melaka Raya and couldn’t stand up.

He sat on the edge of it with his feet on the floor and his face in his hands. He stayed there, crying, until the door opened.

Dec put his head round it.

He didn’t say anything. He looked at Gabe for a while, sitting on the edge of the egg in the grey morning light, crying. Then he went away. Gabe heard the kettle go on in the kitchen.

He came back with two cups. He gave one to Gabe and sat down on the floor with his back against the wall and the other one, and waited.

“I saw it,” said Gabe, eventually.

“I know,” said Dec. “I watched. Penguin’s stream.”

“It’s so small, Dec.”

“Yes.”

“You held it. All night. On the longest night. Round the inside. And then everybody in.” Gabe looked at him over the cup. “And I was at the door.”

“Yes,” said Dec. “So it could be there.” He drank his tea. “That’s what the door’s for. So the small thing can be in the middle.” He looked at Gabe. “You held the door so I could hold that.”

Gabe put the cup down on the floor very carefully. Then he got off the egg and sat down on the floor beside Dec and put his face in Dec’s shoulder and cried. Dec put his arm round him and drank his tea with the other hand, and didn’t say anything else, because there wasn’t anything else to say.

The auntie next door didn’t knock. Gabe thought she probably knew.

They set the stone on the Tuesday. Salty did. Gabe watched him, red-eyed, with the sheep under one arm, from the shore of the hill. He’d think about it for the rest of his life. A man who’d always swung things as though they were too small, on one knee on a crooked stone, lowering a stone the right size into the dark’s own water.

Three hundred and one.

Then, on the Wednesday, they walked home.

It took sixteen days. Down the three hundred and one, counting backwards, so the stones would know they were going home. Gabe said every number out loud and cried at about a third of them.

The hawthorn at two hundred and seventeen had buds on it now, as well as leaves. Small and tight and white at the tips. Kotori said it would flower properly in May. For the first time, she said. In the right month.

Then the dark country, nine days, green.

He wasn’t frightened any more. He noticed it on the second day, south of the still water. He was walking in the middle of the clump with Patience beside him, talking. He’d been talking for about an hour without noticing, about the Thornway, again. He stopped in the middle of a sentence and realised he hadn’t looked at the dark once since the hill. He’d been looking at Kotori. At the sheep. At PenguChi’s spear at the back, every time he looked round.

“I’m not frightened,” he said. Surprised. Out loud.

“No,” said PenguChi, from the back. “You saw it.”

“Saw what?”

“What it’s for,” said PenguChi. “The dark. It’s just dark round something.” She walked on. “Once you’ve seen the something, it’s just dark.”

He thought about that for the rest of the day, and then told everybody about it about forty times, adding something each time. PenguChi didn’t mind. She was at the back. She’d heard worse.

On the sixth night, he took the back.

He hadn’t asked. He’d just watched her for fifteen nights going north and five coming home. PenguChi at the back of the fire with her spear across her knees, facing out, while everybody else slept. She slept, too, eventually. She said the back could sleep once the front had. But she slept sitting up, facing the dark. Gabe had never once seen her lie down.

So on the sixth night, when the fire had burned down and Suzu and Kotori and Salty were asleep, Gabe got up. He took the Anvil and went round the fire to where PenguChi sat. And sat down beside her, facing the dark, with the shield on his arm.

PenguChi looked at him.

“You’re the front,” she said.

“Not tonight,” said Gabe. “Tonight I’m at the back.” He set the Anvil on the heather in front of them, edge down, the way he set it in the north door. “So you can lie down.”

PenguChi didn’t say anything for a long time.

“Nobody’s ever been at the back of me,” she said. Slowly. Not sad. As though she’d only just noticed. “I’m the back.”

“I know,” said Gabe. “Kotori told me. On the hill. You came to be at the back of Suzu.” He looked at the dark. “Somebody should be at the back of you. Once. Who’s never once been anywhere but the front.”

PenguChi looked at him for a long time in the last of the firelight.

Then she lay down.

On the heather, on her side, with her spear beside her and her back to Gabe. Facing the fire, not the dark. She lay there for a while with her eyes open. Then she shut them. And Patience the sheep came round the fire and lay down against her back, because the sheep had always been on PenguChi’s side.

Gabe sat at the back all night.

It was the strangest night of his life. He’d held doors. He knew how to face something coming. He’d never once sat behind people, facing nothing, holding the place where nothing came from. Nothing came. All night. He watched the dark, which was just dark, with edges. He listened to four people and a sheep breathing behind him. And he thought about Dec, on the floor of the back room at Melaka Raya, with a cup, waiting for Gabe to stop crying.

That was the back too. He’d never seen it.

At a quarter to eight, PenguChi woke up. She sat up and looked at him. He was still there, with the Anvil on its edge in front of him, facing north.

“Anything?” said PenguChi.

“Nothing,” said Gabe.

“Good,” said PenguChi. Which was Suzu’s. She didn’t say thank you. She put her hand on his arm for a moment, very lightly, the way you’d touch a wall to check it was still there. Gabe thought it was the best thank you he’d ever had.

They came down the north road on the first Friday in April. The sixth. Ninety-four flames standing, one every mile, and the moor going green all round them. Spring had got to the moor first, and then gone north past the edge, and Gabe had seen it there before he saw it here.

At the eleventh stone, at the second dusk, they saw the dome.

Low and pale at the bottom of the sky. And at the top of it, at the eye, a small gold light. Kei’s lamp, on the Somewhere bench. And beside it, a shape. Small, in a blanket.

“Isa,” said Kotori.

“She’s on the roof,” said Gabe. “At dusk. They carry her up.” He looked at it for a long time. He couldn’t see her face. Nobody could, from there. Except maybe Hyv. “She’s looking north.”

“She’s looking at us,” said Suzu.

“She can’t see us. She can’t see that far.”

“She’s looking anyway,” said Suzu.

PenguChi, at the back, raised her spear. Not high.

The small shape on the roof, a long way off, at the top of the dome, raised its hand.

The north door stuck. It always did.

Gabe put his shoulder to it. He’d been putting his shoulder to it for a year. It gave all at once, the way it did when somebody on the other side pulled at the same moment. He came through it into the Hall of Roads, into the light, nearly falling.

Rae caught him.

Of course it was Rae. She’d been at the north door, apparently, since the eleventh stone. Since Kei’s map showed them coming. Standing in it. She caught him by both arms and held him up. Not a pace behind. Not at his back. In front of him, this time. Facing him. At no paces at all.

“You’re back,” said Rae.

“I saw it,” said Gabe. “Rae. I saw the middle.”

“I know,” said Rae. “Chi streamed it. I watched you cry at it for forty minutes.”

“It was forty minutes?”

“Forty-one,” said Rae. She didn’t let go of his arms. “I timed it.”

Gabe laughed. He was crying again. He didn’t care. He stood in the north door with Rae holding him up, and behind him came Suzu, and Kotori with her empty tins, and Salty with no stone on his back for the first time in a month. And PenguChi, last, with her spear. And the sheep, in front of all of them, walking straight across the Hall of Roads to the chair by the north door. She lay down beside it as though it were hers.

The chair was empty. Isa was on the roof.

They went up the two hundred and twelve. All five of them, to the eye, at the last of the dusk.

Isa was on the Somewhere bench, beside Kei’s lamp, under Ama Corrie’s grey blanket. Ama Corrie was standing behind the bench with a hand on the back of it. Hana was beside her. It was Hana’s bench, and Hana had given it to Isa every dusk since December without being asked.

Suzu knelt in front of the bench so Isa could see his face.

“Three hundred and one,” said Suzu.

“I know,” said Isa.

Suzu looked at her.

“I saw,” said Isa. “On the equinox. At the second dusk.” She was looking north, past him, at the bottom of the sky, where the gold point was. “I can’t see that far. I know. I can’t see faces across a room.” Her old hands were folded in the blanket. “But I saw the dusk. On the day the light and the dark are the same. The sun going down in the west. And the flame in the north. The same colour.” She was quiet. “And for a moment, between them, on the water, there was a line. Gold. Like a road.” She looked at Suzu. “There’s only a line like that when somebody’s standing on the way.”

Nobody said anything.

“Hm,” said Isa. She put her hand on Suzu’s head. “Three hundred and one.”

Gabe was crying. Obviously. He stood at the parapet of the eye, behind the bench, and cried. And this time nobody looked at him, because Hana was crying too. And Kotori. And Ama Corrie, standing behind the bench with her hand on it, wasn’t crying. But she’d taken her hand off the bench and put it over her mouth.

Down in the round market, in the last of the light, the middle pole stood by the fountain.

Green, still, a little paler than at New Year. Bare. Seven ropes going out from its top to the seven road ends, with nothing on them. Jared was up a ladder against it, checking the rings. Azac at the bottom, holding the ladder. Six hundred and twelve lanterns in their crates round the fountain, folded flat. Waiting.

“Tomorrow,” said Hana, beside Gabe, at the parapet. Very quietly.

He looked at her.

“Tomorrow,” said Hana again. She wasn’t looking at him. She was looking at the pole. “Out there. And then in here. At night.” She wiped her face. “You’re back in time.”

“We were always going to be back in time,” said Gabe.

“I know,” said Hana. “Suzu said.” She laughed, a little, wet. “He said it first. At the long table, before you went. We’ll be back the day before. Nobody asked him.”

Gabe looked at Suzu, kneeling in front of Isa’s bench, with Isa’s hand on his head.

“He does that now,” said Gabe.

“I know,” said Hana.
