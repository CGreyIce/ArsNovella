Chapter 66

Good Eyes

2,043 words · 9 min read

The message came on Saturday morning, while Hyvred was on the MRT.

She read it the way she read everything on Earth: the phone held close to her face, a few inches from her nose, one piece at a time. Her eyes were a tube. A small clear circle in the middle, and round it, nothing. Not black. Just not. She’d learned to move the circle instead of the world. Across a menu, down a page, along a platform, a word at a time. So she read the message the way she read a train timetable, sliding the circle along it, holding the phone very still while the train swayed.

Kestrel.

She knew it was Kestrel before she reached the words. The name was at the top. She’d read that name three times on a mill roof on the longest night, and cried. She still had the message. Good eyes.

This one was longer.

She read it to the end. Then she went back to the top and read it again, a piece at a time, sliding the circle down it, while the train went into a tunnel and her reflection came up in the dark window. Then she put the phone in her lap and sat with her hands on it.

VANTA.

The black ones on the island. Thirty of them, quiet, ringed round the Tally on the longest night with their lanterns, holding the middle of Brackenford beautifully. The pro team. The ones who’d come second at the swamp guy’s church by about eleven hours and never once complained about it on stream. The best in the town, and as far as anyone could tell, the best anywhere.

A seat.

She got off at her stop and walked home along the covered walkway, the way she always did, moving the circle from the floor to the next pillar to the floor. She didn’t think about the message at all, the whole way. She was very good at not thinking about things on a walkway. You had to be.

Bee read it on the dyers’ bridge, that evening, in the game.

Hyvred showed her. She opened the message in the air between them, on the parapet, with the river running coloured underneath from the dye-works upstream. Blue and red and a sort of rusty yellow, in long ribbons that didn’t mix. Bee leaned over and read it, with her antennae going very slowly forward and her wings, for once, still.

She read it twice. Then she sat back on the parapet and opened her book on her knees and started drawing something, which was what Bee did instead of having a face.

“Well,” said Bee.

“Well.”

“Good eyes,” said Bee. “She’s still on about your eyes.”

“They’re good eyes,” said Hyvred. “In here.”

“They’re good eyes everywhere.” Bee didn’t look up. Her pencil went on. “It’s just that in here they’ve got the whole room to look at.”

Hyvred looked at her. Bee was drawing the island. The market island in the middle of the town, with the Tally on it, black against the sunset. And the lanterns VANTA kept round it now, every night, since the longest one. They’d stayed. Thirty of them, who could have gone anywhere, had stayed in Brackenford because the swamp guy’s church was a day away and everyone in the world wanted to be near it.

“What are you drawing?”

“A door,” said Bee. “On the island. A very ugly one.” She shaded it. “If you go to VANTA, I’m drawing them a very ugly door.”

“I haven’t said I’m going.”

“I know,” said Bee. “I’m drawing it in advance.”

Hyvred hadn’t told anyone about the other thing yet. Not Bee. Not her chat.

Kei had messaged on Wednesday night. Can I call you this weekend? It’s about the paper. No pressure. I’d just like to ask you something. She’d known what it was before she reached the end of it. Everyone knew about the paper. Everyone knew what the swamp guy had said on Sunday, on his plank, with his feet over the water. Somebody I’ve known a long time is making something. It’s theirs. I’m not in it. Everyone had a guess who. Hyvred didn’t have to guess. She knew Kei. She’d known him for years, on calls, long before any of this.

So she’d said yes. call me. bee says hi.

And on Saturday morning, on a train, VANTA had said a seat.

She thought the universe had a sense of humour, and it wasn’t a very good one.

Kei called at eleven, her time. Two in the morning, his. She’d known he’d call late. He’d have done his roll call first, all fourteen of them, and then whatever came after the roll call, and only then her. Kei did things in order.

She turned her stream off. She didn’t tell her chat why.

“Hi,” said Kei.

“Hi.”

“Sorry. It’s late. There was a—something happened on the call. In Sevenlocks. It took a while.”

“Is everyone okay?”

“Everyone’s okay. Somebody walked a long way to stand somewhere.” She heard him breathe out, a long tired breath, the breath of somebody who’d been careful for three hours straight. “I’ll tell you about it. Not tonight.”

“Okay.”

There was a pause. In Brackenford, the sun was a long way down, and the lanterns were lit on the dyers’ bridge. Bee had wandered off along the bank to sit with old Wynn, who’d been teaching her the names of the dyes. Woad. Madder. Weld. She was drawing them, by the sound of it, because Wynn kept saying no, darker.

“You know what I’m going to ask,” said Kei.

“I know.”

“I’m going to ask anyway. Properly.” He was quiet for a second, gathering it. She could hear him gathering it. “There’s a writ. Twenty names. A round hall where seven roads meet. I’ve got fourteen, and Drew says Tiger’s in, and we’ve got four to find. Nobody’s paid. Nobody’s famous, really, except by accident. It’s just people who already know each other. Lines that were already there.” A pause. “I’d like you in it. You and Bee. I’d like you in it more than almost anybody.”

Hyvred didn’t say anything.

“You don’t have to answer now,” said Kei. “That’s not how it works. The rule’s every time. The people who’d stand next to you decide. But you’d be standing next to me, and I’ve already decided.”

“Why?” said Hyvred.

“Why what?”

“Why me. More than almost anybody.” She was sitting on the mill roof where she’d stood on the longest night, with her legs over the edge and the bow across her knees. She could see the whole of Brackenford from here. The three rivers, the dozen bridges, every lantern. VANTA’s ring round the island. CL’s red sashes on the big bridges. The whole town, edge to edge, all at once. “Is it my eyes?”

Kei was quiet for a moment.

“I thought about the gate,” he said slowly. “The Hall’s got seven doors. Seven roads come in. On the night we sign it, people are going to come up all seven of them. Some of them we’ll want. Some of them we won’t.” Another pause. “I thought somebody should be able to see all seven at once.”

“So it’s my eyes.”

“No,” said Kei.

He said it quickly, before she’d finished. Kei never said anything quickly.

“No,” he said again, more slowly. “I want you. You’re my friend. You’ve been my friend for years. You were my friend when you could see about this much of a room.” She could hear him holding up his hand, a little circle, though she couldn’t see it. “Your eyes come with you. That’s all. If you were at the gate with your eyes shut, I’d still want you at the gate.”

Hyvred sat very still on the mill roof.

“Kestrel messaged me,” she said.

There was a silence on the call. A long one.

“This morning,” said Hyvred. “VANTA have a seat. For me. Not a trial. A seat.” She looked across the water at the island, at the ring of quiet lanterns round the Tally. “She said good eyes again.”

“Oh,” said Kei softly.

“I haven’t said anything. I haven’t answered. I read it on the train and I put my phone in my lap and I walked home, and I haven’t said anything to anybody except Bee. Bee’s drawing them an ugly door.”

Kei laughed, very quietly, despite himself. Then he stopped.

“Okay,” he said. “Then talk to her.”

“What?”

“Talk to Kestrel. Properly. Go to the island, or let her come to you, and ask her everything. What it is. What it’s like. What they’d want you for. Find out.” She could hear him choosing every word. “And then decide. Not because of me. Not because of a call at two in the morning. Because of what you find out.”

“You’re not going to tell me not to.”

“Every time,” said Kei. “It works both ways. We don’t get to decide for you either.” He paused. “I want you in it. I’ll always want you in it. But if VANTA’s right for you, then it’s right, and I’ll be the first person on the bridge to say so.”

“You’d hate it.”

“I’d hate it,” Kei agreed. “I’d still say it.”

Hyvred looked at the island for a long time. The lanterns. The black needle. The thirty quiet people who were the best at this in the world, and who’d watched her on a roof and said good eyes twice.

Then she looked down at the dyers’ bridge. At Bee on the bank beside old Wynn in the lantern-light, drawing woad and madder and weld in charcoal, being told darker. At the three lanterns on the bridge, which they’d kept lit all night on the longest night. At the place on the parapet where Bee had sat with her book and drawn ladders until the dawn came.

“Okay,” she said. “I’ll talk to her.”

“Okay.”

“Kei.”

“Yeah?”

“Thank you for asking properly.”

“It’s the only way I know,” said Kei. And then, very gently, because it was two in the morning in Australia and he’d been careful all night: “Goodnight, Hyv.”

She sat on the roof a while longer after he’d gone.

From up here she could see everything. That was the thing she’d never get used to, and never wanted to. All of it, at once, without moving her head. The river running coloured. The mill wheels turning. A cat on a wall at the far edge of town. A boy at the south end of the dyers’ bridge with a crossbow, sitting on the parapet eating something, whom she half recognised and couldn’t place. The island, and the needle, and the lanterns.

She opened the message again. No hurry. No strings. I mean that.

She typed with one finger, in the air, slowly.

Then she shut the window, and slid down off the roof to the bank, and went to sit with Bee and Wynn. Bee looked up from her book and didn’t say anything. She just moved over on the bench, and turned the page so Hyvred could see. On it, in charcoal, darker, was the dyers’ bridge at night. Three lanterns. A mill roof. And on the roof, very small, a girl with a bow, looking at everything.
