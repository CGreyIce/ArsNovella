Live from Elsewhere · Volume 6: There's a Chair

Chapter 162

Sixty

3,603 words · 16 min read

Hana woke up on Friday under a ceiling so far away she thought for a second she was outside.

She wasn’t. She was lying on a pallet in the gallery that ran round the inside of the dome, halfway up, where the guild put people who’d come in late and hadn’t found a room at the Hart yet. Milli was asleep beside her, on her side, with one hand flat on the stone between them. Jared was snoring somewhere along the gallery. Leaka was talking in her sleep, in capitals. And above them all, the ceiling of the dome of the Hall of Roads went up and up and up, grey stone in the morning light. At the very top there was a round hole full of sky.

She’d seen it on streams. Everybody had. She’d never been under it.

She lay there and looked at it for a long time, and thought about a tunnel. Five months in a tunnel, with a brush and a lantern and three hundred and forty-three little figures walking into the dark. She’d forgotten what it was like to have a ceiling that high. She’d forgotten what up was.

She put her hand in her pocket and found the star.

She showed it to Kei at the first dawn.

She hadn’t meant to show it to anybody yet. She’d meant to sign first, and be in, and then find the right moment. But Kei was at the counter at the first dawn, the way he always seemed to be. He was going over his map with a cup of something going cold at his elbow. And Hana came down the gallery stairs with the star in her pocket and found that her hand had taken it out, and was holding it out to him, before her mouth had decided anything.

Kei looked at it. Then at her.

“It was in the wall,” said Hana. “Under Quarrymouth. At the end. The last little guy. He had a banner, not a light, and the star was in the banner.” She stopped. “I streamed it. You might have—“

“I saw,” said Kei.

He put his cup down. He took the star out of her palm very carefully, between his finger and thumb, the way you’d pick up something that might break. He held it up to the light coming down through the eye of the dome.

Then, without a word, he walked across the Hall and into the banner room. Hana followed him.

The banner was on its hooks on the far wall. Night-blue, edge to edge, with its gold dots and silver lines. Hana had seen it on stream, on Ice’s, on Kei’s. She’d never seen it close. It was bigger than she’d thought, and older-looking, though she knew Bee had painted it in the winter.

And at the very top, near the edge, there was a small faint gold star. Five points. Old gold, the way old gold went.

Kei held the star from the wall up beside it.

They were the same. Not the same size; the one on the banner was bigger. But the same shape. The same five points, the same slight lean to the top one. The same soft old gold. As if somebody, three hundred years ago, had cut them both from the same piece.

“Somebody knew,” said Kei, very quietly.

“Somebody always knows,” said a voice behind them. “Usually it’s somebody dead.”

Ama Corrie was in the doorway with her fen-reed broom. She came in and stood beside them and looked at the star in Kei’s fingers over her spectacles, for a long time.

“Where’d you get that, girl?”

Hana told her. The quarry, the face with half a smile, the door. The tunnel. The wall. Three hundred and forty-three of them, walking in a line, all holding lights. The last one, holding a banner instead, with the star.

“They were walking north,” said Hana. “All of them. The whole line. I checked. North, into the dark. And everybody says the old guild went north and got lost. But they weren’t—“ She stopped. She’d said it on the ledge to Jared and Milli, and on stream to eleven thousand people. She’d never said it to anybody who’d know. “They don’t look lost,” she said. “On the wall. They look like they’re carrying something.”

Ama Corrie didn’t say anything for a long moment.

Then she put the broom against the wall and went out of the banner room. She came back with the old Roll in both arms. The old guild’s book, swollen with water and three hundred years. Hana had seen it on Higu’s stream in June, open on the counter, page by page.

Ama Corrie put it down on the long table and opened it at the back.

“After the last page,” said Ama Corrie. She turned it, past a page Hana could read, in a careful old hand: If we do not come back, keep them anyway. And then on to the next, which looked blank. “Kei’s lot thought these were blank. They’re not. Hold one to the light and there’s writing. Somebody wrote one more thing, after, the night they went. And the water had it.” She put her hand flat beside it. “I’ve had it drying since April and I still can’t read a word.”

Hana looked. Kei looked. The page was brown and rippled, like sand after the tide. There was writing on it, she could see that, lines of it, in an old hand. But the water had got into the ink a long time ago, and spread it, and faded it. So that it was more like the shadow of writing than writing.

“I’ve tried,” said Ama Corrie. “Glass. Candle behind it. Higu tried with her light. It’s gone. Water gets into ink. Ink forgets.”

Hana looked at the page. Then she looked back over her shoulder, through the banner room door, at the gallery stairs.

“Ink forgets,” she said.

“What?”

“Curse,” said Hana. “I need Curse.”

Curse came down the gallery stairs in his coat with his hair standing up, holding his inkwell, looking like a man who’d been woken by Leaka shouting CURSE, HANA NEEDS YOU, IT’S AN EMERGENCY, NO IT’S NOT A BEAR. Which he had.

He came into the banner room and looked at the page for a long time without saying anything.

“Most things forget what they are,” he said at last. “A label reminds them.” He looked at Ama Corrie. “It’s old. Older than anything I’ve tried. It might not remember. It might have forgotten too long.”

“Try,” said Ama Corrie.

So Curse took a blank tag out of his coat. He uncapped the inkwell, and dipped his pen, and stood for a moment with the pen over the tag, thinking. Hana could see him thinking. What was it? What was the thing, the one word for what it was, that the page had forgotten?

He wrote, very slowly, in blue:

ROLL

And then, after a moment, underneath, smaller:

(the page after)

He laid the tag on the brown rippled paper, gently, the way you’d put a hand on someone’s shoulder.

Nothing happened for a moment.

Then the ink moved.

Not much. Not all of it. Most of the page stayed what it had been, brown shadows of words that had gone too far. But one line, near the top, the only line anybody had written on the page after the last page, darkened. As if somebody had breathed on it. The faded brown went darker, and darker, and then it was black. It was clear, in a careful old hand, for the first time in three hundred years.

Ama Corrie made a sound Hana had never heard her make.

Kei read it out loud. His voice wasn’t steady.

“We go north to carry the lights. Set them out behind us, so the rest may sleep.”

Nobody said anything.

The banner room was very quiet. Out in the Hall somebody was laughing, and a door banged, and the pigeons in the eye were making their morning noises. In here there was only the page, and the line on it, black and clear, and four people looking at it.

“So the rest may sleep,” said Kei.

“Sedge’s mother’s line,” said Ama Corrie. Her voice had gone very strange. “From the fen. Set one to watch, so the rest may sleep. I wrote the end of it into the margin of this book in July. From a reed girl’s mother’s bedtime words.” She put her old hand flat on the page beside the black line. “It was theirs. It was their line. They wrote it here, the night they left. And a woman in a fen was still saying it to her daughter at bedtime, three hundred years after.”

Hana looked at the star in Kei’s fingers. Then at the page. Then she thought about three hundred and forty-three little figures in a line, all walking north into the dark, every one of them holding up a light.

Carry the lights.

“They weren’t lost,” she said. “They were carrying them. North. Into the dark. On purpose.” Her voice cracked. “Setting them out. Behind them. Like the stones on the north moor. Every fifty paces.”

The tag on the page, Curse’s tag, in blue, had already started to fade. The black line under it was going slowly brown again, as the label wore off. Curse watched it go.

“It won’t last,” he said quietly. “It’s too old. It’s remembering for as long as the ink does.” He looked at the tag. “That’s not long. Not on something this old.”

“Long enough,” said Ama Corrie. She already had her pen out. In the margin beside the fading line, in her own small hand, she was writing it down. Word for word. We go north to carry the lights. Set them out behind us, so the rest may sleep. And underneath: (Read again by C., with a label, the morning the five came. Written in by A. Corrie, Rollkeeper.)

“There,” she said, when she’d finished. “Now it’s kept.”

They signed after that. It seemed wrong to sign before. It seemed right after.

Ama Corrie set the new Roll on the counter, Constellation’s, and turned it round to face them, and held out the pen. The five of them stood in a row in front of the counter. Most of the guild was behind them in the Hall, because Leaka had shouted IT’S AN EMERGENCY loud enough for everybody to come and see what the emergency was.

“Who’s first?” said Ama Corrie.

Leaka opened her mouth. Then, for possibly the first time in her life, she closed it again. She looked at Hana.

“You,” said Leaka. “You found the star.”

So Hana took the pen.

She looked at the page. Fifty-five names. She could read some of them upside down. Ice, small and plain, three letters. AltheaVT, steady. xolalotl with a wave under it, and Slep under that. Tif2 with a cat. At the top, KeiKatamori, small and upright.

She signed. Hanarisu. In black ink, because it was Ama Corrie’s pen. The ordinary size.

Then she held the pen out to Milli. And Milli, instead of signing on the next line down, leaned over the counter and signed on the line directly under Hana’s. So close the tails of his letters touched the tops of Hana’s. Millianna.

Ama Corrie looked at the two names for a long time.

“The old book does that,” she said. “Pairs. When two came who were going to be one, one day, they signed one under the other. Close. So they’d always be on the same page.” She looked up at Hana and Milli over her spectacles. “Nobody told you that.”

“No,” said Milli.

“Hm,” said Ama Corrie. She didn’t say anything else. But Hana saw her touch the edge of the page, very lightly, by the two names. As if she were sweeping under them.

Jared signed. YoJareddo, in big friendly letters that took up most of the line. Then, after a moment’s thought, he drew a small horizontal line under it.

“What’s that?” said Ama Corrie.

“A beam,” said Jared.

“Hm,” said Ama Corrie, in a tone Hana was learning meant I like you.

Leaka signed. Aleaka. It took up two lines, the A as tall as Hana’s whole name. Ama Corrie looked at it, and then at Historia three pages earlier, which also took up two lines, and said nothing at all, very pointedly.

And Curse.

He didn’t take Ama Corrie’s pen. He held out his own, the fountain pen, and looked at her for permission. She looked at the pen, and the inkwell in his other hand, and the blue on his fingers, and nodded.

He signed in blue. Cursiffe. Small and careful and very straight. The only name in the whole of Constellation’s Roll in blue ink.

“It’ll last,” said Curse. “It’s in pen.”

“Everything in this book’s in pen,” said Ama Corrie.

“Then it’ll all last,” said Curse. And for the first time since Hana had known him, which was years, he smiled with the whole of his face.

The noise when it came was the loudest Hana had ever heard. Louder than Leaka. Louder than the Hall when Ice ate the green apple. It came from everywhere. From the floor of the Hall and the stair and the gallery and the steps and the eye. And from the banner room doorway, where somebody had started banging on the door frame with a hammer, and it was Gabe.

Then Bee came out of the banner room with her paints and her cushion. She knelt down on the floor under the eye, where the whole guild was painted in gold and silver on the old stone. And at the far edge of it, down near a little painted cluster of cliffs that was meant to be Quarrymouth and a little painted wheel that was meant to be Merrowgate, there were five dots with dotted lines.

Bee looked up at Hana and held out the eyelash brush.

“You can do yours,” said Bee. “If you want. Thea did hers.”

Hana knelt down on the stone. She did hers. It ran from a dot near the cliffs all the way in, past knots and clusters, to the middle, to Kei’s. It crossed a lot of other lines on the way. One of them was Ice’s. She’d known it would. She’d known him longer than most people in this room.

Milli did hers next, beside Hana’s, so close they nearly touched. Jared did his in about four seconds, with great confidence, and Bee had to fix the end. Leaka did hers very slowly and very carefully, not saying anything at all, which nobody had ever seen before. And Curse wouldn’t take the brush. He watched Bee do his, and when she’d finished he said, “Thank you. It’s straight.”

Hana sat back on her heels and looked at the floor.

All solid. Every line. Sixty dots, gold, in the middle of the floor of the Hall of Roads, and every line between them solid. Not one dotted line left anywhere.

Except one. At the very top of the painted floor, under the north door, there was a single gold dot that wasn’t anybody, with nothing running to it at all.

“That one’s not anybody,” said Bee, seeing her look. “Yet.”

She found Ice in the banner room in the afternoon.

He was in the first of the nine chairs, at the far end, with his feet up on the rung of the next one. He was looking down the long table at the room. She came and stood beside his chair, and he looked up.

“Alice,” said Ice.

Nobody called her that except him and Jared. Nobody else knew. It was the name from before. Before streams, before Partner, before the game. Hearing it in a stone room in a round town under a ceiling of silver lines made something in her chest go very still.

“Ice,” said Hana.

“You came.”

“We were the last,” she said. “Us and Leaka and Curse. Everyone else was already here.”

Ice looked at her for a moment.

“No,” he said. “You weren’t the last. You were next.”

Hana didn’t know what to say to that. So she didn’t say anything. She sat down on the edge of the table beside his chair, the way you could with people you’d known for years, and looked down the room with him.

It was full. Fifty-something chairs, most of them sat in. Gabe telling a story with his hands. Xola and Leaka at the same end of the table, shouting at each other about something with enormous happiness. Thea by the window, in her chair, quiet. Halfway down the north side, Gigi was passing her a cup of tea through two other people’s hands. Hyvred on the stair outside the door, very carefully not looking in. Kotori and Tif and PenguChi with a pot between them. Deston counting nothing. Suzu doing nothing. Salty and Niko and Chris and Winter. Maigo, and Hako, and Ina, and Wendy just in from the gate. Calvin, Rachael, Dennis with his notebook. Xav at the far side with Malcolm and Rui Xiang and John and Azac. Tiger. Drew. Higu and Kei on the stair to the eye, sitting together.

And Milli coming toward her down the room. Jared behind her with a beam. Curse in his chair by the window with a wooden tag tied to its arm. Hana could just read the word on it from here. HOME.

She looked at Ice, beside her. He’d gone quiet. Not his ordinary quiet. A different one. He was looking down the long table, slowly, chair by chair, from one end to the other. His lips were moving, very slightly, with no sound, the way Kei’s moved when he was rehearsing.

He was counting.

Hana didn’t ask what. She thought she might know. She’d been his friend since long before this game, since a time when all of the people in this room were names in a group chat on the other side of a screen. Scattered across a dozen countries and twenty time zones, most of them never once in the same place. She looked down the table with him and thought: oh.

Ice stopped counting. He didn’t say the number. He just sat there for a while, looking.

“Everybody’s here,” said Hana, very quietly.

Ice didn’t say anything. But after a moment he took his feet down off the rung of the chair beside him, and sat up, and put his hands flat on the long table. And she saw his face do something she didn’t have a word for. It was the face of a man who’d been standing at the far end of a room for a very long time, and had just looked up and found the room had come down to meet him.

That evening, by the window, Milli sat in his chair next to Hana’s. Somebody had set out five more chairs at the end of the long table, past Thea’s six. Nobody had seen her do it. Nobody needed to ask who.

The sun was going down over the west road. The silver lines would come out soon on the ceiling of the dome. Somebody had told Hana they came out at moonrise, every single night, and she meant to stay up and watch.

“Is it closer?” said Milli.

Hana looked at her. At the window, and the long table, and the room full of everybody. At Ama Corrie in the doorway with her broom, which had a small wooden tag tied to the handle now, in blue ink. KEPT. Curse had given it to her in the afternoon. She’d tied it on herself, without saying anything, and then said hm, and then gone very quickly out of the room.

“It’s here,” said Hana.

Milli smiled. He put his hand flat on the arm of his chair, and Hana put hers on top of it.

Two feet. And eleven thousand every morning, out there.

But in here, tonight, in two chairs by a window in a round town, with everybody, it was here.
