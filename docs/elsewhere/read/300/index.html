Live from Elsewhere · Volume 12: Even

Chapter 300

Feet

3,362 words · 15 min read

Drew watched feet, and on the night before the bridge, Lucan’s weren’t moving.

He’d learned it a long time ago, before the game, hosting tournaments. Lobbies full of people waiting for a match to start. Faces lied; faces did what people wanted them to. Feet didn’t. A man about to leave shifted his weight toward the door a full minute before his face knew it. A team about to win stood with their feet pointing the same way. He’d watched feet for two and a half years in the game, and it had never once let him down.

He’d watched Lucan’s feet for a year. Since a night on the shingle at the foot of the island’s tower, after the Crown Below, when Lucan had come second and sat down beside him and not moved for six hours. They’d talked about feet, mostly. Lucan had said nobody had ever sat beside him. People went round him. He was a river.

Lucan’s feet had always been somewhere else. Even sitting. Even on the shingle that night, still for the first time in his life, his feet had been turned a little. Toward the next place. Ready to be there before anybody decided where he was. Drew had watched them for six hours and thought: he doesn’t know how to stop. He’s never once been where he is.

They weren’t turned now.

At the second dusk, on the north terrace, Marrok came along the rail.

Drew was at the rail with Ash and Kite, where he’d said he’d stand. The terrace was filling. People were finding places to sit all night so they’d have them in the morning. Marrok of the Open Hand came along the rail through all of them, big and brown, with the maul on his back he never put down, and his hands at his sides. Asking.

“Who’d stand next to you?” he said. To a woman from the Tidebound. Too fast, too loud, looking at the floor.

She said Marisol, and he said that’s an answer, and moved on. To a man from Meridian. Who’d stand next to you? Harlan. That’s an answer. He’d been doing it for months, Drew had heard. Since Aurelie told him to ask. Badly, every time. He’d got better at saying it. He hadn’t got better at anything else.

He got to Drew.

“Who’d stand next to you?” said Marrok.

Drew looked at his feet.

Pointing at the steps. At the way off the terrace. The whole time he was asking, his feet were pointing at the place he’d go to after, when it was over. He’d been asking for months and he’d never once stood still for the answer.

“Ash and Kite,” said Drew. “And somebody on a bridge.” He was still looking at Marrok’s feet. “Yours are pointing at the steps.”

Marrok looked down.

“When you ask,” said Drew, “they’re pointing at the way out. Like you want it to be over.” He was quiet. “Point them at whoever you asked. Then they’ll know you want to hear it.”

Marrok stood at the rail for a long time, looking at his own feet. Then, very slowly, like a man moving something heavy, he turned them. Toward Drew. And stood there.

“Who’d stand next to you?” said Marrok again. Slower.

“Ash and Kite,” said Drew. “And somebody on a bridge.”

“That’s three answers,” said Marrok.

“I’ve got three.”

Marrok nodded. He didn’t move on straight away. He stood there with his feet pointing at Drew for a moment longer, the way you’d stand after a thing had been said, to let it land. Then he went on along the rail. Drew watched his feet go. At the next person, they turned. Toward her. Before he asked.

“You taught him,” said Ash, beside him.

“I told him about his feet,” said Drew.

“That’s teaching,” said Ash. Which was Kei’s, from the long table, about Suzu.

He found Lucan at the third dawn, on the stones at the foot of the bridge over the third stream.

Not on the bridge. Below it, where the gorge wall came down to the water. There was a narrow shelf of flat wet stones there that nobody used, under the span, with the stream going past fast and loud. Lucan was sitting on one of them with his knees up and his sword beside him, looking at the water.

Drew climbed down the gorge wall. It took a while. He wasn’t built for climbing. He was built for standing still in a place and watching. He got to the bottom and sat down on the next stone, a sword’s length off.

He looked at Lucan’s feet.

Flat on the wet stone. Both of them. Pointing straight ahead, at the stream. Not turned. Not ready to be anywhere. Just there.

“You’re not moving,” said Drew.

“No,” said Lucan.

“Your feet.”

“I know,” said Lucan. “I’ve been practising that too.” He looked at his own feet. “Ves said if I was going to be on time, I’d have to stop being ready to leave. She said everybody can see it. She said Drew can see it from across a room.”

“I can.”

“I know,” said Lucan. “That’s why I came down here. Nobody can see feet under a bridge.” He almost smiled. “Except you.”

They sat there for a long time. The stream went under the bridge, fast and loud, over the stones. Up above them, on both walls of the gorge, the terraces were full of people who’d come to sit there all night so they’d have a place in the morning. Drew could hear them. A low sound, like a crowd before a final. He’d heard it a hundred times at the front of a lobby with a headset on.

He’d never once heard it for a friend.

“Are you frightened?” said Drew.

Lucan didn’t answer straight away. Drew watched his feet while he didn’t. They didn’t move.

“Yes,” said Lucan.

“Of losing?”

“No,” said Lucan. “I lost to him last July. On this bridge. It was the best thing that ever happened to me.” He was looking at the water. “I’m frightened of being early. Tomorrow. In front of all of them.” He tipped his head back at the terraces. “Eleven months learning to be on time. And then he’ll stand there, and I’ll want to be first. I’ll want it so much I’ll move before I’ve looked. And it’ll be July again.” He was quiet. “And Kestrel will see it. From the rail. A little apart.”

Drew thought about it.

“You won’t look at her,” he said.

“No.”

“You don’t look up.”

“Not since March,” said Lucan. “If I look up I start being early for them. For what they want to see.” He looked at his hands. “So I look at where the sword is. And nowhere else.”

“Then you’ll be all right,” said Drew.

“How do you know?”

Drew looked at Lucan’s feet. Flat on the wet stone. Pointing straight ahead.

“Your feet say so,” he said.

Lucan looked at him for a long time. Then he laughed. Quietly, under the bridge, with the stream going past.

“I’d forgotten,” he said. “That you can do that.”

“I’ve been doing it the whole time,” said Drew. “You never asked.”

He told Lucan about the lobbies. He’d never told anybody in the game. Kite knew a bit. Ash knew less. It wasn’t a secret. It just wasn’t the kind of thing he said.

Running the front of a tournament with a headset on, for a long time. Watching people come into a lobby and stand there, waiting for a match. Learning to tell who’d win from their feet. Not from their faces or their names or how good they were on a board. From whether their feet were pointing at the door or at the other team.

“Which ones won?” said Lucan.

“The ones whose feet pointed at the other team,” said Drew. “Not at the door. Not at the crowd. Not at themselves.” He looked at the stream. “At the other team. Like they couldn’t wait to see what they’d do.”

Lucan looked at his own feet. Pointing at the stream.

“Mine are pointing at the water,” he said.

“There’s nobody here,” said Drew. “Tomorrow there’ll be somebody on the bridge.” He paused. “Point them at him.”

Somebody came down the gorge wall at about the fourth hour. Drew heard them before he saw them. Two people, one of them climbing well and one of them climbing very well. A breath ahead of every handhold, the way she did everything.

Ice, and Hyvred.

They got to the bottom and stood on the wet stones at the foot of the gorge wall, under the bridge. Ice with his shield on his back. Hyvred holding his arm with both hands. They looked at Drew and Lucan sitting on their stones, a sword’s length apart.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Drew looked at Ice’s feet. He always did. He’d watched them for two and a half years, more than anybody’s. Ice’s feet in a fight told you nothing. They were always exactly where they needed to be, before you’d seen them move. Out of a fight, they told you one thing, always. Wherever Ice was standing, his feet were pointing at the far end of the room. At the door. Where he could see everybody come in.

They weren’t pointing at the door now. There wasn’t one. They were pointing at Lucan.

“Tank,” said Lucan.

“Lucan,” said Ice.

“You’re rested.”

“Yes.”

“You ate the fish man’s fish.”

“Every day,” said Ice. “He gave me extra. He said you told him to.”

“I did,” said Lucan. He didn’t get up. He looked up at Ice from his stone, at the foot of the bridge, with his knees up. “Who’d stand next to you?”

He’d asked it before. A year ago, on the shingle at the foot of the island’s tower, after the Crown Below. Drew had been there. Tank. Who’d stand next to you? And Ice had said Hyv.

Ice looked at Hyvred, beside him, on his arm.

“Hyv,” he said.

“Then she can watch,” said Lucan. The same as a year ago. “When I ask you. On the bridge. Properly.” He looked at her. “Tomorrow.”

Hyvred looked back at him. Edge to edge.

“I’m going to scream,” she said.

“I know,” said Lucan.

“The whole time. Every hit. Whoever it is. I’m going to be the loudest thing in Masmark.”

“Good,” said Lucan. “I’ve been practising being screamed at.” He tipped his head up at the terraces. “Ves stood on the bridge every morning for a week and shouted while she hit me in the ear with a slate. So I’d stop looking.” He almost smiled. “She’s very loud when she wants to be. Nobody knows.”

Hyvred looked at him for a long time. Then, very slightly, she laughed.

“I like you,” she said. “I didn’t think I would.”

“Nobody does,” said Lucan. “At first.”

Ice sat down. On a stone at the foot of the gorge wall, a sword’s length off from both of them, with Hyvred beside him. Four people on four wet stones under a bridge, at the fourth hour, with the stream going past and half of Masmark on the terraces above, waiting.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Drew watched their feet. All four. Hyvred’s, curled under her, with her tail round them, the way she sat on the bottom step of the Seven Below. Not calling. His own, flat on the stone, the way they always were, the one who stays. Ice’s, pointing at Lucan.

And Lucan’s.

Drew watched them turn. Slowly. Not toward the next place. Not ready to leave. From the stream, where they’d been pointing all night, round on the wet stone, until they were pointing at Ice. And stopped there.

Like they couldn’t wait to see what he’d do.

Drew didn’t say anything. He didn’t have to. He just looked at Lucan’s feet, pointing at the other team, the way the ones who won always had. And then up at Lucan’s face, which was looking at the stream, and didn’t know.

“Your feet,” said Drew.

Lucan looked down at them.

He looked at them for a long time. Pointing at Ice, on the wet stone, under the bridge.

“Oh,” said Lucan.

They didn’t shake hands. Lucan held his out, palm up, the way he’d held it out on the bridge last July at six per cent, with his sword gone into the stream. Ice looked at it.

“After,” said Ice.

Lucan looked at him.

“Last time you held it out after,” said Ice. “I’d like to keep it that way.” He stood up. “If you don’t mind.”

Lucan looked at his own hand for a moment. Then he closed it, and put it on his knee.

“After,” he agreed.

Ice and Hyvred went back up the gorge wall. Hyvred first, a breath ahead of every handhold, holding out her hand behind her at the height his would be. He took it, every time, without looking.

Drew watched them go up. Then he looked at Lucan.

Lucan was looking at the place where Ice had sat. At the wet stone, empty now. His feet were still pointing at it.

“He didn’t move his feet,” said Lucan. “The whole time. He sat there pointing them at me.”

“He always points them at the far end of the room,” said Drew. “At the door. Where he can see everybody come in.” He was quiet. “Tonight there wasn’t a door. So he pointed them at you.”

Lucan didn’t say anything for a long time.

“Nobody’s ever pointed their feet at me,” he said at last. “They point them at where I’m going to be.”

“He’s not going to,” said Drew. “Tomorrow. He’s going to point them at where you are.”

“What do his feet say?” said Lucan, after a while. “In a fight. You’ve watched them longer than anybody.”

“Nothing,” said Drew.

Lucan looked at him.

“Nothing,” said Drew again. “In a fight his feet are always already where they need to be. Before you see them move.” He looked at the empty stone. “I’ve watched them two and a half years. They’ve never once told me what he’ll do. Only what he’s done.”

“Then how will I know?”

“You won’t,” said Drew. “Nobody does. That’s what everybody says about him. It’s like fighting a boss.” He thought about it. “Don’t look at where he’ll be. Look at where he is.”

“That’s what Sketch did. Against the one with the scythe.”

“Yes.”

“It worked once,” said Lucan. “And then they tied.”

“Tied’s not losing,” said Drew.

Ves came down the gorge wall at the fifth hour, with her slate, and sat on a stone at the end of the shelf. She didn’t say anything. She’d come to sit.

“What are you counting now?” said Lucan, without looking round. “You stopped counting falls.”

“Days,” said Ves. “Since the last one.”

“How many?”

“Thirty-one,” said Ves. She wrote something. “Thirty-two, in about an hour.”

Lucan was quiet for a long time.

“Will you count tomorrow?”

“Hits,” said Ves. “Both ways. And whether you look up.” She put the slate on her knee. “I’ll write it down if you do. So you’ll know after.”

“I won’t.”

“I know,” said Ves. “I’m going to write it down anyway. He didn’t look up. So it’s somewhere.”

They sat under the bridge until the first grey came up over the east rim. Then Lucan got up and went to the Hall to sleep, the way Kestrel said you should. We rest first. Drew stayed on his stone a while longer, with the stream going past, and thought about the lobbies.

He’d stood at the front of a lot of finals with a headset on. Watching two teams walk out. He’d always known who’d win from their feet. He’d never once been wrong.

He didn’t know tomorrow.

He’d watched Ice’s feet for two and a half years and they’d never once told him anything in a fight. And he’d watched Lucan’s for one night, under a bridge, turning slowly on a wet stone until they pointed at the other team. Like the ones who won always did.

He couldn’t tell.

It was the first time in his life feet hadn’t told him. He sat under the bridge and found he didn’t mind at all. It meant it was going to be a good one.

He climbed back up the gorge wall in the grey, slowly, not built for it. At the top, on the north terrace, people were asleep in rows along the rail, wrapped in coats. Ash was there, at the rail, awake, where he’d said he’d stand. Kite beside her, asleep sitting up. A space on her other side, a sword’s length, where Drew went.

Further along, a little apart, at the end of the rail: Kestrel. Awake. Standing exactly where she’d said she’d be.

Drew sat down in his space beside Ash.

“Well?” said Ash.

“I don’t know,” said Drew.

Ash looked at him sideways.

“You always know,” she said. “From their feet.”

“Not this time,” said Drew. “His turned round.” He looked down at the bridge, empty, in the grey. “I’ve never seen anybody’s do that.”
