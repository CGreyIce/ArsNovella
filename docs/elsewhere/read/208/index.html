Chapter 208

The Crownless Hall

3,576 words · 16 min read

Thea counted the third flight the way everybody counted the last flight of the two hundred and twelve: out loud, under her breath, with the wind in her face.

There was wind. Under a lake. She didn’t know how. It came up the stair from below, cold and steady, the way the wind came over the rim of the eye of the dome on the last sixty-six steps of the round town. It pushed at her shield. It pushed at all of them, nineteen in a line going down, with a gap in the line where Drew should have been.

“Sixty-four,” said RX, ahead of her. “Sixty-five.”

“Sixty-six,” said Thea, and stepped off the last one.

“And then it’s done,” said RX.

Nobody answered him. Thea looked at Hyvred at the front, beside Ice. She was looking down the passage ahead with her eyes that saw everything, and her tail very still.

At the bottom of the sixty-six there was a hall with a throne in it.

She’d sat with Hyvred at the kept place the night before, after the gallery, after Drew.

Hyvred had been at the edge of the ring, by herself, with her bow across her knees, looking back the way they’d come. At the dark of the passage, where the Long Gallery was. Where a tile on a pillar in the middle of a lake had gone down with a man on it, saluting.

Thea had sat down a pace off. Not close. Near. The way she’d learned to sit next to people in this game who were holding something.

“He went in the breath,” said Hyvred, after a long time. “Before Ice. I saw his feet. Drew went in the breath before Ice’s back foot came round.” Her voice did something. “Nobody’s ever done that. Not Kestrel. Not Xav. Not Lucan.”

“Somebody had to,” said Thea.

“I would have,” said Hyvred.

Thea looked at her.

“If Drew hadn’t,” said Hyvred. “I’d have gone. I was two paces behind him. I’d have gone onto the tile before he did.” She was looking at the dark. “I didn’t think about it. I just knew. I’d have stood on it so he didn’t have to.”

Thea didn’t say anything for a while. She thought about a girl who’d hidden behind a wing for an hour saying mrp. Who’d walked out of a banner room with her face white and gone up two hundred and twelve steps, because a man had said Monday, ahead.

“I know,” said Thea. “That’s why it’s allowed.”

Hyvred turned her head.

“That’s what I told him,” said Thea. “In January. On a message. It’s allowed. He didn’t think it was. He thought the front of the line wasn’t for that.” She looked at the dark too. “And you’d have stood on a tile in a lake for him. And he’d have done the same for you, before you’d decided. And Drew did it for both of you.” She paused. “That’s not the front of the line being for something else. That’s just what it’s always been for. He just never let anybody stand there with him before.”

Hyvred was quiet for a long time.

“You’re very annoying,” she said at last. “Xola says you’re always right and it’s very annoying.”

“Xola’s right,” said Thea. “It’s very annoying.”

Hyvred laughed, a bit, wetly. Then she got up and went back across the ring to where Ice was sitting against an iron post with his shield across his knees, and sat down beside him. Not two paces off. Beside him.

It was the biggest room Thea had ever been in. Bigger than the Hall of Roads. Bigger than the drowned forge. A great round hall under the lake, under a dome of black rock that went up and up into the dark. Round the walls, seven tall stone posts, evenly spaced, like the posts of a lantern ring. Each with a great iron cage on top. Cold, empty, dark.

And in the middle of the hall, on a dais of black stone, a throne.

There was somebody sitting on it.

He was very big. Taller than Salty standing, sitting down. He was made of the same dark as the things that came up out of the pools, but more of it, packed tight, the way a storm cloud is packed tight. He had the shape of a man in armour, sitting on a throne, with his hands on its arms. His head was bowed.

And on his head, where a crown would be, there was nothing. A ring of nothing. A place where something had been and wasn’t.

“Oh,” said Hyvred, at the front. Very quietly. “Oh, that’s sad.”

Thea didn’t know why it was sad. She looked at the king on his throne, with his head bowed and the empty place on his head where the crown had been. She thought she knew, a bit.

“It’s the end,” said RX, behind her. “Right? It’s the end. Sixty-six. A hundred and six and forty and sixty-six. The round town. Two hundred and twelve. That’s the top. That’s the eye.” He was nearly bouncing. “We’ve done it. We’re at the end. This is the boss.”

Kei, in the middle of the line, was looking at his map. He’d drawn the stairs, all three, a hundred and six and forty and sixty-six. He was looking at the shape of it.

“I don’t know,” said Kei.

It woke when they stepped into the hall.

Not all at once. The head came up first. Slowly. The great dark head with the empty ring on it, lifting from where it had been bowed for three hundred years, or three thousand. It looked at them. It didn’t have eyes. Thea felt it look anyway.

Then it looked at the seven cages on the seven posts round the walls.

Dark. Every one.

And it stood up.

“Lamps,” called Hyvred. “The cages. Seven. They’re— it wants them lit. No. It wants them dark.” She stopped. “I can’t tell. I can’t tell which.”

From the pools at the edges of the hall, where water stood black and still at the foot of every post, things began to come up. Thea had seen enough of them now. Tall thin ones. Low flat ones. Coldness moving on the ground. They came up out of the still water at the foot of every post, and they didn’t go for the lamps, because there weren’t any lamps.

They went for the twenty lamps the nineteen were carrying.

“Light them,” said Kei. “The cages. All seven. Light them first. Give them something else to go to.”

“That’s what they want,” said Hyvred. “The things. That’s what the king—“

“I know,” said Kei. “Light them anyway. Threes.” He was already calling it. “Thea, Gigi, Xola. The north post. Salty, Wendy, Xyro, the east. Xav, RX, Ryan, south-east—“ On and on, round the hall, every post. Six threes. “Ice. The king.”

“Alone?” said Hyvred.

“Hyv calls him,” said Kei. “And Bee shields him. And Malcolm. Three.”

Ice was already walking toward the throne.

Thea went to the north post with Gigi on her left and Xola on her right.

She didn’t think about it. That was the strange part. Six months ago, she’d stood on a south-west road with Gigi for the first time since a year of not speaking. Holding a gap with thorn and Turn, saying good thorn, good turn, not looking at each other. She’d thought about every step of that. Every word. Whether it was too much. Whether it was the wrong thing.

Now she went to the north post with Gigi on her left and didn’t think about it at all. Her feet took her there. Gigi’s took her there too. They’d come to step the same, without noticing.

The cage on top of the north post was cold iron, very old, with a star on top. Like the cages on the road of lights. Like the empty one on its hook by the north door, over Isa’s chair.

Thea held up her own lamp. The plain one from her belt, the one she’d carried since Millrace. She reached it up the post as far as she could, and Gigi laid a thorn up the stone for her to stand on, and she held the flame to the old wick in the cage.

It caught.

Gold. Steady. A flame in the iron at the top of the post, thirty feet up, lighting the north end of the hall.

The things from the pools turned. All of them, at the north end, at once. Away from the lamps the three of them were carrying. Toward the post. Toward the cage.

“Thorn,” said Gigi. “Left.”

“Turn,” said Thea. “Right.”

And they held the north post.

She’d tell it afterward to Xola, in the real world, on a call, at two in the morning. The way she’d told her everything for years. Not because Xola hadn’t been there. Xola had been there, right beside her, the whole time. But because that was how they did it. You told the other person, after, even if they’d been there. So it would be real.

She’d say it was the hardest hour of her life in the game.

The things came up out of the pool at the foot of the north post and went for the cage. Up the post, flowing, the way the eleven on the moor had flowed toward the waystone. And Thea held them off it. Her tower shield up against the post, Turn. Swinging the whole of the north side round like a gate, so whatever was coming up met the face of the shield and went back. And Gigi’s thorn laid across every gap the shield left. Lie. Living thorn growing across the stone at the foot of the post, holding. And Xola between them with her trident, glaring, putting it through whatever got past both of them. Saying Thea, you’re at sixty in the voice she used when she meant I’m going to kill you after.

Thorn, left. Lie. Turn, right. Good thorn. Good turn.

Over and over. Like a song. She’d sung once, a long time ago. Every night, on a call, for somebody who played games while she sang. She didn’t sing now. She didn’t need to. The words were the song.

And across the hall, in the middle, on the dais, Ice and the king.

She watched them when she could. Between turns, between gaps, a glance across the hall. It was like watching weather. The king was enormous and slow and every blow it struck was the whole of the dark behind it. Ice stood in front of it and took them. Not going back. Not answering. Holding. His window in the corner of everybody’s eye. 80. 71. 66.

And Hyvred, at the edge of the dais, calling.

“Left. Down. It’s going to sweep. Now.”

He went. He was always there. He didn’t turn his head.

Thea had seen him go without turning his head on a hundred streams. When Hyvred called. She’d seen it on the VOD of the Elm Below, the left heart, and the stream on the longest night, and the north tunnel every morning. She’d seen it, and seen Hyvred’s tail, and seen the whole thing, months ago. Before he had. Before anyone except Xola, who saw everything Thea saw, a breath later.

It’s allowed, she’d written to him. It was always allowed. You just never asked.

She watched him now, across the hall. Going left without turning his head, because Hyvred said left. And she thought, holding the north post with Gigi on her left: he’s not holding anything shut any more.

She didn’t know how she could tell. She’d known him a long time. Since before they’d drifted. Since every night on a call. She could tell.

“Thea,” said Xola. “You’re at forty.”

“I’m fine.”

“You’re at FORTY.”

“Turn,” said Thea, and turned.

At forty minutes, the seventh lamp was lit.

All seven cages round the hall, burning gold on their posts, thirty feet up. Six threes holding them. The things from the pools leaning toward them and being turned back, again and again. And the hall full of light for the first time in however long it had been dark.

And the king stopped.

In the middle of the dais, in the middle of a blow, it stopped. Its great dark arm came down slowly to its side. It turned its head. The empty ring where the crown had been. It looked round the hall. At the seven lamps, all lit.

Then it looked down.

At the floor of the dais. At the black stone under the throne.

“It’s looking down,” called Hyvred. “It’s looking— Ice. It’s not looking at you. It’s looking down.”

Ice stepped back. One step. He didn’t lower the shield. He watched it.

The Crownless King knelt.

Slowly. The way the Last Watcher had knelt in the Long Watch, Suzu said, when the eighty-first light went in. It went down on one knee on the dais in front of the throne. It put one great dark hand flat on the black stone of the floor. And bowed its head, with the empty ring on it, toward the floor.

Toward something underneath.

And the floor of the dais opened.

Not all of it. A square in the middle, in front of the throne, where the king’s hand was. A slab of black stone sliding back, the way the hatch under Ama Corrie’s stair had sunk when Bee hung the banner on its hooks. And under it, a stair.

Going down.

Further down. Under the hall, under the lake, under everything.

The king took its hand away. It stood up, slowly, and turned, and sat back down on its throne. It bowed its head again. Back to where it had been. As if it had only been waiting for somebody to light the lamps so it could show them the way, and now it was done.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

“It’s not the end,” said RX, at last. Very quietly. He’d stopped bouncing. “It’s not the end. There’s more.”

“There’s more,” said Kei.

“But the stairs. A hundred and six and forty and sixty-six. That’s two hundred and twelve. That’s the top. That’s the eye—“

“That’s the round town,” said Kei. He was looking at his map. At the three stairs he’d drawn. “That’s our stair. The round town’s.” He looked up. “This isn’t the round town. This is Masmark.” He looked at the open square in the dais, and the stair going down. “Kestrel said. On the bridge. Ice told me. She said everybody’s going to think they’re nearly there, because they’ve done the steps.”

“Kestrel knew?” said RX.

“Kestrel saw it,” said Ice. He’d come down off the dais. He was at sixty-one per cent, with Bee’s grey Shield still flickering on his arm. “She didn’t decide it. She just saw.” He looked at the stair. “She’ll be further down than us by now.”

“How much further?”

Ice didn’t answer. He looked at the corner of his window instead. At the guild board, the one the Rim Hall put up for the race, that everybody could see.

“VANTA’s in the deep,” said Kei. “Already.”

“Sable Court’s retreating,” said John, reading it over Kei’s shoulder. “Eight of twenty fallen. In the deep. Something down there took eight of Sable Court.”

Nobody said anything.

“Not yet,” said Hyvred, suddenly.

Everybody looked at her.

“Tiger said it,” said Hyvred. “At the first mile. Not yet. When everybody shouted at him to come in.” She looked at the stair going down, with her eyes that saw everything. “It’s not the end. Not yet. So we’re not done. Not yet.”

Thea looked at her. At Hyvred standing at the edge of the dais with her bow, and her tail very still, and her shoulder nearly touching Ice’s arm, saying not yet.

She thought about the girl who’d hidden behind Bee’s wing for an hour saying mrp. Who’d hit him every time he came back from anywhere for five months. Who’d stood up in the banner room with her face white and walked out and gone up two hundred and twelve steps.

And she thought: she’s not hiding behind anything any more either.

They didn’t go down. Not that night. It was half past six in the morning, Malaysia time, and the egg would throw them out at eight. Kei said you didn’t go into the deep with an hour and a half and nineteen people at sixty per cent. So they sat in the Crownless Hall, under the seven lamps, round the throne where the king sat with his head bowed again. A kept place now. Nothing came inside.

Thea sat with her back against the north post. Gigi a pace off on her left. Xola on her right, with her head on Thea’s shoulder, asleep. She’d fought for forty minutes and then said you’re an idiot about nine times and then fallen asleep in the middle of the tenth.

Across the hall, on the edge of the dais, Ice was sitting with his back against the throne. The Crownless King sat above him with its head bowed, like an old man asleep in a chair. Hyvred was beside him, with her head on his shoulder. Holding his hand. Neither of them saying anything.

Thea watched them for a long time.

“You did that,” said Gigi, beside her. Quietly. Not looking at her.

Thea turned her head.

“Higu told me,” said Gigi. “Operation tail. You and Xola. Messaging him for a month.” She was looking at the throne. “It’s allowed.”

Thea didn’t say anything for a while.

“He never asked,” she said, at last. “About anything. For himself. Not in all the years I’ve known him. Not once.” She looked across the hall at the two of them on the edge of the dais. “Somebody had to tell him he could.”

Gigi was quiet for a long time.

“You told me I could,” she said. “At the south-west road. In July. You didn’t say it. But you stood next to me at a gap and said Turn. And I thought, oh. I can.”

Thea looked at her.

Gigi didn’t look back. She was looking at the throne, at the king with his head bowed. Her face doing nothing, the way Gigi’s face did nothing. Her hands perfectly still on her staff.

“Good thorn,” said Thea, after a long time. Very quietly.

“Good turn,” said Gigi.

That was all. It was enough. Fine was enough. They’d said that, on a south-west road, a long time ago. They sat against the north post under a lamp they’d kept for forty minutes together, a pace apart. They didn’t say anything else until the egg threw them out at eight.
