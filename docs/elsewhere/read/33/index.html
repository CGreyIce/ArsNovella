Chapter 33

Cradle Commons

3,992 words · 17 min read

The queue for Cradle Commons started outside a shuttered bubble-tea shop on the fourth floor of a mall in town, at a quarter to seven on Thursday morning. By eight it went round the corner, past the escalators, and down one level.

Maigo was eleventh. He’d been there since six fifty-one. He’d told everyone on the group call he’d be there at seven, and Salty had said “you’ll be there at half six,” and Wendy had said “he’ll be there at half six and he’ll have talked to everyone in the queue by seven,” and they’d both been wrong. He’d talked to everyone in the queue by six fifty-eight.

“That one’s a nurse,” he told Salty, when Salty and Wendy came up the escalator at twenty past seven with a bag of kaya toast. “Night shift. She’s going in after work, she says it’s the only time she gets. That one’s a student, he’s doing it for his little brother, the brother’s twelve and can’t come in till he’s thirteen. That one—“ He pointed at a man in a suit near the front, very upright, holding a briefcase. “That one won’t tell me. I think he’s a lawyer. I think he’s been here since five.”

“Good morning, Maigo,” said Wendy.

“Good morning! Kaya toast!” He took a piece. “Did you know they’ve got forty eggs in there? Forty. In booths. With curtains. It’s like a capsule hotel but you go to another world.”

“It’s a coffin with Wi-Fi,” said Wendy.

“It’s a very nice coffin with Wi-Fi.”

Salty didn’t say anything. Salty never said anything before eight. He stood beside Wendy with the bag of toast, one big arm round her shoulders, looking at the shuttered doors at the front of the queue. A new sign had gone up over them in the night, grey letters on white, very clean.

CRADLE COMMONS Licensed by the ELSEWHERE studio Where are you?

“I’m in a mall,” said Salty, to the sign. “On the fourth floor.”

At eight o’clock the shutters went up.

Inside it was dim and cool and very quiet, with low blue light along the floor like a cinema before the film. It had been a cinema, Maigo realised, looking at the slope of the room. They’d taken the seats out and levelled it in steps. On every step, in a row, stood the eggs. Forty of them, white, humming, each in its own little booth with a grey curtain you could draw across, like the sleeping bays on an overnight train. At the bottom, where the screen had been, there was a long counter with three people behind it in grey polo shirts. On the wall behind them, a screen showed a slow loop of the game. A marsh at dawn. A white cliff. A field of heather. Somebody’s hand on a sword.

“First time?” said the girl behind the counter, when they got there.

“Yes!” said Maigo. “First time! All three of us! Our friend booked us. He’s in Australia. He booked six slots in four cities in the first minute the bookings opened. He says he did it at a completely normal time.”

The girl smiled at him the way people always smiled at Maigo, a bit helplessly. “Names?”

They gave the names. She found them on her screen: booked by KeiKatamori, three in a row, sixteen, seventeen and eighteen. Twelve hours today, the maximum, for opening day, and six hours a night after that until the end of January.

“Lie down, put your thumb on the glass, and the rest is the game’s job,” she said. “Your account’s on your thumb, not on the egg. You can lie down in any egg in any Commons in the world and it’ll know you. Please don’t eat in the eggs.”

Salty put the kaya toast down on the counter very slowly.

The eggs were on the fourth step. Sixteen, seventeen, eighteen, side by side, with a curtain between each.

Salty and Wendy were sixteen and seventeen. Maigo was eighteen, on the end. He stood in the gap between their booths while they took their shoes off and lined them up, and looked at the two of them, and felt something go warm in his chest that he couldn’t have explained.

They’d been together a long time. Long enough that they didn’t really talk about it any more; they just were. And they were going to lie down in two eggs eighteen inches apart, with a grey curtain between them, and wake up in two random towns that could be anywhere in the world.

“You’ll be miles apart,” he said. “In there. You know that? Random towns. You could be on opposite sides of the map.”

“I know,” said Wendy. She had her headphones round her neck, the lavender ones she always wore, and she was holding them with both hands. “We talked about it.”

“We’ll walk,” said Salty.

“It could be a month!”

“Then we’ll walk for a month.” Salty climbed into his egg and lay back in the gel. He looked up at the ceiling. Then he looked sideways, at the curtain, at the shape of Wendy on the other side of it, and reached out and drew it back a little, so he could see her.

“Hey,” he said.

“Hey,” said Wendy.

Maigo went and got into his own egg, quickly, before he cried in a cinema at eight in the morning.

The call came through before the lid had finished closing.

Which is your five in the afternoon. I am in bed, in the dark, on a call with five people who are about to step into a fairy tale without me. “Thumbs,” said Kei. “On three.”

Maigo put his thumb on the glass.

“One,” said Kei. “Two.”

It was raining.

He’d known it would be. He’d watched Ice’s first minute probably fifty times. The small wooden room. The rain on the window. The chair, the mirror, the door. He’d known exactly what it would be like.

He hadn’t known it would smell.

Wet wood. Old wood, warm, like the inside of an old cupboard. Rain on dust. Something green from outside, under the door. He stood in the middle of the little room and breathed in, and breathed in again, and laughed out loud at nothing.

Then he looked in the mirror.

He’d built it two nights ago in the pre-launch creator, on his phone, in bed, a feature at a time, the way you’d build anything you’d been looking at for years. Bark-brown skin with the grain running through it, like a young tree’s. Two branching antlers coming up out of his hair, which was mostly leaves. Small green leaves on the antler tips that moved when he moved his head. Glowing white eyes. It was the face he’d worn on stream for years: the little tree guy, the sapling, the one in the orange hoodie who talked too much.

It was him. It was standing in a room, in the rain, and it was him.

The mirror showed him the sunglasses.

The pixel ones. Black, square, eight-bit, the deal with it glasses that had been on his model since the day he’d drawn them on as a joke and then never taken them off. He looked at them for one second.

“Yes,” he said. “Obviously. Yes. Those. Forever.”

They appeared on his face. The world went very slightly darker and very slightly more square at the edges, and he would never take them off as long as he lived.

“I’m on twenty,” said Wendy. “I’m a normal person.”

He typed Maigo, and held his breath, and it went green. He punched the air in a small wooden room in the rain and said “yes,” and on the call, five people laughed.

He took the staff.

He hadn’t planned to. He’d planned something with a bit more flash: a bow, maybe, or the twin knives, something people would clip. But the rack by the door had a staff on it. A plain one, waist-high to shoulder-high, a length of grey wood worn smooth and dark at the place where a hand would go. It looked less like it had been made and more like it had grown that way and somebody had found it.

He put his hand on it and the wood was warm, the way bark is warm in the sun.

[ What you carry, and how you carry it, will shape what you become. ]

“Hello,” he said to it. Then, because he was on a call: “I’ve taken a stick.”

“Good,” said Kei.

“That’s what they gave Suzu,” said Hako. “On his first day. I wanted a scythe. They gave me a stick.”

“It’s a staff,” said Maigo with dignity. “And I’m a tree. It’s practically family.”

He opened the door.

Orchard End was on a hillside, and the hillside was all apple trees.

That was the first thing. Terraces of them, going up and down, old and low and twisted, in rows along the contour of the hill like green steps. The morning sun came through them in slants. The grass under them was long and wet and full of fallen apples, and the air smelled of apples, and cider, and woodsmoke, and wet grass. Somewhere bees were going about their business in the warm. At the bottom of the hill there was a stream and a mill, and a small stone town round a green with a well in the middle, all honey-coloured stone and moss on the roofs. At the top of the hill, by itself, there was one tree much bigger and older than the others. Its trunk was split and hollow and half dead, and the other half was in leaf.

He stood in the doorway of the little inn on the green, which was where his wooden room had opened, with his staff in his hand and his sunglasses on, and said nothing at all for nearly a minute. It was the longest anyone on the call had ever heard him go without talking. Salty, from his own town somewhere, said “Maigo?” and he didn’t answer.

He was a tree. In an orchard.

“I’ve rolled the best town,” he said eventually, with total conviction. “Out of everyone. In the whole game. I’ve won.”

He went live at nine.

He did it in Follow, the camera floating a little behind and above him, the way he always streamed. The way you could see his antlers and his sunglasses and his orange-ish beginner’s tunic and the whole green hillside behind him. His regulars were there by two minutes past, the way they always were on a stream day.

He cooked.

He spent the whole morning in Orchard End and loved every second of it. He met a woman called Mother Quince, very old and very small, who kept the big half-dead tree at the top of the hill. She looked at him for a long time over a basket of windfalls and said, “You’re a bit wooden, for a traveller.” He said “Thank you!” so warmly that she laughed out loud, and her granddaughter dropped the basket.

He learned that the big tree was called the Old Pippin, and that it was older than the town. It had been struck by lightning in Mother Quince’s grandmother’s time and had been dying, slowly, ever since, one branch a year. Nobody could stop it. Every spring there were fewer leaves.

He learned that Mother Quince grafted. That was what she did, all day, with a little curved knife and a ball of waxed twine. She cut a living twig from one tree and fitted it into a cut in another and bound them together, so tight and true that the two grew into one. Half the trees on the hill were grafts of grafts of grafts, going back a hundred years to the Old Pippin.

“It’s not making,” she said, binding one with her old spotted hands, very quick. “It’s joining. You don’t make it grow. You put the two living bits together, close as you can, and you say hold, and you hold. And then it’s up to them.”

And he learned the staff from her, because there was nobody else in Orchard End to learn it from. She wasn’t a fighter. She was the town’s hedge-healer, which she said was the same as being its grafter, only with people.

It took him eleven tries to get above forty per cent. He didn’t mind. He was patient; he’d always been patient. That was the thing people never guessed about him, because he talked so much. He did it again and again on a scratched-up apple-picker who’d fallen off a ladder, with the staff in his left hand and his right making the little cut-and-bind in the air. He said hold like he meant it, and watched the edges of the scrape come together under his hand like two halves of a twig.

He took Farming because the orchard needed hands, and Cooking because the pickers needed feeding.

There was a long table under the trees at the top terrace where the pickers ate at midday, and nobody had cooked for them since the inn’s cook had hurt her wrist. So at noon Maigo found himself in the inn’s kitchen with a sack of windfalls and a wheel of hard white cheese and a bag of flour, and a pot of mutton from the butcher on the green. He’d checked with the butcher twice, to be sure what it was. He made apple-and-cheese turnovers and a mutton-and-barley stew. Then he carried them up the hill to the long table under the trees, and twenty pickers ate the lot and asked who he was.

“I’m Maigo,” he said. “I’m a tree. I’ll be here all week.”

They laughed, and one of them said, “You’re a funny one,” the way NPCs said it here, warmly, as if they’d remember it. That was the thing about the people in this world. They remembered. Ice had said it on stream, over and over, in the first week. The eel man remembers. Sedge remembers. Be nice. They remember.

He’d be nice.

The others came and went on the call all day, in pieces, the way you’d hear people in other rooms of the same house.

Salty was in a mining town on a cliff, somewhere grey and cold and windy. He’d taken a greatsword nearly as long as he was tall and sounded completely satisfied with his life. “It’s heavy,” he said. “Good.” Wendy was in a forest. She didn’t say much about it except “the trees are very tall,” in a voice that meant she was looking up at them, and then, a while later, “I’ve got a bow. I don’t know what I’m doing. It’s fine.” Hako was in a river town full of trappers and was already Lv 2. He’d taken a spear and was learning to stand in the shallows and spear fish with it, and kept narrating it like a nature documentary. And Kei, from Australia, was somewhere with a hot spring and an old woman who made poisons. He said very little, and listened to everyone, and asked each of them, at some point, quietly, “Where are you? On the map?” and wrote it down.

At five in the afternoon, Ina came on.

“Right,” he said, from a café in England at nine in the morning, his morning, in the voice of a man who’d had three coffees. “Right. I’m lying down. Everyone shut up. I want to hear the rain.”

Nobody said anything. They all shut up. Five people in five towns, in the middle of whatever they were doing, went quiet on the call so Ina could hear the rain on a window in a small wooden room. Maigo, sitting on the long table under the apple trees with his staff across his knees, held his breath.

“Oh,” said Ina, very softly, after a moment. “Oh, that’s lovely.”

At half past eight that night, the game’s night and his own, something happened that he’d never forget.

He was sitting under the Old Pippin at the top of the hill in the dark. The lanterns were lit down on the green and the stars were out over the Leafwold. Seventy-something people were watching him try to graft a dead branch back onto the Old Pippin and fail. He’d tried it nine times. It wasn’t working. Mother Quince had told him it wouldn’t, and he’d wanted to try anyway.

Then his chat stopped.

Not stopped. Went sideways. A window came up across the bottom of his vision, purple, and he didn’t understand it for a second.

He showed them Orchard End in the dark: the green, the well, the mill, the long table under the trees where the pickers ate. He showed them Graft on a picker’s grazed hand, and Mother Quince on her doorstep, who looked at the stream’s little light floating behind him and said, “Your friends are very loud tonight.” He showed them the Old Pippin, split and half alive, and told them about the lightning and the one branch a year, and the grafts going back a hundred years. He told them he’d tried nine times to put a dead branch back and it hadn’t worked.

“Mother Quince says you can’t graft dead to living,” he said. “You can only graft living to living. You can’t make it grow. At a quarter past nine his booking ended and the egg gently told him so.

He said goodbye to nine thousand people the way he said goodbye to twenty, which was at length, to everyone, by name where he could manage it. Then he lay in the gel in booth eighteen in a cinema on the fourth floor of a mall, with the lid lifting over him and the low blue light along the floor, and didn’t move for a long time.

When he finally sat up, Salty and Wendy were already sitting up in their eggs. Their curtains were drawn back, and they were looking at him over the gap.

“Ice raided you,” said Wendy.

“Ice raided me.”

“Nine thousand people.”

“Nine thousand people.” He took his sunglasses off. They weren’t there, of course; he’d left them in Orchard End on a face made of bark. He found he’d put his hand up to his face anyway, to the place where they’d been. “He called me maigotron.”

“He always calls you maigotron,” said Salty.

“I know.” Maigo looked at his phone, which had three hundred and eleven notifications on it and a new follower count that was obviously wrong. Then at the two of them in their eggs, a foot and a half apart in Singapore and who knew how far apart in Elsewhere, with their hands on the edges of the gel. “I know. It’s just, he was in everyone’s chat. For years. Mine too. He was always just there, with twelve people, doing stupid stuff to make me laugh. And now he’s—“ He stopped.

“Now he’s sending them over,” said Wendy. “To us.”

She said it in her ordinary voice, a bit defensive. But she was smiling a small knowing smile at nothing, and her hand on the edge of the egg had found Salty’s across the gap.
