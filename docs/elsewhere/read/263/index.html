Live from Elsewhere · Volume 11: Three Hundred and One

Chapter 263

Edges

3,430 words · 15 min read

Xola had been in the nothing for three weeks going north and she was not, under any circumstances, going back into it.

“It’s not nothing,” said Hyv, beside her, on the heather, at the edge of the still water. “Look.”

Xola looked.

South. Away from the hill. Where the dark country had been nothing on the way up: not black, not anything, just not there. Where you couldn’t see your own hand, and walked on a rope behind a candle with Hako narrating so you’d know where everybody was.

It was dark.

Just dark. The ordinary kind. The kind with depth in it, that you could see into a bit if you waited. Heather going away south, short and dry, under a black sky. And low on the horizon, very faint, where the dark met the ground, an edge. A line. Where the sky stopped and the land began.

There hadn’t been an edge on the way up. There hadn’t been anything.

“Oh,” said Xola.

“It knows where it is,” said Hyv. “Isa said. When the middle’s lit, the dark knows where it is. So it’s just dark.” She was looking at the line on the horizon, edge to edge. “It has edges.”

Xola looked at it for a long time. Then she burst into tears, which she did, with her whole face, the moment she felt anything.

Slep, behind her, put his hand on her back between her shoulders and didn’t say anything.

They walked south for nine days.

No rope. They didn’t need one. You could see each other, a bit, in the dark, by the candles. Nine little lights on the heather, going south, close together. Not in a line any more. In a sort of clump, the way people walk when they’ve been on a rope for three weeks and stopped being on one, and haven’t stopped walking close.

Hako narrated for the first two nights out of habit, and then stopped. He said there was too much to look at now. He didn’t need the voice.

Xola missed it.

She didn’t tell him. She’d tell him later, when they were home. On the third night she said Hako, do the voice, and he did. The one with the trident, a creature of considerable volume. And she hit him, and laughed, and felt better.

She talked. Of course she did. She always talked. On the way north, in the nothing, she’d been too frightened to talk much. Going south in the dark with edges, she talked to everybody. To Kotori about the tea. To Hako about the voice. To Gigi, who’d never in two years talked to her about anything that wasn’t the weather, and who now talked to her for an hour on the fourth night about hawthorn. To Suzu, who said good to everything she said, and once, after a very long story about a fish, that was a good story.

To Thea.

She talked to Thea most. She always had.

She’d held Thea up for a long time. Out there. Before.

Nobody in the game knew all of it. Hyv knew some, because Hyv had asked her, at a cookshop on Spoke Street, what’s he like, and they’d ended up talking about everything. Ice knew some. Gigi knew the rest now, from the twenty-second stone, though Xola didn’t know what Thea had said there and wasn’t going to ask.

It had been a bad thing, between Thea and Gigi, out there, a long time ago. Xola wasn’t going to tell it, not even to herself, not on a hill. She’d only been the one who stayed. The one who said I’m here, as often as it needed saying. Until Thea could stand on her own.

She’d never once said anything to Gigi about it. It wasn’t hers to say.

And now Thea and Gigi walked south across the dark country with their elbows touching. Close. Not near. Thea’s head on Gigi’s shoulder when they stopped. Gigi’s hand finding Thea’s on the heather in the dark without looking. And Xola walked a few paces behind them with Slep on whichever side she wasn’t on. And looked at them, and couldn’t stop.

“You’re staring,” said Slep.

“I’m allowed to stare.”

“You’re crying.”

“I’m allowed to cry,” said Xola. She wiped her face with her sleeve. “I held her up out there. For ages. And then two years in here, nearly, of not close, near.” She looked at them. “Look at them.”

Slep looked at them.

“Good,” he said.

“That’s Suzu’s.”

“I borrowed it,” said Slep.

“You’ve borrowed a lot of things lately,” said Xola. “Good. Okay. Hako’s voice, once, on the third night, when he was asleep and you thought nobody heard.”

“I said one of my own,” said Slep. “On a stone.”

Xola went red, which she never did.

“In here,” she said.

“In here,” agreed Slep. And walked on beside her, on whichever side she wasn’t, with his bent glaive over his shoulder, not saying anything else. He didn’t need to.

On the ninth day, they saw the twenty-two.

Xola saw it first. Not Hyv, for once. She was at the front of the clump, talking, and she looked up in the middle of a sentence and stopped.

Far off, ahead, south, on the line of the horizon. A light.

Small. Gold. Steady. And then, when she looked, another. And another. Not far apart. A line of little lights going away south, close together, every fifty paces.

“The twenty-two,” said Hako. In his own voice.

They were lit. Of course they were. The Lamp Watch came up every month to fill them. Little lanterns in little hollows in the north faces of twenty-two stones, from the old hall to the edge. Xola had walked them herself, the first September, behind Tiger and his sack of lanterns, setting them one by one. The ten of them: Kei and Higu, Tiger, Rafi, Drew, Hana, Curse, her and Thea, and Gigi a pace off. And Thea at the end, setting the last.

She’d never seen them from this side.

From the north. From past the edge. The way Isa’s mothers had seen them, every one of them, for three hundred years, when they walked to the edge once and looked south. And saw nothing.

They weren’t nothing now.

They were twenty-two little lights in a line, standing straight up, very small, in the dark. Coming toward them as they walked. And at the very end, the nearest, a little apart from the others: one more. In a hollow in the north face of a tall grey stone with a five-line star cut into its south face, worn smooth.

Thea’s.

Thea stopped at the edge.

Not at the stone. Before it. On the heather, a few paces short, where the dark country ended and the road began. Where, nine weeks ago, the road had stopped and the world had just not gone on. Xola had seen it then, from the other side. Thea’s lantern lying flat in its hollow, pointing north into the nothing, like a compass needle.

It was standing straight up.

A small yellow flame in a little glass, in the hollow in the stone, standing straight. Not leaning. Not toward anything.

Thea stood on the heather and looked at it for a long time.

Gigi stood beside her. Close.

They didn’t say anything. Xola didn’t either, for once. She stood a few paces back with Slep and watched the two of them stand at the twenty-second stone, where they’d said I’m sorry, both of them, at the edge of the world, nine weeks ago. And where Gigi had said close, can it be close, and Thea had put out her hand.

Thea put out her hand now. To the lantern. Not to Gigi; Gigi was already holding the other one. To the little glass in the hollow. She touched it, very lightly, with two fingers.

“It’s warm,” said Thea.

“It’s been lit since September,” said Gigi. “The one before last.”

“I know,” said Thea. “I set it.” She took her hand away. “I didn’t know if it would still be here.”

“Everything’s still here,” said Gigi.

Thea turned round and looked at Xola. Over Gigi’s shoulder, a few paces back, on the heather.

Xola had known her face a long time. Out there, and in here, in the good bits and the bad thing. She’d seen it do everything. She’d never seen it do this.

“Xola,” said Thea.

“I’m not crying,” said Xola, crying.

“Come here,” said Thea.

She went.

The three of them, at the twenty-second stone, at the edge of the world, with Thea’s lantern standing straight up in its hollow. Thea in the middle. Gigi on her left. Xola on her right.

Xola had never once in her life stood on the other side of Thea from Gigi. Out there, she’d stood between them. That was what holding somebody up meant, sometimes. You stood between them and the thing that was hurting them.

She stood beside Thea now. On the right. And Gigi stood on the left. And nobody was between anybody.

Gigi looked at her across Thea. For a long time.

“Thank you,” said Gigi.

Xola stared at her.

“For holding her,” said Gigi. Very quietly. “When I wasn’t. When I was the thing she needed holding up from.” She didn’t look away. “I know. She told me some. At this stone. I worked out the rest.” She swallowed. “Thank you.”

Xola didn’t say anything. She couldn’t. For the first time in her life, she had absolutely nothing to say.

She put her arms round both of them. Thea in the middle. Gigi on the far side. Her arms round both, at the twenty-second stone, with the lantern standing straight beside them.

Behind them, on the heather, Hako said very quietly, in the voice, the scientific community will be needing a moment. And Slep said Hako, which he never said, because he never said anything first.

They walked the twenty-two at dusk. South. From the edge to the old hall, every fifty paces, a lantern standing straight in every stone. Thea counted them, out loud, the way Suzu had counted the stones. Twenty-two. Twenty-one. So they’d know who was coming home.

At the old hall, they stopped for the night.

The roofless hall on the ridge, in the heather. Walls, some shoulder-high. A doorway with no door. A long room open to the sky, with stone benches round the walls. And on the far wall, a ghost of a star, where three hundred years of sun hadn’t got.

Xola cooked.

She’d been carrying fish since the hill. Not fish; there weren’t fish in the still water, or if there were, nobody wanted to know. Dried fish, from the round town, from the cookshop on Spoke Street where she worked the fires. She’d packed it in November for the way home, in case. She’d been saving it.

She cooked it on Slep’s fire in the corner of the old hall, out of the wind. With Kotori’s pot and the last of the rice and something Gigi found growing in a crack in the old hall’s wall, which Gigi said was fine. And everybody ate it, sitting round the fire in the roofless hall. Nine people who’d been on a hill in the middle of the dark’s own water for four weeks. Eating fish at the old hall, nine days from home.

“This is the best thing I’ve ever eaten,” said Hako. In his own voice.

“It’s dried fish,” said Xola.

“It’s your dried fish,” said Hako. “On the way home.”

Xola looked at him for a long time. Then she went red, which she never did, and looked at the fire.

Thea and Gigi sat on the bench.

The same one. Xola knew it was the same one; Thea had told her. In September, the first time, and on the way north in November. The two of them on the two ends of the same stone bench in the old hall, looking at a star that wasn’t there, not talking. Until the third dawn.

They sat on it now. In the middle. Together. Thea’s head on Gigi’s shoulder.

Looking at the star that wasn’t there.

Xola watched them from the fire and didn’t say anything.

“Go and sit with them,” said Ice, beside her.

She hadn’t known he was there. He’d come over from the doorway with no door, where he’d been standing, facing north, the way he did. With Hyv beside him, of course. She was holding his arm. Both hands round it, the whole way back from the edge. Xola had watched her do it and said nothing, which had nearly killed her.

“It’s their bench,” said Xola.

“It’s a long bench,” said Ice.

She went and sat on the far end of it.

Not too close. She didn’t want to be between them. She’d never be between them again. She sat on the end, and Thea, without lifting her head off Gigi’s shoulder, put out her other hand along the bench. Not looking. And Xola took it.

The three of them on the bench in the old hall, looking at a star that wasn’t there.

“I was so angry at you,” said Xola. Quietly. To Gigi, past Thea. “For years.”

“I know,” said Gigi.

“I’m not now.”

“I know,” said Gigi. “You hugged me at the edge of the world.”

“I hugged her,” said Xola. “You were just there.”

Gigi laughed. Out loud, in the old hall. Xola had never heard her laugh like that. Not in two years in the game. Not out there, before, ever.

Thea, between them, with her head on Gigi’s shoulder and her hand in Xola’s, said nothing at all. She was asleep. Or pretending. Xola could tell from her face. She was pretending.

Later, at the doorway with no door, Xola stood with Ice.

Hyv had gone to sleep in the corner, out of the wind, with Kotori. She’d let go of his arm for the first time since the edge. Xola had watched her do it, very reluctantly, as if somebody were taking something off her.

“She’s been holding your arm for nine days,” said Xola.

“Yes.”

“Both hands.”

“Yes,” said Ice.

“Icey,” said Xola.

He looked at her.

She didn’t call him that much. She had, a long time ago, before. When they’d been best friends, the three of them, her and Thea and him. Before it got complicated, out there, and he’d stepped back from both of them, to look after his own head, and just turned up from further away. She’d told Hyv that, in the cookshop on Spoke Street, when Hyv asked what’s he like. And it had been fine. More than fine.

She called him Icey when it mattered.

“Operation tail,” said Xola.

He went pink.

She’d seen him go pink maybe four times. On a clip at a gate. On a stream at a netloft. She’d never seen it from this close, in person, in the game, at the doorway of a roofless hall in the dark.

“It worked,” said Xola. “Me and Thea. The group chat. it’s about hyv. you’re not a rock. Thea alone: it’s allowed.” She was grinning so hard it hurt. “We did that. In January. We messaged you every day for a week and you said okay and okay I heard and then you were leaving for Masmark and she said me at the gate.” She looked at him. “It worked.”

“You worked,” said Ice.

Xola stared at him.

“Not it,” he said. “You. Both of you.” He looked at the old hall, at the bench, where Thea was pretending to be asleep on Gigi’s shoulder. “You told me it was allowed. I didn’t know. Nobody ever told me.” He was quiet. “You told me.”

Xola didn’t say anything for a long time.

Then she hit him. On the arm. Not hard. The way Hyv did.

“Don’t make me cry at the old hall,” she said. “I’ve cried at the edge of the world already today.”

“Okay,” said Ice.

She hit him again. Then put her arms round him, very quickly, the way you’d hug a friend you’d had a long time and nearly lost and got back. And let go.

“She’s going to be so loud,” said Xola. “When we’re home. Hyv. I can see it coming. She’s been holding your arm for nine days with both hands and she’s never been loud about anything she loved in her life. And she’s going to start.” She grinned. “At the long table. In front of everybody. In front of Ama Corrie.”

“I know,” said Ice.

“Are you ready?”

He thought about it. She watched him.

“No,” he said.

“Good,” said Xola. “Suzu’s. I’m borrowing it too.”

Hyv kissed him in the morning. At the old hall. In front of everybody.

Xola saw it coming. Of course she did. They were all standing round the fire at a quarter to eight, the nine of them, the way they did, for the words. Suzu said set one to watch. And everybody said so the rest may sleep. And then, before the egg, in the few minutes after, Hyv turned round.

She put both hands on Ice’s face and kissed him. Not on the cheek. In the middle of the old hall, in front of eight people, at a quarter to eight in the morning.

And let go. And said, very loudly, to the old hall, red to the horns:

“Mine.”

Nobody said anything for about three seconds.

Then Xola screamed.

Not a word. Just a scream, the longest of her life. It went up out of the roofless old hall into the dark sky with edges and away over the heather. Hako fell off the bench. Thea and Gigi, on the bench, both put their hands over their faces at the same time. Kotori said oh and dropped the pot. Suzu said good.

And Ice stood in the middle of the old hall, pink to the ears. Looking at Hyv, who was looking back at him, edge to edge, red, defiant, enormously pleased with herself.

“You’re going to be like this now,” he said.

“I’ve been like this for ages,” said Hyv. “Inside. Now I’m going to be like it outside.” She took his arm again, both hands. “Get used to it.”

“OPERATION TAIL,” screamed Xola, into Slep’s shoulder.

“Xola,” said Slep.

“IT WORKED.”

“I know,” said Slep. “I was there.”

The egg took them at eight, at the old hall, in the middle of Xola still screaming. Four days from home.
