Live from Elsewhere · Volume 4: The Ninth Heron

Chapter 87

The Paperwork

1,874 words · 8 min read

On Thursday morning, Ice did ten pull-ups.

He did them in the little room on the gym floor at five past seven, with Suzu on the mat counting and Gabe on the bench pretending not to watch. On the ninth, his arms started shaking. On the tenth, they shook so hard he thought he’d drop. He didn’t. He got his chin over the bar, and hung there for a second with his whole body trembling, and then let go and dropped to the floor, and stood there breathing.

“Ten,” said Suzu.

“Ten,” said Gabe, from the bench. He’d stopped pretending. “Ten, Dec. You were four on the first day. Four. You nearly cried.”

“I didn’t nearly cry.”

“You nearly cried,” said Suzu, rolling up his mat. The corner of his mouth went up. “You had a four face.”

Ice stood in the middle of the gym floor with his arms hanging and laughed until he had to sit down on the bench next to Gabe. Gabe put an arm round him, heavy and sweaty, and didn’t say anything.

“I’m going to miss this,” said Gabe eventually.

“You’re not going anywhere. You live here.”

“In the game. I’m going to miss this, in the game.” Gabe waved a hand vaguely, at the gym and at the three of them and at everything. “Standing in front of things with you. I’m going to go and stand in front of Rachael for three weeks, and she’s never even played one of these, and she’s going to ask me what every button does.”

“You’ll love it.”

“I’ll love it,” agreed Gabe. “I’ll still miss this.”

Vivian had the loan agreement ready at one.

She’d had it ready since Monday, Ice suspected. She’d had the orchid on the windowsill when they came up the narrow stairs, which meant paper. On the teak desk, squared to the edge, was a single sheet in a clear plastic sleeve. Beside it, her father’s fountain pen. Beside that, a small plate of kuih from the auntie downstairs, which meant the auntie had been told what this was for.

“Mr Declan. Mr Suzu.” Vivian looked at them over her glasses. “Sit down, please.”

They sat. Gabe had stayed downstairs at the kopitiam, because, he said, it was a two-person conversation and he’d only make it a car conversation.

Vivian slid the sheet out of its sleeve and turned it round on the desk.

It was one page. Plain sentences. Ice had known it would be.

Personal loan. Lender: Mr Declan. Borrower: Mr Suzu. Principal: ten thousand US dollars. Interest: two per cent per annum, simple. Repayment: from the Borrower’s earnings in the game known as ELSEWHERE, in amounts and at times of the Borrower’s choosing. No security. No penalty for early repayment. No penalty for late repayment.

And under it, a line on its own:

Purpose: medical.

“I’ve kept it off the company’s books,” said Vivian. “It’s a personal matter between two individuals. I drew it up because Mr Declan asked me to, and because if I hadn’t, he would have written it on the back of a kopitiam receipt.” She looked at Suzu. “Read it, please. All of it.”

Suzu read it. He read it twice, slowly, the way he read his mum’s post. Ice watched him. When he got to the line about the interest, he stopped.

“Two per cent,” he said.

“Two per cent,” said Vivian. She looked at Ice over her glasses. “Mr Declan informs me that you argued it up.”

“I argued it up,” said Suzu.

“From one.”

“From none,” said Ice. “I said none first. He said then he wasn’t taking it.”

Vivian looked at Suzu for a long moment. Ice had seen her look at bank statements like that, and share registers, and a girl in glasses who’d said I wasn’t going to ask.

“That is acceptable,” said Vivian.

She held out the pen. Black.

Suzu signed. Suzu. Then his full name under it, in small careful letters. Ice signed below him, and Vivian witnessed it, and stamped it, and put it in a folder with a fresh card clipped to the front in her small neat capitals.

LOAN (MR SUZU) — USD 10,000 — 2% (HE ARGUED IT UP) — PURPOSE: HIS MOTHER.

She hadn’t written medical on the card. Ice noticed. She’d written his mother.

“The money went this morning,” said Ice. “To the clinic. For the assessment, and the deposit. The rest is in his account.”

“Monday the fifth,” said Suzu. “The assessment. Of April.” He was looking at the card. “If she’s eligible, they said, it could be the same week. The surgery. They’re moving fast with the first ones.”

Vivian was quiet for a moment.

“Then I hope she’s eligible,” she said. And she reached out and moved the plate of kuih a little way across the desk, toward Suzu. “From the auntie. She asked me to tell you she’s praying for your mother. She asked me which god. I said I didn’t know. She said she’d do all of them.”

Suzu looked at the kuih for a long time.

“Thank you,” he said.

He said it the way he’d said it in the kitchen, out loud. Ice thought he was getting better at it.

“There’s one more thing,” said Vivian, when Suzu had eaten two of the kuih, slowly, and put the third in a napkin for the bus.

She opened a drawer and took out two envelopes. Real paper, heavy, cream, with a small grey logo in the corner that Ice knew. The shape of an egg, with a line across it like a horizon.

“These came to the office,” she said. “Addressed to the company. One for each of you.” She put them on the desk. “I opened them, because that’s my job, and because they had the word agreement on the second page. I’ve read them.” She took her glasses off. “Both of them.”

Ice picked his up.

Dear Ice,

The ELSEWHERE team would like to invite you to join a small group of creators who have helped shape how people first see our world.

He read the rest of it. It was longer than Vivian’s letters. It said Creator Program several times. It said there were a few of them, and that the team had watched them since launch, and that they’d like to talk to them about what came next. There’d be a gathering, in April, in the game, somewhere the rest of the world couldn’t get into yet. A badge on his name. A small amount of money each month. Early looks at things before they came out, which he wasn’t allowed to talk about. And a page of agreement that Vivian had covered in small neat pencil marks.

Suzu was reading his own, very still.

“Who else?” said Ice.

“They don’t say,” said Vivian. “A small group of creators. I imagine they’d like it to be a surprise.” She tapped the agreement with one finger. “There was a clause about exclusivity. You would not have been permitted to stream any other game for the duration. I’ve struck it out.”

“You struck it out?”

“I wrote to them this morning,” said Vivian calmly. “I said that Mr Declan streams what he likes, and that if they wished to discuss it further they could write to me. They wrote back at eleven. They said that was fine.” She put her glasses back on. “There was also a clause about your likeness. That one I’ve kept, with a change. And one about the money. I’ve asked for more.”

Ice stared at her. Beside him, Suzu made a small sound. It might have been a laugh.

“You negotiated with the ELSEWHERE team,” said Ice.

“I’m your company secretary, Mr Declan,” said Vivian. “They wrote to the company. I answered on the company’s behalf.” She looked at him over her glasses. “Did you think I would let you sign something with in perpetuity in it?”

“I didn’t know it had in perpetuity in it.”

“That,” said Vivian, “is why you have me.”

They went down the narrow stairs at half past one. The auntie was waiting at the bottom by the kopitiam counter, the way she always was.

She didn’t say anything this time. She just looked at Suzu, and took his face in both her hands, briefly, the way you’d hold a bowl. Then she let go, and went back to the counter, and shouted something at the kitchen.

Gabe was at a table by the door with three empty glasses of teh tarik.

“Well?” he said. “Was it a car conversation?”

“It was a two per cent conversation,” said Ice.

“And a creator program,” said Suzu, holding up his envelope.

Gabe looked at the cream envelope with the little grey egg on it. Then at Ice’s. Then at the two of them.

“I didn’t get one,” said Gabe.

“You don’t stream.”

“I’m on stream. I’m on your stream, all the time. I’m the hammer man. The auntie calls me the hammer boy.” Gabe looked deeply wounded. “I put a carrot on your light, Dec.”

“I’ll tell them,” said Ice.

“Tell them,” said Gabe. “Tell them the hammer man’s available.” He picked up his teh tarik, found it was empty, and put it down again. “Who else is in it?”

“They don’t say.”

“I bet Drew,” said Gabe. “Drew’s a Partner. And Tiger, because of the canal. And Hyvred, because of the eyes. And Bee, because of the door.” He thought about it. “And the VANTA one. Kestrel. Because she’s good, and she’s got ten thousand people.” He grinned. “And you two. The swamp guy and the scythe guy.”

Ice didn’t say anything. Gabe was very good at guessing things. Nobody ever believed it, because he guessed them so loudly.

That evening, back in the game, Ice opened his messages on the plank at the narrows.

There was one from Tollbury.

[ Messages · VOng
VOng: Mr Declan. For your information.
VOng: The buying stopped at the end of February. Gold has fallen 2.4% since.
VOng: I believe your friends in red have finished shopping. Eighteen rooms. I have counted them on the forum. Seventeen, plus the one they did not get.
VOng: I have also read your creator agreement a third time. I have one further question for them. I will ask it tomorrow.
VOng: Congratulations, Mr Declan. I am told it is an honour.
]

He read it twice. Then he laughed, alone on the plank, with his feet over the black water and the moonbells starting to open across the tussocks.
