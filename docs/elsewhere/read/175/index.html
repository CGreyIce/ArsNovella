Live from Elsewhere · Volume 7: Keep the Lamps

Chapter 175

The Road of Lights

3,725 words · 16 min read

Maigo was the first to say it, because Maigo was always the first to say anything.

“It’s closer,” he said.

They were on the roof of the dome after the second dusk, at about ten, on the last Tuesday in October. Six of them on Higu’s ledge, which was meant for about two. Higu with her lamp. Kei with his map spread on his knees, held down at the corners with four cups. Ina, who’d come up to see what the fuss was about. Hako, who’d come up because Maigo had. And Gigi, at the far end, not quite on the ledge, sitting on the top step with her staff across her knees.

And Maigo, at the front, with his pixel sunglasses pushed up on his antlers so he could see properly in the dark.

North, over the moor, past the line of the road, past where the land went black, there was the light.

You could see it from the roof now. That was new. In September you’d had to walk four days to the old hall, and past its broken wall, and up twenty-two stones to the edge of everything, to see it at all. Now you could see it from the eye of the dome in the round town on a clear night, if you knew where to look. A single gold point, very small, very far, low on the edge of the world. Like a star that had come down too far and stayed.

“It’s closer,” said Maigo again. “It’s definitely closer. Kei, tell them it’s closer.”

“It’s closer,” said Kei.

He had a little brass thing in his hand, a sighting arm Hana had found him in a stall in Quarrymouth. He’d been taking the light’s bearing every clear night for six weeks and writing it on the map. The marks went in a line. Six weeks of little ticks going down the map from the top edge toward the round town. Not fast. Not straight. But going.

“It’s moving,” said Higu, very quietly.

Nobody said anything for a while. The wind came over the rim of the eye and went round them. Below, two hundred feet down, Ama Corrie’s lamp was moving slowly across the floor of the Hall.

“South,” said Gigi, from the top step. “It’s coming south.”

“Toward us,” said Hako.

“Toward everything,” said Kei. “There’s only one road.” He put his finger on the map, on the line that ran north out of the round town through the north door and over the moor. Past the rings, to the old hall and the twenty-two stones. “That one.”

Maigo looked at the gold point on the edge of the world for a long time. He was trying to think of something cheerful to say. It was his job. He’d always thought of it as his job, to be first, and to make the first thing be all right. He couldn’t think of anything.

“Okay,” he said at last. “Okay. So someone’s coming home.”

He hadn’t meant to find the lamps. He’d only gone out to graft a tree.

On the Thursday he walked north with Hako and Ina, because Kei had asked for three to go and look at the rings and see they were still lit. That was the job now. Kei had started a list on the board by the south door, in his small square hand. LAMP WATCH. Under it, names in threes, and days. Tiger had been doing it alone since March, going up the north road with his sack every week to keep the lantern rings. Then in September, after the twenty-two stones, Kei had said, not alone any more. And put up the list.

Nobody had argued. Everybody had signed.

So on Thursday it was Maigo, Hako and Ina, the old three. They’d been three of Kei’s six in the first week, in the Cradle Commons in December, in booths in the dark. Kei had booked them in the first minute.

The north road went out of the north door past Kite’s milestone, the way it always had, into heather and stone and sky. The first waystone was a mile out. A plain grey standing stone about waist high with a five-line star cut in it and a groove on top pointing north. Maigo had walked past it six times. He’d never looked at it.

He wouldn’t have looked at it this time, except for the tree.

There was a rowan growing out of the bank behind it. A little crooked one, wind-bent, the only tree for a mile in any direction. It was split. Something had come down on it, a branch or a beast or a storm. It had cracked right down the middle, half of it lying in the heather and half still standing, weeping sap. It was going to die. You could see it was going to die.

“Hang on,” said Maigo.

“What?”

“There’s a tree.”

Ina looked at him. Hako looked at the tree. Ina looked at the tree. Then Hako put his spear down in the heather and sat on it, the way he did when he knew it was going to be a while, and said in his documentary voice, “And here we observe the Maigo in his natural habitat.”

“Shut up,” said Maigo happily. He was already climbing the bank.

He did it the way Mother Quince had taught him in Orchard End, in the first week, on the apple terraces with the Old Pippin. Cut and bind. You put your hands on the break. You found where the two halves wanted to meet, which wasn’t always where they’d broken. You held them there, and you said the word.

“Hold,” said Maigo.

The bark knitted. Slowly, the way grafts did, a green line creeping along the split, sap going still. The lying half came up off the heather into his arms, heavy, and fitted itself to the standing half like a hand going into a glove.

He stood on the bank with his arms round a rowan, very pleased with himself, and looked down. And that was when he saw the back of the waystone.

The front of the waystone faced south. Toward the round town. That was the side with the star cut in it, the side everybody saw when they walked north and passed it. The back faced north, toward the moor and the edge of everything. Nobody ever saw the back. You didn’t turn round on the north road to look at the backs of stones.

From the top of the bank behind it, Maigo could see it.

There was a hollow in it.

A deep round hollow, cut into the north face, about the size of his two fists together. It was full of lichen and old moss and grit, packed so tight it looked like part of the stone. But it was round. Too round. Nothing in nature made a hollow that round.

“Ina,” said Maigo.

“What?”

“Come here.”

Ina came up the bank. He was careful about it; he was always careful about things. He looked where Maigo was pointing, at the back of the stone.

He didn’t say anything for a long time.

Then he crouched down and put his hand into the hollow, through the moss, and scraped. The moss came away in a soft green lump. Under it, the inside of the hollow was black.

Black as the inside of a chimney. Black all the way round, a thick old crust of it, gone hard as the stone. Ina rubbed it with his thumb and looked at his thumb. It was black too.

“Soot,” said Ina.

He said it in the voice he used when something mattered, the smooth British one that made you want to listen. Down the bank, Hako stood up off his spear.

“Like the twenty-two,” said Maigo.

“Like the twenty-two,” said Ina. He looked at the hollow. Then he stood up and looked north, up the road, at the next waystone. You could just see it, a mile on, a little grey stub on the skyline. “It’s a lamp,” he said. “This is a lamp. They’re all lamps.”

They walked the whole road.

They hadn’t meant to. They’d meant to check three rings and come home. But they couldn’t stop. They went up the north road from waystone to waystone, a mile at a time, and at every one Maigo climbed round the back. He was the smallest and the bark on his hands didn’t mind the moss. And at every one, there was a hollow, and under the moss, soot.

Every one.

At the first lantern ring, where Tiger’s lamps were burning in their twelve cages on their twelve iron posts round the flat star-stone, they stopped for the night. It was a kept place. Nothing came inside. Maigo made dinner on a little fire in the middle, because he always made dinner. A stew of moor rabbit and barley and wild garlic, with no pork in it, because there was never any pork in anything he made. Hako had once asked if it was a rule, and Maigo had said yes, and Hako had said okay, and that had been that. Ina sat with his back to one of the posts and wrote in a little book.

“Twenty-three,” said Ina, when they’d eaten.

“Twenty-three what?”

“Waystones. Between the north door and here.” He looked at his book. “That’s a day’s walk. Twenty-three miles, twenty-three stones, twenty-three lamps. And then a ring.” He flicked back through the pages. “And the twenty-two past the old hall. That’s not the same, those are every fifty paces. But this—“ He tapped the book. “Four days to the old hall. Four rings. A lamp every mile.” He did the sum in his head, his lips moving. “Ninety-something. Ninety-four, ninety-five, if it holds.”

“Ninety-five lamps,” said Maigo.

“On a road,” said Ina. “Going north. All the way from the round town to the edge of the world.” He shut the book. “It’s not a road with lamps on it. It’s a road of lamps. Somebody lit every mile of it. Once.”

Hako was lying on his back in the heather inside the ring, looking at the sky.

“The road of lights,” he said, in his documentary voice, but softly. “Once common across the northern moor. Now believed extinct.”

“It’s not extinct,” said Maigo. “It’s just out.”

“Just out,” agreed Hako. “Conservation efforts are ongoing.”

They came back on the Sunday and told Kei, at the long table, in front of everybody.

Kei listened. He listened the way he always did, without saying anything, with his map in front of him. When Ina had finished, he unrolled the map all the way and put a cup on each corner. He took his pen and began to mark the stones. One every mile, up the north road, in very small dots. Twenty-three to the first ring. Twenty-three to the second. All the way up. Ninety-four dots, it turned out, when he’d done them all. And then the twenty-two past the old hall, every fifty paces, to the edge.

It was a line of dots, going north. From the round town to the gold point on the edge of the map. Like a string of beads.

The long table looked at it for a long time.

“They lit all of it,” said Hana, very quietly. She was by the window with Milli, in their chairs. “Didn’t they. The old guild. When they went north. Every mile.”

“Behind them,” said Higu.

“Set them out behind us,” said Gigi, from chair twenty-one. “So the rest may sleep.”

“Why every mile, though?” said Tiger. He’d been keeping the rings alone for seven months, and you could tell it hurt him a bit that he’d walked past ninety-four lamps and never noticed. “The rings are where you sleep. Nothing comes inside the rings. What are the stones for, in between? You don’t sleep at a stone.”

Nobody knew.

Maigo looked at the line of dots on the map. He thought about the back of the first waystone, and the hollow in it, facing north. Not south, where the travellers were. North. Toward the moor and the edge and the dark. Toward the gold point coming home.

“They face the wrong way,” he said.

Everybody looked at him.

“The hollows,” said Maigo. “The lamps. They’re on the backs of the stones. The north side. If you were walking north with a lamp behind you, you’d put it on the front. So you could look back and see it. So the people behind you could see it.” He frowned. He wasn’t good at this part, the thinking part; he was good at the first part, the saying. But he kept going. “They put them on the back. Where you can only see them if you’re coming from the north.”

The long table was quiet.

“So whatever’s in the north can see them,” said Kei slowly.

“Yes.”

“And nobody walking north can.”

“Yes.”

Kei looked at the map for a long time. Then he looked down the table. Not at Maigo. Past him, to the first of the nine. Ice was sitting there with his feet on the rung and a cup in his hands, and he hadn’t said anything all evening.

“Ice,” said Kei. “The Unlit. On the Long Night. What did they do?”

Ice put his cup down.

“They come up out of still water,” he said. “They go to lights. They lean toward the nearest one before they move. That’s how you know where they’re going.” He thought about it. “They don’t go for people. Not first. They go for lights. If you’re standing between them and a light, they go through you. If you’re not, they go past you.”

“So if you put a lamp somewhere,” said Kei, “they go to it.”

“Yes.”

“And not somewhere else.”

“Yes,” said Ice. And then, after a moment, as if he’d just heard it himself: “Oh.”

Maigo didn’t understand it fully until that night, on the roof.

He went up the two hundred and twelve on his own that night, in the dark before the third dawn. He sat in Higu’s place on the ledge, because Higu was asleep for once. He looked north at the gold point on the edge of the world, and thought about it, slowly, the way you’d think about a graft.

Ninety-four lamps on the backs of stones, facing north. A line of lights that only the dark could see.

Emberly fed its forge till it roared on the longest night. Ina had told him once, in December, about the first Long Night. So the Unlit on the plain walk past the farms to the hill. That was what Emberly was for. The forge was a lure. You lit the biggest light you could, somewhere you could keep it, and the dark went there. Instead of to the farms. Instead of to the houses with children asleep in them.

Ninety-four lamps. All on one road. All facing north.

All pointing at the round town.

He sat on the ledge with his arms round his knees and looked down through the eye at the floor of the Hall. At Ama Corrie’s lamp moving across it. At the seven roads going out of the round market in seven directions. North, south, east, west and the three between. To Brackenford and Quarrymouth and Merrowgate and Millrace and Briarwick and Elmcross and the fen. All the towns anybody he loved had ever woken up in.

Seven roads. One town in the middle where they all met.

Everything comes here, Ama Corrie had said once. That’s what a crossroads is for.

“Oh,” said Maigo, out loud, to nobody.

He told Ama Corrie first. He didn’t know why. He went down the two hundred and twelve and found her at her counter, at one in the morning, with her pen, writing in the Roll the way she always was. He stood in front of the counter with his sunglasses in his hand.

“The lamps on the north road,” he said. “They’re to bring it here. Aren’t they. The dark. On the longest night. They’re to bring it here. So it doesn’t go anywhere else.”

Ama Corrie put down her pen.

She looked at him over her spectacles for a long time. Not unkindly. The way she looked at a chair that needed sweeping under.

“My grandmother’s grandmother,” she said, “told her daughter, who told my grandmother, who told my mother, who told me. When the old ones went north, they left the round town a job.” She folded her hands on the counter. “Keep the Roll. Sweep the banner room on Sundays. Read the ceiling by the moon.” She paused. “That’s what’s written. In the book. That’s what the girl with the lamp read out on the roof in June.”

“I know,” said Maigo.

“That’s what’s written,” said Ama Corrie. “There was another thing. That they didn’t write. That got told.” She looked past him, at the north door, at the chair by it, set apart, facing north. “And if it ever comes south all at once, it comes here. So keep the lamps.”

Maigo stood very still.

“Nobody knew what it meant,” said Ama Corrie. “Not my mother, not hers. Keep the lamps. Which lamps? There’s no lamps on the north road. There’s stones. Nobody’s lit a lamp on the north road since before the hall fell down.” She picked up her pen again. “And now a boy with leaves in his hair comes in at one in the morning and tells me there’s ninety-four of them.”

“Ninety-four,” said Maigo.

“Hm,” said Ama Corrie. She wrote something in the margin of the Roll, very small, in her careful hand. Then she turned the book round on the counter so he could read it.

Ninety-four lamps on the north road, found by three of the sixty, on the backs of the stones, facing north. “They’re to bring it here.” Keep the lamps.

“There,” said Ama Corrie. “Now it’s written.”

Maigo looked at it for a long time.

He thought about being first. He was always first. He’d been first in the booth at the Cradle Commons at 6:51 in the morning on the day it opened. He’d been the first to see it from the roof on Tuesday, it’s closer. He was always the first to say the thing, so the thing would be all right.

He couldn’t think how to make this one all right.

So he did the other thing. The thing you did when you couldn’t make it all right.

“Okay,” said Maigo. He put his sunglasses back on, which was ridiculous, at one in the morning, indoors. “Okay. Then we keep the lamps.”

Ama Corrie looked at him over her spectacles.

“Hm,” she said. It sounded, very nearly, like approval.

On Monday morning the list on the board by the south door had a new heading. Kei had written it in his small square hand, above the old one.

THE ROAD OF LIGHTS. 94 + 22. Every mile, north.

Under it, a list of what they’d need. Ninety-four cages, iron, to fit the hollows. Ask the smiths. Oil, a great deal of it. Ask Bee, ask the chandler, ask everyone. Wicks. Walkers, in threes, every day, all the way up and all the way back, to light them and keep them lit. Nobody alone.

And at the bottom, underlined twice:

By the longest night.

People were already signing. Maigo stood at the back of the little crowd round the board and watched them. Tiger, first, of course. Then Drew. Then Thea, in her big round hand, with Xola under her and Chi at the bottom, small. Then Rafi, who couldn’t spell lamp and didn’t care. Hana and Milli. Leaka, who drew a heart. Riverbank, all eight, in a block. Moonlight Harvest, all nine. Ice’s name was small and plain in the middle of them, with nothing after it.

Somebody had drawn a little tree next to Maigo’s name. He didn’t know who. Probably Bee.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he went to get breakfast for everyone, because somebody had to, and it was usually him.
