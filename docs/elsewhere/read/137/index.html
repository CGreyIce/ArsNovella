Live from Elsewhere · Volume 5: Ask Me Again

Chapter 137

One Day

3,185 words · 14 min read

The first time Rafi ever saw the swamp guy, the swamp guy was covered in mud.

All of it. Head to foot, green-black, fen mud, the kind that smelled of old reeds and older water. He came out of the reeds at the end of the north walk in Fernwick, on a morning in the first week of the game, with a sack over his shoulder. He walked up Sedge’s steps and put the sack down on the top one, and Rafi saw it was full of silverleaf. More silverleaf than Rafi had seen in two days of looking.

Rafi had been sitting on the bottom step, because he’d had nowhere else to sit. The first week. Level two. Nineteen years old, in a game he’d saved a year for, in a fen he hadn’t picked, with a stick for a weapon and no idea what to do next.

The swamp guy looked down at him.

“You know fenwort?” he said.

“I know what it looks like,” said Rafi.

“Two copper a plant,” said the swamp guy. “Bring it here. To these steps. Sedge’ll tell you if it’s wrong.”

And that was it. That was the whole of it. A man covered in mud had looked at a boy on a step with nothing, and given him a job. Two copper a plant. Rafi had picked fenwort for him for two weeks and called him boss from the first day, half as a joke. Then not as a joke.

Bring me back a big sword, boss, he’d said, in December, on the morning they went to the church.

And the swamp guy had.

Now Rafi sat on the top step of the Hall of Roads in Carrow Cross, on the south side, where the south road came in, with the big sword across his knees. He watched three thousand people wait.

He’d been on the gate since the second week of June. Since Higu sat under the eye and told the whole world what it had been keeping for three hundred years, and the whole world came to the round town to ask to be let in. He’d come up from the market on the first day of it and said I’ll do the gate too, if that’s allowed. Wendy had looked at him for a long time and said it’s going to go badly, which he’d learned meant yes.

So he sat on the gate. Wendy on the step above him with her bow and her cup of Gigi’s tea. Kite on the stone lion with no nose, opposite, with his bow strung. And Rafi on the step below, with the Chorister’s Blade, which still hummed a little under his hand if he held it still. Like a choir a long way off.

And people came up the steps, and said can I join, and Wendy said who’d stand next to you. And when they said who’d sent them, Rafi said nobody sent me either, and grinned. Some of them understood. Most didn’t. He kept saying it anyway.

It worked, mostly. People went away and came back with somebody. Not many. Enough. The book on Ama Corrie’s counter had thirty-two names in it, and every one of those names had walked up these steps and had somebody stand next to them.

He had a letter in his coat. He’d had it since May. It had come by carrier from Fernwick, in handwriting he knew from a hundred notes about fenwort on Sedge’s steps. Small and plain and slightly slanted.

Rafi. Read it out to Sedge. She says the steps are kept. She says good. She says “didn’t say” is the cleverest thing you’ve ever done, and that you won’t believe it from me, so it’s from her. — the swamp guy.

He’d read it about a hundred times. He took it out sometimes on the gate in the evenings when it was quiet, and read it again, and put it back.

He didn’t know when the swamp guy was coming. Nobody did. Kei had walked off up the north road three weeks ago with Drew and his map and said I’m going to ask, and hadn’t said anything since. Rafi had stopped looking up every time somebody came up the south road with a shield on their back. Mostly.

At midday on Tuesday, Hyvred shouted from the dome.

She was on the stair to the eye. She’d been up there every day since she came back into the game after her eyes, the doctor thing that everybody knew about now and nobody said. She sat on the steps inside the dome where you could see all seven roads at once. She said she liked to watch them going away, even though she could see them out here now too. In the game she’d always been able to see everything. She said it was different, now that she could see the edges of her own room as well. Rafi didn’t understand that, but he liked hearing her say it.

She shouted down through the eye, through the whole Hall, and out of the south door, and down the steps. Everybody in the round market heard it.

“THEY’RE COMING.”

Rafi stood up.

So did Wendy. So did Kite, on his lion. So did three thousand people in the round market, who all turned round at once to look at the dome, and then at each other, and then at the seven roads.

“NORTH-WEST ROAD,” shouted Hyvred. “TWO MILES. NINETEEN OF THEM.” A pause. “HE’S IN FRONT. OF COURSE HE’S IN FRONT.”

Wendy, beside him, said, “Oh.”

Rafi didn’t say anything. He found he was holding the Chorister’s Blade very hard. It was humming under his hand, louder than he’d ever felt it hum.

They came round to the south side.

That was the thing Rafi would remember, afterwards. The north-west road came into the round town on the far side of the market. They could have come straight in through the north-west door of the Hall, and up to the counter, and nobody would have said anything. There were nineteen of them, and they were the swamp guy and the swamp guy’s people and Riverbank. They’d made world firsts. Half the people in the market had watched them for six months.

They didn’t. They walked all the way round the outside of the round market, past the stalls and the fountain, to the south road. Then they came up it, the way everybody did. The way three thousand people had come up it every night for a month. To the gate.

Kei was in front, with his map. He stopped at the bottom of the south steps and looked up at Wendy.

“Everybody comes through the gate,” said Kei. “Every time.”

Wendy looked down at him. Then past him. Rafi looked too.

Nineteen people at the bottom of the steps. Riverbank in front, the eight of them, with Xav at the head of them with a pencil behind his ear. Moonlight Harvest behind them, the nine. A big man with a hammer. A tall man with a book. A small girl in pale blue with a bell. A boy with long silver hair and a scythe and a jar. A quiet man with a long sword. Three Rafi didn’t know, a short woman with a staff, a man with a spear who kept looking up at the dome, a man with a notebook. Drew, at the very back, with his hands in his pockets.

And at the front of the nine, with a shield on his back, in charcoal and dark leather, with a teal cord on his laces, the swamp guy.

He wasn’t covered in mud. Rafi had never quite got used to that.

Wendy did it properly. She did everything at the gate properly. She’d said, in June, that the gate was the only thing in the guild she was sure she was good at, so she was going to do it exactly right, every time, or not at all.

She looked at Xav first.

“Who’d stand next to you?” said Wendy.

Xav looked up at her from the bottom step. He didn’t hesitate. He’d been first through the door since March.

“Him,” said Xav, and nodded sideways at Kei. “And them.” He nodded back at his eight. “And they’d stand next to me. That’s what Riverbank is.”

Wendy looked at Kite, on his lion.

“Yes,” said Kite.

“Yes,” said Wendy.

Riverbank came up the steps. All eight of them. Jun Liang was trying very hard to be quiet and failing, and Val said something to him in her low voice, and he stopped trying and just grinned. They went past Wendy and past Rafi and through the south door into the Hall, into the shade under the dome. Rafi heard Ama Corrie’s voice inside, dry, saying mind the north door, and Jun Liang saying WE CAME IN THE SOUTH ONE, and Ama Corrie saying mind it anyway.

Then the nine.

Wendy looked down at the swamp guy at the bottom of the steps. He looked up at her. They’d known each other through Salty and Suzu for years, Rafi knew. They’d sat in the same calls. Wendy didn’t do him any favours for it.

“Who’d stand next to you?” said Wendy.

The swamp guy didn’t answer straight away.

He looked up the steps. Past Wendy, past Kite on his lion. And down. To the step below Wendy, where a boy from Surabaya was standing with a black sword in his hand that had been far too big for him in December, and wasn’t now.

He looked at Rafi for a long moment.

“Him,” said the swamp guy.

Rafi didn’t move.

He couldn’t. He stood on the step with the Chorister’s Blade humming in his hand like a choir. Something in his chest had gone very tight and very hot, and he couldn’t have moved if the Hall had fallen down.

Then he remembered what he’d been saving to say. He’d been saving it since March. Since a set of steps in a fen at the second dusk, and a man sitting down beside him and saying go and join them first. I’ll see you there one day.

He’d said it to Wendy, on the steps, the night he arrived. He’d said it to himself on the gate a hundred times since. He’d never said it to the person it was about.

“Boss said he’d see me here one day,” said Rafi.

His voice didn’t work very well. He said it anyway.

The swamp guy looked up at him from the bottom of the steps. His face did something Rafi had seen it do perhaps twice in seven months. Once on Sedge’s steps in March, when Rafi said you’re not coming. And once, from a long way off, on a stream, in a blue hall at the top of the world, when a boy with a jar said go on.

“Here,” said the swamp guy.

He came up the steps.

Not fast. The way he did everything, without hurrying. Up past the people on the lower steps, who moved aside without being asked. Up to the step Rafi was standing on. He stopped there, beside him. Not in front of him. Beside him, shoulder to shoulder, facing out, facing the round market and the three thousand people and the seven roads. The way Bram had stood next to Rafi on these steps in March, when Rafi had nobody else.

Then he reached into his coat.

“From the steps,” said the swamp guy, and held out a reed.

A single long fen reed, cut at the root, folded over twice, tied in the middle with string. Rafi knew it before he’d taken it. He’d watched Sedge cut a thousand of them on the raft gardens, singing. He’d cut them himself, after Bram fell, on the longest night, when she’d sung and the whole town had sung back.

He took it. It smelled of the fen. Of home, he realised. Of the first home he’d had in the game, a set of steps over black water with an old woman on them.

“She says the steps are kept,” said the swamp guy, quietly, so only Rafi heard. “She says good. She says she’s said it twice and she’s not saying it three times because she’s old.” He paused. “And she says one day’s here.”

Rafi stood on the step of the Hall of Roads holding a fen reed. He cried. Right there, at the gate, in front of three thousand people and Wendy and Kite and the nine and the whole round market. He didn’t care. He grinned at the same time, the old grin, the one from the first morning, so wide it hurt. He cried and grinned and held the reed. The swamp guy stood next to him and didn’t say anything else.

Wendy looked at Kite.

Kite was looking at the two of them on the step. Kite, who’d taken fourteen people’s days, and walked a long way, and sat at a milestone for a week not coming closer. And who’d been let in because three people had said you held the left side of a wall.

“Yes,” said Kite.

“Yes,” said Wendy. Her voice had gone a bit. She drank some of Gigi’s tea to hide it, which didn’t work.

Somebody else came down the steps then. Out of the south door, past Wendy, fast.

Bram.

Tall, silent, Dutch, with his spear. He’d fallen at four in the morning on the longest night, holding Sedge’s steps with Rafi and Joy and the eel man. He’d had to watch the end of it from outside, locked out. He’d said one word in all the months Rafi had known him. Ja. On these steps, in March.

He came down past Wendy and stood on the swamp guy’s other side.

Then Joy came out, at a run, and stood next to Bram. And the three of them were there. The three who’d held a set of steps all night when nobody asked them to, standing on a different set of steps on either side of the man who’d paid them twenty silver a day to do it, and then come back.

“BOSS,” said Joy, who’d always said everything loudly. “BOSS, YOU CAME.”

“I said I would.”

“You said one day.”

“It’s one day,” said the swamp guy.

Bram looked at him. He looked at him for a long time, the way Bram looked at everything, silently, all the way through.

“Ja,” said Bram.

Joy screamed. Rafi laughed so hard he nearly dropped the reed. And the swamp guy, standing between the three of them on the steps of the Hall of Roads, laughed too. Out loud, in front of everybody, the way Rafi had never once heard him laugh on Sedge’s steps in all the months he’d sat on them.

The rest of the nine came up the steps after him. Wendy asked every one of them, properly, because that was the rule. Every one of them answered. Him, said Suzu, about Ice. Him, said Deston, the same. Her, said Sheng Yang, about Kotori; he’d promised Gabe. Dest, said Kotori; he counts for me. The big man with the hammer said anybody who needs it, which Wendy said wasn’t an answer, and let him in anyway. The short woman with the staff said Gabe, flatly, as if it were obvious, and the big man went red to the ears. The man with the spear said everybody, ask me about everybody. The man with the notebook said I’m the purse, darling, the purse stands next to everyone. Wendy put her hand over her face, and said yes, and let them all in.

And then, last of all, at the top of the steps, just inside the south door, in the shade, a girl with gold horns and a long thin tail and a pair of glasses pushed up in her hair.

She’d come down from the stair. Rafi hadn’t seen her come. She was standing in the doorway with her arms folded, looking at the swamp guy. Behind her, a girl with wings, who was drawing.

The swamp guy stopped in front of her.

“I saw you coming,” said Hyvred.

“I know,” said the swamp guy.

“Don’t say it like that.”

“Like what?”

“Like you knew,” said Hyvred. “From the north-west road. Two miles out. In front. I saw you two miles out.” She unfolded her arms, and then folded them again, as if she didn’t know what to do with them. “I’m still hitting you.”

“Okay,” said the swamp guy.

She didn’t hit him. Rafi watched her not hit him. She looked at him for a long moment, and then she went pink, all of her, to the roots of her horns. She turned round and went back into the Hall very fast. The girl with wings looked up from her drawing, at the swamp guy, flatly, and said, “Hi.”

“Hi, Bee.”

“She’s been on that stair since seven this morning,” said Bee. “Don’t tell her I told you.”

Then she went back to drawing.

Rafi stood on the gate in the afternoon sun with a fen reed in one hand and a black sword in the other. He watched the last of the nine go into the Hall, into the shade under the dome, where an old woman with spectacles on a chain was waiting at her counter with a black book open and a pen.

He thought about a man covered in mud on the second morning of the game, putting a sack of silverleaf down on a step and saying two copper a plant.

He hadn’t known, then, what that was. He’d thought it was a job.

He put the reed inside his coat, next to the letter, and sat back down on the step below Wendy. In the round market, three thousand people were still looking at the south door. Slowly, one at a time, they started coming up the steps again. Can I join?

“Who’d stand next to you?” said Wendy.

Rafi grinned.

“Nobody sent me either,” he said, to the next one, and meant it more than he ever had.
