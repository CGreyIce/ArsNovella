Live from Elsewhere · Volume 6: There's a Chair

Chapter 153

To an Extent

3,669 words · 16 min read

On Sunday Ama Corrie swept under the chairs. Thea asked if she could help.

She hadn’t planned to. She’d come down from the room over the Hart at the first dawn because she couldn’t sleep. Her face still had the ghost of the Burrow Mother’s claw down one side, though it had healed in the night, and her shoulders still remembered the weight of the Turn. She’d come into the banner room meaning just to sit in her chair by the window and look west for a bit.

Ama Corrie was already there, with the broom.

It was the fen-reed one. Sedge’s. Bound onto a dark old handle that Thea suspected had been a different broom’s handle for a very long time before. Ama Corrie was going down the long table one chair at a time, very slowly, sweeping under each one with short, careful strokes. She bent stiffly, the way old women in the game bent, as if their knees were real. She was humming something under her breath with no tune to it.

She was at chair forty when Thea came in. She didn’t look up.

“Can I help?” said Thea.

Ama Corrie stopped. She looked at Thea over her spectacles for a long time.

“Nobody’s ever asked that,” she said.

“Nobody?”

“Forty years,” said Ama Corrie. “They say thank you. Some of them. The boy from the fen said thank you for keeping it, which I’ll grant was nicely put. But nobody’s ever asked to do it.” She looked at the broom in her hands. Then she held it out. “Under, not round. Everybody sweeps round a chair. Round’s no good. The dust’s under. And you go slow, or you just move it about.”

Thea took the broom.

It was lighter than it looked. The fen reed was soft and fine and caught the light from the eye in a way that made it look like silver-gold. She went to the end of the table, to the six chairs by the window, and started with her own.

Under, not round. Slow.

There was a drift of grit under her chair from Saturday, from the south-west road. Earth and hawthorn and a bit of something pale that might have been a piece of burrow-brood claw. She swept it out into the light, slowly, the way she’d been told. Then Xola’s chair, which had river sand under it, of course. Then Slep’s, which had a single tuft of sheep’s wool. Then PenguChi’s, which had nothing at all, because PenguChi didn’t bring anything in with her that she didn’t mean to. Then Tif’s and Xyro’s, which had so much of everything under them that it took three passes.

Then she kept going.

She didn’t decide to. She just finished the six and went on to the next one, Dennis’s, and swept under that, and it had pencil shavings. Then Rachael’s, then Calvin’s. Mud, a lot of it; Calvin ran everywhere. She went on up the table, chair by chair, slow, under not round. Ama Corrie sat down on the window seat and watched her do it.

She got to chair twenty-one, in the middle of the first twenty, and stopped.

She knew whose it was before she looked under it. She knew from where it was. Halfway down on the north side, near the door. Near where the thorn had been laid across the counter on the night of the founding.

There was hawthorn under it. Not dust. A few small, perfect white petals, May blossom, that must have come down on somebody’s coat from the roots in the south-west road. They lay on the floor under the chair like snow.

Thea looked at them for a while.

Then she swept them out, very gently, with the fen reed, into the light.

Gigi came in at the second dawn with two cups of tea.

Thea was at chair thirty-something by then. She’d lost count. She heard the door and looked up, and Gigi was standing in it the way she’d stood in it a fortnight ago, Higu said. A cup in each hand, looking at a woman with a broom halfway down the long table.

Neither of them said anything.

Then Gigi came in, slowly, and put one of the cups down on the table near Thea. Not next to her. Near.

“It’s hot,” said Gigi. “This one.”

Thea leaned the broom against a chair and picked it up. It was hot. It was also, she discovered, very good. Not like the cold one on the roof.

“This is good,” she said.

“I made it properly,” said Gigi. “The other one was up there an hour.”

“I know.”

They stood there, either side of the long table, holding tea. Ama Corrie, on the window seat, picked up a bit of sewing from somewhere and became extremely interested in it.

“I swept under yours,” said Thea.

Gigi looked at chair twenty-one.

“There was hawthorn,” said Thea. “Petals. From the road.”

“From the roots,” said Gigi. “They were coming down all day. I was covered.”

“I know. I saw. You were behind me.”

Something happened in Gigi’s face.

“I wasn’t watching you,” said Gigi. “The second time.”

“I know,” said Thea. “I could tell. I didn’t have to look behind me once.”

And they stood there, holding tea, with the whole long table between them. Thea thought it might have been the most honest thing either of them had ever said to the other.

They went and sat at the window, in the end. Not next to each other. Thea in her own chair, the one nearest the glass. Gigi on the window seat, at the far end of it, by the wall, because Ama Corrie had gone out and left the window seat empty. Possibly on purpose. Thea was beginning to suspect that most things Ama Corrie did were on purpose.

Out of the window you could see the west road. The road they’d come in on, curving away across the plain to the last hill, where six people and a sheep had stood on Sunday night and held hands.

“I want to say something,” said Thea.

“Okay.”

“I don’t know if it’ll come out right.”

“It doesn’t have to,” said Gigi. “It can come out however.”

Thea looked at the road.

“We’re not going to be what we were,” she said.

“No,” said Gigi.

“I don’t want to be. I don’t think you do either.”

“I don’t.” Gigi’s voice was very quiet. “That wasn’t good. For you. It wasn’t even good for me, and I was the one—“ She stopped. “It wasn’t good.”

“No,” said Thea. “And we’re not going to be friends. Not like that either. Not like me and Xola. Not like you and Higu.” She held the cup in both hands. “I don’t think I’ve got that. For you. I don’t think I’m going to have it. Maybe not ever.” She made herself look at Gigi. “I don’t know if I’ll ever say I forgive you. I don’t know if that’s a thing I’ve got in me. I’ve looked. I don’t think it’s there. Not yet. Maybe it never will be.”

Gigi was very still on the window seat.

“You don’t owe me that,” she said.

“I know I don’t.”

“I mean it. You don’t owe me anything. Not that. Not tea. Not sitting here.” Gigi’s hands were tight round her cup. “Whatever you give me, it’s yours to give. That’s all. I’m not going to ask for any more of it than you hand over. I’m never going to ask you for anything again, Thea. Not one thing.”

“I know,” said Thea. “That’s why I can do this much.”

Gigi looked at her.

“This much?” she said.

“This.” Thea moved her hand a little, at the room, the window, the table, the tea. “Being in the same room. Being in the same ten. Sitting here. Drinking your tea.” She looked back at the road. “We’re not friends. We’re not what we were. But we’re not—I’m not frightened of you. Any more. That’s new. I found out on Saturday, in that hole, when you said my fault. I’d been frightened of you for so long I didn’t know I was, until I wasn’t.”

Gigi put her hand over her mouth.

“So I don’t know what we are,” said Thea. “But I think we could be something that’s fine.”

“Fine,” said Gigi, through her hand.

“Fine.”

“Fine’s a lot,” said Gigi. “Coming from you. To me. Fine’s—“ She didn’t seem to know what fine was.

“Fine’s enough,” said Thea. “For now. I think fine might be enough for a long time.”

Gigi took her hand away from her mouth. Her face was wet. She didn’t do anything about it.

“Guildmates,” she said.

Thea thought about it.

“That’s a word,” she said.

“It’s the word Kei uses. For everybody. Guildmates. It doesn’t mean anything. It means you’re in the same book.” Gigi wiped her face with the back of her wrist. “We could be that. We’re already that.”

“We’re already that,” agreed Thea.

They sat with it.

“Can I call your thorn?” said Thea, after a while. “In a fight. If I need a hole shut. Can I just shout thorn, left, and you’ll lay it?”

“Yes,” said Gigi. “Can I call your Turn?”

“Yes.”

“Then that’s two things.”

“That’s two things,” said Thea. She thought. “No hugging.”

Gigi laughed. It came out of her short and surprised, like Thea’s laugh had on the bridge.

“No hugging,” she agreed. “Tea?”

“Tea’s allowed,” said Thea. “Hot, though.”

“Hot,” said Gigi. “I can do hot.”

And that was it. That was all they said about it, then or for a long time after. But Thea would think about it often, in the weeks that came, sitting in her chair by the window. Two people on a window seat and a chair, a long table’s width apart, making a list of three rules and a kind of tea. It wasn’t what anybody would call making up. It wasn’t forgiveness, or friendship, or any of the big warm finished words. It was smaller than those. It was the size of a chair.

But it held. Like a second row.

Xola came in at noon and found them.

She came through the banner room door at speed, the way Xola came through doors, saying Thea, have you seen my— and stopped.

Thea saw her see it. Thea in her chair, with a cup. Gigi on the window seat with another, at the far end, by the wall. The two of them not talking, just sitting there, looking out at the west road.

Xola’s gills flared.

Gigi looked up. She didn’t stand, or move, or look away. She just looked at Xola, the way she’d looked at her on the steps on Monday.

“Xola,” said Gigi.

“I’m watching,” said Xola.

It came out flat and hard, like something thrown. She stood in the doorway with her trident in her hand and her gills wide open and said it straight at Gigi. I’m watching.

Gigi nodded slowly.

“Good,” she said. “Somebody should.”

Xola opened her mouth. Then she shut it. Then she opened it again, and what came out was a sound Thea had heard her make at Xyro, and at Tif, and once at a pike in a mill pond. It was a sort of strangled noise of pure annoyance.

“Ugh,” said Xola.

She came in and sat down in her own chair, next to Thea’s, so close their chairs touched. She folded her arms. She glared out of the window at the west road as if it had done something to her. Thea, very carefully, didn’t laugh.

They sat there. The three of them. A long time.

“You said my fault,” said Xola eventually. To the window. Not looking at Gigi. “In there. Yesterday. Loud.”

“I say it now,” said Gigi.

Xola didn’t say anything for a while.

“I heard,” she said.

That was all. Two words. Xola didn’t say anything else, and didn’t look round, and kept her arms folded. But Thea had known Xola longer than anyone. She knew what I heard cost her. It wasn’t thank you, or okay, or I believe you. It was just: I heard it. It went in.

From Xola, to Gigi, after the year they’d both had on opposite ends of the same phone, it was enormous.

Gigi seemed to know that too. She didn’t say anything back. She just nodded, very slightly, at the window. Then after a while she got up and went out, quietly, leaving her cup on the sill.

When she’d gone, Xola let her breath out all at once and put her face in Thea’s shoulder.

“I hate it,” said Xola, muffled.

“I know.”

“I hate that she said it. I hate that I heard it. I hate that it helped.”

“I know,” said Thea, and put her arm round her, and looked at the west road.

They ate at the long table that evening, all of them. It was the first time all fifty-five had been in the banner room at once.

Ama Corrie counted. She stood in the doorway with her broom and counted them under her breath, chair by chair, the way she counted everything. When she got to fifty-five she said hm, and went away, and came back with more bread.

Nobody made a speech. Kei stood up at the head of the table as if he might, and looked down it at fifty-five people eating. Jun Liang was explaining something to Tiger at enormous volume. Kotori and Tif were sharing a pot. Dennis was writing something in his notebook, which might have been bread and might have been owed. Kei opened his mouth. Then he closed it again and just said, “Fifty-five.” And sat down.

“Fifty-five,” said the table, all at once, raggedly, and went back to eating.

Gigi was in chair twenty-one, halfway down the north side. Thea was in her chair at the far end by the window. There was most of a long table between them, and a lot of elbows, and they didn’t look at each other. They didn’t need to.

At one point Thea wanted the salt. It was halfway down, near Gigi’s elbow. Thea said could somebody to nobody in particular, and the salt started coming. It came down the table through eleven pairs of hands, Maigo to Hako to Salty to Wendy and on. And the first pair of hands it went through, Thea saw, was Gigi’s. Gigi picked it up without looking round and passed it to Maigo, and went back to her soup.

It arrived at Thea’s end via Dennis, who said, “The purse provides, darling.”

“Thank you,” said Thea, down the table. To all of them. To the first pair of hands too.

Halfway down the north side, Gigi didn’t look up. But Thea saw her spoon stop for a second, and then go on.

Ice came into the banner room at the second dusk to tell Kei he was going.

Thea heard him from her chair. She wasn’t listening on purpose; it was just that the banner room was one long room, and he was at the counter end of it, by the door, and he had the kind of voice that carried without trying.

“West,” he was saying to Kei. “The Fell Country. A week, maybe ten days. There’s a lot of thirty-eight out there and nobody’s levelling it.” He had his pack on already. “MH for the first few days. Then I want to mix it. Sheng Yang. Drew, if he’ll come. Kotori.” A pause. “And Chi, if she wants.”

Thea sat up.

“Chi?” she said, before she could stop herself.

Ice looked down the length of the table at her. Everyone else in the banner room looked too.

“If she wants,” said Ice. “She asked.”

Thea turned round in her chair. PenguChi was sitting two chairs down, sewing, with Patience asleep on her feet. She didn’t look up.

“I want to see him be slow,” said PenguChi, to her sewing. “Up close. For a week. I watched him for two hours on the road and I want to know how he does it.” She bit off a thread. “I’ll come back. I’m patient.”

Thea looked at her for a long moment. Then she looked back down the table at Ice.

“Steal her,” said Thea, “and I’ll come and get her.”

“I know,” said Ice. “That’s why I’m only borrowing.”

PenguChi laughed. Out loud. Thea had heard her do that perhaps three times in ten weeks.

Ice looked at Thea a moment longer. He didn’t come down the table. He didn’t ask her anything. He’d been in the round town nearly a month, through all of it, the gate and the roof and the road. He’d kept himself at the far end of the room the whole time, out of it, on purpose. He just looked.

“You’ll be all right?” he said.

Thea thought about the window seat, and the tea, and three rules.

“I’m in a chair,” she said.

Ice nodded, once, as if that were a complete answer. Which it was.

“Go on, then,” said Thea.

And he smiled. The rare one. Because she’d said it to him on a bridge in Brackenford a month ago, and he’d gone, and now here they were.

She streamed that night, from the chair by the window, which she’d never done before. She just sat there with the camera floating in front of her and the west road in the dark behind her, and talked.

Her chat had seen the south-west road. Of course it had. Tiger had streamed it. Tiger streamed everything. And the clip had gone round by Saturday night. The bottom of a bowl full of holes, and a woman with a shield, scratched and filthy, looking up. And a woman with a staff at the top, shaking. And two short lines of dialogue that somebody had put on the clip in big white letters.

GOOD THORN.

GOOD TURN.

Thea looked at it for a long time. Three thousand eight hundred people. A lot of them had been in Gigi’s chat once. A lot of them remembered.

She thought about eleven hours of VODs, and a woman with a mortar, and a chat full of her name. Not mine to say. Next question.

“Not mine to say,” said Thea.

The chat went still for a second.

“Well,” said Thea. “Half mine.” She looked down at her cup, which was hot, and good. “We’re guildmates. That’s all. That’s all I’m going to say about it. Ever, probably.” She looked up at the camera. “Next question.”

Thea read it in her chair by the window, in the dark, with the moon coming up over the dome and the silver lines coming out on the ceiling outside the banner room door.

She read the last line three times.

Then she put the phone away, and looked out at the west road, and at the last hill, where a small light had burned on top of a dome all night a week ago while she watched it. It was burning now. One small light, at the very top. Two somebodies.

She didn’t go up. It wasn’t hers.

She sat in her chair, and drank the last of the hot tea, and slept there, in the end, sitting up. Ama Corrie came in at midnight with the fen-reed broom and found her. She didn’t wake her. She swept under the chair anyway, very slowly, under not round, with Thea asleep in it.
