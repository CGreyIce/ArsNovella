Live from Elsewhere · Volume 8: Somewhere Else

Chapter 203

The Netloft

3,372 words · 15 min read

Bee was the only one who didn’t look at the harbour, because Bee was the one who drew things, and somebody had to.

She had her sketchbook out before she’d thought about it. She always did. On the road down the crater wall, on the long rise, at the ferry, at the goose. She’d drawn all fourteen days in a book that was nearly full. Now she stood at the back of the ten at the bottom of the north-west road. The banner was strapped across her shoulders and her wings folded under it, and the book was open on her arm, and she drew.

She drew the fish man’s cart. The brazier, the canvas roof, the old man with his boot face and his boot hat, turning sticks.

She drew the nine, with their backs to her, all looking very hard at the harbour.

And she drew the two who weren’t.

Hyv had stopped walking.

Bee had seen it coming for a mile. She’d known Hyv for longer than anybody in the game, except maybe Hyv’s own reflection. She’d bought the egg so Hyv could come in, a long time ago, and said it was for content, which it partly was. She’d sat beside her in the dark on a hundred nights. She’d moved her wing a little, so there was room behind it if anybody needed it, for five months. So she knew what Hyv’s walk looked like when it was about to stop.

It stopped at the bottom of the road. Ten paces from the fish cart. Ten paces from him.

He was standing by the cart with a fish on a stick and his shield on his back. He’d said you’re slow to Kei, and under about the room, and Kei had stepped back and the other eight had turned round. And now it was just him, by the cart, looking at Hyv. And Hyv, ten paces off, looking at him.

Neither of them moved.

Bee drew that. The ten paces. It was the hardest thing she’d ever drawn, because there was nothing in it. Just a gap. Just two people and a gap and the first light coming down the north-west road.

You have to draw what’s there, Ambry the sign-painter had told her, on the first day in Heatherbrook, with a book of making. Draw it true. Draw it whole. Say what it’s for.

She drew the gap, true, and whole. She didn’t know what it was for yet.

He moved first.

Of course he did. He always did. Bee had watched it on a hundred streams and in a dozen halls and on an oak floor at Elmcross: he went first. Before you’d decided. He moved, and you answered.

He put the fish down on the edge of the cart.

That was all. He put the fish down, very carefully, on the wooden edge by the brazier, as if it were the most important thing he’d ever put down. And the fish man looked at it, and at him, and at Hyv, and very slowly moved the fish further onto the cart so it wouldn’t fall off.

Then he walked the ten paces.

Not fast. At a walk, the way he walked at everything. He didn’t stop a pace off, the way he stopped in front of people. He kept walking until there wasn’t any gap. And stopped.

“Hi, Hyv,” he said.

Hyv looked up at him.

Bee saw her face from the back of the ten. She’d seen it do a lot of things. Go red, go white. Go stiff when somebody said you like him. Go so bright on a gate at the first dawn that the whole round market had gone off like a firework round her. She’d never seen it do this.

It didn’t do anything. It just looked at him. Very still. Edge to edge, all of it at once, the way Hyv looked at everything, and all of it him.

“You’re fifty,” he said.

“I said February,” said Hyv.

“You said February.”

“I said it on the seventeenth.”

“I know,” he said. “I was looking at my letters.”

Hyv made a sound. Not a word. Bee had heard her make it once before, in a lodging house a long time ago, the night she’d seen the banner whole for the first time. Edge to edge, every dot.

He held out his hand.

Not to shake. Palm up. The way he’d held the arrow on his palm at the first milestone, a mile out of the round town, with the whole of Constellation watching from the steps. The way you’d hold out something to see if somebody would take it.

Hyv looked at it.

Then she hit him.

Not hard. In the shoulder. With the side of her fist. The way she’d hit him every time he came back from anywhere for five months, without ever deciding to.

“That’s for the goose,” said Hyv.

“I walked round the goose.”

“I walked round the goose,” said Hyv. “All ten of us walked round the goose. It hissed at every one of us. Kei put it on the map.” Her voice was going somewhere. “You could have warned us properly. You just said there’s a goose.”

“There was a goose,” he said.

“There was a goose,” said Hyv.

And she took his hand.

Bee drew it.

She drew it standing at the back of the ten, with the banner on her shoulders and her wings folded under it and her pencil going so fast it nearly went through the page. She drew two people at the bottom of a road by a fish cart, holding hands. One with a shield on his back and a teal streak in his hair, not looking at anything but her. One with gold horns and her tail. Her tail wasn’t round her ankle and wasn’t straight up and wasn’t bottle-brushed. It was just hanging there, loose, at the end, very slightly curled. Bee had never once in five months seen it do that.

The fish man was in it. Leaning on his cart, with the fish moved safely onto the wood, looking at them with a face like a very old boot that had just been given something.

And the nine were in it, with their backs turned, looking at the harbour. Every one. Kei with his map on his arm. Higu with her lamp. Gigi and Thea a pace apart. Xola with her gills flared so wide they were nearly flat against her head, shaking, because she was trying so hard not to turn round. Drew watching everybody’s feet, including theirs. Salty with Right Size. Wendy with her hands out of her pockets for once, pressed together in front of her.

Bee drew it all. True. Whole.

She still didn’t know what it was for. She thought maybe it didn’t need to be for anything.

“Bee,” said Xola, out of the side of her mouth, not turning round. “Bee. Are they— is it—“

“They’re holding hands,” said Bee.

Xola made a noise like a kettle.

“Don’t turn round,” said Thea, beside her, also not turning round, also shaking. “Xola. Don’t you dare turn round.”

“I’m not turning round. I’m dying.”

“Die facing the harbour,” said Thea.

The netloft was at the end of a lane on the very edge of the city, and it smelled of fish, and he’d swept it.

He walked them there. All of them, through the oldest quarter of Masmark, up and down narrow steep terraces where nobody had painted anything in a long time. Still holding Hyv’s hand, the whole way. He didn’t seem to notice he was doing it. Hyv definitely noticed. Bee, walking behind them, could see the back of her neck, and it was red all the way up to her horns.

It was a little stone building on the water, with a roof that sagged in the middle like an old horse and a door that stuck. Ice put his shoulder to the door. It gave. They went in.

It was one room. Bare stone. A window on the lake, the glass old and wavy, with the first light coming through it green and gold. One old bench along one wall. The smell of salt and old rope and nets mended a long time ago by people who’d gone. And at the far end, on the wall opposite the door, two iron hooks. Old, rusted, set into the stone at exactly the height you’d hang a banner.

The floor was clean. Somebody had swept it. Under the bench. In the corners. Under the hooks.

Gigi walked into the middle of the room and looked at the floor for a long time. Then she looked at Ice.

“Under,” she said.

“Under,” said Ice.

Gigi nodded, once. Like Ama Corrie. Then she went and sat down on the end of the old bench and put her staff across her knees. She looked at the hooks.

Everybody looked at the hooks.

Then they looked at Bee.

She’d carried it fourteen days. The second banner. Strapped across her shoulders over her wings, the end sticking up behind her head. Up the long rise and across the ferry and over the rim and down the switchbacks in the dark. The Open Hand had passed it on the road and said nobody.

She unstrapped it. Her shoulders felt strange without it.

She unrolled it. Sixty gold dots on deep blue, and the silver lines between them, the shape of who’d stood next to who at the gate. One white dot in the corner, for Chernn, who wasn’t on the Roll yet. On the bottom edge at the left, in blue ink so small you’d only see it if you looked: Constellation (ours).

And along the top edge, very faint, very old, gold, a star that nobody had painted.

She carried it to the far end of the netloft. To the two iron hooks. She stood in front of them and looked at them for a moment.

In the round town, the hooks had been under Ama Corrie’s stair, on an iron hatch with seven spoke-lines round a ring. When she’d hung the first banner on them, the lines had glowed and the hatch had sunk into a stair. That had been the first banner. The real one. The Seven Below’s.

This was a copy, in a Hall that smelled of fish, at the edge of the biggest city in the world, where nobody had heard of them.

It answers when it’s yours, Isa had said. It comes for who’s carrying it.

Bee hung it on the hooks.

Nothing happened.

She stood there. The banner hung on the old iron, sixty dots and the silver lines and the faint gold star. The first light came through the wavy green glass of the window. Behind her she could feel nine people and Ice holding their breath. And nothing happened. No lines, no glow, no stair. Just a banner on a wall in a room that smelled of fish.

Her stomach went cold.

It’s a copy, she thought. It’s a copy, and I knew it was a copy, and Curse’s ink was just ink, and the star was—

The star moved.

Not much. Along the top edge of the banner, where it had sat faint and gold for fourteen days. It moved. It slid, very slowly, along the edge of the cloth, from the left toward the middle. Like a light being carried along a road. Bee watched it with her pencil in her hand and her mouth open. It went all the way along the top edge to the exact middle, above the sixty dots, and stopped.

And brightened.

Not much. A little. The way a lamp brightens when somebody turns up the wick. Gold. Old. Steady.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then Wendy laughed. Out loud, in the netloft, a short surprised laugh. Then Salty, who never laughed, laughed. And then Xola, who’d been not turning round for an hour, finally gave up and turned round and burst into tears. And Thea, beside her. And Higu, quietly, with her hand over her mouth. And Kei, who’d stepped back to the door, unrolled his map on his arm. With shaking hands, at the very bottom of the new sheet, on the white past the edge of the crater, he drew one small square. And wrote beside it, very small: ours.

Bee stood in front of the banner with her pencil. She didn’t draw it. She didn’t need to. She’d drawn it already. Fourteen days ago, on the long table in the banner room, every dot.

She turned round.

Hyv was standing in the middle of the netloft with Ice. Still holding his hand. Looking at the banner, and the star in the middle of it, with her eyes that saw everything edge to edge.

Then she looked at Bee.

Bee had known her a long time. She knew what Hyv’s face did. She’d drawn all of it. Red and white and stiff and bright. Crying in a lodging house at the banner, whole, for the first time. Behind her wing, for an hour, saying mrp.

This one she’d never drawn.

It was just glad. That was all. Plainly, all the way through. Standing in a fish-smelling room at the edge of the world, holding a man’s hand, looking at a banner her best friend had painted twice.

“Bee,” said Hyv.

“I know,” said Bee. “Shut up. I did it.”

Hyv laughed. It came out of her wet and too big for the room. She let go of Ice’s hand, which was the first time she’d let go of it since the fish cart. She crossed the netloft in four steps and put her arms round Bee, banner hooks and wings and pencil and all.

Bee stood there and let her.

She moved one wing. Not a little, this time. Not so there’d be room behind it if anybody needed it. All the way round. So Hyv was inside it.

Over Hyv’s shoulder, she saw Ice. Standing in the middle of the netloft on his own, with his hand still out a little where Hyv had let go of it. He was looking at the two of them. His face was doing the thing it had apparently been doing since the gate. Bee had seen it on Tif’s clip. A warmth, up the sides of his neck.

He saw her looking.

He nodded. Once. The way he nodded when something was right.

Bee nodded back.

Wendy took the door. Of course she did.

Nobody asked her. She went and stood in it, the netloft’s one door at the end of the lane, the one that stuck. She took her hands out of her pockets and looked out at the lane and the harbour and the biggest city in the world. Four hundred guilds and dozens of roads and the Open Hand, who’d called them nobody on a switchback in the dark.

“I’m the gate,” said Wendy, to the lane.

There was nobody in the lane. It was the oldest quarter of Masmark, at dawn. Nobody came down here.

“Doesn’t matter,” said Wendy. “Somebody will.”

Somebody did. About an hour later, when the ten were sitting on the floor of the netloft eating the fish man’s fish, which Ice had gone back for, all of them. A man in deep blue came down the lane. Not much gold on him. He walked very quietly, the way people walk who don’t like to be noticed. He stopped at the door of the netloft, in front of Wendy.

He looked at her.

“I’ve come to see the GM,” he said.

“I’m the gate,” said Wendy.

The man in blue looked at her for a long moment. Bee, on the floor inside with a fish, saw Ice look up. Saw something happen to his face.

“Everybody gets asked,” said Wendy. “Every time. Doesn’t matter who.” She folded her arms. “Who’d stand next to you?”

The man in deep blue didn’t answer for a long time. Bee thought he wasn’t going to. He had the look of somebody who didn’t say things.

“The GM,” he said at last. Very quietly. Dry. “And Harlan.” He paused. “Don’t tell Harlan.”

Wendy looked at him.

“Fair,” she said, and stood aside.

And Vane came into the netloft, and sat down on the old bench beside Ice, and the fish man’s son came down the lane twenty minutes later with eleven more fish on sticks, because his father had sent them.

Later, much later, at the third dawn, when most of the ten were asleep on the stone floor in their cloaks, Bee sat on the end of the bench with her sketchbook. She drew.

She drew the netloft. The sagging roof and the wavy window and the hooks. The banner on the hooks with the star in the middle, gold, brighter than it had been on the road.

She drew the nine, asleep on the floor in a row, the way they’d slept against walls for fourteen nights. Salty snoring. Wendy with her head on his shoulder. Kei with his map under his cheek.

She drew Vane, on the other end of the bench, asleep sitting up, with his hands folded, like a man who’d forgotten that people lay down.

And she drew the window seat under the wavy glass, where two people were sitting with their backs to the wall, looking out at the lake in the first grey light. Not four paces apart. Not two. Shoulder to shoulder.

He was holding her hand again. Neither of them was saying anything.

At the edge of the page, where the drawing ran off, very small, she drew the tip of a tail. Lying across the toe of his boot, white, loose, very slightly curled.

She looked at it for a long time.

Then, on the bottom edge of the page, at the left, very small, in the place where Curse put his labels, she wrote a word. She wasn’t Curse. Her words didn’t remember anything. It didn’t matter.

Ours.
