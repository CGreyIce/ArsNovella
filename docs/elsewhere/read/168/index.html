The fifth one fought like Xav, and Ice knew it before she’d drawn.

He’d watched her clips on Tuesday, in the back room, with the fan going round and Lim Brothers’ tea going cold on the windowsill. Kaede. Forty-two. Osaka, a company called Shiranui that nobody outside Japan had heard of until about March, when they’d started turning up near the top of things. Two blades, short and slightly curved. On the board her class was Twinfall, and its line said one who is already there.

He’d watched four minutes of her and sat back in the chair.

“What?” Suzu had said, from his pod.

“She’s doing Xav,” said Ice.

Suzu had come over and watched over his shoulder. A clip from a dungeon somewhere in the east, Kaede going in under something with too many legs. Gone, then there, then gone again. Close. Always close. Somewhere before the thing knew she’d moved.

“She’s good,” said Suzu.

“She’s very good,” said Ice. “She’s doing what Hollis taught him. On the first day. Be somewhere before the other man knows you’ve moved.” He watched it again. “She found it herself. Nobody taught her. You can tell. She does it from the hip, not the shoulder.”

“Is that worse?”

“It’s different,” said Ice. “It’s hers.”

On Wednesday night he stepped over the dark stone into the ring and she was already there, the way he’d known she would be. Small, quick, in dark blue, with her blades not drawn yet. She bowed to him from the far side. Not deeply. Exactly as much as you’d bow to somebody you meant to beat.

He bowed back the same amount.

The round market was full again. He didn’t look at it, mostly. He’d learned in the first four nights that if he looked at the crowd, the crowd looked back. Then there’d be a noise, and he’d have to wait for it to stop. So he looked at the ring. Thirty paces. Nine dark stones. The scorch from Monday by the third stone, which wouldn’t come up however much he swept. The crack between two flagstones where Ferris’s sword had stood.

And just outside the ring, between the fifth and sixth stones, cross-legged on the grey, Xav.

He’d been there every night. Same place. Sketchbook on his knee. He didn’t look up when Ice came in. He was drawing something. He’d been drawing the whole week, Ice thought, and hadn’t shown anybody.

The sixth one was a healer.

He’d thought about that one more than any of the others. Tif had put her at the top of the Hand tab without comment. Ice had seen what she’d written at the gate, under who’d stand next to you. Everyone I’ve ever healed. He’d read it in the back room on Thursday and put the phone face down on the desk for a while.

Her name was Liway. Forty. Manila, a guild called Kettle & Crown, which had been on Xav’s list in January when it was nineteen people. It was two hundred now. She was short and round-faced and she carried a little bronze bell on a stick instead of a staff. When she rang it, people got better.

“You know you can’t kill me,” she said, on Thursday night, at the edge of the ring. She said it very cheerfully, before Kei had said anything. “I mean. You can. But you’d have to be very quick. I heal everything.” She rang the bell, once, experimentally, and a small gold light went out across the paving and faded. “I want to see if you can do it. That’s all. Nobody’s ever tried.”

“I don’t do much damage,” said Ice.

“I know,” said Liway. “I’ve watched. You’re a tank. That’s why I came.” She beamed at him. “I think I can outlast you.”

He looked at her for a while.

He thought she might be right. That was the interesting thing. On paper she was right. A tank with no healer, against a healer with no damage. He hit for not very much, and she healed for a great deal. He could do it all night and so could she. Whoever got tired first.

He thought about Deston, on the bass line at the foot of the drowned church steps. Low and steady and a little flat. One and one and one.

“Okay,” said Ice.
