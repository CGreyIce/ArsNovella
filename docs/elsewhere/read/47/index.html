Chapter 47

Founder

3,122 words · 14 min read

The six of them walked up to Brackenford together, which was the only time they ever would.

Ice knew it while they were doing it. He knew it the whole way up the bank from the old ford, through the alders and up onto the causeway in the gold afternoon. Moonlight Harvest, six names, the party maximum, for about two hours. He’d look at it later in the party log and it would be there, one line: Leveti joined · Sketch left. Two hours apart. He wanted to remember what it had been like.

It had been like this.

Xav in front, the notebook under his arm, walking fast, the way he walked when he’d decided something and didn’t want to give himself time to undecide it. Deston beside him, humming under his breath, a few bars of the reed-cutting song from Monday night that he couldn’t get out of his head. Suzu at the back, hands in his pockets, half-shut eyes on the road. Kotori bobbing between them all with her staff and her bell, saying sorry to the grass when she stepped on it.

And Sheng Yang, tall and easy, beside Ice, with the long sword on his back.

“You’re Kotori,” he said, as they came up onto the causeway.

“Yes! Sorry. Hi. Yes.” Kotori looked up at him. She had to look quite a long way. “You’re very tall.”

“I am.”

“I didn’t mean—sorry. You’re very good. At the stone. You didn’t move at all.” She went pink. “Nobody else didn’t move.”

“Neither did you,” said Sheng Yang. “On your rock. I watched you all afternoon from the trees. You healed sixty people coming out of a river before they’d finished coughing, and you never once stood anywhere a mudwolf could reach.” He said it in the same clear calm voice he said everything. “That’s harder than the stone.”

Kotori didn’t say anything at all. She went very pink and looked at her feet and walked into Deston.

“Sorry,” she said to Deston.

At the back, Suzu said, without opening his eyes, “You still owe me.”

“For what?” said Sheng Yang.

“The winter.”

Sheng Yang thought about it. Then he laughed, a short low laugh, the first one Ice had heard from him in the game.

“I’ll pay you in silver,” he said.

“Hm,” said Suzu, which meant that would do.

Penhallow was behind the long counter in the Adventurers’ Hall with a pen behind each ear, the way he’d been on Thursday, and on Sunday.

He looked up when they came in. Six of them, which made the queue at the counter turn round. He looked at Xav, and then at the five behind him, and then back at Xav.

“Wednesday,” he said.

“Wednesday,” said Xav.

Penhallow reached up without looking to the top-left pigeonhole behind the counter. He took out a folded sheet of heavy cream paper with a slip tucked into it, unfolded it, and laid it flat on the counter. The red wax seal at the bottom was still soft. It had been waiting for six days.

[ Charter of Company
An adventurer company may hold up to twelve travellers under one name, one banner and one purse.
The Hall will keep its name, its roll and its debts.
Fee: 150 gold.
The founder may not belong to any named party.
Name: ______ ]

Xav read it. Ice watched him read it, all of it, every line, the way he’d read it the first time on Thursday and every time since. His eyes went down to the fourth line and stopped there for a moment.

Then he took a small heavy purse off his belt and put it on the counter.

Penhallow counted it. He did it quickly, without looking up, the coins clicking under his fingers in stacks of ten. A hundred and fifty gold. Everything Xav had brought up out of the drowned church, all of it, gone to this.

“Name?” said Penhallow.

Xav looked at the blank line at the bottom of the charter.

“Later,” he said. “When they’re all here. It’s theirs too.”

“Blank names cost nothing,” said Penhallow. “Changing them costs ten gold.”

“Then I’ll only do it once.”

Penhallow looked at him over the counter for a moment, with the look NPCs here gave you when you’d said something they’d remember. Then he dipped a pen in the inkwell and turned the charter round.

“Founder signs,” he said. “And thumbs the wax.”

Xav signed. Small, neat capitals, the way he wrote everything. SKETCH. Then he held his thumb over the soft red seal at the bottom, and didn’t press it. Not straight away.

He turned his head and looked at Ice.

Ice didn’t say anything. He’d said everything on Saturday, by the heron-stones, in the moonlight. He just nodded, once.

Xav pressed his thumb into the wax.

“That’s yours, then,” he said. “Mind it.”

They stood on the steps of the Hall afterwards, in the evening, with the market island below them and the three rivers going gold.

The others had gone ahead. Deston had hugged Xav for a long time without saying anything and then gone off toward the inn by the east bridge, humming. Kotori had said “sorry” and then “congratulations” and then “sorry” again, and cried a bit, and hugged him, and gone. Sheng Yang had shaken his hand, and said, “Nine years and you’re leaving him to me,” and Xav had said, “Don’t let him go out alone at night,” and Sheng Yang had said, “I’ve met him,” and gone. Suzu had stood in front of Xav for a moment with his hands in his pockets. Then he’d said, “Good,” the way he said it when he meant it, and walked off down the steps after the others without looking back.

So it was just the two of them on the steps.

“Don’t,” said Xav.

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You were going to say something.”

“I wasn’t.”

“You’ve got a face on.”

“It’s my face,” said Ice.

Xav looked out at the town. At the Tally on the market island, the black needle catching the last of the sun. At the dozen bridges, and the mills, and the three rivers coming together.

“I’m going to stay here,” he said. “In Brackenford. It’s central. Everyone’s twelve days away at most when they hit ten. I’ll get a room at the inn by the east bridge and I’ll level and I’ll mine. I’ll plan. And on the first of January eight idiots are going to come out of eight random towns all over the world, and walk here, and I’ll be on the bank.” He paused. “It’s a morning’s walk from Fernwick.”

“I know.”

“So you’ll see me around.”

“I’ll see you around.”

“All the time. You’ll be sick of it.”

“I know,” said Ice.

Xav was quiet for a moment.

“Dec,” he said. “The plank. Monday night. The end.” He didn’t look round. “I said I couldn’t draw that bit. I couldn’t. I sat in the water behind that thing for three hours trying to draw it and I couldn’t. I didn’t know what you’d do.” He stopped. “I’ve never once in nine years not known what you’d do.”

“I didn’t know either.”

“I know you didn’t.” Xav laughed, a little. “That’s the thing. That’s the thing I’m going to miss. Not knowing. You standing on a plank and me not knowing.” He turned and looked at Ice then, properly, for the first time since they’d come out of the Hall. “Don’t stop doing that. Even without me. Promise me.”

“I promise.”

“Good.”

And then Xav hugged him, after all. Very quickly, very hard, on the steps of the Adventurers’ Hall in front of everybody. Then he let go, and turned, and went down the steps into Brackenford with the charter in his hand. He didn’t look back. He never did, when he’d decided something.

Ice stood on the steps until he couldn’t see him any more.

They took Sheng Yang to the ring that night.

That was the rule, though nobody had ever said it out loud. You could be in Moonlight Harvest without knowing where the moonbells grew. But you couldn’t really be in it. Not properly. So at the fen’s moonrise Ice went dark on stream, and Tif’s counter ticked to thirty-four. The five of them went out along the north walk, past the narrows with its cracked plank and its split post, round the deep water, across the tussocks where the dark grass had roots. And they stepped in between two of the nine grey stones, into the warm clear water, with their boots in their hands.

Sheng Yang stood in the middle of the ring and looked round, slowly, at the long grey necks bent toward him. At the shut green fists in the water, beginning, one by one, to open in the moonlight. At the sound they made: a high clean ringing, like a wet finger round the rim of a glass.

“So this is where you go dark,” he said.

“This is where we go dark.”

“Hm.” He crouched and looked at a moonbell, close, without touching it. “Nobody knows?”

“Nobody knows.”

“Good.” He stood up. “I won’t either.”

They sat on the stones with their feet in the water, the way the three of them had sat on the second night. Deston on his flat stone at the north side. Kotori on the edge, humming very quietly with the bells. Suzu with his back against the ninth heron, the one that faced the wrong way, with his eyes shut.

“I saw something,” said Ice. “On Monday. At four in the morning.”

They looked at him.

He told them. The lull, and the hundreds of Unlit standing in the old fen in the dark, heads down. And the round gap in the middle of them, out here, where they weren’t. How they’d gone round it when the wave came. Not over it, not through it. Round, the way a river goes round a rock.

“Nothing comes inside the stones,” said Deston slowly. “Sedge said.”

“She said it on the second day,” said Ice. “I thought she meant the lurker.”

Nobody said anything for a while. The moonbells rang. Out in the old fen, very far off, a heron called.

“So if it ever goes wrong,” said Kotori, in a small voice, “really wrong. There’s somewhere.”

“There’s somewhere,” said Ice.

Suzu opened his eyes and looked up at the ninth heron above him, the one that faced north. Then he closed them again and didn’t say anything at all.

Sheng Yang sat on his stone with his long legs in the warm water and the sword across his knees.

“Can I ask you something?” said Ice.

“You can ask.”

“Why didn’t you tell anyone? Really. Not it slipped my mind.”

Sheng Yang thought about it. He did it properly, the way he did everything. He looked out at the old fen in the moonlight for a while before he answered.

“I wanted to see how far I’d get,” he said. “On my own. No group chat. No one telling me where to go. I’ve always had people telling me where to go, in games. Usually you.” He said it without any edge at all. “I wanted to see what I’d do if nobody did.”

“And how far did you get?”

“Nineteen.”

“I’m twenty.”

Sheng Yang smiled, slow and easy and completely confident, in the moonlight.

“For now,” he said.

On her stone, Kotori made a small delighted noise and then, for once, didn’t say sorry. On his, Deston laughed out loud. Against the ninth heron, without opening his eyes, Suzu said, “Good.”

He found the clip at eleven that night, lying in bed at the flat with the fan going round and his phone over his face.

He hadn’t been looking for it. He’d been reading the forum, the way he did now, a little, before he slept. The Long Night thread had eleven thousand replies. The trials thread had four thousand. And at the top of the page, pinned by the moderators, there was a video. Forty-four seconds long. HE GAVE IT BACK, it was called.

He knew who’d made it before he looked.

It was the end of the Long Night, from his own eyes. The shield filling the frame, Osric Fell’s boss in the middle of it. The dark leaning on it out of the sky. The gold coming across the fen. Now? — Not yet. — Now. Then the half-step back and the pull, and the whole enormous dark of the Deep coming forward off its legs, over him, into the channel. The water closing. The sun.

Somebody had cut it so the pull landed exactly on the first note of the reed-cutting song as Sedge started it again on her porch, which wasn’t how it had happened. It was better than how it had happened.
