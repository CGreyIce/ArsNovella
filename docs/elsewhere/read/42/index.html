Chapter 42

The Open Gate

3,303 words · 14 min read

The gate of the Waymeet was propped open with a stone, and it had been propped open with the same stone, Nell said, every longest night for as long as there’d been a Waymeet.

It was a big round river stone, grey, worn smooth on one side where generations of hands had rolled it into place. Nell rolled it herself at half past ten on Monday night, Drew’s time. She bent with a grunt and pushed it across the cobbles of the yard to the foot of the old wooden gate in the waist-high wall, and wedged it against the bottom bar. Then she straightened up and dusted her hands off on her apron and looked out through the open gate at the four roads.

They went away from the crossroads in four directions, into four kinds of dark. North, toward the hills Drew had come over. South, toward a river nobody at the Waymeet had a name for. East and west, toward nothing much: grass, and more grass, and in the far distance, the faint lights of farms. A lamp hung over the gate on an iron bracket. Every window in the long low inn behind them was lit. The big tree in the yard had a lantern in its lowest branch.

“There,” said Nell. “Now we’re open.”

He’d logged in at eleven exactly, his time. Eight at night in Malacca, where Ice was. Not a minute before. Ice’s friend Xav had a rule about it, and half the internet had worked out why. If the night went till the game’s next real dawn, eight on Tuesday morning in Malacca, eleven for Drew, the egg would give him exactly twelve hours. Not a minute short.

“Drew,” said Tiger, on the call, out of breath.

“Tiger.”

“I’ve lit the first gate.” Behind Tiger’s voice there was a sound like a waterfall. All seven locks of Sevenlocks were standing open, both gates of each, all the way up the hill, and the river was roaring through all of them at once. “It’s so loud. You can’t hear anything. The whole hill’s a river. Pell’s at the Top. I can see her lamp from here. I’ve got the bottom four. It’s—“ A thud, a scrape, a gasp. “It’s amazing.”

“Don’t fall in.”

“I’m not going to fall in.”

“Probably once.”

“Probably once,” Tiger agreed happily, and was gone again into the roar of the water.

The sun went down over the Waymeet at twenty past eleven, his time.

[ The Long Night has begun.
Keep your lights. Keep your towns. ]

Drew read it standing in the gateway, with his iron knuckles on and his back to the yard.

Keep your towns. He’d said, on an empty road on Wednesday, to eleven hundred people and the wind, I haven’t got a town. Then he’d said so I’ll find one, and walked four days to find it. And here it was. Thirty-one lights. A wall you could step over. An old couple, a tree, a dog, and twenty-three travellers who’d come in off the four roads in the last three days because it was the only wall for three days in any direction.

And a gate, propped open with a stone.

“Why open?” he’d asked Nell on Sunday night, by the fire, when he first came in.

“So they can get in,” Nell had said, as if it was obvious.

“The dark things?”

“Anybody.” She’d poured him a beer. “On the longest night, there’s people on the roads. Always. People who were going somewhere and didn’t get there. People whose lights went out. People running from something.” She’d put the jug down. “If the gate’s shut, they stand outside it in the dark and knock, and you have to decide. Every time. Who’s this. Is it safe. Do I open it.” She’d looked at him. “If it’s open, you don’t have to decide. They just come in.”

“Even if you don’t know what they are?”

“Especially then,” said Nell.

The Unlit came up out of the ditches.

There was no deep water at the Waymeet. No river, no lake, no mine. Just the four roads, and along each of them a ditch, half full of black winter water, going away into the dark. That was enough. A little after sunset the water in the ditches bulged, all along all four roads, and things came up out of it. Long and thin and wet and dark, with their heads hanging down. They came walking up the roads toward the crossroads. Toward the only lights for miles. Toward the open gate.

[ The Unlit · Lv 11 ]

He stood in the gateway and watched them come.

Watch their feet, Coll Rudd had said, on his crate, with his mug held out. Everything starts in the feet.

They didn’t have feet.

That was the first thing he saw, and he didn’t like it. They didn’t walk. They flowed. Their long legs moved, but nothing landed. There was no weight going down, no heel turning, no back foot digging in before a blow. Just a long dark shape coming up the road, smooth as water going downhill. Nothing to read.

The first one reached the gate and leaned over him toward the lamp on its bracket. He hit it.

He hit it the way he hit things, from the back foot up, iron knuckles into the place where its neck met its body. It came apart into black water and splashed over the cobbles of the yard. The dog, a big shaggy brown thing called Harrow, barked at the puddle as if it had personally offended him.

[ The Unlit is returned to the dark. ]

The second one hit him.

He didn’t see it start. There was nothing to see. It came out of the dark on his left, and its long head came down on him like a wet sack full of winter. He felt the cold go into his shoulder and through his chest and out the other side. Twenty per cent pain, and it still took his breath away.

He staggered. He got his hands up. He answered it, late, badly, and it came apart, but not before a third one had leaned past him toward the lamp. One of the travellers behind him put a spear through it, a tall Argentinian girl called Paz.

“You okay?” said Paz.

“Can’t read them,” said Drew.

He stood in the gateway and breathed and watched.

He watched for a long time. That was the thing he was good at. Not hitting. Watching. A fight was a conversation. Everybody told you what they were going to do before they did it, if you were patient enough to listen. A miner turned his back foot. A bear dug in. A man at a tournament, years ago, in a different game, always reloaded one shot early, and Drew had won a bracket on it.

Everything told you. You just had to find where.

The Unlit came up the four roads, and he watched them. Long and smooth and footless, flowing. And every one of them, before it moved on a light, did the same thing.

It leaned.

Not toward him. Toward the light. Just before it went for a lamp, the whole long dark body tipped toward it, a few degrees, like a plant toward a window. The head came round and the neck stretched and the body followed, half a second behind. They couldn’t help it. They wanted the light more than anything, and wanting it made them lean.

And if a man was standing between them and the light, the lean came first, and then the blow.

He watched the next one come up the north road. He watched it reach the edge of the lamplight. He watched it lean, a few degrees, toward the lamp over the gate, with him standing right under it.

He moved his head two inches to the left.

It came down where he’d been. Its long cold head went past his ear into the gatepost. And while it was still there, leaning, all its wanting gone forward into the lean, he answered it. Iron knuckles, right hand, from the back foot. It came apart.

“They want it too much,” he said, to two thousand people and a dog. “That’s all. They want the light so much they can’t hide it.”

People came in all night.

That was the other thing. Nell had been right. They came down the four roads out of the dark, toward the only lights for miles. Travellers, and not travellers. The gate was open, so they came in.

A pedlar from the south road, an old man with a cart and a mule. He’d been on his way somewhere and not got there. He came in white-faced and shaking, and the mule wouldn’t stop trembling. Tobias took them both round the back to the stable and gave the old man a blanket and the mule a bucket of oats.

Three travellers from the west road, a little before one in the morning, Drew’s time. Two men and a woman, running, with their lanterns out. Their town had gone dark, they said, gasping, in the yard. A beginner town somewhere west, small, too small. The Unlit had come up out of the town well, right in the middle of the square, and the lights had gone out one by one until there weren’t any. The town had been taken, the window said. So they’d run toward the only light they could see. They’d run for two hours. They sat by the fire in the inn with their hands round mugs and didn’t speak for a long time.

A woman from a farm on the east road with a small boy on her hip. Not a traveller. She’d seen the Waymeet’s lamp from her window when her own lamps went out. She’d walked across three fields in the dark carrying her son, because the gate would be open. It always was, on the longest night. Her grandmother had told her.

And at a quarter past two, a man in black.

He came up the north road out of the dark at a run, with three Unlit behind him. He was hurt, holding his left arm against his body. He was shorter than Drew, thin, with a bow on his back and no arrows in his quiver. And on the collar of his good black coat, stitched small, wings out, a dark bird.

Drew knew the bird. He’d watched Ice’s stream the night the north walk was held. He’d seen it on four collars, coming out of the dark at the narrows. He’d seen Ice’s friend Xav come up out of the reeds behind the one with the bow and say were you calling someone. He’d seen the window afterwards.

Traveller Kite (Nightjar) has fallen.

He’d come back, then. And he’d been out on the roads on the longest night, doing whatever Nightjar did on the longest night. And now he was running up the north road toward an open gate with no arrows and a bad arm and three Unlit behind him.

Drew stood in the gateway.

He could step back. He could step to one side and let the three Unlit have him. They’d reach him in about four seconds. Kite would go down in the road, outside the wall, in the dark, and lose his day. And nobody at the Waymeet would ever know who he was or what the bird on his collar meant. Nightjar killed people. Nightjar waited on the roads outside towns and took people’s days, for fun. Nightjar had come for the swamp guy’s friends in the dark.

If the gate’s shut, you have to decide. Every time.

The gate was open.

Drew stepped out of the gateway into the road. He went past Kite as Kite came through. He stood in the road in front of the three Unlit with his iron knuckles up, and watched them lean.

He slipped the first. He answered it. He slipped the second, and it came apart. The third leaned past him toward the lamp over the gate, and something went over his shoulder from behind, very fast. An arrow. Into the thing’s long neck, just where it bent. It came apart.

Drew turned round.

Kite was standing in the gateway with his bow in his good hand and a borrowed arrow from Paz’s quiver on the string. He was breathing hard, and looking at Drew, and his face was doing something complicated.

“You saw the bird,” said Kite.

“I saw the bird.”

“And you let me in.”

“Gate’s open,” said Drew. “Not my rule. Nell’s.” He nodded past Kite, into the yard. “There’s a fire. And a woman called Nell who’ll put something on your arm and won’t ask you anything, because she doesn’t need to.” He paused. “I watch Ice’s stream. I know what you are. I don’t care tonight.”

Kite looked at him for a long moment.

Then he looked past him, at the north road, at the dark. At the ditch, where the black water was already bulging again.

“I’ll take the wall,” he said. “Left side. There’s a gap you can’t see from the gate.”

“Thanks.”

“Don’t,” said Kite, and went.

Tiger had been running for three hours.

Drew could hear it. All night, under everything, on the call. The roar of the open locks, and Tiger’s breath, and his feet. Kiss it, don’t marry it, the way the runner girl had taught him. Along a lock lip, up the steps, round, never across, because you couldn’t cross an open gate. Every few minutes a short burst of words.

“First gate. Lit.”

“Third gate’s out. Going.”

“Pell’s lost two at the Top. She’s swearing. I can hear her from here.”

“Second gate. Lit. They came up out of the lock and leaned on it and I—” A thud. “—didn’t fall in. I didn’t fall in. Drew. I didn’t fall in.”

“Good.”

He’d been running for three hours with lamps. Up and down the bottom four locks of a hill with a river roaring down it. Eight gates, each with a lamp on its bracket, and the Unlit coming up out of the churning water to lean over them and put them out. Then back down to Old Gaspard’s for another, and up again. He hadn’t stopped. He’d said he wouldn’t, and he hadn’t.

“How many?” said Drew, at two in the morning.

“Lamps? I don’t know. Gaspard’s counting. He’s got a bet on with the lock-keeper again.” A gasp of a laugh. “Thirty? Forty? I’ve lost count. Drew. Drew, the whole town’s watching. They’re on the roofs. All of them, all down the hill, with their own lamps. Watching the runners. Pell said they would. It’s the runners’ night.”

“I know. I can see it. Your stream’s on my other screen.”

“You’re watching?”

“I’m always watching,” said Drew.

At half past two, his time, the sky began to grey.

He saw it from the gateway, over the east road. A line of dark blue along the bottom of the sky, over the grass, over the faint far lights of the farms. Lighter than the black. On time.

In the ditches, the Unlit stopped.

All of them, all along all four roads, at once. Their long heads came up out of the black water and turned east, and they stood there, perfectly still, like herons. Watching.

The yard went quiet behind him. The three from the taken town at the window. The pedlar in the stable door. The woman from the farm with her son asleep against her shoulder. Paz with her spear. Kite on the wall, left side, where the gap was. Nell on the step of the inn with a lamp in her hand. Everybody stopped and looked east.

“Tiger,” said Drew quietly. “It’s coming up. The grey. Can you see it?”

“I can see it.” Tiger was breathing hard. “They’ve stopped. In the locks. They’re all just standing in the water looking at it. Drew, it’s so weird, they’re all—“

The grey went lighter, along the whole edge of the sky. Lighter. Almost light enough to see colours.

At three o’clock, his time, it went out.

All of it. All at once. Like a hand closing over a lamp. And in the ditches along all four roads every long head went down together, and they came on again, faster, toward the gate.

[ The dark has not lifted. ]

And on the call, at the same moment, from the other side of the world, there was a sound Drew had been half waiting for all night.

A splash. A big one.

There was a long second of nothing but roaring water.

Then Tiger came up, spluttering, coughing, half drowned, laughing so hard he could barely breathe.

“That’s one!” he shouted, over the roar of the locks. “Drew! That’s one! I stopped! The sky went out and I stopped and I fell in! That’s ONE!”

“That’s one,” said Drew.

He was laughing too. He couldn’t help it. He stood in the open gate of an inn at a crossroads in the middle of nowhere, with the dark coming up all four roads at once and the sky gone black, laughing. Behind him, in the yard, Nell put her lamp down on the step, and came across the cobbles, and stood beside him in the gateway.

“So it’s long, then,” she said, looking at the black sky.

“It’s long.”

“Then the gate stays open longer.” She folded her arms. “Same as always.”

“How long?”

Nell looked out at the four roads. At the black water bulging in the ditches. At the dark, and whatever was in it, and whoever else was out there on a road somewhere, running toward the only light for miles.

“Till they stop coming,” she said.

On the call, Tiger had got out of the lock. Drew could hear him, dripping, scrambling up the steps, and Old Gaspard shouting something from his doorway that was either a cheer or a curse. And then Tiger was running again, up the hill, with a new lamp.

“Drew,” he said, between breaths. “Still there?”

“Still here,” said Drew, in the gateway, with his knuckles up.
