Pink

3,640 words · 16 min read

!pink had passed !dark on the eleventh of April, and Tif had screenshotted it, obviously.

She had the dashboard open on the second screen in her room in Singapore on Sunday night, the way she always did. Curtains shut. The egg in the corner, humming, with Xyro in it. He was in the round town at the forge with Ina tonight, doing something with bellows. And she’d stayed out, because it was Sunday, and on Sundays she stayed out and modded properly. Somebody had to.

She’d made !dark on the first day of the game, two and a half years ago, at four in the afternoon. He’d cut the stream for the first time because he’d found a door. She’d thought it would get to ten. It had taken two and a half years to get to four hundred and thirty-one. Every time he cut the stream for something he didn’t want three hundred thousand people to see. A door. A hill. A gate in the round town where a girl said me.

She’d made !pink in January, the day after the old hall. As a joke.

It had four thousand two hundred and six uses in three and a half months.

She put the two numbers side by side in the folder. ice (do not delete). And looked at them for a long time.

She’d watched the whole spring. Every night. From inside the egg, mostly, and then on the clips afterwards, and on Sundays like this, from out here.

She’d watched the walk home from the hill, nine days south in the dark with edges. Then Hyv at the old hall putting both hands on his face in front of eight people and saying mine, and Xola screaming. Chat had made a noise she’d never seen chat make. Three hundred thousand people typing the same word at once. MINE MINE MINE MINE. She’d slow-moded it for the first time in four years. Not because it was bad. Because she couldn’t read anything else.

She’d watched the draw. From the step in the Seven Below, through his eyes. Sixty-one minutes, and then both at one per cent, three paces apart, and nine more minutes of nothing. Three hundred and fourteen thousand people watching two men not move. Then a calm voice off-frame saying it’ll be March. She’d clipped it. Of course. IT’LL BE MARCH. Two million in a day. Malcolm had written her a letter, in here, for the first time ever: please stop sending me the clip. She’d sent it to him again.

She’d watched him laugh. On the fountain rim, at New Year, when Leaka jumped into the fountain on purpose and took Curse with her. Not the small one he did in fights. A real one, the whole of it, out loud, with the stream shaking because he was. She’d heard him laugh like that maybe twice in four years of his chat. She’d clipped it and called it nothing. Just the timestamp. People had found it anyway.

She’d watched the lion eat the green. On the first morning of the year, from his eyes on the fountain rim, tapping the beat on his shield. A red lion going up the south steps on its back legs to an old woman holding out a lettuce on a pole. Leaka had typed EAT SOMETHING GREEN at the top of his chat every night for years, in capitals, to a man in a swamp with a sack of herbs who never did. And on the morning the lion ate it, chat had typed it back. All of it. Three hundred thousand people. EAT SOMETHING GREEN. Tif had made !green that morning, crying.

She’d watched the spring stone on two streams at once. His, from the round town, with nothing on it but the long table and Hyv. And Chi’s, from the hill, PenguChi streaming from the back of everything the way she always did. Five people and a sheep in a dark country going green. A man on one knee on a crooked stone, lowering a stone the size of a table-top into black water on ropes, until it stopped. Chat on Chi’s stream had been smaller, and slower, and kinder. Somebody had typed right size when the stone settled, and the whole of Chi’s chat had typed it after them, quietly. Tif had clipped it. RIGHT SIZE. Salty had written her a letter. One word. thanks.

She’d watched Gabe cry at the flame for forty-one minutes on Chi’s stream. She hadn’t clipped that. Some things you didn’t.

She’d watched the roof go on from the eye, through his eyes. He’d been at the foot of the green pole that night, with his sword. The stream had been dark, all of it, for an hour. Just the sound of sixty people holding their breath. Then Jared’s voice saying now, and the boom, and six hundred and twelve lanterns coming on at once, red and gold, and the camera going straight up to the eye. Two small figures standing up on a bench.

SOMEWHERE, chat had typed. Three hundred thousand people. Because the round town had.

And she’d watched the windows.

That was the one she kept thinking about. On the twenty-first. He’d been at the banner room window with Hyv, with a candle on the sill. Old Tess. The stream had shown the round town through the glass. Four hundred and thirty-one windows lit, every one, small and yellow and steady. And Deston and Chernn walking across the market, hand in hand, very slowly, with nobody coming out.

And chat had stopped.

Not slow-mode. She hadn’t touched anything. It had just stopped. Three hundred and two thousand people watching two people walk through a town full of lit windows. Not one of them had typed anything. For eleven minutes.

She’d sat in her room with her hand over her mouth and watched the chat window. Empty. Scrolling nothing. Eleven minutes.

Nobody had told them. Chernn had said small, at the long table, honest, nothing please. And Kei had told the round town, and the round town had told nobody else. But chat had known. The way chat knew things, sometimes. Three hundred thousand strangers all over the world watching through a man’s eyes. Out of every one of them, somebody had decided, all at once, not to say anything. So the two of them could walk through it quiet.

At the end of the eleven minutes, as Deston and Chernn reached the milestone, one message.

That was all.

Tif had cried so hard Xyro came out of the egg to see what was wrong.

On the Thursday before, in the egg, she’d been at Ama Corrie’s counter for a key.

Tif didn’t go to the counter much. She was a Hearth. She kept fires, mostly, in the Hart and the banner room, and she went where Xyro went. She stood at the counter with her ears flat while Ama Corrie looked for the key. And Ama Corrie, not looking up, said:

“You’re the one who watches him.”

Tif’s ears went up.

“From out there,” said Ama Corrie. She found the key. She didn’t hand it over. “The travellers look at the air. Sixty of them. I stopped wondering what at in the first month.” She looked at Tif over the Roll. “But you look at it more than any of them. And when you do, you look at him.”

“I mod his stream,” said Tif. Then, because Ama Corrie wouldn’t know what that meant: “I watch him. From out there. So other people can. I keep it tidy.”

“Hm,” said Ama Corrie. “How long?”

“Four years.”

Ama Corrie looked at her for a long time.

“What’s he like?” she said. “From out there.”

Tif thought about it. Four years. Twenty-one people on the first day of the game, and ten before that, for years, on streams nobody watched. A man in a swamp. A man at the far end of every room. A man who’d never once asked her for anything.

“The same,” said Tif. “He’s the same. Out there and in here.”

Ama Corrie put the key in her hand.

“Good,” she said. “Most people aren’t.”

Tonight was ordinary.

That was what she liked about Sundays. Nothing happened, mostly. He was at the Hart, at the long table, with Hyv on his knee, which wasn’t anything any more. It was just what the stream looked like now. Hyv’s tail round his ankle, at the bottom of the frame. Hyv’s hand coming in from the left every so often to take something off his plate. Hyv’s voice, very close, saying something that made the stream go slightly pink.

Tif typed nothing. She didn’t need to. She just watched.

At ten, Kei put a sheet of paper on the long table.

Tif saw it come into the frame from the top. Kei’s hand, and his pencil, and a fresh sheet, which he put down in front of the first of the nine chairs. Where the stream could see it. She wasn’t sure he’d meant that. With Kei you were never sure.

It was a board. The way his boards always were. Small neat writing, a few lines, nothing wasted.

MASMARK · JULY the bridge · properly Everybody who wants to watch On foot · fourteen days Nobody alone

Under it, a dotted line, from a small circle that was the round town to a bigger one that was a lake.

Ice looked at it for a long time. The stream looked at it with him, three hundred thousand people. Hyv, on his knee, put her chin on his shoulder and looked at it too.

“Everybody?” said Ice.

“Everybody who wants to watch,” said Kei. “That’s what it says.”

“It’s a fight. On a bridge.”

“It’s your fight,” said Kei. “On Lucan’s bridge. With half of Masmark on the terraces.” He put his pencil down beside the sheet. “The guild’s never once walked to watch one of its own do anything. We’ve walked to hold things. Doors. Roads. Hills.” He looked at Ice. “This year we’ll walk to watch.”

The pencil went round the long table. Tif watched it go, hand to hand, through his eyes. Every hand. Nobody passed it on without writing. Gabe wrote his name very large. Rae wrote hers under it, very small, at no paces. Leaka drew a lion. PenguChi wrote Patience, which could have meant either of them.

Hyv took the pencil out of his hand and wrote, at the very bottom, in letters bigger than Gabe’s: SCREAMING.

Then, about eleven, a letter.

It came up in his window the way letters did. Folded small, the plain black wax VANTA used, with a single white line through it. He opened it at the long table. Hyv read it over his shoulder with her chin on it, which meant three hundred thousand people read it over his shoulder too.

Tif read it twice.

Not to watch you. To watch him.

She thought about Kestrel, a year and a half ago, in a hall at the top of a long stair, losing to him in eleven minutes. Saying it was like fighting a boss. Kestrel had wanted to beat him ever since. And now she was going to stand on a bridge in Masmark in July and watch somebody else try. Somebody who’d fallen over four hundred times learning the slow one. And she wasn’t going to watch the man who’d beaten her. She was going to watch the one who was learning.

Tif thought that was the most grown-up thing she’d ever heard of a person doing. She put it in the folder.

And then he did something he’d never done.

At twenty past eleven, at the long table, with Hyv on his knee, he looked up.

Not at anything in the room. Up, and a bit to the right. At nothing. At the corner of the air in front of him, where, if you were in an egg, you’d know the stream was. Where the little eye that looked out of you sat.

He never did that. In two and a half years he’d never once looked at the stream. He streamed his own eyes because it was easier than not. He didn’t talk to chat. He didn’t read chat while he was in. Hi, Tif, at Year Two, was the most he’d ever said to it. And he’d said that to the air, not to the camera.

He looked straight at it now.

“Tif,” he said.

Tif sat bolt upright on her bed.

On the screen, Hyv had turned her head and was looking at him, very close, with her eyes that saw everything. He didn’t look at her. He was looking at the stream. At Tif.

“Tomorrow night,” he said. “The end of it. A quarter to eight.” He paused. “Be there.”

That was all.

He looked away again, at the long table, at Kei, at his plate with no chestnut on it. As if nothing had happened.
