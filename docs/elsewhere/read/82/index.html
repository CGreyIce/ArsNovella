Chapter 82

The Gate

2,889 words · 13 min read

On the last day, Kei woke at five in the morning in Australia, and didn’t go back to sleep, and didn’t try.

He lay in the dark with his eyes open and did the sums. He’d done them so many times that they’d stopped being numbers. Twelve names on a page in a book on a counter in a round hall. Four strangers at the gate. Four friends on a road, a few hours out, walking through the night with nineteen people round them. One of them at fourteen and ninety-eight per cent. A writ that ended at midnight. Twenty people in red sashes sitting round a fountain, who’d been sitting there for four days and could sit there forever.

He got up and made tea. He drank it at the window, watching the sky go grey over the roofs. Then he went and stood in the shower for a long time.

At eight he went to the café. At nine he lay down.

Carrow Cross was full.

It hadn’t been, a month ago. A month ago it had been a round town on seven roads with four thousand travellers in it. On the morning of the fourth of March there were nearly six thousand. People had heard. Not everything. Nobody but the twelve and the call knew everything. But they’d heard that something was happening at the round hall, that the swamp guy had walked here and wasn’t in it, that the red ones were waiting at the fountain, that tonight was the night. So they’d come. To watch. They sat on the walls of the round market and the steps of the houses and the rims of the stone troughs, and bought pies from the stalls, and waited.

Kei came out onto the steps of the Hall at the game’s first dawn and stood there and looked at them.

“Oh,” he said quietly.

“You’re famous,” said Gigi, beside him. She’d been there since before him. She’d been there every morning since she arrived, on the third step, with her pocket watch. “I told you it’d be a disaster.”

“You said it’d fall down.”

“Same thing,” said Gigi. “It’s falling up. That’s worse.”

Nine thousand. A month ago, twenty-five people had watched him do anything at all. He looked at the number in the corner of his eye for a moment, and then he stopped looking at it. It didn’t matter. It was the day. The number would still be there tomorrow, or it wouldn’t.

At the fountain in the middle of the round market, twenty people in red sashes were having breakfast. The neat young man with the ledger, CL_Steward_03, saw Kei on the steps and raised a cup of something, politely. Kei nodded back. There was no point being rude. They weren’t doing anything wrong. They were just waiting for him to fail.

They came in at noon.

Hyvred saw them first, from the ledge round the eye of the dome. She’d been up there since dawn, watching the south road, the way she’d watched all seven for a month. At five to twelve her voice came on the call, calm and low.

“South road,” she said. “Four miles. Nineteen of them.” A pause. “They’re all walking together. I can see Gabe’s hammer. I can see Ice’s shield. I can see Tiger.” Another pause. “Wait. Tiger’s on the south road. Why is Tiger—“

“He went out to meet them at dawn,” said Drew, from the steps, very calmly. “He didn’t tell anyone. He just went.”

“Of course he did,” said Hyvred. And then, a moment later, softer: “Chris is in the middle. Everyone’s round him. Like a wall.”

Kei closed his eyes for a second on the steps of the Hall.

“Thank you, Hyv.”

They came up the south road into Carrow Cross at a quarter past twelve, and the round market stood up to watch them come.

Twenty of them, with Tiger. Niko in front with his sword and his book, unhurried. Newish beside him with the drum on her back, talking. Winter with the harpoon and the coil of rope over her shoulder and her horns down, saying nothing. Round them, Moonlight Harvest and Riverbank, in no order at all, just walking. Ice with his shield on his back. Xav with his notebook under his arm. Azac with a shield with a hole in it, where a harpoon had been. At the back, on his own, with his bow strung, Kite.

And in the middle, where everyone was round him, Chris.

He looked exhausted. He looked like a man who’d walked eleven days and crossed a gorge on a rope and not slept. He came across the round market to the steps of the Hall with everybody round him. He stopped at the bottom of the steps and looked up at Kei.

“Ferrowmere,” said Chris. “Here.”

“Here,” said Niko.

“Here,” said Newish. “Here. I walked to his ugly town. Then I walked here. I’ve walked further than anyone. Write that down.”

“Here,” said Winter.

Kei wrote it down. His hand was shaking. He let it.

They signed at one.

Niko first. Nikorasu. An unhurried hand, the letters even, the way he’d written still and wait and hold in a warden’s book on a bridge. He wrote it, and looked at it, and put the pen down.

“Thirteen,” said Niko.

Newish second. somebodynewish. She signed it with a flourish so big it went off the end of the line, and said “Fourteen, and I want it noted that I’m fourteenth and Chris isn’t even on it yet.”

“Noted,” said Chris, from the counter, where he was leaning on Azac’s shield.

Winter third. Winter. Plain. Six letters.

“Fifteen,” she said.

Then Chris. He came to the counter, and looked at the pen, and didn’t pick it up. He looked at the book, at the fifteen names on the page, and the five empty lines under them, and the writ at the top in fresh black ink. The thirtieth day ends at midnight on the fourth of March.

“I can’t,” he said.

He said it calmly, the way he said everything. Like a number in a sheet.

“Not yet,” said Kei.

“Not yet,” agreed Chris. He stepped back from the counter. “Two per cent.” He looked at Kei. “Do the gate. I’ll be here. I’m not going anywhere.”

The gate was the Hall.

Kei had thought about it for a month, on the steps, every night. What the gate should be. Where. How. If the gate’s shut, you have to decide. Every time. In the end he’d decided it should be nothing special. No ceremony. Just the round hall, in the middle of the day, with the seven doors open and the eye in the dome open, and the light coming down through it onto the floor. The fifteen of them who’d signed, standing round the edge. And whoever wanted in, in the middle, under the eye, saying why.

Moonlight Harvest stood in the north door. Not in the Hall. In the door. Riverbank stood in the west door beside them. Ice had said they would. Not members. Friends. We’ll stand in the door. Ama Corrie stood at the counter with her keys and her spectacles and the book.

Four of them came into the middle, one at a time.

Tobi first.

He came in from the south door, where he’d been at the milestone every day for two weeks, practising with his crossbow. He stood under the eye in the light with the crossbow held across him, very straight, and said “Good afternoon” to the whole Hall, formally, the way you’d greet somebody’s mother. Somebody in the north door laughed, quietly, and kindly. It was Kotori.

“I’m Tobi,” he said. “I’m from Lagos. I’m sixteen. Level sixteen. I mean, I’m nineteen. I’m level sixteen.”

“Why do you want to be in it?” said Kei.

Tobi looked round the round hall at the fifteen of them. At Hyvred, on the ledge high up round the eye, looking down.

“On the long night,” he said, “I ran to three lanterns on a bridge, because they were the only ones I could see. And someone on a roof called my shots all night, and never once asked what level I was.” He looked up at Hyvred. “The red ones asked. They said go away then. Nobody here’s asked me.” He swallowed. “I want to be in a thing where nobody asks.”

“He waves at the dome every evening,” said Hyvred, from the ledge. “At me. Even though I can’t see him wave. I can, though.”

“Yes,” said Tiger, at once.

“You can’t just say yes,” said Gigi. “There’s a process.”

“The process is yes,” said Tiger.

Kei looked round the Hall. At fifteen faces. Nobody said no.

“Yes,” said Kei.

Tobi didn’t move for a second. Then he said “Thank you” so formally that Kotori laughed again in the north door, and went to the counter, and signed. Tobi. In careful block capitals, like a form.

“Sixteen,” said Kei.

Bram and Joy came together. They’d always come together.

They came in from the north-west door, the one Moonlight Harvest had walked in by, and stood under the eye side by side. A square silent Dutchman with a spear, and a girl from Cebu with a round shield. They didn’t say anything for a long time. Bram didn’t say anything at all. He never had.

“We held some steps,” said Joy eventually. “In Fernwick. On the longest night. Sedge’s steps, at the end of the north walk. Bram fell at four in the morning. He came back the next day and held them again.” She looked at Bram. “He doesn’t talk. He just stands. That’s what he’s good at.”

“You stood at the cross with us,” said Salty. “On Saturday. In the dark. You didn’t run.”

“No,” said Joy.

“Yes,” said Salty.

“Yes,” said Wendy beside him, quietly.

Nobody said no. Bram signed, Bram_V, without a word. Joy signed, Joy, and her hand only shook a little.

“Seventeen,” said Kei. “Eighteen.”

Kite came last.

He’d been standing in the north door with Moonlight Harvest, at the back, with his bow. When Kei looked at him, he came out of the door into the Hall. He walked to the middle, under the eye, and stood in the light where everyone could see him. The narrow face. The dark patch on his collar, the loose threads. The bow, strung now, for the first time since he’d walked into Sevenlocks.

He didn’t say anything. He waited.

Nobody said anything either. The round hall was very quiet. Out in the market, six thousand people had gone quiet too, because they could see through the open doors. At the fountain, the twenty red sashes had stopped eating.

“You know who I am,” said Kite eventually.

“We know,” said Kei.

“I was Nightjar.” His voice was steady. “For two months. I was on the north walk in Fernwick when we went for the swamp guy’s lot in the dark. I shot at him. I was at the waystations at dusk.” He stopped. “I took people’s days.”

“How many?” said Maigo.

It wasn’t a kind question. Maigo wasn’t being unkind. Kei knew him. It was the question somebody had to ask, out loud, so that it was asked. Kite looked at him.

“I don’t know,” he said. “I stopped counting.”

“Fourteen,” said Chris.

Everybody turned. Chris was leaning on Azac’s shield by the counter, very tired, with his sheet open in the air in front of him. A tab Kei recognised. Nightjar — known members, kills, locations.

“Fourteen that I’ve got,” said Chris. “From the public death log. Fourteen days, taken. Since day ten.” He looked at Kite. “There might be more. That’s what I’ve got.”

Kite didn’t look away.

“I can’t give them back,” he said. “I know I can’t.”

“No,” said Wendy.

She said it quietly, from the edge of the Hall. Kei looked at her. Wendy, who’d said on the call a month ago that she’d like to be at the gate. Who’d frozen on the longest night, and let go, and let go again on Saturday at a stone cross in the heather.

“You can’t give them back,” said Wendy. “But you were running toward a light. On the longest night. Drew said.” She looked at Kite. “And on Saturday you told Kei where Ash would be. And on Wednesday you shot an arrow out of the air over a gorge so Chris could cross.” She was quiet for a moment. “That’s three. It’s not fourteen. It’s three.”

Nobody said anything.

“It’ll fall down,” said Gigi. “If we let him in, somebody’ll say you let a Nightjar in, and it’ll fall down.” She looked at Kite. “I gave you a cup. Was it horrible?”

“It was horrible,” said Kite.

“Did you drink it?”

“All of it.”

“Hm,” said Gigi. “I’d rather it fell down because we let somebody in.”

“Yes,” said Tiger.

“Tiger, there’s a process—“

“Yes,” said Drew.

Everybody looked at him. Drew hadn’t said anything all afternoon. He was standing by the east door with his knuckles on, watching Kite’s feet the way he watched everybody’s feet. He’d been watching them since a gate on a crossroads on the longest night. A boy with a bird on his collar had come running up the road with no arrows and three things behind him, and Drew had stepped out and held the road.

“Yes,” said Drew again. “Gate’s open. Not my rule.”

Maigo was still looking at Kite. He looked at him for a long time. At the bow. At the collar. At his face.

“He took the bird off the post,” said Maigo slowly. “At the gorge. Winter said. He took it down and put it in his pocket.” He breathed out. “Yes. But you stand next to me. Where I can see you.”

“Okay,” said Kite.

“And you stand at the gate,” said Wendy. “With me. When it’s open. That’s the job. That’s a lot of standing.”

“Okay,” said Kite.

Kei looked round the round hall. Fifteen faces. Nobody said no.

“Yes,” said Kei.

Kite stood under the eye in the light for a moment longer, very still. Then he walked to the counter. Ama Corrie held out the pen, black, without a word. And he signed, slowly, in a hand that didn’t shake at all.

Kite.

He put the pen down. He looked at his own name on the page for a long time. Then he reached up to his collar, to the dark patch where the bird had been, and pulled off the last two loose threads, and dropped them on the floor of the Hall.

“Nineteen,” said Kei.

He went out onto the steps afterwards, because he had to breathe.

Ice was there. He’d come out of the north door and round the outside of the Hall, and was sitting on the third step, Kei’s step. He moved over when Kei came out, without a word, so that Kei could sit beside him.

The round market was full of people. At the fountain, the twenty red sashes had gone back to eating, very slowly, watching the steps.

“Nineteen,” said Kei.

“I heard.”

“Chris is two per cent.”

“I know.”

“The writ ends at midnight.” Kei looked at the sky. It was early afternoon in the game, and the first dusk was hours off, and midnight was further. “Nightjar’s on every road. Hyv says. Seven roads, all of them. If Chris goes out to fight something, they’ll take his day. If he dies, he’s thirteen. If he’s thirteen, he can’t sign.”

“I know.”

“And I won’t let him go out.” Kei looked at his hands. “I won’t. I’d rather lose the room.”

Ice was quiet for a long time.

“Then he doesn’t go out,” he said. “He gets it in here.”

“How?”

Ice looked up at the Hall behind them. At the dome. At the round grey stone gone gold, and the seven doors, and the north door that stuck, and the roof that leaked in three places, and the eye at the top, open, that Gigi had forced after sixty years and broken a nail on.

“Life skills feed the bar,” said Ice. “Every draught I ever brewed in that swamp moved it a bit.” He looked at Kei. “What’s Chris’s production skill?”

Kei thought.

“Engineering,” he said.

Ice looked at the north door, which stuck. Then at Ama Corrie, coming out of it with her keys, jangling, frowning at the hinges.

“Then he fixes the Hall,” said Ice.
