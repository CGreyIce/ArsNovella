Second

3,478 words · 15 min read

“Ice,” said Hyvred.

Not left. Not now. His name.

He’d been waiting for it. He’d been standing at the edge of the cavern at the bottom of the lake for forty minutes, with the pull going round him like water round a stone. With nineteen people in his lee and the crown of light turning over the black pool, coming lower. He’d been waiting the way he’d waited for a year with his back to a heron-stone in a fen. On the back foot. A breath.

And when she said his name, he knew it was time. Not because she’d told him. Because she’d said his name in the voice she’d used once before, on the longest night, at a stream, with a lake standing up. The voice that meant I can see it. It’s here. I’m not going to say anything else because you already know.

He went.

The back foot came round. He stepped back, half a step, off the edge of the rock onto the stone behind it. The way he had on a plank at a ford, and at a stream under the round town. And let go.

All of it.

[ Undertow · 11,406 ]

He’d never pulled anything that big.

He’d pulled Brann off his feet in a market. He’d pulled Xav across the floor of the Seven Below. He’d pulled a lake into a stream. That had been nine thousand, and he’d felt it go out of him like something tearing.

This was more.

It went out of him into the crown like a hook into the sky. The crown was turning above the edge of the pool. Low, close, so bright its light was on his face, trying to pull him in the way it pulled everything. And for one moment the two pulls met. The crown’s, going out, pulling everything that fell in the lake down to the bottom. And his, coming back, everything he’d held for forty minutes.

Two currents. Meeting at the bottom of a lake.

He’d been an undertow for a year. Still water on top, a current underneath. He’d never met another one.

He held.

The crown stopped turning.

He felt it. Through the shield, through his arms, through his feet on the old stone. The crown’s pull and his, one against the other, and neither of them moving. A second. Two. Like two people leaning on opposite sides of a door. His window in the corner of his eye going down, very fast, as the pull took everything out of him.

And then the crown’s pull went.

Not because it was weaker. He’d think about that for a long time afterward. It wasn’t weaker. It was older and bigger than him and it had pulled everything that had ever fallen in Great Lake Masmark down to the bottom for as long as there’d been a lake.

But he wasn’t falling. He was standing still. He’d been standing still for forty minutes, on purpose, with nineteen people behind him. And it had never once pulled anything that stood still.

It fell.

Off itself. Out of the air over the pool, down onto the rock at its edge, in front of him. Like a wheel coming off a cart. It hit the stone with a sound like a bell struck under water, and lay there. A ring of light the size of a cart wheel, on the ground, not turning. Still bright. But still.

He was on one knee. He didn’t remember going down. His shield was flat on the rock in front of him and he was looking at the crown over the top of it, three paces off, lying on the ground. Everybody had loosed at it already. Every bow, every spell, every blade, when it came low. Everything they had, already gone.

Now, he thought. Somebody. Now.

Nobody had anything left.

Then an arrow went past his ear.

He heard it before he saw it. A thin sound, very close, from behind and to the right, where the two bows had been. It went past his head so close he felt the wind of it in his hair. Over his shield, over the three paces of rock. Into the crown, lying on the ground, not turning.

Into the middle of it. Into the place in the ring where there was nothing, the way there’d been nothing on the Crownless King’s head. Where something had been, and wasn’t, and now was.

The crown went out.

Not all at once. From the edges in, the way the red had gone out of Salty’s sword in the stream in the dark. The ring of light going grey, slowly, from the rim toward the middle, where the arrow was. And then only a point of light in the middle, round the arrow, like an ember. And then that went too.

And the ceiling of black water, the whole of Great Lake Masmark held up above them by nothing, let out a breath. A long sound, very deep, from everywhere at once. Like a whole lake settling.

No world first. It came plain, in the corner of everybody’s window, the way it came for anybody. Cleared.

Second.

He turned round on one knee.

Xyro was standing at the back of the cavern with his bow still raised, and his face doing something Ice had never seen it do. Wendy beside him had both hands over her mouth.

“You waited,” said Ice.

“For the second one,” said Xyro. His voice was very odd. “Wendy said. And Slep. You always wait for the second one.”

Ice looked at him for a long moment.

“Yes,” he said.

Hyvred got to him first. Of course she did. She’d been two paces behind him the whole fight, and she was there before he’d finished turning round. On her knees beside him on the rock, with her bow dropped somewhere, her hands on his face.

“Nineteen,” she said. “Nineteen per cent. You went to NINETEEN.”

“I know where.”

“I KNOW you know where.” She was crying. She was laughing. She was doing both, the way she did both. “You pulled a crown. Out of the sky. You pulled the whole lake. It was pulling everything in the world and you just stood there—“

“It doesn’t pull things that stand still,” said Ice. “I found out.”

“You found out,” said Hyvred, and put her forehead against his.

He let her. He knelt on the rock at the bottom of Great Lake Masmark with his shield flat in front of him and Hyvred’s forehead against his. Nineteen people coming across the cavern toward them. He didn’t move.

Kei had his map out. He was kneeling on the rock a few paces off with it unrolled, drawing. A small round thing at the bottom of the new sheet, at the very end of the deep. And beside it, very small, a word.

Ice could see it from here. Kept.

They came up the deep at dawn. Four hours, and the Crownless Hall, where the king on his throne had lifted his head as they passed. He looked at them with the empty ring on his head, and then bowed it again, and Ice thought he looked lighter. And the third flight and the Long Gallery, where the floor had come back while they were below, every tile. And the gap where the keystone had been, with a new grey tile on a new pillar, waiting for whoever came next. And the drowned forge, still roaring, the way Jun Liang had left it. And the first flight, and the door, and the steps between the two walls of green water.

And out onto the island, into the morning.

He’d been in the egg for twelve hours less nine minutes. The clock in the corner of his eye said 7:51. They had nine minutes.

The island was full. Thousands of people on the rocks and the shingle at the foot of the tower, the way it had been at the tenth dusk. And boats all round, all over the lake. Waiting. They’d been waiting all night.

When Constellation came up the steps between the walls of water, the crowd made a noise.

He didn’t know what it was at first. It wasn’t a cheer. It was lower than that, and longer. Like the sound the lake had made when the crown went out.

Then he saw them. At the front of the crowd, at the top of the steps, in plain grey, under a black banner with one white line. Twenty of VANTA. They’d been up since an hour after midnight. They’d come out first. And they’d stayed, all night, on the island, at the top of the steps. Waiting for whoever came second.

Kestrel was at the front.

She was standing at the very top of the steps with her bow on her back. She was looking at him.

He came up the last of the steps and stopped in front of her. Two paces. He was at nineteen per cent and he’d drunk two draughts on the way up and he was at thirty-one. His shield had a dent in it the shape of a crown. Hyvred was beside him with her hand in his, the way it had been since the cavern. She didn’t seem to have noticed she was doing it.

Kestrel looked at the hand. Then at him.

Neither of them said anything for a long time.

Behind her, VANTA stood very still. Brann with his tower shield. Ottie. Mags. Ren. The fifteen. Behind him, Constellation came up the steps one at a time and stopped. Kei with his map. Xyro with his bow.

“First,” said Kestrel.

“I saw,” said Ice. “The window. Six minutes in.”

“You saw it in the fight.”

“I was in the fight.”

“And you said good for them.” Her voice did something. “Tif clipped it. It’s everywhere. You were at the bottom of a lake fighting a crown and the window came, and you read it, and you said good for them, and went left.” She stopped. “Everybody in Masmark heard you say it.”

“I meant it.”

“I know you meant it,” said Kestrel. “That’s the problem.”

She looked at him. Ice saw her face do something he’d never seen on any of her streams, or on an oak floor at Elmcross, or at a ring in a market at first dawn. Something like a door coming open somewhere a long way off.

“Four times,” said Kestrel. “Second four times. Every time, you. And every time you didn’t even know you’d beaten me. You were just there, first, at the front, without trying.” She took a breath. “And this time I was first. And you said good for them. And you meant it.” She shook her head. “I wanted to beat you so much. For so long. And you’ve never once wanted to beat anybody.”

Ice thought about it.

“I wanted to beat Lucan,” he said. “Once. On a bridge. A long time ago.”

“Did you?”

“I don’t remember,” said Ice. “I think I stopped wanting to.” He looked at her. “You saw the shape. The steps. Kei told me. You didn’t decide it.”

“I didn’t decide it.”

“Then you were first because you were there,” said Ice. “Not because you were trying.” He paused. “That’s the good kind.”

Kestrel stared at him.

Then she laughed. Out loud, at the top of the steps on the island in Great Lake Masmark, in front of thousands of people. Not a short laugh. A long one, with her head back, the kind Xav had seen once on a roof at first dawn when he’d said teach me and sweep it after. Behind her, Brann smiled. It wasn’t something he did much.

“Good for you,” said Ice.

“Good for me,” said Kestrel. She wiped her eyes. “Good for me.” She looked at him. “Next door.”

“Next door,” said Ice.

Drew was on the shingle below the steps. Out of the lockout, a day later, at fifty. He’d come out to the island in the fish man’s boat at midnight and sat on the stones all night, waiting. He was sitting with somebody.

Ice saw who, and stopped.

Lucan of Sable Court was sitting on the shingle beside Drew with his knees up. In black, with silver at the cuffs. Out of the race since the second night, with eleven of his twenty fallen in the deep. He wasn’t moving. For once, every part of him was still. He’d been sitting there with Drew, Gabe would say afterward, for six hours. Talking about feet.

Drew got up when he saw Ice. He came up the shingle to the bottom of the steps and stopped. He didn’t say anything. He raised two fingers to the side of his eyebrow.

“o7,” said Drew.

“o7,” said Ice.

That was all. It was enough. Drew went back down the shingle and sat down again beside Lucan, and the two of them went on looking at the lake.

Lucan looked up at Ice from the stones.

“Second,” he said.

“Second.”

“I was out on the second night,” said Lucan. “Something in the deep took eleven of us. It came from behind.” He was quiet. “Your edge-man stood on a stone in the middle of a lake for forty seconds so nineteen people could cross. I watched it on the board. I didn’t know what it was, at first. Then he sat down next to me here and told me.” He looked at Drew. “He said he watched your feet. For fifteen months. And went in the breath before you.”

“He did,” said Ice.

“Nobody’s ever gone in the breath before you,” said Lucan. “Not in the wind game. Not me.” He looked back at Ice. “I’ve been sitting here all night thinking about that.”

Ice didn’t say anything.

Lucan got up. Slowly, for him. He stood on the shingle at the bottom of the steps and looked up at Ice, and Hyvred beside him with her hand in his.

“Tank,” said Lucan. “Who’d stand next to you?”

Ice looked at him.

It was Wendy’s question. From the gate. Lucan must have heard it somewhere. On a stream, on the forum, at the netloft door. He was asking it properly. On a beach, at dawn, in front of everybody, the way you’d ask at a gate.

“Hyv,” said Ice.

“Then she can watch,” said Lucan. “When I ask you. On the bridge. Properly.” He paused. “Not today. You’re at thirty-one and you’ve just pulled a crown out of the sky. When you’re ready.”

“When I’m ready,” said Ice.

“I’ll ask again,” said Lucan.

“I know,” said Ice. “You always do.”

Lucan laughed, short, out loud, and turned and went back down the shingle to sit by Drew again. And up on the rocks above the steps, where Meridian stood under the gold line, a small woman in plain blue caught Ice’s eye across the crowd. Third, the board said now. Meridian had come out of the deep twenty minutes after Constellation. She nodded. Once.

He nodded back.

The egg took them all at once, on the island, at the top of the steps. VANTA and Constellation together, and the thousands on the shingle, and the boats on the lake.

He lay in it in the back room at Melaka Raya at eight in the morning with the fan going round. Gabe was beside him on the edge of his pod, which he’d come out of an hour ago, with two cups of kopi already made, too sweet. He’d been watching on his phone.

“Second,” said Gabe.

“Second.”

“You pulled a crown out of the sky, Dec.”

“Xyro shot it.”

“You pulled it out of the SKY,” said Gabe. He handed him the kopi. His eyes were wet. “And then you went up the steps and Kestrel said first and you said good for you and she laughed.” He wiped his face. “Tif’s clip’s got four million views. It’s called GOOD FOR THEM. In capitals. It’s just you, at the bottom of a lake, reading the window, saying good for them, and going left. Over and over. On a loop.”

Ice drank the kopi. Too sweet.

“Tiger messaged,” said Gabe. “From the round town. He says the road’s lit. All ninety-four. He says tell Dec.”

“Tell him I said thank you.”

“And Hyv—“ Gabe stopped.

Ice looked at him.

“Hyv’s on the call,” said Gabe. “Bee’s call. With Thea and Xola. She’s been on it since eight. She’s crying. Bee says she’s been crying since the cavern.” He looked at Ice. “Bee says she’s fine. Bee says it’s the good kind.”

Ice looked at his phone on the shelf by the pod. Face down. Buzzing.

He picked it up.

He put it face down.

Beside him, Gabe, who’d been watching his face and saying nothing very carefully for about a minute, put both hands over his own face and made a sound like a man trying very hard not to cry, and failing.

“Dec,” said Gabe, through his hands.

“I know,” said Ice.

“You never—“

“I know.”

Gabe took his hands away. He looked at Ice for a long time, with his eyes wet, on the edge of his pod. Then he put his arm round Ice’s shoulders, the big heavy arm of a man who’d been his friend since a school choir and had never once asked permission to do that. And Ice let him. And they sat on the edges of two pods in the back room of a shoplot in Malacca in the morning, with the fan going round. Two cups of kopi, too sweet. A phone face down on a shelf, buzzing and buzzing.

Second.

He’d never been happier in his life.
