Live from Elsewhere · Volume 4: The Ninth Heron

Chapter 111

Nothing to Read

2,085 words · 9 min read

Suzu had read his mother her post every morning for as long as he could remember.

Not just post. Everything. Labels on medicine. The backs of forms. The number on the bus. The price on the fish. Which button on the lift. What the letter from the bank said, and what it actually meant. Which of the green packets was the good tea. When he was small he’d done it standing on a chair. When he was older he’d done it on the way home from school, and then on video calls, holding things up to the camera, and then, since November, from Malacca, on the phone, every morning at seven before he went into the egg.

He was good at it. He’d never thought about whether he liked it. It was just what his voice was for.

On Friday morning, at nine, on the eleventh floor of a hospital in Singapore, a doctor with a tablet asked his mother to sit very still. And Suzu stood by the window with his hands in his pockets, and understood that in a few minutes there might be nothing left for his voice to do.

She had a small thing behind her right ear now, under the skin, from Monday. And a pair of glasses on her face that weren’t really glasses. A thin black frame with a tiny camera in the bridge, like an eye between her eyes. A wire went from the frame down behind her ear.

“It won’t be like before,” said the doctor. Kind, quick, the same one who’d said eligible nearly three weeks ago. “Whatever before was. It’ll be light first. Then shapes. It takes the brain a while to learn what it’s being told. Weeks. Months, for some things. Don’t expect faces today.”

His mother nodded. She was sitting very straight in the chair, the way she always sat, with her hands folded in her lap. She’d put on her good blouse. He hadn’t said anything about it.

“Ready?” said the doctor.

“Yes,” said his mother, in English. Then, in Mandarin, without turning her head, to the window, where she knew he was standing: “Are you there?”

“I’m here,” said Suzu.

The doctor touched the tablet.

Nothing happened.

His mother sat in the chair with her hands folded. The doctor watched the tablet. The nurse watched his mother. Suzu watched all of them, and counted, the way Deston counted, because he didn’t know what else to do. Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight.

On nine, his mother’s hands came unfolded.

“Oh,” she said.

She didn’t say anything else. She sat there with her hands open in her lap and her face turned a little toward the window. Toward the light. The morning sun was coming in across the floor in a long bright square.

“There’s a light,” she said, in Mandarin, very quietly. “On the left. Big. Is that the window?”

“That’s the window,” said Suzu.

He found that his voice wasn’t working very well. He said it again, to be sure she’d heard.

“That’s the window, Ma.”

It was light first. The doctor had been right. Light, and then, over an hour, shapes. Dark against bright. The edge of the window. The door, which was a darker shape. The nurse, who was a shape that moved. The doctor asked her to point at things and she pointed, slowly, and was right more often than she was wrong. Every time she was right, the nurse made a small pleased sound.

Suzu stood by the window the whole time and didn’t move.

Then the doctor said, “Is there anyone in the room you’d like to try to look at?”

His mother turned her head.

Slowly, the way Hyvred had turned hers in the waiting room three weeks ago, pointing her whole face like a torch. Past the doctor. Past the nurse. To the window, and the bright square of light, and the dark shape standing in front of it with its hands in its pockets.

She looked at him for a long time.

He didn’t move. He didn’t know what she could see. A shape. A darker thing against the light. He stood there and let her look, the way you’d hold still for a photograph. And he found, standing there, that he couldn’t remember the last time she’d looked at him. Not toward him. At him. He’d been small. He couldn’t remember being anything else.

“Come closer,” said his mother.

He came closer. He knelt down in front of the chair, so that his face was where her face was, close. The way she held letters up close.

She looked at him through the thin black glasses with the eye in the bridge.

“Oh,” she said again.

Then she put her hand up. She’d put her hand on his face all his life, to know where it was, the way the auntie at the kopitiam in Malacca had held his face in March. She did that now, but differently. Not to find it. She knew where it was. She put her hand on it the way you’d touch something to make sure it was real, after you’d already seen it.

“You look tired,” said his mother, in Mandarin.

Suzu laughed. He didn’t mean to. It came out of him like something breaking, and then he couldn’t stop. He knelt on the floor of the eleventh floor of a hospital in Singapore with his mother’s hand on his face, laughing, and somewhere in it he started crying as well. He didn’t try to stop that either.

“Are you eating?” said his mother.

“Ma.”

“You look like your voice,” she said. “I always wondered. You look exactly like your voice.”

Afterwards, in the corridor by the lifts, while his mother was in the bathroom with the nurse, he sent two messages.

He’d promised the first one. He’d said yes on a terrace in a city with no name, to a girl with horns holding onto his arm. So he sent it first.

The same word from Ice as on Monday, the same four letters. Suzu stood by the lifts and looked at it, and knew exactly what it meant. He’d always known what Ice meant. It was the one thing about him nobody else seemed to understand. He never said more than the true thing, and the true thing was always enough.

They went home in a taxi. His mother sat by the window the whole way and looked out of it.

She didn’t say much. She pointed at things. A bus, which was a long dark shape. A tree. The sky, very bright, too bright; she shut her eyes against it and then opened them again at once, as if she were afraid it would go. At home she stood in the middle of the room. She turned slowly in a circle, with her hand out, not touching anything, just looking.

“It’s small,” she said, surprised.

“It’s always been small, Ma.”

“I didn’t know,” said his mother. “I thought it went on.”

He made her tea. The good one, from the green packet. He didn’t have to tell her which was the good one. She watched him take it out of the cupboard. When he turned round with it she was looking at the packet in his hand, close, with her head on one side.

“Green,” she said.

“Green,” said Suzu.

She sat at the table with the tea and looked at it for a long time. Steam, going up. She could see steam. Then she said, without looking up, “You’ll still read me my post.”

It wasn’t a question. He stood there with the kettle.

“You don’t need me to,” he said. “Soon. The doctor said. Big letters first. Then—“

“I know what the doctor said.” She looked up at him. At him, through the glasses with the eye in the bridge. “I like your voice. I’ve liked it your whole life. You’ll still read me my post.”

Suzu looked at his mother.

“Yes,” he said.

He sat on the floor of his room in the afternoon, with his back against the bed, and thought about the jar.

It wasn’t here. It was in the game, in his pack, at the foot of a grey hill at the top of the world, where he’d left it on Thursday night with seventy-four lights in it. But he could see it anyway. He’d been looking at it since December. Small blue lights in a sweet jar, pressing against the glass. Wanting to go north. Wanting to go home.

He’d told Sheng Yang on the heron road why he’d kept them. I’ve been her eyes since I was small. These have been in a jar since December because I couldn’t let them go.

He hadn’t said the rest, because he hadn’t known it. He knew it now, sitting on the floor of his room.

He’d never let go of anything. Not once. You couldn’t, if you were somebody’s eyes. You held on to everything. The number on the bus, the button on the lift, the green packet, the post. You held all of it, every day, for someone who couldn’t. If you let go, they fell. So you didn’t. You got very good at holding. You got so good at it that you didn’t know there was anything else.

And then you went into a game, and there were lights in a drowned church that needed somewhere to go. Every instinct you’d ever had said hold them. So you held them, for five months, in a sweet jar, and couldn’t put them down.

In the next room, his mother laughed at something. He didn’t know what. Something she’d seen.

Suzu sat on the floor and listened to her laugh. Then he got his phone out.

He didn’t wait to see the answers. He knew what they’d be. He put the phone face down on the carpet and sat with his eyes shut for a long time, with his mother laughing in the next room at something she could see.

When he picked it up again, there was one answer under all the others. Short, the way it always was.

At half past eight he walked to the old cinema on the fourth floor of the mall, where the eggs were.

He’d played there before, on weekends, when he came home. Booth twenty-two, on the steps where the seats used to be, with a curtain. The woman at the desk knew him. She said going in late tonight and he said yes. Then, because he’d never said anything else to her in five months and he wanted to tell somebody: “My mum saw me today.”

The woman at the desk looked at him. Then she smiled, very wide.

“Then go on,” she said. “Go and do something good.”

He went into the booth, and pulled the curtain, and lay down in the egg. He put his thumb on the plate.

At the foot of a grey hill at the top of the world, in the mist, he opened his eyes. The pack was on his back, and the jar was glowing through the canvas so brightly that it lit the hillside.

Four people were sitting in the grass by the door, waiting for him.
