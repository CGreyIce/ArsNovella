Chapter 186

The Floor

4,751 words · 21 min read

Ama Corrie opened the hatch for them at a quarter past eight, with her lamp.

“Downstairs,” she said.

“Yes,” said Ice.

“The three of you.”

“Yes.”

She looked at them over her spectacles. At him, with his shield and his sword and the lamp in his hand. At Hyvred, beside him, with her bow, very straight, looking at the hatch and not at him. At Bee, behind both of them, with her sketchbook under her arm and a pencil behind each ear and her wings folded down her back.

“Mind the third step,” said Ama Corrie. And then, after a moment, quieter: “Come up when it’s light.”

“We will,” said Ice.

He went down first. He held the lamp the way he always held it, a little out to the side, so the light went where the person behind him would put their feet.

The Seven Below was quiet.

He’d been down here every morning for months. He knew it the way he’d known the fen. The big round room of pale old stone, seven arched mouths going off into the dark, one under each road. The seven silver lines cut in the floor running in from the mouths to the ring in the middle, faintly lit, always, as if the stone remembered something.

Tonight they were brighter.

He noticed it as soon as his feet touched the floor. Not much. A little. The lines were glowing more than they ever had, silver going toward white, the way a coal glows when somebody breathes on it. Hyvred noticed too. He heard her stop on the bottom step.

“They’re lit,” she said.

“Yes.”

“They’re never that lit.”

“It’s the longest night,” said Ice. “Everything’s lit.”

He walked out into the middle of the room, to the ring where the lines met, and turned round. Seven mouths. Six of them went up under the six other roads, to the east and the west and the south and the three between. And one went north, under the north road. Past where the Delvers used to sleep, past the stream, to the cold place where the old stone met the new. And past that, under the old stone, still water.

“They’ll come to the lines,” said Hyvred, looking at the floor. She was standing at the edge of the ring now, turning slowly, looking at all seven mouths at once the way she looked at everything. “Won’t they. They’re light. They’ll come up the tunnels to the lines.”

“Some of them,” said Ice. “Yes.”

“From all seven?”

“From wherever there’s still water,” said Ice. “Hyv. Can you see?”

She looked. He watched her look. Her eyes, the ones that saw edge to edge, all of it at once, went round the seven mouths and down each of them into the dark, as far as she could see. Which was further than anyone.

“Six of them are dry,” she said slowly. “Mostly. Puddles. A cistern under the east one.” She turned to the north mouth and stopped. “Not that one.”

“No.”

“That one’s—“ She stopped again. Her tail went round her ankle. “Ice. There’s a lot of water under that one. Under the old stone. It’s like a lake.”

“I know,” said Ice. “That’s why I’m here.”

Bee came out into the middle of the room with her sketchbook open. She’d been listening. She looked at the seven mouths. Then at Ice. Then at the book.

“Six walls,” she said.

“Can you?”

“I’ve drawn uglier things,” said Bee.

It was a book of making. Bee had found it on the first day, in Heatherbrook, from Ambry the sign-painter. Draw it true, draw it whole, say what it’s for. Ice had watched her learn it on her stream in the winter. The first shield at thirty-four per cent, because she’d drawn the grain before the shape. The first wall, at forty-one, ugly but enough. She drew things, and they were there, for as long as she kept them.

She went to the east mouth and drew a wall across it.

He watched her do it. She stood in the arch with the book open on her arm and the pencil moving, fast, sure, not looking at the page. She looked at the arch the whole time, the stone, the shape of it. Then she said what it was for, under her breath: keep. And a wall stood up across the mouth of the east tunnel, pale grey, the colour of the pencil, the grain of it showing. It filled the arch from side to side.

“Seventy-seven,” said Bee. “That’s good. That’s the best I’ve done.” She went to the next one.

She did all six. South-east, south, south-west, west, north-west, north-east. Six walls across six mouths, each one grey and true and a little different. Bee drew every wall to the shape of its arch, the way you’d draw a portrait to the shape of a face. When she’d finished, she stood in the middle of the room and looked at them, breathing hard. She’d put a lot into them. You could see it.

“I’ll have to keep them,” she said. “All night. If something hits one, I’ll have to draw it again.”

“I know.”

“I can do that.” She sat down cross-legged in the ring with the book open on her knees and a pencil in each hand. “I can do that all night.”

And that left one mouth. The north one, open, dark, with the cold coming out of it.

Ice walked to it and stood in the arch.

Nothing came for a long time.

He’d known it wouldn’t, for a while. The dark came down the north road from the edge of the world. It would take hours to get here. The still water under the old stone was under the old stone. It would come when it came. Things moved away from where something was coming, and the Delvers had gone weeks ago. It wasn’t here yet.

So he stood in the north arch with his shield up and his sword down, the way he always stood. He waited.

Kei’s call was open in the corner of his window. All sixty, and Chernn. He could hear them. Not all at once; Kei had set it so you only heard the eye, mostly, unless you called the eye yourself. John’s voice coming down from the eye, two hundred and twelve steps up and then under the floor. The edge. The twenty-two. They’re going out. Higu: The carried one. It’s still there. The well, at ten, Milli’s calm voice asking for nothing. The blue door at half past eleven. Thank you, fountain.

He listened to all of it. He didn’t say anything. He didn’t need to.

At midnight, the dawn didn’t come. He couldn’t see it from down here. But he knew, because the window told him.

Hyvred was sitting at the edge of the ring, four paces from Bee, with her bow across her knees, looking at the north mouth past him. She’d been looking at it for three hours. He knew without turning round. He could feel it. The way you can feel somebody looking at the back of your neck.

“Hyv,” said Ice.

“Mm.”

“You don’t have to watch it the whole time. It’s not here yet. I’ll know.”

“I know you’ll know,” said Hyvred. “I’m watching anyway.”

He didn’t say anything to that.

After a while she said, not looking at him, very fast, as if she’d been holding it in for three hours and had to let it out before it went off:

“I wrote my name.”

“I saw.”

“On the list. In your three. Kei left it blank and I—“ She stopped. “I didn’t know if you’d mind.”

He thought about it. About a list on the board by the south door, at the second dusk on a Monday. And his name with two spaces after it, and in the first space, very small, much smaller than everybody else’s names, in careful upright letters. Hyvred. And in the second, big and loopy, with a bee drawn after it.

“I don’t mind,” said Ice.

He didn’t say anything else. He didn’t turn round. He stood in the north arch with his shield up and the lamp hanging from his belt, out to the side, the way he always hung it.

Behind him, he heard her breathe out. Very slowly, all the way.

In the middle of the ring, Bee, without looking up from her walls, said, “Good,” to nobody, and drew a little line on the north-east one where a crack had started.

At ten past two, Tiger said not yet a mile up the north road. Ice heard it on the call and stood very still in the arch. He didn’t say anything. At the north door, a quarter of a mile above him and to the south, the wall said it for him.

At twenty past two, the still water under the old stone began to move.

Hyvred saw it first. Of course she did.

“North,” she said. Flat, carrying, the voice she called with, not the one she talked in. “The end. The cold place. It’s coming up.”

He felt it a second after. The cold. It came down the north tunnel at him from a mile off like a wind, except there wasn’t any wind. The lamp at his belt flickered. The silver lines on the floor behind him went brighter. And up the tunnel, a long way off, in the dark, there was a sound. A sound like water moving where water doesn’t move. Like a lake breathing in.

“It’s coming up out of the water,” called Hyvred. “Under the old stone. Up through the floor at the end. Small ones. Lots. They’re— Ice, they’re coming down the tunnel. To the lines.”

“To me,” said Ice.

“To the lines,” said Hyvred. “You’re in the way.”

“Yes,” said Ice. “That’s the idea.”

They came all night.

Small ones first. Low and flat, ink on the floor of the tunnel, flowing. He killed them in the arch. It wasn’t hard. They didn’t fight him. They came at the light behind him, the silver lines, the way they’d come at the lamps on the road. He was standing between them and it, so they went through him. Or tried to. He turned them on the shield’s slope the way he’d turned leeches in the fen on the first day. And every one he turned went under the surface.

He didn’t let it go. Not yet. He held it.

Hyvred called them. Every one, from the edge of the ring, without stopping. Low. Left. Two. The wall. Behind the wall. Some of them didn’t come down the north tunnel at all. They found cracks in the old stone between the tunnels and came at Bee’s walls from the other side. And Bee, in the middle of the ring with a pencil in each hand, drew. A crack in the south wall: a line, keep. A hole in the west: three lines, keep. She didn’t look up. She didn’t stop. She sat cross-legged in the ring with her wings half open and kept six walls standing all night with two pencils. Ice thought it was one of the bravest things he’d ever seen.

At three, she drew something on him.

He felt it. A warmth on his shield arm, sudden, and a weight on the shield itself, as if somebody had laid a second shield over his. He glanced at the window.

“I’ve got a spare hand,” said Bee, not looking up. “I’m not wasting it.”

“Thank you.”

“Shut up and hold the door,” said Bee.

At four, he drank the first draught.

He’d brought twelve. Good ones, the bitter kind, the kind everybody complained about. He’d made them himself in the back of Joss’s smithy over the last fortnight, in the corner by the quench trough, because Kei had said you’ll be down there with no healer. And Ice had said I’ll have Hyvred and Bee, and Kei had said neither of them heals. And Ice had gone and made twelve Good draughts and not said anything else about it.

He drank one between turns. Bitter. Like Mr Lim’s tea, from Jonker Street, on the windowsill at Melaka Raya. Like what he made.

“You’re at sixty,” said Hyvred, from the ring. Not calling. Talking. “You were at sixty.”

“I’m at seventy-nine.”

“You were at sixty,” said Hyvred again, as if that were the important part.

“I know,” said Ice. “I know exactly where.” And it was true. He was on fifty. He could feel every one, every cold hit that had come round the shield’s edge in six hours. He knew where they all were.

He drank the next one sooner than he needed to. Because somebody at the edge of the ring had said you were at sixty in a voice that didn’t sound like calling.

At six, the big one came.

He’d known it would. He’d told the banner room. They get bigger as the night goes. The biggest come near the real dawn. Hollis had said it, on the first Long Night, on a plank at a ford. It’s not a long night till the dawn doesn’t come.

The cold changed first. It had been cold all night, a wind with no wind. At six it stopped being a wind and became a weight. Like being underwater. Like the whole of the lake under the old stone had stood up.

The small ones stopped coming.

They didn’t stop. They went back. Up the tunnel, away from him, away from the lines. All of them at once. Flowing back up the north tunnel toward the cold place, the way things move away from where something’s coming.

“Ice,” said Hyvred. Not calling. Not talking either. Something in between. “Ice. The cold place. The end. It’s—”

She stopped.

He’d never heard her stop. Not once. Not at the Elm Below, not at the Warden of Seven Ways. Not on the first Long Night at Brackenford, where she’d called every Unlit edge to edge for eleven hours. She saw everything. She always said what she saw.

She’d stopped.

“What is it?” said Ice.

“It’s the lake,” said Hyvred. “The whole lake. It’s standing up.”

He saw it at the end of the north tunnel, a mile off, in the dark, because it was so big he couldn’t not see it. It filled the tunnel. Not like the herons of night at the north door, tall and thin. Like water fills a pipe. It came up out of the floor at the cold place where the old stone met the new, and it came down the tunnel toward him. Toward the lines. Toward the Seven Below, and the hatch, and Ama Corrie’s stair. Toward the Hall behind the wall, and the banner room, and the chair by the north door.

It didn’t lean. It didn’t need to. It was the lake, and the tunnel went downhill.

It would come into the Seven Below like a flood into a cellar, and then up.

He stood in the north arch and looked at it.

He thought about the fen. About the first Long Night, a year ago, at the Long Ford. A plank over a deep channel. The Deep coming up out of the water at dawn, the biggest thing he’d ever seen then. And him on the plank, holding, holding, until he stepped back half a step off the pale new wood onto the old, and pulled. And the Deep had fallen forward off its legs into the channel. And the current had taken it down.

The current.

It comes up out of still water. Not out of running.

He looked at the floor of the north tunnel. At the groove that ran down the middle of it, a hand deep. The little stream. The branch of the one in the fourth tunnel, where Salty had quenched his sword in the dark and it had sung like a bell. Black water, running. Down from somewhere under the north road, under the old stone, under everything, south. Into the dark under the round town and away.

Running.

“Hyv,” said Ice. “Bee.”

“What,” said Bee.

“Can you keep the six?”

“I can keep the six.”

“I’m going up the tunnel,” said Ice. “To the stream. Where it’s deepest. Before it gets here.” He didn’t turn round. “Hyv. Will you call it?”

There was a pause. A long one.

“You’re not going up there alone,” said Hyvred.

“No,” said Ice. “You’re calling it.”

Another pause.

“Nobody alone,” said Hyvred.

“I know.”

He heard her stand up. He heard her bow come off her knees and her feet on the stone. And he heard her come to the north arch and stop, behind him and to the left, two paces back. Not four. Two.

“Then I’m coming,” said Hyvred. “To call it. Bee’s got the walls. Bee’s not alone, she’s got six walls and a pencil. I’m coming.”

He thought about saying no.

He didn’t.

“Okay,” said Ice.

They met it at the stream.

A quarter of a mile up the north tunnel, where the groove in the floor was deepest and the black water ran fastest, singing very faintly over the stone. He stood with his back to the east wall of the tunnel and the stream at his feet, between him and the west. Hyvred was behind him and to the left, two paces back, with her bow up.

The Still came down the tunnel at them like the end of the world.

It hit the shield.

He’d been hit by a lot of things. A Warden of Seven Ways from behind on his left side at twenty-two per cent. A thing with two hearts. Brann’s shield edge, three times, on purpose. Kestrel’s held draw, which he’d walked into. Xav, three times, in a round room, which had been the best thing that had happened to him all year.

He’d never been hit by a lake.

He waited.

He waited on the back foot. The way he’d waited for a year with his back to a heron-stone in a fen, for things to come up out of the water. The way Xav had drawn in the margin of a sketchbook: New. Weight forward, the step ready, and the back foot staying. A breath. So the step could go either way.

The Still leaned.

For the first time. All of it, the whole lake, leaned. Not left, not right. Down. Toward the stream. Toward the groove in the floor with the black water running in it. Away from the running water, the way the Unlit did, because they came up out of still and not out of running. And to get away from it, the whole weight of it shifted, for one moment, onto the side away from the stream. Onto its own feet, if a lake had feet. Off balance.

“NOW,” called Hyvred. “Ice, NOW—“

He’d already gone.

The back foot came round. Late, a breath late, the right way. He stepped back, half a step, the way he had on a plank a year ago, off the pale new stone onto the old. And he let it go.

The pull went into The Still like a hook into a fish. It dragged it toward him. Off its own weight, off its own feet, toward him. And between him and it, at his feet, was the stream.

The Still fell forward off itself into the running water.

It went in like the whole of the night going down a drain. The groove was a hand deep and a few hands wide, and the lake went into it, all of it, through a gap that size. The black water took it. Running. Down, south, under the round town and away. Somewhere under everything, a long way down, Ice heard it go. A sound like a bell struck under water.

Then nothing.

The tunnel was empty. The stream ran on in its groove, black, quiet, singing very faintly over the stone. The cold was gone. Not less. Gone.

Not four paces off. Not two. Beside him, against the wall, close enough that her shoulder was nearly touching his. She had her bow across her knees. She was shaking. Not much. He could feel it through the stone.

The egg took him. Halfway across the Seven Below, with Bee’s hand reaching for his and Hyvred’s on his arm and the silver lines going white under his feet. The dark of the egg came down over everything like a lid.

He lay in it, in the back room at Melaka Raya, in the morning, with the fan going round.

He didn’t open the lid straight away. He lay there with his hands on his chest, breathing, at fifty per cent pain in a body that didn’t hurt, the way you do. He knew exactly where every one of them had been.

Then a hand knocked on the glass. Twice. Gently.

He opened his eyes. Suzu was sitting on the edge of his own pod beside him, with his phone, the way he always sat.
