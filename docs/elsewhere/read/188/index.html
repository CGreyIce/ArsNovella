Live from Elsewhere · Volume 7: Keep the Lamps

Chapter 188

The Other Corrie

4,396 words · 19 min read

Gigi had her thorn across the gap when Kei said let her in, and for one second she didn’t move it.

Not because she didn’t want to. Because she’d been holding it there all night. Eleven and a half hours, at the left of the north door, with Thea’s tower shield on her right and the dark in front. Thorn, left. Lie. Hundreds of times. Thousands. Every time Thea’s shield turned, Gigi’s thorn went across the gap it left. And every time the shield came back, the thorn came down. Like breathing. Her hands had stopped being hers somewhere around three. They were the thorn’s.

Now Kei was shouting down the call from the top of the dome, it’s her, the carried one, let her in. And there was somebody walking up the north road toward them in the gold of the fire. And her hands didn’t know how to let go.

“Gigi,” said Thea, beside her. Not loud. “Gigi. Down.”

She let it go.

The thorn sank back into the stone of the road the way it always did, the way it had come. Thea’s shield turned, slowly, the whole left side of the wall swinging open like a gate. Xola stepped back out of the way on the far side, glaring at nothing, from habit. And there was a gap in the north door of the Hall of Roads. A gap, two shields wide, on the longest night, with a fire behind it, and nothing in front of it now but the grey road.

And her, coming up it.

She was so small.

That was the first thing Gigi thought, and she’d be ashamed of it later, but it was true. All autumn, since September, since twenty-two stones on the moor and a light past the last one that didn’t go out, Gigi had been thinking of whoever was carrying it as somebody big. Somebody like Rook at Elmcross, like Brannagh at Emberly, like the old guild in Ama Corrie’s stories. Somebody who’d walked into the black three hundred years ago and kept a light.

She was a little old woman.

Smaller than Chi. Bent nearly double over a stick. In a long grey coat, patched and patched again until there was more patch than coat, with the hood up. And in her other hand, held out in front of her at the end of a thin arm, a lamp. A small black iron cage, very old, with a hood on one side and a five-line star on the top. Inside it, a flame. Gold. Steady. Not big. It didn’t need to be.

She came up the last fifty paces of the north road in the light of the fire, very slowly. Leaning on the stick. Every step looked like the last one she had in her. And Gigi stood in the gap with her staff in her hands and watched her come, and couldn’t move, and couldn’t speak.

She stopped at the edge of the north door.

She looked at the gap. At Thea, on the right of it, with her tower shield turned. At Xola, beyond, still glaring. At Salty in the middle of the wall with a black sword the length of a man point-down on the stone in front of him, at nine per cent, swaying. At Azac on the far right with frost on his shield from rim to rim. At the fire, roaring in its iron basket behind them all, the biggest light anybody had made in the round town in three hundred years.

Then she looked at Gigi.

She had a face like a walnut. Brown and lined and very small inside the hood. And eyes in it that were bright and black and tired, the most tired eyes Gigi had ever seen. They looked at Gigi’s hands, still curled on the staff. At the place on the road at Gigi’s feet where the thorn had gone back into the stone.

“Thorn,” said the old woman. Her voice was very thin, very dry, like a page turning. “You held a gap with thorn.”

Gigi couldn’t speak.

“Briarwick,” said the old woman. “That’s a Briarwick thing. Lay the hedge. Dig the ditches outside it.” She looked up at Gigi’s face. “My grandmother’s grandmother’s mother walked through Briarwick once. Going north. She said they had the best hedge she ever saw.”

“It’s still there,” said Gigi. She didn’t know where her voice came from. “The hedge. It’s still there.”

“Good,” said the old woman. “Somebody kept it.”

And she went in, through the gap, past Gigi and Thea and Xola. Leaning on her stick, under the arch of the north door, past the great roaring basket of fire. She didn’t flinch from the heat. She didn’t even seem to feel it. Into the Hall.

“Go,” said Thea, very low. “Gigi. Go with her. Nobody alone.”

She went. And Thea came with her, and Xola, the three of them, the way they’d been all night. They left the wall to the other two threes, because the dark was gone now. It had gone down into the road like water into sand at seven, and there was nothing left in front of the north door but the morning.

The Hall of Roads at seven fifty-nine on the morning after the longest night was full of grey light. It was coming down through the eye at the top of the dome, getting brighter. Bee’s gold dots and silver lines on the old stone floor, sixty of them. The iron hatch under Ama Corrie’s stair, shut. The banner room door at the north end, open, with lamplight coming out of it.

And Ama Corrie, coming round the end of her counter.

Gigi had never seen her hurry. Not once in eight months. Ama Corrie didn’t hurry. She swept under, not round, slowly, on Sundays. She walked across the Hall the way the moon went across the sky. You could watch her go from the counter to the stair and back and never see her feet change pace.

She was hurrying now.

She came across the floor of the Hall with her lamp in her hand, nearly running. Seventy years old, at least, in her apron and her spectacles. Across Bee’s gold dots, to the small grey figure coming slowly the other way with a stick and a lamp of its own. And she stopped, a pace off. And the two of them stood there in the grey light under the eye of the dome and looked at each other.

The little old woman pushed back her hood.

Her hair was white and very thin, pulled back tight. Her face, under it, was the walnut face, brown and lined. And Gigi, three paces behind, with Thea on one side of her and Xola on the other, saw it.

She had Ama Corrie’s chin.

Not like it. It. The same small sharp chin, the same set to it, the one Ama Corrie pointed at things with when she wanted you to look. The same nose under the same eyes. Older. Much older. As if somebody had taken Ama Corrie and left her out in the weather for a hundred years.

“Is the Roll kept?” said the old woman.

Ama Corrie didn’t answer for a long moment.

“It’s kept,” she said. Her voice was not her voice. “Sixty names this year. And you’ve ninety-four on the road.”

“Ninety-four,” said the old woman. “Yes. I counted.” She leaned on her stick. “I lit them. Coming down. I lit every one of them again. It went out in front of me all night and I lit them behind it.” She was swaying. “I’m late.”

“Three hundred years,” said Ama Corrie.

“I know,” said the old woman. “I’m sorry.” She looked past Ama Corrie, at the banner room door, open, with the lamplight in it. At the north end of the room beyond, just inside the door, by itself, facing north. Toward her.

The chair.

“Is that for me?” she said.

Ama Corrie’s face did something Gigi had never seen it do.

“It’s been waiting,” said Ama Corrie.

The little old woman walked past her. Slowly, across the last of the floor, through the banner room door. To the chair, set apart, facing north, toward the door she’d come in by. She turned round, slowly, leaning on her stick. And she sat down in it. She put her lamp on her knees, still lit. And she let out a breath that sounded like it had been held for longer than Gigi had been alive.

The sun came up.

It came down through the eye in a great gold shaft, all at once, the way the game did dawns. It came across the floor of the Hall and through the banner room door. It fell across the chair, and the old woman in it, and the lamp on her knees. Gigi watched it touch the lamp, and the lamp’s little gold flame and the sun’s gold light were exactly the same colour.

And the egg took her.

She lay in it a long time with the lid down.

She was very tired. She’d been tired before, in the game, after the Burrow Mother and the hall of forty holes and the first Long Night at Briarwick. She’d been alone at the old gate gap then, with the thorn, all night, and no one beside her. She’d been tired then like somebody who’d been holding a door shut with their back.

This was different. This was the tired you were after holding something open.

She lay in the egg and thought about a chin.

She went back in at eight that evening, Malaysia time, the first moment she could. Everybody did. When she came out of the dark into chair twenty-one in the banner room, every chair was full. Sixty of them, and Chernn on her stool, and nobody making a sound.

They were all looking at the north end of the room.

The old woman was asleep in the chair.

Somebody had put a blanket over her. Ama Corrie’s, Gigi thought, the grey wool one from behind the counter. Somebody had taken her stick and leaned it carefully against the wall beside her. The lamp was still on her knees, her two small brown hands round the bottom of the cage, still lit. The same steady gold.

Ama Corrie was sitting beside her on a stool from the kitchen. Not sweeping. Not writing. Just sitting, with her hands in her lap, the way she sat on the window seat when somebody had done something that needed watching.

Gigi had never seen Ama Corrie sit beside anyone.

Nobody spoke. Nobody moved. Down the long table she could see them all: Kei at the head with his map, not unrolled. Higu beside him. Tiger with his head on the table, asleep, because he’d kept the last lamp on the road for twenty minutes and said not yet. Salty with a black sword across the back of his chair. Wendy with her head on his shoulder. Riverbank. Hana’s five. Thea’s six. Moonlight Harvest, at the far end, all nine, the first of the nine at the end, with Ice in it.

He was sitting very still, with his hands on the table, looking at the chair. He’d been under the floor all night, Bee said. With a lake. He’d run for the hatch at seven fifty-nine when Kei called, and not made it, and the egg had taken him halfway across the Seven Below.

He’d never seen her come in. He was looking at her now as if he were trying to see it anyway.

Gigi got up.

She didn’t know why. She just found she was standing. She went down the side of the long table, past Thea’s six, past Hana’s five, quietly, to the corner by the north door where Ama Corrie kept the broom. The fen-reed one, with KEPT on its tag in blue ink. She picked it up.

Ama Corrie looked at her over her spectacles.

Gigi didn’t say anything. She went to the chair by the north door, where the old woman was sleeping. And very gently, very slowly, under not round, she swept under it.

There was something under it. Road dust, grey, from the north road. And a little heather, dried. And a few small black flakes, like soot.

She swept them out into the lamplight and looked at them.

Then, because she didn’t know what else to do, she swept under all the others too. Down the long table. Every chair. Under, not round, with the old woman asleep at the end of the room and sixty people sitting very still in their chairs while she swept round their feet. Nobody said anything. Nobody moved. When she got to Thea’s chair, Thea lifted her feet without being asked, and Gigi swept under, and Thea put them down again.

When she’d done all sixty, she put the broom back. She went and sat down in chair twenty-one.

“Hm,” said Ama Corrie, very quietly, from her stool. “That’s five.”

The old woman woke at the second dusk, and looked at them all, and said, “Oh. There’s a lot of you.”

“Sixty,” said Ama Corrie. “And a stool.”

“Sixty,” said the old woman. She looked down the long table. At every face, slowly, the way Ice looked at a room. “We were forty. When we went.” She looked at the lamp on her knees. “That’s more than we were.”

She was very old. Close up, in the lamplight, she was older than anybody Gigi had ever seen, in the game or out of it. Her hands round the lamp were like roots. Her voice was dry and thin, but it carried. It went all the way down the long table and nobody had to lean forward to hear it.

“Who are you?” said Kei, from the head. He said it gently. Kei always said hard things gently.

The old woman looked at him. At the map rolled under his hand.

“You’re the one who made the map,” she said. “I saw it. The road. You drew a line. Ninety-four dots.” She nodded slowly. “I saw you from the edge, on the roof, with your little lights. You and the girl with the lamp and the boy with the mirror. I saw you every night.” She looked at Higu, and then at John, down the second table. “Looking for me.”

Higu put her hand over her mouth.

“My name’s Isa,” said the old woman. “Isa Corrie.”

The banner room was very quiet.

“There were two Corries,” said Isa. “When the old ones went north. Two sisters. One stayed, to keep the Roll, and sweep the banner room, and read the ceiling by the moon. And one went with them, north, to carry the lights.” She looked at Ama Corrie, on her stool beside her. “Your grandmother’s grandmother’s grandmother. And mine.”

Ama Corrie didn’t say anything. Gigi didn’t think she could.

“We went a long way,” said Isa. “Past the edge. Past where the lamps go. Further than anyone. We found the place it comes from, the dark. Still water. A great deal of it. And we set a ring of stones round a hill there, in the middle of it, and lit one lamp. A kept place. And we kept it.” She looked at the lamp on her knees. “This one. For three hundred years. Mother to daughter. Nobody ever let it go out.”

“Why?” said Kei.

“So there’d be one light in there,” said Isa simply. “In the black. That it couldn’t put out. So it would always have something to look at that wasn’t you.” She turned her head slowly and looked at Kei. “And so that one day, when somebody lit the road again, there’d be somebody out there to come home along it.”

Nobody said anything.

“We weren’t lost,” said Isa. “Everybody thought we were lost. That’s what my grandmother said they’d say. They’ll say we went north and got lost. We weren’t lost. We knew exactly where we were.” She smiled, a small dry walnut smile. “We were waiting.”

She told them the rest slowly, the way you tell things you’ve only ever told yourself.

Every daughter walked to the edge once in her life, to look south, to see if the road was lit. Every one, for three hundred years. And every one stood at the twenty-second stone and looked down the line, and it was dark. And walked back north to the kept place and the one lamp, and told her daughter. Not yet.

“Once,” said Isa, “one of us came all the way. To the round town. My great-grandmother’s mother. She was stubborn. She walked down the dark road on her own, four days, past every stone. And she came to the round town and stood in the market and nobody knew her. And she went to the smith and asked him for five bars of iron. And she bent them into a star, in his fire, with her hands. So there’d be one thing of ours here, in case.” She looked at the lamp. “And then she walked back. Because the road was still dark, and somebody had to keep the light.”

Gigi thought about Old Joss, in his chair at the back of his smithy, with tears on his face. My father’s father used to say he’d seen one of those made. An old woman from the north road, the last of somebody.

“And in September,” said Isa, “I was at the edge. Looking. The way we do. I was the last; there’s no daughter. I thought I’d look once more before I couldn’t walk any more.” She was quiet for a moment. “And somebody lit the twenty-two.”

Thea made a sound, very small.

“One after another,” said Isa. “Up the line toward me. Ten of you. I watched you come. A boy with a sack of lanterns. A boy with a map. The girl with a lamp. A girl with thorn.” She looked at Gigi. “And a girl with a shield who set the last one. With the thorn on her left. Not close. Near.”

Gigi didn’t look at Thea. She didn’t need to. She could feel her, three chairs down, gone very still.

“And then you went home,” said Isa. “And I sat by my lamp past the last stone and looked at the twenty-two. Lit, every one. The first time in three hundred years. And I thought: they’ll light the road next. Because that’s what you do, when you’ve lit the first bit. You can’t stop.” She smiled. “And you did. Three of you. Ninety-four. I watched every one come on. From the north. The only side you can see them from.”

Down the second table, Maigo had his sunglasses off and was wiping his eyes with his sleeve. Hako, beside him, was crying openly. Ina had his mask in his lap and his hand over his face.

“So I waited for the longest night,” said Isa. “Because it comes south all at once on the longest night. And the road behind it is the safest place in the world. It’s already been.” She looked at the lamp. “And I walked behind it. All night. And every lamp it put out, I lit again. So the road would be lit behind me. For whoever came after.”

“So the rest may sleep,” said Gigi.

She hadn’t meant to say it. It came out.

Isa looked at her. At chair twenty-one.

“Yes,” she said. “That’s the line.”

Ama Corrie got up from her stool.

She went to her counter without a word, slowly, at her own pace again. She came back with the old Roll in her arms, the black book from the ruined hall, swollen with damp, its last page readable now. She opened it at the back. Past If we do not come back, keep them anyway. Past the page after, where Curse’s label had brought back We go north to carry the lights. Set them out behind us, so the rest may sleep. Past the line she’d written herself in September, about a light past the last stone.

She took out her pen. She held it over the page.

Then she stopped, and held the pen out instead. To Isa.

“It’s yours,” said Ama Corrie. “The book. It was always half yours.” She paused. “Write in your mothers.”

Isa looked at the pen for a long time.

Then she took it, in her root hand, and very slowly, in a careful old hand that looked exactly like the old hand in the book from three hundred years ago, she began to write names.

It took her a long time. There were a lot of names. Mother to daughter, three hundred years, every one who’d kept the lamp. Every one who’d walked to the edge once to look and gone back north and said not yet. She wrote them all, one under the other, the way the Roll did it. And at the bottom, last, her own.

Isa Corrie. Came home through the north door. Kept the light.

She held the pen out to Ama Corrie.

Ama Corrie took it. She looked at the page for a long moment. Then she wrote one more line, under it, in her own small hand.

Kept.

And then Ama Corrie, who had kept the Hall of Roads for forty years, and her mother before her, and hers before her, sat down on her stool. She put her face in her hands and cried.

Nobody moved. Nobody said anything. It was hers.

Gigi went up to the roof afterward, two hundred and twelve, because Higu was there, and because she couldn’t stay in the banner room any more without doing something stupid with her face.

Higu was on the ledge with her lamp and two cups. She moved over. Gigi sat.

North, over the moor, there was no gold point any more. It had come home. It was on an old woman’s knees in a chair by the north door. There was nothing on the edge of the world now but the edge of the world.

“She saw us,” said Higu, after a long time. “Every night. From the edge. Me and John and Kei, with our lights. Looking for her.”

“I know.”

“I never thought anybody could see back.”

“Somebody always knows,” said Gigi. “Ama Corrie said. Usually it’s somebody dead.” She looked at the empty north. “This time it wasn’t.”

Higu laughed, wetly. Then she passed Gigi the second cup.

Gigi held it for a while without drinking. She was thinking about the north door, at seven fifty-eight. About her thorn across the gap, and her hands that wouldn’t let it go. About Thea, on her right, saying Gigi, down. Not loudly. The way you’d say it to somebody whose hands you trusted.

“Gu,” she said.

“Mm.”

“Thea said down. At the door. When I couldn’t.” She looked at the cup. “She knew I couldn’t. And she said it. So I could.”

Higu didn’t say anything. She just leaned her shoulder very slightly against Gigi’s on the ledge, and left it there.

Down through the eye, two hundred feet below, the banner room door was open, and the lamplight came out of it onto the floor. Through it, at the north end of the room, Gigi could see the chair. The old woman asleep in it again, with the lamp on her knees. Ama Corrie on her stool beside her.

And beside Ama Corrie, on the floor, cross-legged, with her tower shield across her knees, sitting watch, Thea.

Gigi watched her for a long time.

Then she drank her tea. It was hot.
