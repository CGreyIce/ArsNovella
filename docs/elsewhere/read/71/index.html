Chapter 71

The First Name

3,240 words · 14 min read

On the first of February the pod cafés went to twelve hours, and Gigi didn’t celebrate, because she didn’t celebrate things.

She just walked into the café on Monday morning at nine. She sat down in her booth. She looked at the little screen on the egg that said 12:00:00 instead of 6:00:00, and made a small noise that wasn’t a celebration. Then she lay down and went in.

Kei was fourteen. Higu was fifteen. Gigi was fourteen, and seventy-one per cent, and furious about it.

“You were fifteen on Sunday,” she said to Higu, on the steps of the Hall, at the game’s first dawn. “You walked here slower than me. You stopped to map a ruin. And you’re fifteen.”

“Sorry,” said Higu. “The ruin had a ghost in it. It was worth a lot.”

“I didn’t get a ghost.”

“You got a ring of thorn round a whole village, and a very rude old woman who loves you.”

“She doesn’t love me,” said Gigi. “She tolerates me. It’s different.” She took out the pocket watch, opened it, looked at nothing, shut it. “Kei. How long till you’re fifteen?”

Kei was sitting on the step below them with his notebook. He didn’t look up.

“Chris says Wednesday,” he said. “If I do twelve hours today and twelve tomorrow. Above my level, all of it.”

“Then we go above your level,” said Gigi, and stood up.

The barrow was on the moor road. Ama Corrie told them about it, the way she told them about everything: grudgingly, accurately, and as if it were their own fault for not knowing.

It was half a day out of Carrow Cross on the road to the north-west, where the land went up into heather and stone. A long grass mound on the ridge above the road, older than the town, older than the Hall. A low stone doorway in one end, lintel and two uprights, black inside. Things lived in it that came out at dusk and walked the moor road until dawn. Travellers went round the ridge at night, the long way, rather than pass it.

“One of them’s old,” said Ama Corrie. “The one at the back. My grandmother said it was there before the road was.”

Gigi looked at the three of them. Kei at fourteen, Higu at fifteen, herself at fourteen and seventy-one per cent. Four levels under. The bonus would be enormous. If it went wrong, the fall would be enormous too.

“It’ll go wrong,” she said.

“Probably,” said Kei. He was already walking.

They got there at the second dusk, with the sun going down red behind the ridge and the long shadow of the barrow lying across the road like something asleep.

Higu went first. She always went first, but not with her feet. She stood at the bottom of the ridge with her hand up and said ahead, and the light lifted off the end of her crook and went up the slope and in through the black doorway of the barrow. Into the dark. And Higu stood very still, with her eyes half shut, seeing from it.

“Three in the passage,” she said, in the voice she used on the lake on the longest night. Calm, low, like a weather report. “Small ones. Bone and old armour. They’re waking up. Behind them, a chamber. And at the back of the chamber, on a stone—“ She stopped. “Oh. That’s big.”

“How big?”

“It’s sitting on a stone chair with a sword across its knees,” said Higu. “It’s looking at my light.” A pause. “It’s standing up.”

“Okay,” said Kei. He took the long plain knife out of his belt. Then he took a small glass vial out of his coat and unstoppered it with his teeth, and let three drops fall onto the blade. Three. Measured, the way Ysolde had taught him. Not two. Not four. Poison and medicine are the same thing. “Gigi.”

“I know,” said Gigi.

She went up the slope to the barrow’s mouth.

The doorway was narrow. A lintel and two uprights, and a gap between them about as wide as two people. Wide enough that three small things could come out of it together and get round you. She stood in front of it with her hawthorn staff. She could hear them coming up the passage now, the small ones, a dry scraping sound like branches on a window. She thought about Briarwick, and the old gate gap in the hedge that had stood open for three hundred years. Granny Sloe on her bench, saying nobody ever thought to just lay the bloody thing.

She cut. Not with a blade. With the staff, a short hard stroke across the gap, the way you’d cut a hazel stem most of the way through and leave the hinge alive. And she said the word.

“Lie.”

Thorn came out of the ground.

It came up out of the turf on either side of the doorway and went across the gap, low, at the height of a knee, thick and black and growing. Briar and blackthorn, twisting together, laying itself down across the mouth of the barrow the way a hedge lies when it’s been laid properly. Not a wall. A wall would have been all or nothing. A hedge was a gap you could only come through one way, slowly, bent over.

The first small thing came out of the dark and into the thorn and stuck there, rattling. The second came behind it and couldn’t get past. Kei stepped up beside the gap, and the long knife went in once, under the old armour, and the small thing fell apart. He didn’t waste a stroke. He never did.

“Left,” said Higu, from the bottom of the slope, calmly. “The third one’s going over the top.”

Gigi saw it. She bent the thorn. She didn’t know she could do that until she did. She just pulled, with the staff, the way you’d pull a laid stem down into place, and the top of the hedge came down across the third one as it climbed, and held it.

“Kei,” said Gigi.

The knife went in.

The Warden came out last.

They heard it before they saw it. Not a scraping. Footsteps. Slow and heavy, coming up the passage from the chamber at the back. Old stone under old iron. Higu’s light came out of the doorway ahead of it, backing away, the way a person backs away from something they don’t want to turn their back on. Then the light went out of the doorway altogether and came floating back down the slope to Higu’s crook, and she said, very quietly, “Gigi.”

It came out of the dark into the thorn.

It was tall. Taller than the doorway; it had to stoop under the lintel. It was mostly armour, black and green with age, and inside the armour there wasn’t much. Something grey and dry, and two small cold lights where a face should be. The sword in its hand was as long as Gigi was tall.

It looked at the hedge across the barrow’s mouth.

Then it swung.

The sword came down on the thorn and Gigi felt it, all of it, through the staff and up her arms and into her teeth, as if it had hit her. The hedge bent. Black thorn snapped, a whole armful of it, and flew. She held on.

It swung again.

It’ll fall down. She thought it the way she always thought it. Clearly, the first thing, before anything else. It’s going to fall down. It’s a hedge against a sword the size of me, and I’m fourteen, and it’s eighteen, and it’s going to fall down.

And then the second thing, the thing she’d learned in Briarwick, from a very rude old woman on a bench.

So lay it like it’s going to.

She didn’t try to hold the hedge up. She let it bend. When the sword came down she let the thorn go with it, down and sideways, the way a laid hedge gives in a wind. When the sword came up again, the thorn came up with it, round it, into the joints of the old black armour at the elbow and the wrist, and grew.

The Warden’s arm stopped.

It pulled. The thorn held. It pulled harder, and the thorn went deeper into the joint, and the great sword hung there, caught in a hedge, a foot above Gigi’s head.

“Now,” said Higu.

Kei stepped in under the caught arm, close, closer than Gigi had ever seen him get to anything. The long knife with three drops of venom on it went into the gap in the armour under the arm, and out again. Once. Measured.

The Warden went still.

Not dead. Still. The two cold lights in its face flickered. The thorn round its arm held. And Kei stood back, and waited, with the knife held loosely at his side, exactly as if he were waiting for a draught to settle in a glass.

“What did you do?” said Gigi, through her teeth. She was still holding the hedge.

“Slowed its heart,” said Kei. “It’s got one. Somewhere in there.” He watched it. “Three drops. It’ll steady. Then it’ll stop.”

The Warden swayed. The lights in its face went dim, and bright, and dim. It tried to lift the sword one more time, and couldn’t. And then, very slowly, the way an old tree goes over in a storm, it went down on its knees in the barrow’s mouth, in the thorn. It knelt there for a moment, as if it were thinking about something. Then it went forward onto its face.

Kei looked at the window in front of him for a long time.

“Kei,” said Higu, from the bottom of the slope, very softly.

“I know.”

“You’re fifteen.”

“I know,” said Kei.

Gigi let go of the hedge. It lay down across the barrow’s mouth on its own, the way a laid hedge does when it’s done, and stayed. She sat down on the turf beside it because her arms had stopped working. She looked at her own bar.

Fourteen. Ninety-six per cent.

“Oh, come on,” she said, to the moor.

She got it the next afternoon on her own.

She didn’t tell anybody she was going. She went back up the moor road while Kei and Higu were in the Hall with Ama Corrie, and laid a hedge across a fox’s earth. A very big fox, with a lot of opinions. She got the last four per cent from it in eleven minutes, and lost a nail, and swore at the fox the whole time. And when the window came up she didn’t say anything at all. She just sat in the heather in the sun and looked at it.

Then she walked back to Carrow Cross, and didn’t hurry, and went up the Hall steps at the second dusk as if she’d been out for a walk. Kei and Higu were sitting there. Higu looked at her. Gigi looked back.

“Don’t,” said Gigi.

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You were going to say something.”

“I was going to say well done, Chi,” said Higu.

“Well, don’t,” said Gigi, and sat down next to her, very close.

Chris had the purse.

He’d had it for two weeks, in a sheet. Everybody put in what they could every Sunday, and sold what they made, and Ice sold them things at cost the way he’d said he would, and Chris wrote down every copper. On Tuesday night, on the call, he read out the total in his calm, slightly apologetic voice.

“Five hundred and four gold,” he said. “Twelve silver. Nine copper.” A pause. “The four’s from Newish. Don’t ask.”

“It was a drum,” said Newish. “I sold a drum. I’ve got another drum.”

“She sold her drum,” said Winter flatly.

“I’ve got another drum.”

So on Wednesday, the third of February, the eightieth day, with the cafés open for twelve hours and the call open in Kei’s ear, they went into the Hall of Roads at the game’s second dusk. Kei and Higu and Gigi, with five hundred and four gold in a bag. Ama Corrie was waiting at the counter with her keys and her spectacles, and a book.

It was the biggest book Gigi had ever seen. Bound in old black leather gone grey at the corners, with brass on the edges. Ama Corrie opened it on the counter with both hands. The first pages were brown and spotted and covered in writing so old Gigi couldn’t read it. Names, columns of them, in faded ink, one after another. Pages and pages. Then blank pages, cream and clean, for a long, long way.

On the first clean page, at the top, in fresh black ink that hadn’t been there yesterday, was the writ.

“Pen,” said Ama Corrie, and held one out. Black. “By hand.”

Kei took it.

He didn’t sign at once. Gigi watched him not sign. He stood at the counter with the pen in his hand and looked at the page. Then he looked up, past the lamps, into the dark of the dome, where the eye was open again and the stars and the lines were waiting for the moon. Then he looked at his notebook, open on the counter beside the book, with the map in it. Then at the two of them.

“On the call,” he said. Not to them. To his ear. “Everybody. I’m going to sign.”

Kei signed.

KeiKatamori.

His careful, upright hand. Small. He’d always signed small.

“One,” said Kei.

And from all over the world, one after another, like lights coming on in windows along a road: “Here.” “Here!” “Here.” “Here,” said Drew, on a road. “HERE,” said Tiger, beside him. “Here,” said Hyvred, somewhere on the way from Brackenford, and “Here,” said Bee, after her.

He held out the pen.

Higu signed second. higuVT. Careful, upright, like Kei’s. The hand of somebody who’d mapped nine sheets of a ruin by moonlight.

“Two,” said Higu.

Then she held out the pen to Gigi.

Gigi looked at it.

She looked at the page. The two names on it, alone, at the top of all that cream paper. Eighteen empty lines below them. Thirty days. Seven roads. Twenty people, most of them still a week away or more, most of them still not fifteen. A purse that had taken two weeks and a drum. Crimson Ledger, who’d filled theirs in an afternoon. Nightjar, waiting on the roads in the dark for people walking alone with lanterns.

It’ll fall down.

She wrote it the way she laid a hedge. Cut most of the way through, keep the hinge alive, bend it down, expecting the whole thing to come apart in her hand. It didn’t. It lay there on the page, black, next to the other two.

“Three,” said Gigi.

Afterwards, Ama Corrie unlocked the small door between two of the big ones. The banner room. The writ held it now.

It was a small round room with a stone floor and nothing in it. She swept it on Sundays, she’d said, and she had. Iron hooks high up on one wall, black with age. And under the hooks, on the pale stone, a rectangle that was paler still. A shape where something had hung for so long, so many years, that the lamp smoke had never reached the stone behind it.

Higu went very still.

Gigi looked where she was looking. Inside the pale rectangle, fainter still, so faint you’d only see it if you knew to look, there were marks. Small and round, like the ghosts of buttons. A scatter of them, all over the pale shape. As if something on the old banner had been stitched with a thread that kept the smoke off, in a pattern.

“Gu,” said Gigi.

“I know,” said Higu. “I know. They’re the same.”

She didn’t need the moonlight to see it. Neither did Gigi, now. The same scatter. The same stars. Whatever guild had hung its banner in this room, before Ama Corrie’s grandmother was born, had sewn the ceiling onto it.

“Oh,” said Gigi. “Oh, that’s horrible.”

“Horrible good?” said Higu. “Or horrible horrible?”

Gigi looked at the pale ghost of a banner on the wall of an empty room, and at the hooks, and at the place where theirs was going to hang. She looked at Kei, standing in the doorway with his notebook pressed against his chest.

“Both,” she said.

She sat on the steps on her own afterwards, at the edge of the round market, with the seven roads going away into the dark.

On the north road, at the milestone just outside the town, somebody was sitting. A narrow-faced young man with a bow across his knees, unstrung. He’d come in that morning, after nine days, and gone to the Hall door, and stopped, and gone back out to the milestone. He’d been there all day. He hadn’t come any closer.

Gigi looked at him for a while. Then she went inside, and came back out with a cup of something she’d made. It was horrible, and it was good for you, the way her things usually were. She walked across the market and down the north road to the milestone.

He looked up at her. He looked as if he was expecting to be told to leave.

“It’ll fall down,” Gigi told him, and gave him the cup. “Drink that. You look terrible.”

Then she walked back to the steps, and sat down, and took out her pocket watch, and opened it. It didn’t tell any time that mattered in here. It ticked anyway.

Three of twenty.

She’d been right about everything her whole life. Everything always fell down. She’d built hedges expecting it, and they’d stood. She’d walked nine days expecting the town to be a hole, and it wasn’t. She’d waited five hours on a step expecting nobody to come up the south road, and someone had.

She’d never wanted so much to be wrong.
