Day One Thousand

3,525 words · 15 min read

Ice had gone dark four hundred and thirty-one times in two and a half years, and the four hundred and thirty-second was the only one he’d ever planned.

Tif told him the number. Out there, in the kitchen at Melaka Raya, at a quarter past eight on the Friday morning, with the kettle on and the phone face up on the counter for once.

hyv’s ring was on PenguChi’s stream, wrote Tif. at the back. when everybody came up the road. she held it up and screamed HE ASKED. three hundred thousand people heard it on Chi’s stream

He looked at that for a long time.

idiot, wrote Tif.

There was a long gap. He watched the dots come and go.

good, wrote Tif. that’s how it should be. yours, and then everybody’s

thank you for being there, he wrote. you saw everything

i always do, wrote Tif. 4 years. idiot. And then, after a moment: congratulations.

Gabe was on the counter with his kopi. He’d been on the counter since eight. He hadn’t said anything. He’d come out of the egg and walked into the kitchen and sat on the counter and looked at Ice with his whole face.

He’d kept it for a day and a half. In here and out there. The hardest thing he’d ever done, he’d said, on the road, after, with his face in Rae’s shoulder.

He didn’t have to keep it any more.

“DEC,” said Gabe.

The auntie next door knocked on the wall before he’d even got to the end of the word.

Suzu came out of the guest room, a third awake. He took the tea off the counter out of Ice’s hand without asking and drank half of it.

“Morning,” said Suzu.

“She said yes,” said Gabe. To Suzu. Who already knew. “Out there too. She said both.”

“I heard,” said Suzu. “From the bottom of the road.” He looked at Ice over the cup. “Good.”

“That’s yours,” said Ice.

“I know,” said Suzu. “You can have it back today.” And went back to bed.

The phone went again. Not Tif.

come here

He looked at it.

i’m out here, he wrote. out there. it’s morning.

i know, wrote Hyv. i said yes out here too. remember

i remember

good, wrote Hyv. so come here. tonight. in there. the road home. i want to hold your hand

just my hand?

just your hand, wrote Hyv. i’ve done the arm for seven months. i want to see what the hand’s like for a bit

He stood in the kitchen with the phone and the kettle clicking off behind him and Gabe on the counter crying into his kopi. He was pink. He could feel it. He didn’t mind at all.

okay, he wrote.

They walked home in thirteen days.

Down out of the crater on the far side, west, onto the long white road. Fifty-nine and a sheep. The goose went ahead of them. It had walked off west from the notch at sunrise on the Friday, and they never once caught it up. Hyv held his hand the whole way. Not his arm. His hand, just one. She’d look at it sometimes, walking. At her own hand in his, with the black ring on it, and the small gold stone catching the light from wherever she looked at it.

She didn’t say much. She’d said the loudest thing of her life at the top of a crater wall. She said she thought she’d used up about a year’s worth of loud in one go. She’d be quiet for a bit, now, while it grew back.

He didn’t mind that either.

At the ferry, on the eighth day, the ferryman looked at them coming down the road and spat in the river.

“Fifty-nine,” he said. “And livestock.” He looked at Hyv’s hand in Ice’s. At the ring. He looked at it for a long time. “Four trips.”

On the west bank, Ice stopped at the door.

The ferryman’s door, covered in o7s. Thousands of them now. He’d cut five, over two and a half years, going across and back. He took out his knife and cut a sixth. Small. Not under the others. Next to the first one, from January a year and a half ago. That one had been on his own.

Hyv took the knife out of his hand.

She’d never cut one. She’d crossed with the ten, a year and a half ago in February, going to Masmark. She hadn’t cut one then. She’d said she was going to somebody, not coming back from anywhere.

She cut one now. Next to his sixth. Not under. Next to.

“You’re coming back,” said the ferryman, watching her do it.

“I’m going home,” said Hyv. “That’s coming back.”

The ferryman looked at her for a long time. Then at the ring.

“Hm,” he said. He’d been talking to Ama Corrie, Ice thought. Everybody had.

At the bend by the old stone barn, on the eleventh day, the goose was standing in the road.

It had got home first. Of course it had. It stood exactly where it always stood, in the middle of the road at the bend, grey and white and large, with an expression of permanent enormous offence. Looking at them come round the corner.

Patience walked up to it. They looked at each other, sheep and goose, a pace apart.

The goose looked past Patience at the fifty-nine. One at a time, from one end to the other, the way it had on the way out. Counting. Dennis, beside Ice, had his ledger open and his lips moving.

It got to the end. It looked at Ice and Hyv, standing together, holding hands.

It didn’t sit down.

It walked over. Across the road, past Patience, through the front of the walk, which moved for it. To Ice and Hyv. It stopped in front of them and looked up at them for a long time. Then, very deliberately, with enormous dignity, it sat down on Ice’s foot.

Nobody said anything for about three seconds.

Then fifty-nine people made a noise like a roof coming off.

“It’s sitting on you,” said Gabe, faintly. “Dec. It’s sitting on you.”

“It’s seen two,” said Hyv. She was laughing so hard she had to hold on to Ice’s arm, for once, both hands, the old way.

Dennis wrote it down. Day 11 home. The goose sat down. On him. He looked at it for a long time. Then he wrote under it, because it was true: It counted sixty-one. Me, and it.

They came home on the fifteenth of August. A Wednesday.

Tif wrote it out there in the morning, before the night, the way she always did for days with numbers.

day 1000

!day1000

1000 days since the egg. 21 on day 1. 21 still here. one goose, sitting on him

He came over the last rise in the second dusk with Hyv’s hand in his and looked down at the round town. The dome, low and pale at the bottom of the valley. The seven roads going out. The green pole by the fountain, bare, with something at the top of it that caught the last of the light and held it. And at the top of the dome, at the eye, a small gold point.

Kei’s lamp. On the Somewhere bench.

Still lit. Thirty-nine days, and nobody to light it but a kept wick. And beside it, a small shape in a grey blanket.

“Isa,” said Kotori.

“She kept it,” said Kei, at the front. He’d stopped on the rise and was looking at it with his map under his arm. “She said she would. She can’t see it.” He was quiet. “She kept it anyway.”

Tobi was in the gate.

Nineteen, very serious, with his crank crossbow on his back and his hands at his sides. He’d kept it thirty-nine days on his own. Kite stopped at the bottom of the south steps and looked up at him for a long time, and Tobi looked back. Kite didn’t say anything. He just nodded, once, the way you’d nod at somebody who’d held a thing exactly as you’d have held it.

“Who’d stand next to you?” said Tobi.

He asked Kei first, because Kei was at the front. Then all of them, one at a time, up the south steps. It took an hour. Nobody minded.

Ice came last but one, with Hyv. PenguChi behind them, last, with the sheep.

“Who’d stand next to you?” said Tobi.

Ice thought about it. Properly. Even now.

“Hyv,” he said.

Tobi waited. He’d heard Ice give three answers going out. He was waiting for the other two.

“That’s one answer,” said Tobi.

“It’s the first one,” said Ice. “The others are behind it.”

Tobi looked at him. Then at Hyv, beside him, holding his hand. At the ring on it, black and gold.

“Fair,” said Tobi. And let them in.

Isa was at the top of the south steps in her chair, under the grey blanket. Ina had carried her down from the eye when Kei’s lamp showed the walk on the last rise. Ama Corrie stood behind her with a hand on the back of the chair.

Isa couldn’t see them. Not from there. Not faces. She turned her face toward the sound of them coming up the steps anyway, the way she always did.

“The one under the floor,” said Isa.

“Hi, Isa,” said Ice.

“You’re slow,” said Isa.

“We walked.”

“Hm.” She held out her old hand from the blanket. Not to him. Past him. “And the one who sees everything.”

Hyv stopped. She looked at the hand held out. Then she let go of Ice’s hand and took Isa’s in both of hers.

Isa’s fingers found the ring.

She held it for a long time. Her thin old fingers on the black iron and the small stone. She couldn’t see it. She felt it, very carefully, all the way round. The way she’d felt the jar, and the lion’s nose, and the bench at the eye.

“Iron,” said Isa. “The kind that doesn’t shine. That’s quenched in the dark.” She was quiet. “And a stone. That catches.” She turned it a little on Hyv’s finger. “My mother’s grandmother had one of these. From the man who carried water. He made it from a nail out of the old hall’s door. Iron. And a stone from the still water, the only one in three hundred years that ever caught any light.” She let go. “She wore it on the hill for forty years. Next to.”

Hyv didn’t say anything. She couldn’t.

“Hm,” said Isa Corrie. And put her hand on Hyv’s head, between the horns, the way she put it on Suzu’s. “Good.”

Ama Corrie had the Roll open on the counter when they came into the Hall of Roads.

She always did. She looked at them, one at a time, the way she looked at people who came in through doors. At fifty-nine people and a sheep, home, with a week of the east road on their boots. She didn’t say sweep. Not yet.

She looked at Hyv’s hand last.

She looked at it for a long time. Ice watched her do it. She didn’t say anything. She didn’t say hm. She picked up her pen.

Then she turned the Roll to a page. Ice knew it. He’d seen it a hundred times. The page from the first winter, with a list of names in a dozen hands. Ice, near the top, in his. And a long way down, in hers, from a winter later, small: Hyvred.

There was a line under Ice. It had always been empty. He’d never thought about it.

Ama Corrie wrote on it. In her small hand. Not his.

He didn’t lean over to read it. He didn’t have to. She turned the Roll round on the counter so he could see. She’d never once in two and a half years turned the Roll round for anybody.

Asked, at the notch, at the second dawn out. Answered.

And under Hyvred, on her line, in the same hand:

Answered. Loudly.

“That happened in here,” said Ama Corrie. “So I can write it.”

She turned the Roll back round. She blotted it, carefully, the way she did everything. She closed it.

“Hm,” said Ama Corrie.

Then: “Well.”

Then, after a long moment, very quietly, to the Roll, so that only the people nearest the counter heard it: “About time.”

And then she picked up her broom and looked at fifty-nine people and a sheep with the whole of the east road on their boots.

“Sweep,” said Ama Corrie.

They swept. All of them, the whole Hall of Roads. Hyv swept one-handed, so that she could keep the other in his. Ama Corrie watched her do it and said nothing.

At the end, at the long table, at a quarter to eight, everybody sat down. Sixty-one chairs and a stool, full. Ash on her stool at the end, who’d gone to Ama Corrie’s counter after the sweeping. She’d asked something, properly, with her hands at her sides. And Ama Corrie had turned back a long way in the Roll to the first winter and put her finger on a name. A girl from the west, on her own. She’d come in through the south door with a day lost at a crossroads, signed, stayed a week, and gone on east to Masmark. Ash had looked at the name for a long time. Then she’d gone and sat on her stool, and Kite and Drew had sat either side of her, a sword’s length off each.

She’d got there. The girl. Ash said so, to nobody, at the long table. She got to Masmark.

Suzu beside Kotori, awake for the words. Xav’s September on the board under Suzu · the ring · Sundays. Gabe and Rae at no paces. Hana and Milli, who’d hung a door on a room above the dyer’s on Spoke Street on the Thursday after the roof came down, it turned out. It had been standing in a yard behind the Hart going nowhere. Now it went somewhere. It swung on two hinges with stars on them, for a hundred years if you oiled it.

And Hyv beside him. Not on his knee. Beside him, on her own chair, with her hand in his, on the table, where everybody could see it.

Two windows came while they were sitting there. For all of them, and then for him.

Kei stood up.

“Set one to watch,” said Kei.

“So the rest may sleep,” said sixty-two people and a sheep.

Hyv said it quietly. For once. She was still growing her loud back.

He said it with her. Not a breath late. Not a breath early. On time, with somebody next to him.

He went up to the eye after. On his own, for a minute, before the egg.

Isa was on the Somewhere bench, asleep, under the grey blanket, with her hand on the base of Kei’s lamp. She’d kept it thirty-nine days. Ina had carried her up every dusk. She’d sat beside a light she couldn’t see, knowing it was there.

He stood on the north parapet and looked out.

North, at the bottom of the sky, the gold point. The hill, with three hundred and one stones, and a hawthorn at two hundred and seventeen that had flowered in May in the right month. And thrift growing inside the ring on its own, Gigi said, between the ninth stone and the tenth.

West, the long road. Fourteen days to a crater with a city in it, and a bridge over the third stream. A man there learning to hear a tik before it came, with a woman with a slate counting. A man with two blades walking it in September to fight a man who sat down.

And here, below him. The round town. A green pole with an eye at the top. Forty-one squares of red on forty-one doors, one upside down. A door on two hinges above the dyer’s. A long table with sixty-two chairs and a stool. And a girl with a black ring and a gold stone, waiting for him at the bottom of two hundred and twelve steps with her hand held out sideways, not looking, at the height his would be.

He’d been at the far end of every room he’d ever been in. He’d stood up at one, once, in April, and said a thing out loud. He’d gone first at the top of a crater wall, once, in August, and asked.

Twice.

She’d say it was twice.

He went down the two hundred and twelve. At the bottom, she was there, with her hand out. He took it.

“Even,” said Hyv.

He looked at her.

“You went first twice,” said Hyv. “I went first about a hundred times.” She held on to his hand. “But you went first at the two that counted.” She was quiet. “So that’s even.”

He thought about it. Properly. The way he did.

“Even,” he agreed.

The egg took him at eight with her hand in his. Out there, in Malacca, on the morning of the thousandth day, he sat on the edge of the egg for a long time with his feet on the floor and his hand on his chest. Then he went and put the kettle on.

Two cups. Suzu would take one.
