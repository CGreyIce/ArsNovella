Live from Elsewhere · Volume 2: The Long Night

Chapter 30

Charter

3,551 words · 15 min read

The charter cost a hundred and fifty gold, and Xav had read it four times.

He stood at the long counter in the Adventurers’ Hall in Brackenford on Thursday afternoon with the paper flat under his hand and read it a fifth. Nothing on it had changed. He hadn’t really expected it to. It was heavy cream stock with a deckled edge and a red wax seal at the bottom, still soft, waiting for a thumb. The words were written in a clerk’s hand, very upright, as if the letters were standing to attention.

[ Charter of Company
An adventurer company may hold up to twelve travellers under one name, one banner and one purse.
The Hall will keep its name, its roll and its debts.
Fee: 150 gold.
The founder may not belong to any named party.
Name: ______ ]

He had a hundred and sixty-two gold, four silver and some copper. He’d had it since Saturday afternoon, when he’d sold every single thing he’d brought up out of the drowned church, into eight named contracts at Dec’s prices. I want cash, he’d said, by the heron-stones, and Dec had looked at him the way Dec looked at a back foot, and put it away for later, and not asked.

It was the fourth line he kept reading.

The founder may not belong to any named party.

“You’ve been in four times,” said the clerk.

The clerk was an old man called Penhallow, with ink on his cuffs and a pen behind each ear. He’d been watching Xav over the top of a ledger for some time, the way NPCs in this game watched you when you did something strange: politely, and with interest, and as if they’d tell someone about it later.

“I’m thinking,” said Xav.

“Think faster, son.” Penhallow tipped his head at the room behind him. “There’s a queue.”

There was a queue. There was always a queue at the Hall now. Twenty or thirty travellers, most of them new, most of them in Brackenford because they’d walked down to Fernwick to look for a door and walked back again, holding notices and party slips and arguing about level brackets. Two of them were looking at him. One nudged the other and said something, and he caught the word Sketch.

He folded the charter in half and gave it back.

“Keep it for me,” he said.

“For how long?”

“Till Wednesday.”

Penhallow looked at the folded paper. Then at him. Then he put it in a pigeonhole behind the counter, the top left one, on its own, and wrote something on a slip and tucked it in beside it.

“Wednesday,” he said. “Then it goes back in the drawer with the others.”

He logged out in the ring with nobody else there and came up out of the gel into the cold.

That was the thing nobody warned you about, playing ELSEWHERE from Japan in December. In the game it was always the fen, and the fen was always warm, and the water round your ankles in the heron-stones was warm as a bath. Then the lid lifted, and you were in a room the size of a pod and a half on the third floor of a building with thin walls, in a Japanese winter. The heater had turned itself off two hours ago to save electricity, because he’d told it to.

He sat up in the egg with his breath showing and reached for the hoodie he’d left on the end of it.

The room was the pod. That was all it was now. The pod along one wall, his futon rolled up against the other, and the low table folded flat against the wardrobe because there was nowhere to unfold it. Instant noodles on top of the wardrobe. A stack of lecture notes on the floor by the door, held down with a mug. The window had condensation on the inside and a view of the building opposite, eleven feet away, where somebody had a string of small Christmas lights round their window that went on and off, on and off, all night.

He’d bought the pod in Akihabara at eleven in the morning on launch day, after watching Dec log out of his swamp. He’d paid for same-day delivery and got it at eleven at night. Two men had carried it up three flights of stairs, swearing politely in Japanese, and stood it on its end to get it round the door. Then they’d looked at the size of the room and looked at him with a kind of pity.

He’d never regretted it for one second.

He turned the heater on and picked up his phone. It had been buzzing in the other room of his head all afternoon.

The poly group chat had been going for nine years.

It had started the first week of their first year, when Malcolm and Dec had decided, for reasons that had never been fully explained, that their class was going to be the most bonded class in the school. They’d made a group chat and added everyone and talked to everyone until it worked. Most of the class had drifted out of it since. Jobs, other countries, other lives. Eight of them hadn’t. Xav, Rui Xiang, Malcolm, John, Azac, who’d come in through the others on a Discord call and never left, and Ryan, and Val, and Jun Liang. And Dec, once. Dec was still in it. He just hadn’t said anything since he moved to Malacca, apart from happy birthday on the right days, always first, always at midnight.

Since Friday night it had been on fire.

He hadn’t answered that one.

He’d typed things. He’d typed about eight things, and deleted all of them, because every one of them was a plan. First week, everybody levels alone, nobody joins anything, nobody answers CL. Get to ten. Pick an intermediate town on the river, Brackenford or one like it, central, so nobody’s more than twelve days’ walk. Meet there. Charter a company the day the last one walks in, so CL can’t—

That was the problem. He knew exactly what he’d do. He’d known on Saturday, by the heron-stones, before Rui Xiang had even asked. He’d known when he sold everything. He’d known when he walked into the Hall on Monday for the first time and asked Penhallow how much a charter was, and Penhallow had told him, and he’d done the sum and found it came out at a hundred and fifty, and he had a hundred and sixty-two.

He knew exactly what he’d do. And the fourth line of the charter said he couldn’t do it and stay.

He put the phone face down on the futon and lay back and looked at the ceiling. Across the gap, through the wet window, the Christmas lights went on and off.

He called Deston at eleven, which was ten in Singapore.

Deston picked up on the second ring. He was in his room, back from Malacca since that morning on the six o’clock bus. He had his feet up on his desk and a bubble tea in his hand, and he looked like a man who’d been waiting for this call since Sunday.

“Have you told him,” said Deston.

“Saturday.”

“You said that last Saturday.”

“I said it on Sunday. Last Sunday. I said next weekend.” Xav rubbed his eyes. “It’s this weekend now.”

Deston drank his bubble tea and didn’t say anything. That was Deston’s trick. He’d always had it, for as long as Xav had known him. He’d just let a silence sit there until you filled it yourself.

“I went to the Hall again today,” said Xav.

“And?”

“And I didn’t buy it.”

“Because of the line.”

“Because of the line.” He looked at the ceiling. “Deston. We’ve been a party for five weeks. Five. I planned the ford. I planned the door. I’ve got a list of every guild under thirty members in four continents. I know where Kotori’s going to stand before she does. I know which way the fen’s lurker turns.” He stopped. “I’m good at it. I’m good at it here. With him.”

“Yes,” said Deston.

“So why would I—“

“Xav.” Deston put the bubble tea down. “What do you want him to say?”

Xav opened his mouth.

He’d been asking himself the wrong question all week. He’d been asking what would Dec say, and the answer was obvious, and that was what made it so bad. Dec would say go. Dec would say it immediately, without blinking, the way he’d sent the haul out into eight guilds’ hands instead of one. Dec would say it the way he said everything that cost him: plainly, and fast, so that nobody had to watch him pay for it.

The question was what Xav wanted him to say.

“I want him to say stay,” he said.

Deston didn’t say anything.

“I want him to say we need you. And then I’ll tell them no, and they’ll be fine. They’ll be fine. Rui Xiang can lead. Azac can lead. They’ll be—“ He stopped. “They won’t be fine. Malcolm’s right. They’ll be picked off one at a time. Jun Liang will be in a party with three strangers by Tuesday because he’ll have made friends with all of them by Monday. Val won’t answer anyone at all and she’ll level alone for a month and she’ll be brilliant and nobody will know. And Rui Xiang will try to hold the rest together by himself, and he’ll do it, and he’ll be so tired.” He breathed out. “And I’ll be in Fernwick with Dec, planning where Kotori stands.”

“Do you want him to say stay?” said Deston, very gently.

The Christmas lights went on and off across the gap.

“No,” said Xav.

“No,” Deston agreed.

“I want him to say go.” He laughed. It came out wrong. “And I want him to mean it, and not just say it because he says that. I want to know it’ll be fine. You and him and Suzu and Kotori and Gabe. That you’ll find someone who stands where I stand. That I’m not leaving a hole.”

“You are leaving a hole,” said Deston. “That’s what happens when people go. You can’t not leave one. That’s not the question either.”

“Then what’s the question?”

Deston thought about it. Then he picked up his bubble tea again.

“Whether it’s yours to fill,” he said. “Or theirs.”

On Friday, Fernwick was getting ready for something nobody could describe.

He’d logged in at dawn, the fen’s dawn, eight in the morning in Japan. He’d come out of the heron-stones with his breath still showing on the inside of his head, where the cold of the room hadn’t quite let go, and walked into town along the north walk. The whole town was doing things.

Not big things. Small, careful, fen-shaped things. The eel man had his boat upside down on two trestles at the end of the Landing and was tarring the bottom with a brush, slowly, all along the seams. Marta had a cart outside her door with two barrels of lamp oil on it and was rolling them inside one at a time, refusing help from three different travellers. At the militia house, Hollis had the rack out in the yard. He was going along it with a rag and a pot of grease, oiling the heads of a dozen old spears that Xav had never once seen anyone use.

Nobody had told them about the window. NPCs didn’t see the windows. But it was Friday, and the year turned on Monday, and the fen had been the fen for longer than there’d been travellers in it. And everybody in Fernwick was getting their lights ready.

Keep your lights. Keep your towns.

He walked the boardwalks for an hour with his notebook out.

He couldn’t help it. That was the trouble. He was drawing it in his head before he’d decided to. The north walk, five hundred paces of narrow planking to the old fen, with the narrows on the four tall posts where Dec had held Nightjar on Monday night. The Landing in the middle of town, wide enough for twenty abreast. The long bridge to the causeway, where everything from Brackenford came in. Sedge’s steps at the end of the north walk, where Rafi stood all day with the black sword on his back, too big for him, grinning at everyone. Three ways in. One way out, if it went wrong, and it was the causeway, and the causeway was long.

He drew it. Where the lanterns were. Where a tank would stand, if a tank had to stand somewhere. Where a healer could see all five without being reached. Where a man with a book could stand and count, and be heard by everyone.

“What are you drawing?”

Kotori was standing beside him on the Landing with her staff in both hands and her head on one side, looking at the notebook. He hadn’t heard her come up. He never heard her come up.

“Fernwick,” he said.

“For Monday?”

“For Monday.”

She looked at the page for a while. At the little marks. At the circle he’d drawn round the top of one of the eel man’s mooring posts, out in the channel off the north walk, the one she’d jumped to on Monday night.

“That’s my post,” she said.

“That’s your post.”

“You’re always drawing where to stand.” She said it as if she’d only just worked it out. “For everyone. You were doing it on the bridge, the first day. You drew where I’d be before I was there.”

“Somebody has to.”

“Ice says you’re the best at it.” She smiled at him, a little anxiously, the way she did. “He said it on stream, after the church. He said Sketch drew the whole thing and we just stood in it. Sorry. I wasn’t supposed to tell you. I think he wanted to tell you himself.”

He looked down at the notebook so she wouldn’t see his face.

“Thanks,” he said.

“Sorry,” said Kotori, and went back to her post.

Dec was on the north walk, teaching Rafi to hold the sword.

Rafi had made level nine on Wednesday night, standing on Sedge’s steps in the dark and killing whatever came up out of the water at him, one leech at a time, with the hatchet. At nine the Chorister’s Blade would let him lift it. He was lifting it now, on the boardwalk, in front of nine thousand people’s worth of ghost-chat. Dec was standing behind him with his own sword sheathed and his hands in his pockets, talking low.

“Back foot.”

“Boss, it’s heavy.”

“I know it’s heavy. That’s why you need the back foot. Don’t hold it up with your arms. Hold it up with the floor.”

“I don’t know what that means.”

“I know. Do it anyway. Look through it. Where it’s going, not where it is.”

Rafi brought it down. It was terrible. The blade went into the boardwalk a hand’s width from his own foot with a thunk, and stuck there. Dec didn’t laugh. He just reached past him and pulled it out of the plank and handed it back.

“Again,” he said.

Xav stood at the end of the walk and watched them for a long time.

Five weeks. He’d known Dec for nine years and he’d learned more about him in five weeks than in all the nine. Not the big things; he’d always known the big things. The small ones. How he stood. How he never made a sound at fifty per cent. How the first thing he did when anyone new walked up to him was check where their weight was. How every single plan Xav had made in this game, every one, had started with the same four words, without Xav ever having to write them down.

Dec stands here. Then.

The ford. Dec stands on the second stone. Then. The door. Dec holds the step. Then. Every fight, every walk, every night in the fen. He’d been planning around Dec for five weeks, the way you’d plan a house around the one wall you knew would never move. It was the easiest planning he’d ever done in his life. It was easy because he’d always known where Dec would be.

And the thing he hadn’t let himself think, all week, came up and sat in front of him on the boardwalk, and wouldn’t move.

I don’t know what I’d plan around, if it wasn’t him.

He wanted to know.

That was all it was. He wanted to find out. Eight people who’d known each other nine years, on the first of January, in eight different random towns, with Crimson Ledger in their DMs and nobody to tell them where to stand. A group chat that had been going since the first week of poly, that Dec and Malcolm had started by talking to everyone until it worked. Xav had been the one who’d kept it going after Dec moved and went quiet. It had been the poly lot’s thing, and then it had been, quietly, his.

He wanted to see what he’d build. Not because he wanted to beat anyone, not even Dec. Not instead of Moonlight Harvest. Just because he’d spent five weeks drawing where other people should stand, and he was good at it. He wanted to draw a whole map of his own and see what it looked like when everyone stood in it.

On the boardwalk, Rafi brought the black sword down again. This time it went where he was looking. Not well. But where he was looking.

“Good,” said Dec. “Again.”

Xav took out his notebook and turned to a clean page. He wrote, at the top, in small neat capitals:

FIRST WEEK. EVERYBODY LEVELS ALONE.

Then he looked at it for a while, and closed the notebook, and put it away, and walked down the north walk to stand next to Dec.

“Saturday,” he said.

Dec didn’t look round. He was watching Rafi’s back foot.

“Saturday,” he said.
