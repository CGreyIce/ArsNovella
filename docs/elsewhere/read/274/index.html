Live from Elsewhere · Volume 11: Three Hundred and One

Chapter 274

The Back

3,615 words · 16 min read

“Penguin,” said Ice, at the north door.

PenguChi kicked him in the shins.

He saw it coming this time. Of course he did; it had been two years. He let it land anyway. Not hard. She’d never once kicked him hard. He stood in the north door with his shield on his back and took it on the shin with a small oof, and looked down at her, very pleased.

“You’re not coming,” said PenguChi.

“No.”

“You’re always coming.”

“Not this time,” said Ice. “It’s Suzu’s.”

She looked at him for a moment longer. Then past him, at the five of them on the north steps in the grey of the first dawn, waiting. Suzu at the front, with his scythe and the empty black-iron case on his back, because he wanted it with him. Kotori with her tins. Gabe, enormous, with the Anvil on his arm, looking up the north road as though it might bite him. Salty, with the stone.

And Patience, the sheep, in front of all of them, on the top step, facing north. As if she’d organised it.

“Hi, Chi,” said Ice.

“Hi,” said PenguChi.

The stone was the size of a door.

Not a door. A table-top. Flat and grey and thick as a man’s hand is long, rough on the bottom and smooth on the top. Salty had gone down the fourth tunnel for it in February, with Wendy and the lamp on. Down past the black iron seams, to the mouth where the dark stream came out of the rock. And he’d chosen it out of the stream bed in an hour. He’d carried it up on his own, on his back, a hundred and something steps, in a harness Milli made him out of three hides and a cat’s-paw stamp.

“It’s the dark’s own stone,” Salty had said, at the long table. “Same as the iron. It’s been in the dark stream since before the round town. It doesn’t shine.” He’d put his hand flat on it. “The still water won’t mind it.”

Suzu had looked at it for a long time.

“Good,” Suzu had said.

Now Salty had it on his back on the north steps. The harness creaked every time he breathed. He didn’t seem to notice. He’d been carrying it round the round town for a week to get used to it, everywhere, to the Hart, to the forge, to the long table. Wendy said he’d slept in it on Tuesday.

“How heavy is it?” said Gabe.

“Right size,” said Salty.

They went out through the north door at the first dawn on the third of March. It stuck, the way it always did, and Gabe put his shoulder to it and it gave.

PenguChi went last.

That was her place. She stood behind everybody. She was a Sentinel. Somebody had asked her once, a long time ago, in Fernwick, who she stood in front of. And she’d said nobody, and meant it. She stood behind. With her long spear, at the back, where she could see all of it. Every back in front of her. Every foot. Every place a thing could come from that the people at the front hadn’t looked at, because they were at the front.

She’d stood at the back of Thea and Xola and Slep and Tif and Xyro across half the world. She’d stood at the back of a stone cross on a moor, and the Grey Lurcher had come out of the heather behind Tif, and she’d held it eighty-four seconds. She’d never once minded being at the back. You saw more.

She looked back once, from the first stone, before the road went over the rise.

The north door was shut again. But the little window at the top of it had a square of red under it, upside down, that Curse had put there. And on the north steps, small, in her chair, under the grey blanket: Isa.

Suzu had carried her out. Before dawn, before anybody else was up. So she could see them go.

She couldn’t see them. Not from there. Not faces. PenguChi knew that. But Isa sat on the north steps with her stick across her knees and her face turned up the north road anyway, the way she looked at things she couldn’t see. And at the bottom of the steps, Ama Corrie stood with her broom and didn’t sweep.

PenguChi raised her spear. Not high.

Isa couldn’t see it. She raised her hand anyway.

The north road was easy. Ninety-four stones, a mile apart, every flame standing.

Patience walked in front the whole way. She walked at exactly the pace she wanted, which was the pace PenguChi wanted. Everybody else adjusted. Gabe kept trying to go faster and then finding himself in front of the sheep and being looked at. Suzu didn’t try. Suzu never hurried anything he didn’t have to.

They slept at the old hall on the fourth night. On the stone benches round the walls, out of the wind, under the ghost of the star on the far wall.

Gabe didn’t sleep.

PenguChi knew. She was at the back, even sitting down. She’d taken the bench by the doorway with no door, where she could see the whole hall. And Gabe was on the bench across from her, enormous, with the Anvil propped against his knee, looking at the doorway. At the north. At the dark.

“You’re frightened,” said PenguChi.

Gabe jumped.

“I’m not—“ He stopped. He looked at her. He’d known her two years and she’d never once told him anything he didn’t already know. “Yes.”

“Of the dark?”

“Of the bit after the edge,” said Gabe. “The nothing.” He was very quiet, for Gabe. “Dec went through it. In November. Nine of them. On a rope, with Hako talking all night so they’d know where everybody was. And I stood in the north door at home on the longest night, and it came, and Rae came the last pace, and I held.” He looked at his hands. “I can hold a door. I’ve never been past one.”

“It’s not the nothing now,” said PenguChi. “Xola said. It’s just dark. With edges.”

“I know.”

“You know, and you’re frightened.”

“Yes.”

PenguChi thought about it. She did things slowly. She’d built a whole life on it.

“You don’t have to be brave the whole way,” she said. “Just the next stone.”

Gabe looked at her across the old hall.

“That’s it?” he said.

“That’s all there is,” said PenguChi. “Next stone. Then the next one.” She leaned her spear against the wall. “I’ve been doing it for years. On everything. Nobody notices. They think I’m calm.”

“You are calm.”

“I’m slow,” said PenguChi. “It’s not the same.” She lay down on the bench, her back to the wall, facing the doorway. “Go to sleep. I’m at the back.”

Gabe didn’t go to sleep. But he lay down. And after a while, Patience the sheep got up off the floor by the fire, walked across the old hall, and lay down against his bench. Right against it, where his hand hung off the edge. Gabe’s hand went into her wool and stayed there.

PenguChi watched him fall asleep about an hour later. Then she shut her eyes, because she was at the back, and the back could sleep once the front had.

The twenty-two were standing. Every lantern, every stone, from the old hall to the edge. And at the end, a little apart, in the hollow in the north face of a tall grey stone with a five-line star on its south face, Thea’s.

Standing straight.

PenguChi stopped at it. She’d known Thea for years. Out there, before. She’d been at the back of her across half the world, in here. She’d been at the back of her the night Thea stood at a stone cross on a moor and put her hand on the lichen and said nothing. She knew what this lantern was.

She put her hand on the stone, below the hollow. Not on the glass. That was Thea’s.

“Hm,” said PenguChi. Which was Isa’s. She’d borrowed it.

Then the edge.

It wasn’t nothing.

She’d been told. Everybody had told her. Xola had told her for about an hour at the long table, with her hands. It’s just dark. With edges. There’s a LINE. On the HORIZON. But PenguChi hadn’t been past the edge before. She’d been at the north door on the longest night, at the back of Gabe and Rae. She’d heard about the nothing from Hako, who did a voice about it, and from Hyv, who’d gone quiet when she talked about it, which Hyv never did.

She stepped off the road onto the heather past the twenty-second stone, and stood there, at the back, and looked.

Dark. Ordinary. The kind with depth in it. Heather going away north, short and dry, under a black sky. And low on the horizon, very faint, a line where the sky stopped and the land began.

An edge.

“Oh,” said Gabe, at the front. Very quietly. “It’s just dark.”

“Xola said,” said Kotori.

“Xola says a lot of things,” said Gabe. “She was right.” He stood on the heather with his shield on his arm. PenguChi watched his back from behind, and saw it come down, all at once, out of his shoulders. “It’s just dark.”

“Next stone,” said PenguChi, from the back.

He looked round at her. Then he laughed. Out loud, on the heather, past the edge of the world. It went away north into the dark and didn’t come back, and nobody minded.

“There aren’t any stones,” said Gabe. “Not for nine days.”

“Then next step,” said PenguChi.

They walked north for nine days. No rope. Five candles in a clump on the heather, and a sheep.

It was spring. PenguChi hadn’t thought that would mean anything past the edge. It had been three hundred years of dark here, the old woman said. The heather had never flowered. But it was March, and the middle was lit, and the dark knew where it was. And on the fourth day, at the back, where she could see everybody’s feet, PenguChi saw something under Kotori’s boot.

Green.

A shoot. In the heather. A small pale green thing, about the length of a finger. Coming up out of the dry brown stems, all on its own, in the dark.

She stopped. She crouched down by it with her spear across her knees and looked at it for a long time.

The others went on a few paces and then stopped too, because the back had stopped, and when the back stopped you looked round.

“What is it?” said Salty.

“Heather,” said PenguChi. “New heather.”

Kotori came back and crouched beside her and looked at it. Kotori, who’d made eleven tins of tea out of leaves that grew on the backs of the waystones.

“It’s never done that,” said Kotori softly. “Isa said. It’s never once come up green here. In three hundred years.”

“It has now,” said PenguChi.

They stood round it in the dark, five people and a sheep, looking at one green shoot about the length of a finger. Patience came and looked at it too. She sniffed it. Then she ate it.

“Patience,” said Gabe.

PenguChi didn’t say anything. She couldn’t. She was laughing too hard. She sat down in the heather at the back, past the edge of the world, with her spear across her knees, and laughed until she couldn’t breathe. And after a while Gabe sat down beside her and laughed too. Suzu, at the front, said good.

“Eat something green,” said Kotori, very quietly. Which was Leaka’s.

After that, she saw them everywhere. At the back, looking down. Green shoots in the heather, small, one every few paces, then more. By the seventh day the dark country was full of them. Not so you’d notice from the front. From the front it was dark, with edges. But from the back, where you looked at feet, the whole heather was coming up green, very faintly, in the dark. As if it had been waiting three hundred years to be told it was allowed.

Patience ate a great many of them. Nobody stopped her. It seemed only fair.

On the sixth night, Kotori made tea.

She made it on a fire of heather stems in a hollow out of the wind, with water from the last of the waystone skins. The leaves came out of the eleventh tin, the one she’d kept back since November. She poured it into five cups and a bowl for the sheep. Then she sat down at the back, beside PenguChi, which nobody ever did, because the back was PenguChi’s.

“Why did you come?” said Kotori.

PenguChi drank her tea. It was very good. It tasted of the backs of stones.

“Somebody asked,” she said.

“Suzu asked everybody. At the long table. Thirty people said yes.” Kotori looked at her over the cup. “Kei picked five. You asked Kei to be one.”

PenguChi hadn’t known Kotori knew that.

She thought about it. Slowly. The way she did.

“He’s at the front now,” said PenguChi. She looked at Suzu, on the far side of the fire with his back to them, facing north. The empty black case beside him. “He’s never been at the front. Not in two years. He was always at the side. Saying good.” She drank. “Now he goes first. He says things first. He said no to Ice.”

“I know.”

“Somebody should be at the back of him,” said PenguChi. “That’s all. The first time he’s at the front of something, somebody should be behind it. Who’s never once been anywhere else.”

Kotori didn’t say anything for a long time.

“He’ll never know you came for that,” she said.

“No,” said PenguChi. “That’s how the back works.”

Kotori laughed, very softly, into her tea. Then she leaned over and put her head on PenguChi’s shoulder for a moment, the way you would with somebody you’d known a long time, at the back of a fire, in the dark. And then she got up and went to make Suzu another cup, because he’d finished his.

On the seventh night, PenguChi saw an Unlit.

Only one. Far off, to the east, at the very edge of the candlelight and past it. A tall dark shape against the line where the sky met the ground, walking. Slowly. Not toward them. Along the horizon, going south. The way you’d walk along a road you knew.

Nobody else saw it. They were at the front, looking north.

PenguChi stopped.

She didn’t say anything. She didn’t move. She’d learned it in Fernwick, a long time ago, on her first day, at the back of a fight with something she couldn’t beat. The one thing a Sentinel could do that nobody else could. You held still. Not braced, not ready. Just still. So still that whatever was coming forgot you were a thing that could move.

It was a long way off. It didn’t turn its head. It walked south along the edge of the world, tall and slow, not looking.

PenguChi held.

The others had stopped, a few paces on, because the back had stopped. Gabe started to turn round. She lifted one finger off her spear, very slightly, and he stopped turning. He’d known her two years. He knew what one finger meant.

Two minutes. Three.

The tall shape went down behind a fold in the heather, far off to the south-east, and didn’t come up again.

“What was it?” said Gabe, very quietly, when she started walking again.

“Weather,” said PenguChi. Which was Tiger’s word. Everybody had a word of somebody else’s now.

Gabe looked east for a long time. Then he walked on, closer to the sheep.

On the eighth night, Salty took the stone off.

He’d carried it every step. Up the north road, through the old hall, past the twenty-two, past the edge. Eight days in the dark country with the harness creaking. He’d never once asked anybody to take it. He took it off on the eighth night because Suzu asked him to. He put it down on the heather, flat, with a sound like a door closing in a very quiet house, and sat on it.

“Dylan,” said Suzu.

Salty looked up. Nobody called him that. Only Ice, about once a year.

“You can rest,” said Suzu. “I’ll carry it tomorrow.”

“No,” said Salty.

Suzu looked at him.

“It’s yours,” said Salty. “The stone. It’s for you to set. I know.” He put his hand flat on it, the way he had at the long table. “But I chose it. In the dark stream. With Wendy holding the lamp. And I’ve carried it. All the way from the fourth tunnel.” He was quiet. “I’ve always swung things like they were too small. He told me. Ice. You’ve always swung it like it was too small. Everything I’ve ever picked up.” He looked at the stone under him. “This isn’t too small.”

Suzu didn’t say anything for a long time.

“Okay,” said Suzu.

And PenguChi, at the back, wrote it down in her head. A man who’d always swung things like they were too small, sitting on a stone the size of a table-top in the dark, saying this isn’t too small. She’d tell Wendy. Wendy would want to know.

On the ninth day they heard the still water.

Not heard. PenguChi was at the back; she heard the change. The others heard it first and stopped, and she heard them stop. Then she heard what they’d heard. The sound of the heather under their boots going a different way. Softer. Wetter.

And ahead, past Suzu, very faint at the bottom of the north, a point of gold.

The hill.

It was a long way off. Across the still water, black and flat and perfectly smooth, like a floor. Three hundred stones away. A small gold light standing straight up over a hill in the middle of the dark’s own water, where it had stood since the first of December. Where it would stand now on its own, Suzu said. Watching itself.

They stood at the edge of the still water, five and a sheep. The first stone was in front of them. Sunk a hand deep, very old, worn by three hundred years of feet.

Gabe was crying. Of course he was. PenguChi could see his back from behind. It was going.

“Next stone,” said Gabe. Before she could. To himself.

“Tomorrow,” said Suzu. “We’ll count them tomorrow.” He looked at the hill. “Out loud.”

PenguChi sat down at the back, at the edge of the still water, on the new green heather. She laid her spear across her knees and looked at all four of them in front of her, and at the sheep.

She’d stood at the back of a lot of things. This was the best one.
