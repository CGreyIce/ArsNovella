Live from Elsewhere · Volume 3: Constellation

Chapter 72

The Middle

2,244 words · 10 min read

Everybody had said it would be a waste, and for five weeks Niko had let them.

He didn’t argue with people. It wasn’t worth it. If you told Niko something was a bad idea, he’d say yeah, maybe, in the chill voice that made people think he hadn’t been listening. Then he’d go and do it anyway, quietly, for a very long time. Sometimes it turned out to be a bad idea. Mostly it didn’t. He’d been doing it since he was a teenager on a community server, where he’d met a boy called Ice who did exactly the same thing, louder.

So on the first day in Tallbridge he’d taken a sword and a book, both, off the rack in the little wooden room. And five weeks later he was level thirteen, slower than everybody, with two weapons that were each about half as good as anybody else’s one.

Just pick one, Newish said on the call, most nights. Just pick one, Niko, I’m begging you, it’s painful to watch.

“Yeah, maybe,” said Niko.

Tallbridge was a town of bridges over a hole with water in it, as Newish had said on the first day, and she’d been right, and it was the best place he’d ever been.

The gorge was so deep that the sun only reached the bottom of it for an hour at midday. The river at the bottom was white and loud and fast, and you could hear it everywhere in the town, all the time, like a held breath. The town itself was built along both lips of the gorge and across it. Seven bridges, one above the other, the highest so high that the spray off the river came up through the planks in the morning as mist. People crossed them all day with barrows and goats and baskets, as if there weren’t two hundred feet of nothing underneath.

And on every bridge, at both ends, there was a word.

They were carved into the stone posts at the bridgeheads, very old, worn soft. HOLD on some. STILL on others. WAIT on the highest. Niko had asked about them on the first day. The old woman who kept the bridge-book had looked at him sideways and said, so they’ll stand. As if that explained it. Her name was Wenlock, and she was the bridge-warden. Every morning at dawn she walked all seven bridges and wrote down in a fat leather book how high the river was, how far the mist came up, which planks had moved. She’d been doing it for fifty years. Her mother had done it before her.

She’d taught him the book. Not the sword. Nobody in Tallbridge used swords. The sword he’d taught himself, from a sheet that Chris made, and from watching a man in a swamp hold a narrow place.

Words that stand, Wenlock called it. You wrote it in the book, and you said it, and for a moment, whatever you said it to, it did what the word said. Hold. Still. Wait. Just for a heartbeat. Just long enough.

And the sword was a plain one, straight, single-edged. Every day for five weeks he’d stood on the second bridge and done Falling Cut until his arms shook. Then he’d sat on the parapet with the book and said words at the river until his voice went.

He was bad at both. Fifty-something per cent at the cut. Sixty at the words. He’d been those numbers for a fortnight.

“Pick one,” said Wenlock, every evening, when she came back across the bridges with her book. “You’d be good at either. You’re nothing at both.”

“Yeah, maybe,” said Niko.

It happened on Thursday, on the fourth bridge, at dusk.

He hadn’t been trying. That was the strange part. He was crossing the fourth bridge with his sword on his hip and the book under his arm, on his way back from the cliff path where the ledge-goats were, and something came up out of the gorge.

[ Spray Wraith · Lv 16 ]

He’d heard about them. Everybody in Tallbridge had. They lived in the mist at the bottom of the gorge and came up at dusk, when the mist rose. Long pale shapes, half water and half something else, that drifted across the bridges and took whatever they touched over the side. They’d taken a goat on Monday. A barrow on Tuesday. Wenlock had stopped mid-sentence on Wednesday when one went past the end of the third bridge, and written something in the book very hard.

This one came up through the planks in front of him like mist through a floor, and stood up, and it was taller than him, and it reached.

He did what he’d done every day for five weeks. He stepped back, set his feet, and brought the sword up and over. Falling Cut. Fifty-something per cent.

And because he’d been saying words at the river all afternoon, and because the book was under his arm, and because he was frightened, he said one.

“Still.”

He didn’t mean to say it on the cut. It just came out at the same moment. The word, and the blade coming down. Together.

The sword went into the wraith.

And the word went with it.

He felt it happen. The way you’d feel a key turn in a lock you’d been trying all day. The word didn’t go at the wraith, the way it did when he said it at the river. It went along the blade. Out of his mouth and down his arm and into the steel and into the thing, at the exact point where the steel went in. And everything around that point went still.

Not the wraith. The wound. The place where the blade was. The mist that the wraith was made of stopped moving round the cut, the way water stops round your hand if it’s cold enough. It held. The wraith couldn’t close round the sword. It couldn’t pull away from it. It just hung there on the blade, a long pale shape in the dusk, caught.

He looked at it for half a second.

Then he pulled the sword out and cut again, and said “Still” again, on the cut, on purpose this time. And again. Three cuts, three words, and each time the place where the blade went in froze. The wraith came apart in pieces, one held piece at a time, and drifted down into the gorge like mist going home.

He stood on the fourth bridge in the dusk with the sword in his hand and the book under his arm. Mist was coming up through the planks. Two hundred feet below, the river was white and loud. Nobody had seen it. He didn’t stream; he never had. There was just him, and the bridge, and the word.

“Yeah,” said Niko, to nobody, very quietly. “Okay.”

Wenlock was at the end of the bridge when he came off it.

She’d seen. She had her book open on the stone post with WAIT carved into it, and she was writing something, very slowly, with her mouth pressed tight. When he came level with her she didn’t look up.

“Said it on the cut,” she said.

“Yeah.”

“My mother used to say somebody would. One day.” She wrote another word. “She said that’s what the words on the posts were for. Not for saying at a river. For saying when something’s on the bridge with you.” She closed the book. She looked at him sideways, the way she’d looked at him on the first day. “Fifty years I’ve been saying them at the river.”

“Sorry,” said Niko.

“Don’t be sorry.” Wenlock put the book under her arm. “Be on the bridge at dawn. You’re going to teach me.”

The call came on at nine, his time.

It wasn’t Kei’s call. Kei’s call was at ten, all sixteen of them, and Niko never missed it. This was the old one. The four of them, the way it had been for a year and more, on Apex, with Ice in the middle doing something stupid. Since the first day in the game it had been on every night before Kei’s, out of habit, for an hour. Nobody had ever suggested stopping it.

“Fourteen,” said Niko.

“What?” said Newish. “You were thirteen this morning. You’ve been thirteen for a week.”

“I said a word on a sword.”

There was a silence.

“I’m going to need you to say that again,” said Newish, “in a way that makes sense.”

He told them. Not much. He wasn’t good at much. The wraith, the bridge, the word going down the blade. Wenlock’s mother.

“Told you,” said Chris quietly, when he’d finished. “Day one. Niko: sword and book. Going to make it work. It’s in the sheet.”

“You put he will in brackets.”

“Because you would.”

“Fine,” said Winter. Which from Winter was a speech.

“Where’s everyone at,” said Niko.

He knew. He always knew. He just liked hearing them say it.

“Thirteen,” said Winter. “Sixty per cent. I harpooned a seal.”

“Twelve,” said Newish. “Eighty. I played a drum at a bee. The bee didn’t care.”

There was a pause.

“Chris,” said Niko.

“Ten,” said Chris.

Nobody said anything.

“Ten and forty per cent,” said Chris. He said it in his calm, apologetic voice, the one he used when the numbers weren’t fun. “I can do twelve hours on Saturdays and Sundays. Four on weeknights, after work and the ferry. By my sheet I get to fifteen on the twenty-eighth of February. If I don’t die. If nothing goes wrong.”

“Something always goes wrong,” said Winter.

“I know,” said Chris. “I’ve got a tab for it.” A pause. “The writ ends on the fourth of March. I’d have to be fifteen and in Carrow Cross by then. Carrow Cross is eleven days from Ferrowmere, walking. I’d have to leave by the twenty-first.” Another pause. “Which is before I’m fifteen. So I’d have to level on the road. Which is slower. So—“

“So it doesn’t work,” said Newish.

“It works,” said Chris, “if everything goes right.”

Nobody said anything for a while. Niko could hear the river under his bridge, two hundred feet down, white and loud.

He thought about the sheet. Niko: sword and book. Going to make it work. (He will.) Chris had written that on the first day, in a café in an old cinema in Singapore, at the bottom of a list. Before Niko had even picked up the sword. As if it were just a fact.

“Then we go to him,” said Niko.

“What?”

“We go to Chris. Ferrowmere.” Niko was looking at the mist coming up through the planks. “All three of us. We walk to the Iron Hills, and we stand round him, and we fight things four levels above him until he’s fifteen. Like they did with Gabe in the swamp. Then we all walk to Carrow Cross together.”

“That’s—“ said Chris. “No. You’d be fifteen in a week. You’d be at Carrow Cross by the twentieth. You’d—“

“Yeah, maybe,” said Niko.

“Niko. It’s not on the way.”

“It’s not on the way,” agreed Winter. “Ferrowmere’s east. Carrow Cross is south. It’s the opposite of on the way.”

“It’s on the way now,” said Niko.

There was a long silence on the call.

“I’m coming,” said Newish, suddenly. “Obviously. I’m coming. I’m going to be so annoying about it. I’m going to tell you every day, Chris, that I walked to your ugly iron town for you. Every single day.”

“Fine,” said Winter. “Seven days from here. Maybe six. I’ll bring the harpoon.”

Chris didn’t say anything for a long time.

“I’ll add a tab,” he said eventually. His voice had gone strange.

“Don’t add a tab,” said Niko.

“I’m adding a tab.”

“Yeah,” said Niko, and smiled at the river. “Okay.”

He told Kei at ten, on the big call. Kei listened to all of it, the way Kei did, and then said only, “Okay. Four, together. I’ll put it on the map.” And on the map, Niko knew, somewhere in a drawer in Australia, a line would go from a gorge to the Iron Hills. Then another from a lake, and another from a grey sea. All three to one dot.

Afterwards he sat on the fourth bridge in the dark with his legs over the side and the book open on his knee. He wrote in it, in the warden’s way, very small.

Still.

Wait.

Hold.

And under them, because Wenlock said you could write any word that would stand if you meant it:

Together.

He didn’t know yet if that one worked. He’d try it on the cut tomorrow.
