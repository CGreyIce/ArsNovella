Chapter 116

Piece One

1,800 words · 8 min read

Eleven.

He got the eleventh pull-up on Monday morning at the gym, at a quarter past seven, with nobody watching. He hung there for a second at the top with his chin over the bar, shaking, not quite believing it. Then he came down and stood under the bar with his hands on his knees and laughed, quietly, at nothing.

Gabe would be unbearable about it. Gabe had been there for ten. You had a four face, Gabe had said, in March. That was a four face. You did ten with a four face.

He didn’t tell anyone. He’d tell Gabe at breakfast, and Gabe would tell everyone, the way he’d told everyone about Calvin’s egg.

Suzu was in the kitchen when he got home. He’d come back from Singapore on the last bus on Sunday night, after Fernwick, after Sedge. He was standing at the counter with the kettle on and two mugs out. The ones that didn’t match. He’d put the chipped one on his own side without thinking about it.

“She read a number,” said Suzu.

Ice stopped in the doorway.

“The bus,” said Suzu. “Yesterday. On the way to the market. The big one on the front, in lights.” He poured the water. “She stopped on the pavement and looked up at it for a long time. Then she said it. The number. Out loud. Before I could.” He put the kettle down. “It was right.”

He didn’t say anything else. He made the kopi, two, and gave Ice his, and stood there holding the chipped one with both hands. He looked at the window the way his mother had looked at the window on Friday.

“Good,” said Ice.

“Yes,” said Suzu.

Gabe came in five minutes later in a towel with his hair sticking up. He looked at the two of them standing in the kitchen not saying anything, holding mugs.

“Is this a car conversation?” said Gabe.

“Bus,” said Suzu.

“Bus conversation?”

“His mum read the number on a bus,” said Ice.

Gabe stood in the doorway in his towel. Then he came across the kitchen and picked Suzu up off his feet, mug and all, the way he had on the heron road. Suzu let him. Kopi went everywhere. Nobody minded.

“Also,” said Ice, when Gabe had put him down, “eleven.”

Gabe turned round very slowly.

“Eleven?”

“This morning.”

“Without me?”

“You were asleep.”

“I’m telling everyone,” said Gabe, and went to get his phone, and did.

Gabe’s three were fifteen now. Rachael had made sixteen on Sunday night on a hot hillside somewhere in the south, and told nobody, and Calvin had told everybody. Gabe said they’d be thirty by the end of May if they kept going like this. The middle of June if Calvin kept finding things that were asleep and waking them up.

“He’s better,” said Gabe, at breakfast, with toast. “He goes ahead and he comes back. Every time. He does it on purpose now. You should see him. He comes back so fast he overshoots.” He put the toast down. “And Rae. Dec. Rae’s going to be better at your thing than you.”

“Good.”

“I’m serious. She looks at a wound for one second and tells you what’s wrong with it and what’s going to be wrong with it in an hour. She brews like she’s angry at the pot.” Gabe looked at him. “She asked me if you’d mind. If she got better than you. At potions.”

“What did you say?”

“I said you’d be thrilled,” said Gabe. “I said you’d buy them off her. At cost.”

“I would.”

“I know,” said Gabe. “That’s why I said it.”

He streamed from Sedge’s hearth on Monday night. Nothing big. Draughts.

That was all he did, some nights, and the chat liked it more than they’d admit. Him at a table in a room on stilts over black water, with bunches of herbs hanging from every beam and the old woman grinding something across from him. Brewing. Moonbell, silverleaf, fenwort. The long slow quiet work of it, the measuring and waiting and pouring. Thirty thousand people watching a man make potions in a swamp at nine o’clock on a Monday, and nobody leaving.

Ice read it at the edge of his eye and didn’t say anything. He was measuring moonbell, a drop at a time. Kei’s town measured to the drop, and so did Sedge, and so, now, did he.

Then the alert went off.

It had a sound. Two notes, the chime every streamer’s alert made. He’d heard it a few thousand times now. Gifted subs, bits, the odd donation from somebody who’d been watching since December and wanted to say so. He usually didn’t look up. He usually said thank you without stopping what he was doing.

He looked up this time, because the chat stopped.

Ice put the dropper down.

He could send it back. He knew that. There was a button somewhere, or there would be if he asked Vivian. He could send it back, all thirty-nine dollars and fifty cents. Then she’d send it again, with forty arrows’ worth of capital letters. And he’d send it back, and she’d send it again, and in the end she’d be angrier than she’d been on the terrace in the white city, and he’d deserve it.

Or he could take it.

It sits on me, she’d said once, to Bee, about owing. He’d heard it secondhand. It sits on me like a— and she hadn’t had the word.

He’d put something on her that sat. He’d known it would. He’d done it anyway, because the other way she’d have sat on a list for a year selling arrows forty at a time. He’d decided that was worse. He still thought it was worse.

But it sat on her. And she’d decided how she was going to take it off. A piece at a time, out loud, with her name on it, where everybody could see. It was the only way she knew how to put a thing like that down.

So he’d let her.

“Thanks, Hyv,” said Ice, out loud, to the chat.

Across the table, Sedge looked up from her grinding.

“You’re smiling at your far-glass,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Somebody on it?”

“Somebody paying me back,” said Ice, “who doesn’t have to.”

Sedge thought about that.

“Then let her,” she said, and went back to grinding.

At the very end of the night, when the stream was off and the draughts were stoppered and stood in a row on Sedge’s table, the chat did one more thing.

He found it afterwards, in the VOD, scrolling back to check something. Twenty minutes after Hyvred left, somebody had typed a line he hadn’t expected. With a green sword in front of it. One of the only two people who’d ever had a sword in his chat.

Ice laughed out loud in the back room in Melaka Raya, at midnight, on his own. Jun Liang, who’d typed in his chat a handful of times in two years, one of them OUR LEADER IS NINTH, in capitals. Jun Liang had come out of the egg for his dinner and spent it making a counter, because Tif had said somebody should.

Then, because he couldn’t help it, he did one more thing before he logged off.

He opened the notes on his phone. He’d never used them for anything much. A shopping list from February. Vivian’s office hours. He made a new note, and looked at the blank page for a while, and typed one line.

HYV — 39.50.

He didn’t know what he was going to do with it. Nothing, probably. He just wanted to keep count of something she’d decided was important.

He looked at it, and put the phone face down on the folding table, and went home to sleep.
