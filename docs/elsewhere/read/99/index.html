Chapter 99

The North Road

2,947 words · 13 min read

Higu had four sheets of paper in an oilskin under her coat, and she’d checked they were still there nine times before they got to the north door.

They were the dome. The whole of the ceiling of the Hall of Roads, every silver line, drawn on the night the moon came through the eye. She’d drawn them with Gigi holding the lantern and Kei calling out the stars, and nobody had breathed for an hour. She’d copied them twice since, on better paper. The copies were in Kei’s pack. The first ones were under her coat, against her ribs, because they were the ones she’d drawn while it was happening. She didn’t trust anything she hadn’t seen happen.

She did, slightly. She didn’t say so.

They went out through the north door at the second dawn on Tuesday. Nine of them. Kei, with his map rolled under his arm and his long knife at his hip. Gigi with her hawthorn staff, already saying it would rain. Drew, with his iron knuckles in his pocket and his hands loose. Tiger, who had already fallen in the fountain on the way to the door. Maigo, very loud, with a staff and a pair of pixel sunglasses pushed up on his head. Salty, silent, with his greatsword. Hako, with his spear, narrating. And Rafi, the boy from the fen, with a black sword that hummed, walking at the back the way new people walked, a little too close to everyone, smiling at nothing.

The north door didn’t stick. Gabe had fixed it. Higu still pulled it the way Ama Corrie had taught them, because some habits were worth keeping even after the reason went.

Up on the dome, the weathercock pointed north.

“And here,” said Hako, in his low documentary voice, as the nine of them filed out into the morning, “we see the guild, leaving its burrow. Note the alert posture of the scout. The anxious grooming of the cartographer—“

“I’m not grooming.”

“—the cartographer, who is checking her oilskin for the tenth time—“

“Hako.”

“—and in the rear, the juvenile, newly admitted to the colony, who has no idea what he has got himself into.”

“I’ve got some idea,” said Rafi.

“He does not,” said Hako, kindly.

The north road ran out on the first afternoon.

It didn’t stop. It just got thinner. First it was a road, with ruts and milestones. Then the milestone where Kite had sat for a week in February, with a cup of Gigi’s tea, not coming closer. Then a track. Then a path through heather, and then nothing. Just a long brown moor going up into a long grey sky, with the wind going across it the way wind went across places nobody lived.

And a stone.

It stood on its own in the heather, about as tall as Gigi. Old grey stone gone green with lichen on the north side. Somebody had cut a star into the face of it, a small one, five lines. And under the star, a single straight groove, as long as Higu’s forearm, pointing north.

Higu stopped.

She took the oilskin out from under her coat. Nine checks, and she’d never once got it out on the road, not even to look. She unfolded the first sheet in the wind, with Gigi holding the other corner without being asked. She found Carrow Cross, the round town, and the north door. She found the silver line that ran out of the north door across the dome, straight up to the big gold star at the top, off every map.

She looked at the line on the paper. She looked at the stone in the heather. She looked from the stone back to Carrow Cross, a grey smudge in the south. Then north, up the moor, where the groove under the star was pointing.

“Chi,” she said.

“I know,” said Gigi. “I can see.”

The stone was exactly on the line.

They found the second one an hour later. Then a third. Every few miles, on its own in the heather, the same small star, the same groove pointing north. Higu stopped at every one, and held up the sheet, and every one was on the line. Exactly on it. Not near it. On it, as if somebody had laid the dome flat on the land and pushed a pin through every star.

She drew them as they went. Stone one, stone two, stone three. Distances. The angle of the groove. She knew she was being slow. Nobody said anything about it. Kei walked beside her at her pace and said nothing at all, which from Kei was a whole speech.

“It’s a road,” said Rafi, at the fourth stone, looking at it. “Like the herons. The swamp guy’s herons, up north of the fen. One every mile, so you know which way to go.”

Everybody looked at him.

“I watched,” said Rafi, a bit defensively. “Every night. It’s the same. Somebody made a road you’d only see if you knew to look.”

Higu wrote that down too. Rafi: like the herons.

The light went at twenty past eleven, her time, and there was no town.

They’d known there wouldn’t be. Kei had said so in the Hall: four days, nothing on the map, we sleep where we stop. Everybody had nodded. Everybody had been on the Long Night. Everybody knew what came up in the dark in places where nobody lived.

They made a ring.

Nobody told them to. They just did it, the way they’d done it on the Long Night in six different towns, the way they’d all learned it the hard way. Lanterns in a circle on the heather, as close as they could get them. Everyone inside. Backs to the middle. Higu in the middle, with her lantern staff, because her light went further than anyone’s.

“It’s going to be horrible,” said Gigi.

“Probably,” said Kei.

It was.

They came up out of the moor pools at half past eleven. Higu didn’t see the first one. She felt it, a cold on the left side of her face as if somebody had opened a door. When she turned her head there was a shape standing at the edge of the light, just outside the nearest lantern. Tall and thin, the colour of the dark behind it, with its head bent down. It was looking at the lantern.

Then there were nine of them. Then twenty. Standing round the ring in the heather, just outside the light, heads bent. All looking at the lanterns.

“Oh, no,” said Maigo, with great feeling.

The first one bent over the north lantern. It closed its hands round it, slowly, the way you’d cup a moth. And the lantern went out.

“Ahead,” said Higu.

She lifted her hand and sent her light out of the lantern on her staff. It went out of the ring like a thrown coin, over the heather, bright and small, and she saw everything it saw. Twenty-two of them. Six more coming up out of the pool to the east. And the place where the north lantern had been, dark now. And the gap it made in the ring.

“North,” she said. “Two. Coming in.”

“Mine,” said Drew.

He went to the gap. He didn’t hurry. He never hurried. He stood in the dark where the lantern had been, with his iron knuckles on and his hands loose and low. When the first Unlit came through the gap with its long arms reaching, he let it show him what it was going to do. Then he wasn’t there. He was a half-step to the side, and inside its reach, and his right hand went up into the place under where its chin would have been. It came apart into black water all down the front of him.

The second one came through behind it. Drew was already waiting.

“Lie,” said Gigi, on the east side.

Thorn came up out of the heather where her staff touched it, black and green, and laid itself across the gap between two lanterns like a hedge going down. The three Unlit coming from the east pool walked into it and bent over it. They couldn’t get past without bending, and Salty was there with the greatsword. He didn’t make a sound. He never did. He just swung, low and flat, once, and the three of them went into the heather as black water.

Tiger went over the thorn and out into the dark. Higu’s stomach went with him. But he came back. He skimmed out along the line of them where they stood bent over the lanterns and cut, cut, cut, two short swords, and came back over the thorn before any of them had straightened up. He was grinning. He’d fallen over twice.

Kei was on the west side with his long knife, very calm. He didn’t kill anything. He just went to one that had its hands round the west lantern and put the knife into it once, three drops, and stepped back. It stood there with its hands round the lantern for a long moment. Then it let go, and the lantern was still lit, and the Unlit went down slowly into the heather as if it had decided to sit.

“Steadied,” said Kei, to nobody.

And Rafi.

Rafi had been told to stand by Higu and not do anything clever. He was standing by Higu, with the black sword up. When an Unlit came over the south lanterns, through a gap nobody had seen, straight for the middle of the ring where Higu stood with her eyes shut and her light out on the moor, Rafi stepped in front of her.

The black sword hummed. Higu heard it, even with her eyes shut. A low note, steady, like a choir a long way off, keeping time.

The Unlit reached for her lantern. Rafi swung on the hum. Not fast. On the beat, the way somebody had taught him on a set of steps in a fen. Hold it up with the floor. The black sword went through the Unlit from shoulder to hip. It came apart, and the black water went all over Higu’s boots, and Rafi stood there breathing hard with the sword still humming.

“Sorry,” he said. “About your boots.”

“Ahead,” said Higu, and sent her light out again, because there were more.

It lasted forty minutes.

Maigo kept them up the whole time. He went round the inside of the ring with his staff, saying hold to every cut and bruise as if he were telling a dog to sit. Every wound he said it to stayed shut. Hako narrated the whole fight in a whisper (“the colony defends the perimeter… a remarkable display of cooperation…”) until Gigi told him to stop. Then he narrated that.

At ten past twelve the last of them went down into the heather by the north lantern. Drew relit the lantern, carefully, with his iron knuckles still on, as if he were lighting a candle on a cake.

Nobody said anything for a while. They stood in their ring on the moor, breathing, with the wind going across the heather and the lanterns hissing.

“We can’t do that every night,” said Kei, eventually.

“No,” said Drew.

“The café shuts at one.”

“And if we log out here,” said Gigi, “our bodies stay here. In the dark. With those.” She looked out at the moor. “So we’re going to have to stay up till dawn. Which is three in the morning. On a Tuesday.”

Higu didn’t answer. She had her eyes shut. She’d sent her light out again, north, up the line, as far as it would go. A long way, further than she’d ever sent it, because she’d stopped needing to fight and she could put all of herself into the going.

It went over the heather in the dark. Past the fifth stone, with its star and its groove pointing north. Up a rise. And on the far side of the rise, three hundred paces from where they stood, it found something.

Posts.

Iron posts, black with age, standing in the heather. Twelve of them in a circle, each about as tall as a man. Each with a little iron cage on top, the kind you’d hang a lantern in, empty, rusted open. A ring. Somebody’s ring, a long time ago. In the exact middle of it, a flat stone, and on the flat stone, cut deep, a star.

Higu opened her eyes.

“There’s a ring,” she said. “Up the line. Three hundred paces. Somebody built one.”

Tiger lit it.

He took everybody’s spare lanterns and ran up the rise in the dark with Higu’s light going ahead of him, and hung one in every cage. He went round all twelve in less than a minute, the way he’d once run the lamps for a whole canal town. The ring came up out of the dark one post at a time, gold light on black iron, until it was a circle of light on top of a hill on a moor at the top of the world.

They walked up into it.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then Gigi sat down on the flat stone in the middle with the star cut in it. She looked round at the twelve posts with their lanterns in, the cages rusted open and lit again for the first time in nobody knew how long.

“Oh, that’s horrible,” she said, very quietly.

“Horrible good?” said Kei. “Or—“

“Both,” said Gigi. “Somebody walked up here with twelve iron posts and planted them in a circle on a hill. So the next lot would have somewhere to sleep.” She looked at the star. “Somebody knew.”

Higu was drawing it already. Twelve posts, a stone, a star. And under it, in small letters: a day’s walk from Carrow Cross. Exactly.

There was another one the next night, a day’s walk on. And another the night after. Every one exactly where you’d stop if you’d walked all day up the line. Every one with twelve posts and a stone and a star, and cages rusted open, waiting.

Tiger lit them. It became his job. He’d run on ahead at dusk with the spare lanterns while the rest of them walked up, and by the time they got there the ring would be standing gold on its hill. Every night it felt like coming home to a house somebody had left the lights on in.

On Thursday night, in the third ring, Higu turned her stream back on. She’d had it dark for two days.

“I’ll tell you when it’s time,” said Higu. “Not yet. But soon.”

On Friday, the fourth evening, the land went up. A long slow climb out of the moor to a ridge, with the stones getting closer together the higher they went. Every half mile, then every quarter, as if whoever had cut them had got excited, or frightened, near the end. They came up onto the ridge in the last of the light, the nine of them, one behind the other.

On the far side, the ground went down into a wide shallow valley. Across the valley, on a hill of its own, stood a hall.

It was round, and grey, and it had been big once. Three doors, not seven. The dome had fallen in, half of it, so that the top of the hall was open to the sky like a broken eggshell. In the evening light Higu could see the line of the last stones running down the ridge and across the valley floor, straight as a ruled line on paper. Up the hill to the hall’s south door.

She didn’t need the sheets. She got them out anyway, in the wind, with Gigi holding the other corner.

On the dome in Carrow Cross, on the silver line, halfway between the north door and the big gold star off every map, there was a small star she’d drawn and never understood. A little round one, with a ring round it, on its own.

She looked at it. She looked across the valley.

“Chi,” she said.

“I know,” said Gigi.

The moon was coming up behind the hall, through the hole in its roof. On the far side of it, north, the line went on.
