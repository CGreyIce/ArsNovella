Live from Elsewhere · Volume 4: The Ninth Heron

Chapter 110

The Wrong Way

2,406 words · 10 min read

Sheng Yang had played for two months on his own before the trials and told nobody, because he’d wanted to see how far he could get.

He’d got quite far. Fifth on the Tally at Brackenford. Sixth. Fifth again. He’d liked it. Not the number. The getting there. The feeling of the sword coming round and landing exactly where he’d meant it to, ninety-something per cent, and the thing in front of him going down. Being good, on his own, at a thing nobody had taught him.

He’d thought that was the point. On the heron road a month ago, watching Gabe walk south alone into the dusk, he’d thought for the first time that maybe it wasn’t.

He’d been thinking about it ever since. He was still thinking about it at a quarter to one on Thursday night, standing in the mouth of a stone gallery at the top of the world, looking at a watcher the size of a tower that had just turned round to look at him.

“Let’s see what it wants,” said Declan.

They went into the hall.

Five of them, the way they always went. Declan first with the shield. Suzu wide on the left with the jar on his back, glowing so bright through the canvas it lit the floor around him. Deston in the middle, counting. Kotori behind, with her bell. And Sheng Yang two paces from Kotori, the way he’d promised Gabe on a rise by a headless heron. Look after her.

The hall was round and enormous and dark. Their lamps lit a little of the floor, and the blue lanterns in the gallery lit a little more behind them. All round the curved walls, in the alcoves, stood the other watchers, faceless, dark lanterns up. They faced inward, toward the middle of the hall, where the Last Watcher stood.

It had been looking north when they found it, with its back to them, like all the rest. Now it was looking south. At the gallery. At them.

At the light.

It moved.

Slowly, the way something that big had to move. One stone foot came up off the floor of the hall and came down again, closer, and the floor shook under them. Then the other. The great dark lantern swung in its stone hand like a bell. It didn’t come for Declan, out in front with his shield. It didn’t come for any of them.

It came for Suzu.

“Anchor,” said Declan.

He went across the floor in front of it, fast, faster than Sheng Yang had ever seen him go. He planted himself between the Last Watcher and Suzu, with Steadfast up and humming, and the dark lantern came down on the shield.

Sheng Yang heard it land. Everybody in the hall heard it. A sound like a door slamming in a cathedral. Declan went back across the floor on his heels, three yards, four, with his boots scraping on the stone. And he stayed up. Somehow he stayed up, and stayed between it and Suzu.

Fifty-four, off one hit. Through the Anchor. Through Steadfast.

Declan didn’t make a sound. He never did. But Sheng Yang saw him shift his left hand on the shield grip, the way you’d shift a hand that had just been hit with something very heavy. And he thought about fifty, and what fifty per cent of that must have felt like, and he didn’t want to think about it any more.

“One,” counted Deston. “Two.”

On the eighth, Kotori rang. Vigil went round them all, warm, and then her Mend came after it, hard, onto Declan. His health came up. Sixty. Seventy.

“Undertow,” said Declan.

The pull went out from him. Sheng Yang felt it go past, a long slow tug toward the shield, the one that had dragged a drowned thing off its legs in December and a maul man across a moor in February. It hit the Last Watcher.

The Last Watcher didn’t move.

It was too big. Too heavy, too old, too much stone. The pull went into it and stopped, the way a wave stops against a cliff.

“Breaker,” said Sheng Yang, under his breath. He didn’t decide to. He went.

He went in from the side, the way he always did. Behind whatever’s looking at Declan. That was his place, Gabe had said. Xav had been a knife in the dark. Sheng Yang was a door slamming.

The Last Watcher was looking at Suzu. At the jar. It didn’t see him come. He came in low from its left, under the swinging lantern, and planted his back foot on the stone. He brought the long sword round, both hands, all of him in it, everything he’d ever been good at on his own.

He hit it on the back of the knee.

Ninety-eight. The best he’d ever done. The stone cracked. He felt it go, under the blade, a long crack running up the back of the great stone leg. The Last Watcher’s health bar, high above him, dropped a long way, a fifth of it in one blow. The whole enormous figure lurched, and swayed, and put a hand out to the floor.

“YES,” said somebody. It was Kotori. Kotori never said yes like that.

Then Sheng Yang watched the crack close.

Not fast. Slowly, the way a bruise fades. The dark of the hall seemed to lean in toward the broken stone, and the stone drank it in, and the crack drew together from the bottom up. And the health bar, high above him, crept back up. A fifth. All of it.

“It mends,” said Deston. He’d stopped counting. That was how Sheng Yang knew it was bad. “In the dark. It mends.”

The Last Watcher straightened up. Then it turned its faceless head, and looked down, for the first time, at Sheng Yang.

He was two paces from nobody. He’d come in from the side, alone, and he was standing under it in the dark with his long sword still out from the cut. Kotori was twenty yards away. Declan was between the thing and Suzu. Gabe, who could have stepped in front of this, was a long way south with three people at level twelve.

The lantern came round.

He didn’t see it. He heard it, the cathedral door, and then he was on the floor of the hall a long way from where he’d been. His health was a red sliver in the corner of his eye. He couldn’t feel his left side, which at twenty per cent meant it was very bad indeed.

He thought, quite calmly, twenty-five per cent, plus ten for every level it’s got on me. It’s got four on me. Sixty-five. Most of a level. A whole day locked out. On the night before Suzu’s mother saw for the first time.

He thought, I came in alone.

Then Declan was there.

Not between him and the lantern. Declan had come across the hall, away from Suzu, and got hold of Sheng Yang by the back of his collar with his left hand, the hit hand, and pulled. Just pulled, like you’d pull somebody out of a river. Across the stone floor, backwards, toward the mouth of the gallery. Toward the blue light of the thirty-second lantern.

The Last Watcher came after them, one stone foot, then the other. The floor shook.

“Vigil,” said Kotori, from somewhere, and rang, and rang, and rang, not on the eighth any more, just ringing.

They went into the blue light. Declan, dragging, and Sheng Yang, dragged, and then all of them, Suzu with his arms round the jar, Deston, Kotori.

The Last Watcher stopped.

It stood at the mouth of the gallery, huge, in the dark of the hall, with its dark lantern up. It didn’t come in. It looked at the blue lantern in the stone hand of the thirty-second watcher, and didn’t come any closer. Then, slowly, so slowly that it took a minute, it turned round again. It walked back into the middle of its hall and stood there, facing north, with its back to them, the way it had been when they found it.

Nobody said anything.

Sheng Yang lay on the floor of the gallery in the blue light, at six per cent, and looked at the ceiling. Kotori was kneeling beside him ringing her bell over and over, saying something, probably not sorry. His health came back up a little at a time. His left side came back. It hurt, at twenty, quite a lot.

“I came in alone,” he said, to the ceiling.

“Yeah,” said Declan.

He was sitting against the wall of the gallery with his shield on his knees. His left hand was flat on the shield, very still, the way Sheng Yang had seen him hold it after the first hit.

“Gabe would’ve—“

“Gabe’s not here,” said Declan. “That’s fine. Next time come in two paces from somebody.”

Sheng Yang turned his head and looked at him.

“Even the door,” said Declan, “can’t be the door on its own.”

Suzu was standing at the mouth of the gallery with the Heron’s Eye up, looking out into the hall.

He stood there a long time. Sheng Yang watched him from the floor. He was looking round the walls of the great hall, slowly, all the way round, at the alcoves. His lips were moving very slightly. Counting.

“Thirty-two,” said Suzu, at last. He didn’t turn round. “In the gallery. Forty-eight round the walls.”

“Eighty,” said Deston.

“And him.” Suzu lowered the Eye. He looked at the Last Watcher, standing in the middle of its hall with its back to them and its great dark lantern up. “Eighty-one.”

Nobody said anything.

“It needs the last one,” said Suzu. “When the others are lit. All the others. It’s waiting for the last one.” He looked down at the jar in his arms. Seventy-four small blue lights, pressed against the glass toward the hall. “It can’t keep its watch in the dark. Nobody can. That’s why it turned round. It’s been looking for the light.”

“It went for you,” said Kotori quietly. “Not us. You.”

“It went for these,” said Suzu.

He sat down on the floor of the gallery, in the blue light, with his back against the plinth of the thirty-second watcher. He held the jar in his lap. He’d been holding it in his lap since December, one way or another, on hillsides and in rings and on the floors of trains.

“Tomorrow,” said Suzu, “they switch it on.”

“I know,” said Declan.

“I’m going to be there. In the room. When she—“ He stopped. “I’ll decide after.”

“Take as long as you want,” said Declan.

Sheng Yang looked at him. Then he got his messages up, the corner of his eye, and there it was. It had come in while he was on the floor at six per cent.

VANTA · the bottom. boss.

Kestrel was at her last room. Maybe already through it. Maybe first, by morning.

He thought about the Tally at Brackenford. Fifth, sixth, fifth. We could be first. He’d said that on the hill on Thursday afternoon, and meant it, and Declan had said it’s his lights.

He looked at Suzu, sitting on the floor of the gallery with seventy-four lights in his lap. At Kotori, still kneeling beside him with her bell. At Deston, against the opposite wall with the book closed for once, not counting. At Declan, with his hand flat on his shield.

He’d come in alone tonight, because he was good, and it had nearly cost him a whole day and most of a level. Declan had dragged him out by the collar with the hand that had just stopped a cathedral door.

Maybe the point was who you got there with.

“It’s not a race,” said Sheng Yang.

They all looked at him.

He hadn’t known he was going to say it. He said it again anyway, to be sure.

“It’s not a race,” said Sheng Yang. “Not if it’s his lights.”

Declan looked at him for a long time across the gallery. Then he nodded, once, the way he’d nodded at Kite at a milestone in February.

They logged out at the door, in the mist, at half past two.

Before he went, Sheng Yang saw Declan sitting on the grass by the door, typing. He was typing for a long time, which Declan never did. Sheng Yang had seen him answer a message from Hyvred in two words and a message from Kei in five. He’d never seen him type for longer than about ten seconds.

He was still typing when Sheng Yang left.

The next afternoon, Gabe sent all of them the same message. It came from somewhere a long way south, from a man two paces from three people at level twelve.
