Chapter 65

The Left Side of the Wall

2,748 words · 12 min read

He knew him by his feet.

That was the thing Drew would remember about it afterwards. Not the face, not the bow. He was sitting on the edge of the bottom basin on Saturday evening, with his boots over the water and a cracked knuckle-plate in his lap that he was trying to decide whether to mend or remake. Somebody came down the east road into Sevenlocks, a long way off, in the last of the light. Drew knew who it was before he could see anything but the shape of them walking. It was the way the left foot came down a fraction heavier than the right. The way the weight sat back on the heels. A man who’d been hurt in the left leg once and had learned to walk around it.

He’d watched those feet for twelve hours, across a dark yard, on the left side of a wall.

He put the knuckle-plate down.

He was thinner than Drew remembered. Or the light was. A narrow-faced young man in a dark coat gone grey with road dust, with a quiver on his back that had arrows in it this time, and a bow. Not on his back. In his hand, held low, unstrung. The string was looped round the stave and tied off, so that anyone could see from a long way away it couldn’t be used.

He stopped on the cobbles at the edge of the basin, ten feet from Drew. He didn’t come any closer. He stood there with his unstrung bow and looked at Drew sitting on the stones, and didn’t say anything.

Drew looked at his collar.

There was no bird on it. There was a place where a bird had been. A small patch of cloth a shade darker than the rest of the coat, where something had been stitched for a long time and kept the dust off. And a few loose dark threads at the edge of it, where somebody had cut the stitches out one at a time with a small knife, not very well.

“You saw the bird,” said Kite. It was the first thing he’d said to Drew, on the longest night, from the other side of a gate.

“There isn’t one,” said Drew.

“No.”

“I can see where it was.”

“Yeah,” said Kite. He looked down at his own collar, as if he’d forgotten. “Yeah. You would. You watch everything.”

He sat down on the stones a little way along the basin, not too close, with his bow across his knees. Drew didn’t ask him to. He just did it, the way he’d taken the left side of the wall: without being told, at a distance that meant something.

Drew waited. That was the thing he was best at.

“I went back,” said Kite eventually. He was looking at the water. “After your inn. After the dawn. I went back to them. Ash and the others. There’s a place we—they—use, off the north road. A ruined mill.” He turned the unstrung bow over in his hands. “They wanted to know where I’d been all night. I said I’d been held up. They laughed. Ash said held up by what, and I said a gate, and she stopped laughing.”

“Ash,” said Drew.

“You know her?”

“I know of her. She was at Fernwick. On the north walk. With you.”

Kite’s hands went still on the bow.

“Yeah,” he said, after a moment. “Yeah. I was there. The one with the daggers killed me. Before your—before the longest night.” He almost smiled. “He asked if I was calling someone. I was. I was calling Ash. He knew before I’d finished the word.”

“That’s Xav.”

“I know who it is now. Everybody knows who it is now.” Kite looked at the water. “Anyway. I went back. And we went out on the roads again, the next week. The way we do. The way they do. You wait outside a town at dusk. Somebody comes along on their own. You take their day.” He was quiet for a while. “And somebody came. A girl. On her own, on the south road out of Holloway, with a lantern. And Ash said go. And the girl saw us and ran. Toward the town. Toward the light.” He stopped. “And I didn’t go.”

Drew didn’t say anything.

“I just stood there,” said Kite. “On the road. With my bow. And I thought about your gate. And the old woman in the yard. Even if you don’t know what they are.” He swallowed. “And I couldn’t. I just couldn’t. The girl got to the town. Ash looked at me for a long time.”

“And then?”

“And then I told her I was done.” Kite laughed, short and not happy. “You know what she said? You don’t leave Nightjar. You get left.” He touched the dark patch on his collar. “So they left me. On the road. All of them, at once. Took my day. It’s the only time I’ve ever been on the other end of it.” A pause. “I came back the next morning in a church in a town I’d never heard of, a level and a half down. And I sat on the church steps and cut the bird off with my skinning knife. It took an hour. I’m bad at sewing. I’m worse at unsewing.”

“Why here?” said Drew.

Kite looked at him.

“Because of the swamp guy,” he said. “Last Sunday. On his stream.” He looked away again, at the lock gates going up the hill in the dusk. “Everybody watches the swamp guy. Even them. Especially them. Ash watches him every night. She hates him. She watches him the way you’d watch a door you can’t get through.” A pause. “He said somebody was making something. And that he wasn’t in it. And that if you wanted to be in it, you should walk there yourself, and they’d decide. Every time.”

Drew went very still.

“I’d heard that before,” said Kite. “Every time. An old woman said it to me in a yard, at a gate, on the longest night. And you were standing next to her.” He shrugged, awkwardly. “So I thought. Whoever it is. Whatever it is. You’d be in it. And everybody in the world knows where you were walking. The Long Walk to Tiger.” He looked down at his bow. “So I walked here. Six days. I watched the swamp guy raid you on my phone, one night when I was out of the egg. I didn’t have anywhere else to walk to.”

“What do you want?” said Drew.

It was Niko’s question. He hadn’t meant to borrow it. It just came out, because it was the right one.

Kite was quiet for a long time.

“To stand somewhere,” he said. “Somewhere that isn’t a road.”

Drew called Tiger down.

He didn’t have to call loudly. Tiger was on the Top with Pell, running the gates in the dusk, and he came down the hill as if the whole staircase of locks were one long slope. He skimmed the last three gates, landed on the cobbles of the basin, and stopped dead.

He’d seen the bow.

His ears went flat. Drew had seen them do that on stream maybe four times in six weeks, and every time something had been about to go very wrong. Tiger’s hands went to the two short swords on his hips and stayed there, not drawing, just there.

Then he saw the collar.

Drew watched him see it. Watched him look at the dark patch and the loose threads, and then at the unstrung bow, and then at Kite’s face. Then at Drew. He watched the ears come up again, very slowly, one at a time, the way they did when Tiger was working something out and didn’t like how it was coming out.

“That’s him,” said Tiger. “From the inn. The left side of the wall.”

“That’s him.”

“He’s Nightjar.”

“He was.”

Tiger looked at Kite for a long time. Kite looked back, and didn’t look away, which Drew thought was the bravest thing he’d seen anyone do in Sevenlocks.

“He’s got good feet,” said Tiger finally, unexpectedly. “He stood still when I landed. Most people jump.”

“He’s got good feet,” agreed Drew.

“That doesn’t mean yes,” said Tiger quickly. “I’m just saying. Feet.”

“I know,” said Drew. “It’s not yes. It’s not anyone’s yes. Not on their own.” He took a breath. “That’s the whole point of it.”

He opened the call.

It was the usual time. Roll call had just finished. Fourteen, now, with Tiger. Kei had said fourteen at the end of it in a voice that sounded as if he’d been waiting all day to say it. Then he’d said he had to go early tonight, because he was calling Hyvred, and Maigo had made a noise like a kettle.

Drew told them.

Not all of it. Kite’s story was Kite’s. He just said: the boy from the left side of the wall is here, in Sevenlocks, with his bow unstrung and the bird cut off his collar. He walked six days. Nightjar left him on a road for not taking a girl’s day. He heard Ice on Sunday. He wants to stand somewhere.

“He’s here,” said Drew. “Next to me. He can hear you.” A pause. “I think he should. If it’s every time, then he should hear it.”

There was a long silence on the call.

“Nightjar,” said Maigo at last. Not loud. Maigo was never not loud, and he wasn’t loud. “Nightjar were on the north walk. At Fernwick. They went for Ice’s lot in the dark. They went for Kotori on her post.” He stopped. “She said it on Ice’s stream, after. She said they were people.”

“I was there,” said Kite.

Everybody on the call heard it. It was the first thing he’d said.

“I was on the north walk,” said Kite. “I had a bow. I wasn’t shooting at the healer, I was shooting at the big one with the shield. The swamp guy. It didn’t matter, he didn’t even turn round.” He breathed. “The one with the daggers killed me. I’m not going to say I wasn’t there. I was there.”

Nobody said anything.

“He held the left side of the wall at the Waymeet,” said Drew quietly. “All night. On his own. A gap you couldn’t see from the gate. Nobody told him to. Nothing came through it.” He looked at the water. “I’m not saying that makes it even. I’m just saying it’s also true.”

“He was running toward a light,” said Wendy.

She said it softly, from Ashcombe, from her forest of red clamps. Everybody stopped.

“On the longest night,” said Wendy. “Drew said. He came up the road with three of them behind him, and no arrows, running toward the only light for miles.” She was quiet for a moment. “That’s all. I just—that’s what I keep thinking. He was running toward a light.”

“It’ll fall down,” said Gigi.

“Gigi,” said Higu.

“It will. If we let a Nightjar in, it’ll fall down. Somebody’ll say you let a Nightjar in, and somebody’ll take sides, and it’ll fall down.” A long pause, from inside her hedge. “But if it’s going to fall down anyway—and it is—then I’d rather it fell down because we let somebody in than because we kept everybody out.” Another pause. “That’s not a yes. That’s a—“

“Prediction,” said Salty.

“Shut up, Salty.”

Kei didn’t decide.

Drew had known he wouldn’t. That was why it was Kei. He let all of them talk. Maigo and Wendy and Gigi, and Salty, who said he’d do whatever Wendy did. Ina, who said the forge had seen worse, and Hako, who said nothing in his documentary voice. Chris, who said very quietly that he had a tab for Nightjar, and that Kite’s name was on it, and that he’d add a column. Newish, who said the drum didn’t care. Winter, who said “Fine,” and nobody knew which way.

And then Kei said:

“Not tonight.”

Kite, beside Drew on the stones, didn’t move.

“Not because no,” said Kei. Carefully, the way he measured a dose. “Because it’s not the gate yet. The gate’s in Carrow Cross, in the first week of February, when I’m fifteen. Everybody who walks there walks there. And at the gate, the people who’d stand next to them decide. All of us. Every time.” A pause. “Kite. You can walk there. Same as anybody. Same as Tiger. Same as me. And when you get there, we’ll decide. And it might be no.”

“I know,” said Kite.

“If it’s no, you’ll have walked a long way for nothing.”

“I’ve walked a long way for nothing before,” said Kite. “This is the first time I’ve walked a long way for something.” He looked down at his unstrung bow. “Even if it’s no.”

There was a silence on the call.

“Hm,” said Kei, in a voice Drew had never heard him use, very like an old woman in a round hall looking at a map.

Before Kei went to call Hyvred, Kite said one more thing.

He said it to all of them, quickly, the way you’d say something you’d been holding for six days and couldn’t hold any longer.

“Ash knows,” he said. “About the writs. About the Halls. She watched the swamp guy say it on Sunday. She said—“ He stopped, then made himself go on. “She said the roads to the Halls are going to be full of people walking to sign things. On their own. In the dark. With lanterns. All in the same month.” He looked at the lock gates going up the hill. “She said it’s going to be the best month Nightjar’s ever had.”

Nobody said anything.

“She doesn’t know where yours is,” said Kite. “Not yet. But she’ll find out. Everybody finds out everything, in the end, on this server. And if it’s a Hall where seven roads meet—“ He shook his head. “Seven roads is a lot of roads.”

“Thank you,” said Kei, after a moment.

“Don’t,” said Kite. And then, when he realised what he’d said, he looked at Drew. Something happened in his narrow face that was almost a smile.

He slept at the inn by the basin that night. Drew paid for the room. Kite argued, and lost. Wat the lock-keeper looked at him sideways all evening, and then, at about ten o’clock, gave him a bowl of eel stew without a word. Which Tiger said was the Sevenlocks equivalent of a medal.

Drew sat on the Top gate with Tiger afterwards, in the dark, with the town lit below them like a staircase of gold.

“Do you think they’ll say yes?” said Tiger.

Drew thought about it.

“I think he’ll walk there either way,” he said.

Tiger was quiet for a bit, kicking his heels against the gate.

“I would’ve,” he said. “Said yes. Right there. When he said to stand somewhere. I’d have just said yes.”

“I know you would.”

“Is that bad?”

“No,” said Drew. “It’s why you’re at the gate too.” He looked down at the basin, where the inn’s one lit window was. “You’re the yes. Kei’s the not tonight. Gigi’s the it’ll fall down. Wendy’s the running toward a light.” He paused. “That’s the gate. All of them, standing in it. Not one of them on their own.”

Tiger thought about that for a long time.

“What are you?” he said eventually.

Drew looked at the inn window, and the dark east road beyond it that went away over the hills toward a round hall with seven doors.

“I’m the one who watches the feet,” he said.
