Live from Elsewhere · Volume 5: Ask Me Again

Chapter 131

Both Ways

1,932 words · 8 min read

Gigi had said tomorrow would be worse. She was right. She usually was. It was the least comforting thing about her.

By Wednesday morning, her time, two hundred and eighty thousand people had gone and asked.

They asked the old woman on the corner. They asked the man who lit the lamps. They asked the innkeeper, and the ferryman, and the woman who sold bread out of a window and always put one loaf on the sill at dusk and never sold it. And the old ones said what Higu had said they’d say. It’s just what we do. And the travellers said why. And the old ones, almost every one, said I don’t know. My mother did it.

Then the travellers did it too.

The forums filled up overnight with towns. Hundreds of them. Ashcombe: leave the foxfire lit on the path home. Otterbank: count the otters at dusk, out loud. Saltwhistle: a boat on the water at every high tide, even if it’s empty. Tinner’s Cove: ring the cove bell three times when a stranger arrives. People posted their town’s line under a picture of themselves doing it, badly, with an old NPC in the background looking baffled and pleased. Somebody started a map. Somebody else started a better map. Higu, at seven in the morning, was quietly correcting both.

And in Briarwick, nine days’ walk west, a hundred and forty travellers turned up at the gate in the old laid hedge. They all wanted to see the ditches. Granny Sloe stood in the gap with her billhook and told every single one of them to bugger off.

Gigi watched it on somebody’s stream on her phone at the café, and laughed until she had to put her head on the table.

The gate was the problem.

Not the hedge one. Theirs. The top step of the south side of the Hall of Roads, where Wendy sat every evening with her bow across her knees and a cup of Gigi’s tea, and Kite on the noseless lion opposite. Where for two months people had come up the steps and said can I join and Wendy had said who’d stand next to you.

On Wednesday a thousand people came up the steps.

By Thursday it was three thousand. They queued down the south road. They camped in the round market. They came from every town on Higu’s list and every town that wasn’t on it. All of them wanted to be in the guild that had found it, the guild with the banner of dots and lines. The one that had sat under a ceiling by moonlight and told the whole world what it had been keeping for three hundred years without knowing.

Wendy had said it’s going to go badly. She sat on the top step on Thursday evening with three thousand people in front of her and said it again, flatly, to Gigi, who’d brought her the tea.

“I said it would go badly.”

“You did.”

“I’m always right. It’s horrible.” Wendy drank the tea. “This is horrible.”

“It’s good for you.”

“Everything you make is good for you, Gigi. That’s the problem with it.”

But the gate held.

That was the thing. Gigi stood on the step and watched it hold, all evening, three thousand people. Wendy asked every one of them the same question. Who’d stand next to you? Most of them didn’t have an answer. Then not yet. Get to know somebody. And Kite, on the lion, nodded or didn’t, and said very little. And Rafi, who’d come up from the round market at noon with his black sword and said I’ll do the gate too, if that’s allowed, sat on the step below Wendy. He said nobody sent me either to every person who mentioned who’d sent them, and grinned.

The gate held. Not because it was a wall. Because it was a question.

She found Drew on the north steps at midnight.

He was sitting where he always sat when he wanted to think, at the top of the north steps by the door Gabe had fixed, with his iron knuckles off and his hands loose on his knees. He was looking north up the road at nothing. Drew could look at nothing for hours. He said it was good for you. Gigi said that was her line.

She sat down beside him with two cups. She gave him one. He drank it without making a face, which was a thing only Drew and Higu had ever done.

“Long day,” said Drew.

“Long day.”

They sat for a while.

“Can I ask you something,” said Drew. “You don’t have to answer.”

Gigi didn’t say anything. Drew took that as yes. Drew took most things as yes, and was usually right.

“When Higu says Thea’s name,” said Drew, “you go somewhere.”

Gigi went very still.

“You do it every time,” said Drew. He didn’t look at her. He looked at the road. “In the guild call. On the roof, on Tuesday, when Higu read the list and Xola’s stream was on in the corner of everybody’s eye. On Wednesday, when somebody said Thea’s lot are levelling round Brackenford. You go somewhere for about a second, and then you come back, and you say something about tea.” He drank his. “I’m not asking where.”

“Then what are you asking?”

Drew thought about it. He always thought about it.

“I’m not,” he said. “I’m telling you something. Nell’s thing. The gate.” He turned the cup in his hands. “If the gate’s shut, you have to decide. Every time. I brought it here. We found out in April it wasn’t Nell’s. It was theirs, from before.” He looked at her. “I always thought it was about strangers. People coming up the steps you didn’t know. Whether to let them in.”

“It is.”

“It’s not just about strangers,” said Drew. “It works both ways. It’s about people you used to know, too. People you shut a gate on. Or who shut one on you.” He looked back at the road. “Even if you don’t know what they are any more. Especially then. That’s what Nell said. Especially then.”

Gigi sat on the north steps of the Hall of Roads at midnight with a cup of her own horrible tea going cold in her hands, and didn’t say anything for a long time.

She knew what she’d been.

That was the thing nobody understood, that she’d never said out loud, not to Higu, not to anyone. She knew. She’d known for a long time. She’d been the one who found the wrong in everything Thea did. If Thea did badly, it was wrong. If Thea did well, it was wrong, in a different way. She’d done it so long she’d stopped hearing herself do it. And Thea had kept trying to get it right, over and over, as if there were a right that Gigi would accept. There hadn’t been. There never had been. Gigi had made sure of that without ever deciding to.

And Xola had been on the phone for all of it. Every night. Gigi knew that too.

And then Thea had ended it, and Gigi had told herself for a long time that she was the one who’d been wronged. It had been easier. Everything fell down. She’d always said so. She’d said it about them, at the end. It was always going to fall down.

It hadn’t been going to. She’d pushed.

She’d been trying, since. Higu knew that. Higu was the only one who did. Trying to be better than she’d been. Laying hedges, building things like they were going to fall down so they’d stay up. Making tea that was good for people. Asking, in April, if Thea was all right, and meaning it, and being afraid of the answer.

“I know what I was,” said Gigi.

She hadn’t meant to say it out loud. It came out anyway, small, into the dark.

Drew didn’t say anything for a while. He didn’t ask what. He didn’t tell her it was fine, or that she wasn’t, or any of the things people said. He just sat there, the way he’d sat with Kite at a milestone, the way he’d held a road on the longest night for a man with a bird on his collar.

“Then you know what you’re deciding,” said Drew.

She didn’t decide anything that night. Not really. She sat on the north steps until Drew went in. Then a while longer, alone, with the cold tea, looking north up the road the way Drew had, at nothing.

She thought about Thea with a tower shield.

She’d seen it on Xola’s stream. Everybody had. Thea, at the front, in a town of wheels, with a shield taller than she was. Thea had always been behind. Behind Gigi, mostly. Holding the plan, holding the call, holding everything up, and getting it wrong anyway, because Gigi had decided it was wrong. And now she was at the front of something. She’d fallen over and got up, and learned to turn things. Gigi had watched the clip of her getting up eleven times.

She got her phone out, in the corner of her eye. She opened her messages, and found Higu’s name, and looked at it for a long time.

She didn’t write to Thea. She couldn’t. Not yet. A message from her, straight to Thea, out of nowhere, after all of it, would be a weight. She’d put enough weight on Thea for one life.

But she could put something down at the gate. Something small. Somebody else could decide whether to pick it up.

Gigi put the phone away and sat on the north steps of the Hall of Roads, at nearly one in the morning, with her pocket watch ticking in her pocket. She cried. She hadn’t done that in a long time either.

She’d built it like it was going to fall down. That was how you made things stay up. She’d said that in January, about a guild, and been right.

She thought, for the first time, that maybe it worked the other way too. Some things you’d knocked down yourself. You couldn’t build them back. But maybe you could put down one small thing where they used to be, and leave it there. And see if anything grew.
