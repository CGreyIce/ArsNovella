Live from Elsewhere · Volume 1: Moonlight Harvest

Chapter 14

Arrivals

2,971 words · 13 min read

Hana was an hour early, and she’d known she would be.

She’d made a plan for being an hour early. She’d made it on Tuesday, in the small square notebook she kept for things that mattered, under a heading that just said FRIDAY: Leave at 6.45. MRT, not Grab, Grab will be stupid in the morning. Get there at 8. Plane lands 8.55. Doors around 9.40 with immigration and bags. Sit by the waterfall. Do not stand at the barrier for an hour like a crazy person.

So at five past eight on Friday morning she was sitting by the waterfall in Jewel, with a kopi she didn’t need and her notebook in her bag. She was not standing at the barrier like a crazy person. She was sitting near it, at a distance that was very reasonable, and checking the arrivals board on her phone every forty seconds.

The waterfall came down through the middle of the glass dome from a round hole in the roof, a great falling column of water, and landed in a pool forty metres below with a sound like wind. Around it, terraces of trees and ferns went up and up in rings, all the way to the glass. In the early light the whole place was soft and green and bright, and full of people with suitcases standing still to look at it, the way people did. She’d been here a hundred times. She still looked at it every time.

She took out her phone again. LANDED, said the board, under the flight from Amsterdam. Eight fifty-one. Four minutes early.

She put the phone face down on her knee and sat very still and breathed for a while.

She’d been on hiatus for five weeks.

She’d told chat there was something big coming that she’d tell them about when it happened. She’d turned off the stream schedule on her channel page, and her calendar, which had said STREAM in blue on Tuesdays and Thursdays and Saturdays for three years, had gone very empty. It had been strange. She was a person who liked a schedule. She’d made a new one for the hiatus, out of habit. Clean the flat. Buy the good towels. Make a list of everything he has to eat. Make another list of everything he’s not allowed to leave without seeing. The second list was two pages long. She’d crossed things off it already, imagining.

She opened Ice’s stream while she waited, because it was Friday morning and that was what she did now.

It was the eleventh day of ELSEWHERE, or the twelfth. She’d lost count. He was where he always was: in his swamp, on the end of a wooden walkway, at dawn, with a hundred and something people watching him explain how to stand. He was level ten now. He’d been made something called a Holdfast on Wednesday, and Hana had read the window it came in four times and then had to go and make tea, because it had said you did not flinch, and she’d known him since she was nineteen.

A minute later, on the walkway in the swamp, Ice looked up, at nothing, at her.

“Morning, Alice,” he said. He was the only one who called her that, him and Jared. He sounded pleased. He always did, when she turned up. He’d sounded pleased when she turned up on his stream when she was nineteen and just starting out, and he’d sounded pleased every single time since, and she’d never once told him how much that had helped.

On the screen, a minute later, Ice laughed out loud in his swamp, a big surprised laugh.

“Oh,” he said. “Oh. Alice. Okay. Go. Go, go. Put your phone away. Tell him hi from me too.”

She smiled at her phone, and didn’t put it away. She left the stream running on her knee with the sound turned down, his voice saying back foot to a hundred people. It was company. It had been company for a long time.

There was a screen on the wall of the arrival hall that hadn’t been there in October.

It was enormous, the size of the side of a bus. For a long time it had shown the same rotation of ads for perfume and banks and a resort in Bintan. Now, in between the perfume and the banks, it went black, and stayed black for a second longer than was comfortable. Then, out of the black, came rain. Rain on a window, close up, the sound of it filling the hall. Then a room of pale wood. Then a door opening, slowly, onto somewhere bright.

One line of white text, in the middle of the dark:

Where are you?

And then, very small, at the bottom:

ELSEWHERE

People stopped to look at it. She watched them do it. A family with a trolley stood there and watched the whole thing through, and when it finished, the father said something to the mother in Mandarin. Hana caught the word for two and a half thousand, and the mother laughed and shook her head, and the son, who was about twelve, said in English, but Pa, everybody’s. They moved on. A pair of girls in matching hoodies filmed it on their phones. An old man in a cap stood in front of it with his hands behind his back, perfectly still, until it had finished and the perfume had come back, and then walked away slowly.

She thought of Ice in his swamp, a hundred and twenty people watching him explain feet. She thought of the moonbells, the night they’d opened on his stream. She’d been on her bed with the lights off and the phone on her chest, and she’d cried a bit and not told anyone.

Where are you?

She looked at the arrivals board on her phone. BAGGAGE ON BELT.

She stood up.

She was at the barrier like a crazy person when he came through the doors.

She saw him before he saw her. He came out of the sliding doors with a backpack on and a big grey suitcase, slowly, looking tired and rumpled and very Dutch, the way he always looked to her for the first few minutes when she hadn’t seen him in a while, before he went back to just looking like Milli. He stopped just past the doors and looked round the hall, the way everybody did, at the screens and the crowd and the glass and the green beyond it. She watched his face do the thing it did when he was overwhelmed and didn’t want to show it: go very calm and very still, as if he’d decided to simply wait the whole world out.

Then his eyes found her.

She didn’t remember deciding to run. She remembered the floor under her feet and the barrier going past and somebody saying aiyo as she went round them. Then he’d let go of the suitcase handle, because he knew. He’d always known. He turned round and crouched down a little, and she jumped onto his back with her arms round his neck.

He staggered. He straightened up. She put one fist in the air.

People stared. A whole tour group stared. She didn’t care. She was on Milli’s back in the arrival hall at Changi with her chin on his shoulder and her fist in the air, the same way they’d been in the one photo of them everybody had seen. It had been on both their channels, the two of them laughing. He was laughing now, the low, surprised, helpless laugh he hardly ever did, and she could feel it go through his back into her.

“You’re early,” he said.

“You’re early. The plane was early.”

“I meant you’re early to the barrier. You said in the message you were going to sit by the waterfall and not stand at the barrier like a crazy person.”

“I was sitting by the waterfall.”

“For how long?”

“Long enough,” said Hana, and put her face in his neck.

He held her legs and stood there in the middle of the arrival hall, very still, the way he did when he was overwhelmed and didn’t want to show it. They stayed like that for a while. Somebody’s suitcase went over behind them. Somebody’s child asked loudly why the lady was on the man’s back. Somebody’s mother said shh.

“Hi,” said Milli, eventually, into the air in front of him.

“Hi,” said Hana, into his collar.

He stepped out of the terminal doors into the taxi rank and stopped dead.

“Oh,” said Milli.

It was ten in the morning in Singapore and thirty-one degrees, and the air was wet and thick and warm and smelled of rain that hadn’t fallen yet. Hana didn’t notice it any more. She watched him notice it, all of it, in about two seconds: the heat landing on him like a blanket, his shirt starting to stick before they’d even reached the queue, his face doing several things at once.

“It’s like breathing soup,” he said.

“It’s November,” said Hana. “It’s the nice time.”

“This is the nice time?”

“It rains in the afternoon. It’s lovely.”

Milli looked at her with deep suspicion and the beginning of a laugh, and she took his free hand and pulled him toward the taxis. He let her. He always let her.

He fell asleep in the taxi before they’d reached the end of the airport road, with his head against the window and his mouth open. She sat beside him and watched the city go past. Rain trees along the expressway, the sea flashing silver between the buildings on the right, HDB blocks in their soft pastels. She didn’t wake him. She held his hand on the seat between them, and once, in a traffic jam on the PIE, she lifted it and just looked at it for a while. Then she put it down again, because it was the middle of the day and she was being ridiculous.

At home, he managed to say your flat is very clean, and you bought new towels, and you have a list on the fridge. Then he lay down on the bed with his shoes on and slept for eleven hours.

She took his shoes off. She put a fan on. She sat at the end of the bed with her notebook and crossed off FRIDAY, and looked at the next page. It said TWO MONTHS, and under that, a list.

It was a good list. It had everything on it. Chicken rice at the place she always went to, and Maxwell, and the satay at night at Lau Pa Sat. The Botanic Gardens early in the morning, before it got hot. The beach at East Coast, and the night safari, which he’d said was a tourist trap and which she’d said he was going anyway. Her favourite bookshop. The long walk round the reservoir. The roof of a car park in Toa Payoh where, if you went at the right time, the whole sky went pink.

Near the bottom, in pen that was slightly different because she’d added it later, it said: Talk about one day.

They’d talked about one day so many times now, over calls at strange hours across seven time zones, that it had stopped being a phrase and become almost a place. Somewhere they were both going. One day they’d live in the same city. One day they’d stop counting time zones. One day they’d stand somewhere in front of people who loved them and make it official. They’d stopped saying if a long time ago. It had always been when, and one day. And the one thing neither of them had ever been able to work out, in all the calls at strange hours, was how long one day was.

She looked at two months, and the list under it, and the line at the bottom.

Then she closed the notebook and lay down beside him on top of the covers in her clothes. She listened to him breathe, there, in the same room, without a headset. She didn’t sleep. She didn’t try.

He woke up at a quarter past two in the morning, completely awake, the way people do when their body is still in another country.

She knew he would. It had been on the list too. First night: he’ll wake up at 2. Have snacks. She had snacks. She had biscuits and the good kaya and a packet of the green bean things he’d liked last time, and she had them on a plate on the balcony before he’d finished sitting up and saying what time is it.

They sat out on the balcony in the warm dark, eight floors up, with their feet on the railing. The city hummed below them, never quite asleep. The block opposite had lights on in three windows, a cat on one sill, and somebody’s laundry hanging out on a pole, which Milli found deeply funny for reasons he couldn’t explain.

“Aleaka said hi,” said Hana. “And Ice said hi. And Aleaka said to eat something green.”

“I’m eating something green.” He held up a green bean biscuit.

“I don’t think she meant that.”

“She didn’t specify.” He ate it. “Ice said hi?”

“I told his stream you were coming. At the airport.”

“You told his stream?”

“A hundred and twenty people know you’re in Singapore now,” said Hana. “Sorry.”

Milli thought about this. “That’s fine,” he said. “That’s fewer people than know I’m in Singapore because of you jumping on me at Changi in front of a tour group.”

She laughed, and put her head on his shoulder.

After a while he said, “Show me.”

“Show you what?”

“The thing. The game. You’ve been watching it. You watched it in the taxi when you thought I was asleep. You watched it on the bed when you thought I was asleep. You have it on your phone now, upside down, on your knee.”

She did. She’d forgotten. She turned it over.

She didn’t show him the stream; it was the middle of the night and Ice was asleep in Malacca. She found the clip instead, the one everybody had shared. The night the moonbells opened, cut down to two minutes by somebody in chat: the warm water, the stones, the moon coming up fast over the reeds, the first bud opening with its tiny clear note, and then all of them, one after another, the silver lights and the sound like a glass harmonica in another room. And Ice saying nothing at all, for the whole two minutes, which was the most remarkable part.

Milli watched it all the way through. He didn’t say anything until it finished. Then he watched it again.

“That’s the Minecraft bingo guy,” he said.

“That’s him.”

“He’s in a swamp.”

“He’s very happy in his swamp,” said Hana. “He’s levelled up a lot. He has a company now. He hired Suzu. Suzu moved to Malaysia yesterday.”

Milli looked at her.

“It’s been a big two weeks,” said Hana.

He looked back at the clip, frozen on the last frame: thirty silver lights standing up out of dark water, and a ring of grey stones, and the edge of a sword hand at the bottom of the screen. He was quiet for a long time, in the way that meant he was doing a sum.

“How much is the egg?” he said.

“Two thousand four hundred and ninety-nine dollars. US.”

He did the sum. She watched him do it, the way she’d watched him do sums on calls for years: his eyes going slightly unfocused, his lips moving very slightly, euros, then Singapore dollars, then back.

“Hm,” he said.

“Not now,” said Hana. “We’re not talking about it now. It’s half past two in the morning and you’ve just got here.”

“I’m not talking about it. I’m doing a sum.”

“Don’t do a sum.”

“It’s already done,” said Milli. “I can’t undo a sum.” But he put the phone down on the plate between them, face down, next to the last biscuit. Then he put his arm round her, and they sat there with their feet on the railing and looked at the city.

The block opposite turned off one of its three lights.

“Two months,” said Milli, quietly, after a while.

“Two months,” said Hana.

Neither of them said the next bit, about what happened at the end of the two months. They didn’t need to. They’d had a lot of calls at strange hours, and the end of every visit was always the same: a gate at an airport, and the long flight, and then one of them in an empty flat, putting the headset back on.

The cat on the windowsill opposite stood up, and stretched, and went inside.
