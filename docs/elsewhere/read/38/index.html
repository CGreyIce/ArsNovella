The Lamp Run

2,874 words · 12 min read

Tiger watched Drew walk for five days, and it was the best thing he’d ever watched.

He watched it on his phone, mostly, propped against the end of the egg, while he ate. He watched it on the call, too, at night, when Drew stopped at a waystation and lit a fire and talked to him. And he watched it out of the corner of his eye in Sevenlocks, in the little window at the edge of his vision, while he ran. THE LONG WALK TO TIGER: DAY 6. A man in a navy jacket with a silver nameplate on his chest, walking down a road. That was all it was. It was perfect.

Drew waved at another sheep.

He’d argued about it on Saturday night. When Drew had said yours, on the call, and Tiger had sat bolt upright in the gel and hit his head on the lid.

“You can’t walk here.”

“I’m walking there.”

“It’s the whole world.”

“Twenty-four days,” said Drew calmly. “Kei did the sums.”

“Then I’ll come to you. I’ll meet you halfway. I’m fast now, I’m a Skimmer, I’ll—“

“You’ll fall in a river.”

“I won’t fall in a—“

“Tiger.” Drew didn’t raise his voice. Drew never raised his voice. “You’ll fall in a river on day two and a different river on day five. You’ll make forty friends and get lost and turn up in Whitecairn in March, wet, with no idea how you got there. And I’ll have been sitting in the snow the whole time, waiting.” A pause. “I’m already walking. Stay where you are. Learn your locks. I’ll come to you.”

Tiger had opened his mouth to argue and found he didn’t have anything. Every word of it was true. That was the trouble with Drew.

“Nine years,” he’d said instead.

“Nine years,” Drew had agreed. “Go to sleep.”

So he learned his locks.

He’d thought he knew them. He’d thought, running the Top Lock with a letter in his teeth, that he’d got them. Then Pell took him out on Sunday morning and showed him he’d only learned one way up the hill. There were dozens. Over the barges, under the bridges, along the gutters of the leaning houses where they hung over the side canals, across the lock-keepers’ roofs. The runners had a different route for every time of day, depending where the barges were, which gates were open, which way the wind was blowing the spray.

“You go where the water isn’t,” said Pell. “That’s the whole thing. The water moves, so you move.”

He ran with them all week. Not as one of them. Pell was very clear that he wasn’t one of them. You had to be born in Sevenlocks to be a runner and wear the red satchel, and he was a traveller and a tiger and had fallen in twenty-one times. But they let him run alongside, and carry the letters they didn’t want, the long ones to the far canals. And on Wednesday the old runner who ran the runners gave him a satchel. It wasn’t red. It was brown, plain, a bit worn. It had belonged to somebody once.

“Spare,” the old man said, and went back inside.

Tiger stood on the towpath holding a brown satchel and couldn’t speak.

On Wednesday the window came, the same as it came for everybody.

He was on the lip of the Fifth Lock with a letter when it opened in front of his face. The year turns in five days. On the longest night, the dark will not lift when it should. Keep your lights. Keep your towns. He didn’t fall in. He read it while he was running. He read it twice, along the whole length of the lock, without stopping, and came down on the dry stone at the end. Then he stopped.

He watched Drew’s stream that night with his knees up and his phone in both hands.

Drew had been on an empty road between two hills when it came. He’d stood in the middle of the road and read it. Then he’d said, to eleven hundred people and the wind, I haven’t got a town. And then, after a while, so I’ll find one, and started walking again.

“You haven’t got a town,” said Tiger on the call that night.

“Not yet.”

“Monday. You’ll be on a road on Monday.”

“I’ll be somewhere on Monday.” Drew was sitting at a waystation fire. Tiger could hear it crackling. “Kei’s looking. He’s got the map. He says there’s something four days on from here. An inn at a crossroads. Old place. People live there.”

“Four days. You’ll get there Sunday.”

“Sunday night. If nothing goes wrong.”

“And if something goes wrong?”

There was a pause. The fire crackled.

“Then I’ll be on a road on Monday,” said Drew. “And I’ll keep the road.”

Kei called him on Thursday.

Kei didn’t usually call. Kei messaged, carefully, in full sentences. But on Thursday afternoon, Tiger’s time, he was halfway along the towpath by the Second Lock with the brown satchel when his ear went Kei and Kei’s calm voice said, “Have you got a minute?”

“I’ve got a satchel!”

“I saw. Congratulations.” Kei always sounded like he meant it. “It’s about Drew.”

Tiger stopped on something dry.

“I’ve been doing the map,” said Kei. “Everybody’s map. Everyone tells me where they are and I put it together.” A pause. “Drew’s going to make the crossroads on Sunday night, if he keeps walking the way he’s walking. It’s called the Waymeet. It’s on four roads, in the middle of a lot of nothing, and it’s been there a long time. There’s an old couple who keep it, and some travellers have been staying there. It’s got a wall. Not a big one. A wall.”

“Okay.”

“It’s the only thing with a wall for three days in any direction,” said Kei. “If he doesn’t make it by Sunday, the next place is the waystation he’d be at instead. That’s a fire and a stone.” Another pause. “I wanted you to know. So you’d know what he’s walking toward. He won’t tell you how close it is. You know he won’t.”

Tiger stood on the dry stone at the top of the Second Lock steps with the satchel on his shoulder. The barges went up the hill beside him, slow and patient.

“He’ll make it,” he said.

“I think he will,” said Kei. “He’s very steady.”

“He’s the steadiest person I know.”

“I know,” said Kei. “That’s why I’m telling you and not him.”

On Friday, Sevenlocks started getting ready.

He hadn’t known what it would look like, a canal city getting ready for the longest night. It looked like this. The lock-keepers came out of their cottages, all seven of them, the old men and two old women. They walked up and down the hill together, slowly, looking at every gate and every paddle and every chain, and talked to each other in low voices. The chandler, Old Gaspard, sold every lamp he had by noon and sent his boy down the river for more. The bargemen moored their barges in the long basin at the bottom of the hill, nose to tail, and didn’t take them up. And on every gate, on both ends of every lock, from the river to the Top, somebody hung a hook.

“For the lamps,” said Pell.

“Whose lamps?”

“Ours.” She was sitting on the top beam of the Fourth Lock gate with her legs swinging, the way she’d been sitting the day she’d laughed at him. “On the longest night they don’t close the locks. They open them. All of them. Both gates, all the way up the hill, and they let the river run. All night. Top to bottom.”

“Why?”

“So it doesn’t go still.” She looked down at the water. “Still water’s where things come up. Old Gaspard says. His grandfather said. If you let the canals go still on the longest night, things come up out of the deep bits and walk into the town. So you keep the water moving. All night. Every gate open, the whole hill running like a river.” She looked back at him. “And you can’t run on a gate that’s open. Not across. The gap’s too wide. So the lamps on the gates go out, and nobody can reach them to light them again.”

“Unless somebody runs.”

“Unless somebody runs,” said Pell. “The runners do it. All night. It’s called the lamp run. You take a lamp up the hill, gate to gate, down one side of the lock and up the other. Not across, round. And you hang it, and you go back down for another. And you keep every lamp on every gate lit, all the way up, till the sun comes back.” She swung her legs. “It’s the hardest night of the year. My mum did it. Her mum did it. It’s the runners’ night. Everybody watches.”

Tiger looked up the hill. Seven locks, one above another, like a staircase. Fourteen gates. All open, all night, with the river roaring through every one of them and a lamp on each.

“Can I run?” he said.

Pell looked at him for a long time.

“You’ll fall in,” she said.

“I won’t.”

“You’ll fall in at least once.”

Tiger thought about it honestly.

“Probably once,” he said.

Pell laughed, out loud, delightedly, the way she’d laughed at him the first day from the top of this gate.

“I’ll ask,” she said.

On Saturday she came back and said yes.

Not because he was a runner. He wasn’t, and he never would be, and the old man who ran the runners had made that very clear. But there weren’t many runners in Sevenlocks any more. The young ones went down the river to the big towns, and there were fourteen gates and only nine of them. And he’d run the Top Lock with a letter in his teeth and jumped the gap in the gates while they were opening, and the old man had seen it. Everybody had seen it. Old Gaspard had lost three silver on it.

“Bottom four locks,” said Pell. “Not the Top. You’re not doing the Top. The Top’s mine.” She handed him a lamp, unlit, brass, heavy. “That’s yours. You hang it on the First gate at sunset on Monday, and then you go back down and get another one, and you don’t stop till the sun comes up.”

He held the lamp in both hands, on the towpath, with the river running past.

“I won’t stop,” he said.

“I know,” said Pell. “That’s why.”

On Sunday, Xav left Ice’s party.

Not yet. On Wednesday, he said. But he said it on Sunday, on Ice’s stream, standing in the middle of a causeway in the sun: I’m leaving Moonlight Harvest on Wednesday. Tiger was on the towpath with his phone. He stopped walking. Not stopping on something dry. Just stopped, on the wet stone, and didn’t fall in, because he wasn’t moving.

Some friends of his are coming in. Eight of them. People he’s known nine years.

Nine years. Tiger stood on the towpath and thought about it. About Ice in his swamp, who’d had Xav at his shoulder for five weeks, planning where everybody stood. About Xav standing on a causeway saying Dec told me to. About eight people coming in on the first of January who needed somebody to tell them where to stand.

And about Drew. On a road, day nine, in the hills, waving at sheep. Walking most of the way across the world because Tiger would fall in a river.

It wasn’t the same. Xav was leaving to go and stand with his eight. Drew was walking to come and stand with him. But it was something like the same. It was what you did, with people you’d known nine years. You went where they needed you. Sometimes that was away. Sometimes it was across the whole world, one waystone at a time.

He walked on, carefully, along the wet towpath, and didn’t fall in.

Drew made the Waymeet at a quarter past eleven on Sunday night, Tiger’s time.

Tiger was watching. Of course he was. He’d logged out of Sevenlocks an hour early so he could lie in his egg and watch on his phone with the call open. Fourteen hundred people were watching with him. Drew came over the last hill in the dark, with the moon behind him, on the ninth day of the walk. The road went down into a wide shallow bowl of grass, and there, where four roads met, there was a light.

A low stone building. A wall round it, waist-high, old, with a gate. A big tree in the yard. A lantern over the door. Smoke coming out of the chimney. Behind the windows, the shapes of people moving.

Drew stopped at the gate in the wall with his pack on his back and his hands in his jacket pockets, and looked at it. Then he laughed, very quietly, the way he did, and went in.

An old woman opened the door before he knocked. Small, round, with an apron and a lamp held up, peering at him.

“You’re late,” she said. “Everyone’s late, this week. Come in before it’s properly dark.” She looked him up and down, his knuckles and his pick and his nameplate. “What’s that say?”

“Eighty-eight experience.”

“Of what?”

“Good question,” said Drew, and she laughed, and let him in.

The call came through at midnight.

[ Call · Drew ]

“I’ve got a town,” said Drew.

“I saw.” Tiger was lying in his egg with the lid up and the phone on his chest and his eyes doing something embarrassing. “You’ve got a pub.”

“I’ve got a pub with a wall. Twenty-three travellers, an old couple called Tobias and Nell, a tree, a dog, a lot of beer.” A pause. “They’ve already started on the lamps. Nell says on the longest night you light every window and leave the gate open. For anyone on the road who needs it.”

“Leave the gate open?”

“That’s what she says. That’s what the Waymeet’s for.” Drew’s voice was very calm. “Somebody walks out of the dark on the longest night, you let them in. Even if you don’t know what they are.” A pause. “Especially then.”

Tiger lay there with that.

“I’ve got fourteen gates,” he said. “Well, eight. The bottom four locks. Pell’s doing the Top. I’ve got a lamp. They’re opening all the locks so the water doesn’t go still, and I have to keep running lamps up the hill all night so the gates stay lit. It’s called the lamp run. Sevenlocks remembers who ran.”

“Of course it does.”

“I’m going to fall in.”

“You’re going to fall in,” Drew agreed. “Once.”

“Probably once.”

There was a silence on the call. Not a bad one. The good kind, the nine-years kind, where you could hear the other person being there.

“Monday,” said Drew.

“Monday.”

“Keep your lamps lit.”

“Keep your gate open.”

“If it gets bad,” said Tiger, “I’ll run.”

“Where?”

“To you.”

“It’s fifteen days.”

“I’m fast now,” said Tiger. “I’m a Skimmer. I don’t stop.”

Drew laughed, out loud this time, in a pub on a crossroads in the middle of a lot of nothing, most of the world away.

“Go to sleep, Tiger,” he said.

Tiger went to sleep, with the brass lamp from Sevenlocks on the floor of his mind beside the egg, unlit. Tomorrow it would go on the First gate at sunset. And it would stay lit, gate by gate, lock by lock, all night. However long the night was.
