Live from Elsewhere · Volume 1: Moonlight Harvest

Chapter 6

Moonlight Harvest

4,054 words · 18 min read

Deston went into the water on the fourth tussock.

He’d been warned. Ice had warned him on the first tussock and the second and the third, and Deston had nodded seriously every time and said he understood, and then on the fourth he’d looked up at a heron going over and stepped onto a hummock of grass that was grass on top and nothing underneath. He went in up to his chest with a sound like a dropped bag of shopping.

He came up spitting.

“It’s cold,” he said, in a voice of complete betrayal.

“I told you.”

“You said it was wet. You didn’t say it was cold. Twenty per cent of cold is still cold, Dec.” He grabbed the edge of the next tussock and hauled himself out, streaming, and sat on it, and looked down at himself. “There’s a frog in my shirt.”

There was a frog in his shirt. It climbed out of the collar, looked at the three of them with great disapproval, and jumped back into the fen.

Xav, behind them, hadn’t gone in once. He was doing what Ice had taken half a day to learn: testing every tussock with the toe of his boot before he put his weight on it, slowly, and he’d worked out within ten minutes which ones held. “The ones with the dark grass,” he said, to nobody in particular. “Pale grass is floating. Dark grass has roots.”

Ice looked at the tussocks. Xav was right. He’d been crossing them for a day and a half and never noticed.

“Why didn’t you say?”

“I just did,” said Xav.

They fought two leeches on the way, and it was the first time Ice understood what it was going to be like with three.

It wasn’t just easier. It was different. When he’d fought the leeches alone, every fight had been a sum he did in his head while he was being bitten: how much health, how much stamina, how many more cuts. Now when the first leech came up out of the black water at the side of a tussock, he did what he’d always done and put the slope of his shield in its way. It hit the slope and turned, and in the half second it was turned, Xav was already there.

He hadn’t seen Xav move. One moment Xav had been two tussocks back, and the next he was on the leech’s far side, low, both blades going in where the slick black body bent. He was gone again before it could turn on him.

And from behind them both, Deston’s voice, careful and in time: Let the MANY move as ONE.

“Seven,” said Deston, reading his share. “That’s—hang on, how much is a level?”

“Two hundred at level one.”

“Two hundred?”

“Welcome to the game,” said Ice.

By the time the stones came up out of the reeds ahead of them, grey and leaning in the long gold light, Deston was level two and Xav was nearly there, and all three of them were wet to the waist, and Deston had been bitten by a leech in a place he refused to discuss.

They stepped inside the ring as the sun went down.

He watched them feel it: the doorway feeling, wind to shelter. Deston actually stopped mid-step, one foot in the warm water, and looked back over his shoulder at the stone he’d just passed, as if it had said something.

“It’s warm,” said Xav. He’d crouched and put his hand flat in the water. “Why is it warm? Everything else out here is freezing.”

“I don’t know. Sedge doesn’t know either. Her mother didn’t.” Ice waded in a few steps. “Nothing comes inside the stones. That’s the rule. I spent last night in here and something the size of a bus walked round the outside. It didn’t come in.”

Deston was looking at the herons. All nine stones, all nine birds, standing on one leg in the same patient pose, cut so long ago they were almost gone.

“They’re all looking in,” he said.

“Eight of them.”

Xav’s head came up. “Eight?”

“Tomorrow,” said Ice. “Tonight’s the other thing.”

He told them the rules then, the ones that weren’t his. Pick one in three. Never a whole clump. Twist and lift, root whole. Don’t tell anyone where they grow, not ever, not on stream, not in a forum, not to a friend who promises they won’t tell. He said it like Sedge had said it to him, and they both listened like he had listened to her, and he saw Xav file it away in the place where Xav kept things he’d decided were important.

“The far-glass,” said Deston. “That’s what she calls the stream?”

“Yeah.”

“I love her already,” said Deston.

The moon came up fast, and the first bud opened, and Deston forgot to breathe.

Ice watched it happen to them. He’d been waiting all afternoon to see their faces, and it was better than he’d hoped. The silver light coming down over the reeds and the stones. The tiny clear note as the first white petals came apart. Then the next note, higher. Then three. Then all of them, thirty little lights standing up out of the warm water in the middle of the ring, and the sound of them, like a glass harmonica played in another room.

Xav sat down on a flat stone without seeming to decide to.

Deston didn’t sit down. He stood very still in the water with his book held against his chest, and his head tilted the way it used to tilt in choir when he was listening for the note above his.

“They’re in tune,” he said quietly.

“What?”

“The flowers. Listen.” He was almost whispering. “That one’s a C. That one’s an E. That one’s a G. They’re—Dec, they’re making a chord. They’re all opening on the same chord.” He laughed, and the laugh shook. “Who does that? Who sits in an office somewhere and says, okay, the moon flowers, let’s tune them?”

Ice hadn’t noticed. He’d heard it as pretty. He listened now, standing in the warm water up to his knees, and of course Deston was right. Under the prettiness, every note sat on top of the one before it like a stone on a wall. A chord, ringing on and on in the dark, very softly.

[ Moonbell · Rare
Open to the moon. ]

They picked, slowly, and Xav did the sums.

He did them out loud, crouched in the warm water with a moonbell held carefully in his fist, its root dripping. He’d counted the clump. He’d counted the ones Ice had picked last night and the new buds that had come up where they’d been. Already, after one night. Little and tight and not ready, but there.

“One in three,” he said, “and they come back in about a day. Maybe two. So this ring gives, what. Ten a night? If we’re careful. If we’re the only ones who know.”

“We’re the only ones who know.”

“For now.” Xav turned the moonbell over in his fingers. “This is the most valuable thing in this town. Probably in this whole fen. The day anybody finds this ring, it’s over. They’ll pull every one by the roots by morning and sell them by the fistful, and then there’ll be none.” He looked up. “So we don’t sell these. Ever.”

“I know.”

“No, listen. We don’t sell the flower. If you sell the flower, someone asks where it grows.” Xav put the moonbell in his pack, gently. “We sell what it does. Good draughts. Nobody can trace a draught back to a pond.”

“That’s what I’ve been doing.”

“I know. I’m saying it out loud so it’s a rule.” Xav picked the next one, twist and lift. “We’re going to want rules. There are going to be a lot of people. In a month, there’ll be people out here looking. If we’ve got rules now, we won’t have to make them up in a hurry later.”

Deston was smiling at the pair of them across the moonlight.

“What?” said Ice.

“Nothing,” said Deston. “Just. All these years and you two are still exactly the same.”

They talked, after.

There was nowhere to go until dawn and nothing to do but keep the ring company. So they sat on the flat stones in a row with their feet in the warm water and the moonbells chiming softly between them, and talked, the way you can only talk at night with people you’ve known since you were children.

Deston’s finals were in two weeks, the last paper on the first of December. He was supposed to be revising. He’d revised this morning, he said, from six until a quarter to eight, with the pod sitting at the end of his bed looking at him the whole time like a dog that wants a walk. He was going to revise tonight, after, he said. Probably.

“You’re not going to revise tonight,” said Xav.

“I’m going to try.”

Xav was living in a room in Japan the size of a pod and a half. His lecture this morning had been on something he described only as very important and extremely boring, and he’d ask someone for the notes. It was cold there already, he said. He’d bought a heater. The heater and the pod were on the same socket, and he was a little bit afraid of what would happen if they both came on at once.

“Don’t burn your flat down,” said Ice.

“I won’t burn my flat down. Probably.”

And Ice talked about Malacca. The flat, which was two flats with the wall knocked through, his and his parents’, so it was one long home with two front doors. How quiet it got at night, compared to Singapore. How he could go three days without speaking to anyone his own age who wasn’t on a stream or a call. How he didn’t regret any of it, and also, some evenings, after his parents had gone to bed on their side, he’d sit up in the dark on his and listen to the ceiling fan and think that it might be nice to have someone down his end of the corridor. Someone who’d still be up.

He hadn’t meant to say any of that. It was the moon, maybe. Or the warm water. Or the way none of them were really in the same place and all of them were, right now, sitting on the same stone.

Deston didn’t say anything for a while.

“We’re in the same place now,” he said eventually.

“Yeah,” said Ice. “We are.”

Outside the ring, very far off in the dark, something big turned over in the water. None of them looked round.

At dawn he took them to the ninth heron.

He showed them how its beak pointed out, not in. He showed them the way past it into the reeds, where the water went black and deep. He told them, quietly, as they waded, about the thing that had come up out of that water yesterday morning with eyes a hand apart, and how it had turned left slowly, and how it had very nearly killed him. The water was still this morning. Nothing moved. Respawns in the old fen seemed to take their time.

And then the mist thinned, and there was the mound, and the sunken arch, and the herons cut wing to wing around it, and the slab, and the five hollows.

FIVE VOICES, AND NONE BELOW FIFTEEN.

Xav read it out loud, slowly, and then stood very still.

“A dungeon,” he said. “A five-person dungeon. In a beginner fen. That nobody’s found.” He turned and looked at Ice. “That’s what you went dark for.”

“That’s what I went dark for.”

“Nobody’s found one yet. Anywhere. I read every thread last night. Nobody.”

But Ice wasn’t looking at Xav. He was looking at Deston.

Deston had heard it first. Ice saw the moment: they’d been ten paces from the arch, and Deston had stopped in the water and put his head on one side. Now he was standing right against the slab with his ear almost on the stone, and his eyes were shut.

The singing came through the stone the way it had yesterday. Very faint, very slow, many voices, high and low together, in a language none of them knew. A choir, somewhere underneath them in the dark, singing as if it had forgotten it had ever started.

“There’s a bass line,” said Deston, without opening his eyes. “Under it. Listen. Right at the bottom.” And then, very softly, so softly Ice almost didn’t hear it over the water, he started to hum along.

He was a little flat. He’d always been a little flat on the low notes. It didn’t matter.

Ice didn’t say anything. He stood up to his chest in black water in a swamp at dawn and listened to his oldest friend humming with a drowned choir through a stone door, and he didn’t trust himself to say anything at all.

After a while Deston stopped, and opened his eyes, and they were wet.

“Sorry,” he said. “That was. Sorry.”

“Don’t be.”

“Thirteen years ago we were in the back row,” Deston said. “Mouthing the high bits.”

“I know.”

“And now there’s a choir in a door.” He laughed, a bit shakily, and wiped his face with a wet hand, which didn’t help. “Okay. Okay. Five voices.”

He put his right hand flat into the second hollow. Ice put his into the first. After a second, without a word, Xav put his into the third.

[ Three voices. ]

The slab didn’t move. The singing didn’t stop. But for a moment, Ice was almost sure it changed. As if somewhere underneath them in the dark, the choir had heard someone come in on the right note, and had leaned toward it.

“None below fifteen,” said Xav, reading the line again. “We’re two, two, and three. That’s weeks.” He took his hand out of the hollow. “And two more voices.”

“I know.”

“Who?”

“I don’t know yet,” said Ice. Then, because it was true: “I’ve got an idea for one.”

Deston looked at him, and didn’t ask, and Ice loved him for it.

It was Deston who said they needed a name.

They were wading back toward the ninth heron, slowly, with the light coming up gold behind the reeds, and Deston said it as though it had been sitting in his mouth for a while.

“We should have a name. If we’re going to come back here. If we’re doing this.” He looked at the two of them. “If it’s going to be us.”

He opened the party window as he said it. Ice saw it come up in front of his own face a second later, the little list with three names on it, and a line at the top that had been blank since yesterday.

[ Your party has no name.
Name it? ]

“You found it,” said Deston. “You name it, Dec.”

Ice looked down at his pack. It was full of moonbells, wrapped in linen, a night’s picking. One in three. He thought about standing in warm water at midnight with the chord ringing all round them, and about the three of them sitting in a row on a stone with their feet in a pond, saying things they didn’t say in daylight. He thought about the fen, and the night, and a harvest nobody else knew was there.

“Moonlight Harvest,” he said.

Xav looked at him. “That sounds like a farming game.”

“Xav, we spent all night picking flowers in the dark. It is a farming game.”

“I love it,” said Deston.

When Ice looked up, it was there over their heads, under their names, in small pale letters. Kai, and under that, Moonlight Harvest. Sketch, and under that, Moonlight Harvest. He couldn’t see his own, but he knew it was there too.

It was the stupidest thing. It was just words in the air. He couldn’t stop looking at it.

He went live again from the end of the north walk.

Sedge met them on her porch, and looked at Deston, and at Xav, and back at Ice.

“These are the two,” she said.

“These are the two.”

She looked at Deston’s book. Something in her face moved. “That’s Amon’s book. Or one of them.”

“He gave it—he opened it for me,” said Deston. “This morning. He said nobody’d opened one in thirty years.”

“Thirty-one,” said Sedge. “I remember the last.” She looked at him a little longer, and her face did the thing Ice was starting to think of as the look, the measuring one. It came out the other side as something almost soft. “Well. Don’t go and hold a bridge with it.”

She went inside. Deston turned to Ice with his mouth open.

“How does she know about the bridge?”

“Fernwick’s not that big,” said Ice.

He brewed at Sedge’s fire for the rest of the morning, with Deston and Xav sitting on her porch steps and the stream watching over his shoulder.

He had a rhythm now. Picking, he was fine. He’d been fine since the heron-stones. Grinding was the hard part. Round and even and don’t stop, until the leaves gave up their green with that wet squeak, and your arm burned in the long muscle from elbow to wrist, and your mind tried to wander off and you had to drag it back. Then into the water on the edge of the fire, and stir, and smell. Cut grass. Cut grass. Cut grass, sharper, nearly there. Off. Strain through the square of cloth. Cork, thumb, twist.

Then the moon.

He’d cut a moonbell’s stem with his thumbnail, the way Sedge had shown him, and let a single silver bead fall into the bottle, and watched the green go clear and bright from the top down, like mud settling. Every time, it was the best thing he’d ever seen. He didn’t think that would wear off.

He’d done it eleven times that morning. On the twelfth, it happened.

There was a sound like a spoon on a glass, the little level-up chime, but a different note. A window opened right in front of his face, gold-edged.

[ Apothecary · Lv 2 ]

He had about half a second to feel pleased about that. Then a second window opened under it. It was different from any he’d seen: wider, with a thin bright line of white along its top edge that seemed to hum.

[ Title earned: First Hand
You are the first traveller in Elsewhere to reach Lv 2 in Apothecary.
Draughts you make have a small chance to come out one grade better. ]

He read it twice. He read the first traveller in Elsewhere a third time.

“Oh,” he said.

On the porch steps, Xav’s head came up. Deston said, “What?”

“I’m the first.”

“First what?”

“First in the—“ Ice turned round with the bottle still in his hand. “Apothecary two. First in the world. There’s a title.”

He hadn’t been trying. That was the ridiculous thing. He hadn’t known it was a race. He’d been making potions because people in Fernwick had bitten ankles and nobody else was making them. Somewhere out there, in a thousand towns, there must be people who’d picked Apothecary on day one too. But they’d picked it and gone off to fight rats, or they hadn’t found a Sedge, or they’d found one and she’d told them to get out, or they hadn’t had a ring of stones with the moon in the middle of it. And he’d stood at a fire for two days sniffing pots.

He was laughing. He couldn’t help it.

A minute later, chat saw exactly what he’d seen, from exactly where he’d been standing, when he’d seen it.

“I wasn’t even trying,” he said to them, and to the fen, and to Sedge’s back, which was bent over her own pot at the other end of the table and didn’t turn round.

A small window came up after the others, quieter.

[ Show title beneath your name? ]

He thought about it for about a second.

First Hand under Ice under Moonlight Harvest, floating over his head in every street in Fernwick. Every player who walked past would read it. Every one of them would go and look it up, and find out it meant potions, and wonder where a man in a beginner fen was getting the herbs to be first in the world at potions. Some of them would wonder hard enough to follow him.

“No,” said Ice, and closed it.

They fought until the evening.

He’d thought the title would change something. It didn’t. The rats on the south walk didn’t care, and the leeches on the edge of the old fen didn’t care, and his health bar went down exactly as fast as it had before, and his stamina emptied, and his arm ached. Deston got better at Accord as the hours went on. He got it to sixty, then sixty-four, once, when he stopped reading the line and just sang it, a phrase of three notes that sounded like the start of a hymn, and Xav and Ice both moved like one man.

By the time the window told Ice he’d been in Elsewhere for nearly twelve hours, Deston and Xav were level three and he was level four.

They logged out on the end of Sedge’s porch in a row, with their boots off.

[ Rest well, Ice. ]

He woke up in the egg with the fan turning and his legs gone strange again, and lay there for a while in the dark.

Later, in bed, his phone buzzed once on the pillow beside him at a minute past midnight. Not a message. The companion app.

[ ELSEWHERE
Your remaining calls have expired. ]

He looked at it for a long time. Then he put the phone face down, and lay on his back, and listened to the ceiling fan.

Soon, he’d said.

He’d have to work out how.
