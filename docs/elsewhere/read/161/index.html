Live from Elsewhere · Volume 6: There's a Chair

Chapter 161

Green This Time

3,633 words · 16 min read

Leaka had been loud her whole life, and she’d decided a long time ago that it was a feature.

It wasn’t a choice, exactly. It was just how she came out. Some people came out quiet and some came out medium, and she’d come out in capitals. She’d been told to turn it down plenty of times. And somewhere along the way she’d decided she wasn’t going to. If she was going to be loud, she was going to be loud on purpose. Loud for the people she liked. Loud in their chats, so they’d know she was there. Loud at them to eat something green.

It had worked out fine, mostly. She had a small channel and fifteen regulars who liked it. She had Jared, who’d known her for years and said she was the best person he knew, which she’d told him to shut up about. She had Hana and Milli. She had Curse, who’d said you’re louder than I thought on the first night in the game. And when she’d said I’M EXACTLY AS LOUD AS YOU THOUGHT he’d said yes, that’s what I mean, and she’d thought about that for a week.

And she had CGI.

She’d been in his chat since before anyone. Always there. Day one, twenty-one viewers, a man in a swamp with a sack of herbs, and her in capitals at the top telling him to eat something green. She’d been in his chat for years before that, too, on the old streams nobody watched. She knew him better than almost anyone in that chat. She’d thought she did, anyway.

Then on Sunday night she’d watched him take apart the best PvP player in the game in eleven minutes and forty seconds without breaking a walk. And she’d sat in her room on the other side of the world at five in the afternoon screaming CGI YOU ABSOLUTE at her phone. And under the screaming, in a small quiet place she didn’t let anyone see, she’d thought: I didn’t know.

All those years. All that chat. And she hadn’t known.

They left Merrowgate on Saturday at the first dawn. Five of them now, and a beam.

Odette came to the end of the footbridge to see them off. Merrowgate’s old letterer, who painted every sign in the town and wore a brush behind each ear. She’d taught Curse that a label could make a thing remember what it was. She stood there in her paint-spattered apron with her hands folded, looking at Curse. Then she held something out to him.

A tag. A small plain wooden tag on a loop of string, with one word on it in black ink, in her neat old capitals.

HOME

Curse looked at it for a long time.

“For wherever you’re going,” said Odette. “Tie it to something there. Something that’ll be yours. So it remembers.”

“What if I don’t know what’s mine yet?”

“Then carry it till you do,” said Odette. “It’ll keep. It’s in ink.” She looked at him over the brush behind her left ear. “Write it in pen, boy. Everything that matters.”

“I know,” said Curse, and took the tag. He held it the way he held his inkwell. Then he put it inside his coat, next to his chest.

Leaka watched him do it and had to look at the river for a bit.

The hot-apple woman had come down to the dock too, with a basket on her arm. She’d shouted apples! every forty seconds for five months, and she gave Leaka a free one every morning because Leaka was the only traveller who’d ever shouted it back. She held out the basket now.

“For the road,” she said.

Leaka looked in the basket. Red apples. Shiny, perfect, hot. The same as every morning.

“HAVE YOU GOT ANY GREEN ONES,” said Leaka.

The hot-apple woman stared at her.

“Green ones?”

“GREEN. GREEN APPLES. IT’S IMPORTANT.”

“They’re sour, the green ones. Nobody wants the green ones.”

“I WANT THE GREEN ONES,” said Leaka. “THEY’RE NOT FOR ME. THEY’RE FOR SOMEONE WHO DOESN’T EAT ANYTHING GREEN. IT’S A THING. IT’S A WHOLE THING.”

The hot-apple woman looked at her for a long moment. Then she went back across the footbridge, and came back with six green apples in her apron. They were small and hard and very sour-looking. She tipped them into Leaka’s arms.

“Mad,” said the hot-apple woman. “All of you. Mad as hatters.” She looked at Leaka. “Shout it back for me, wherever you’re going. Somebody should.”

“APPLES,” shouted Leaka, at the top of her voice, from the end of the dock.

And over on the island, in his cage by the carousel, old Gilly the bear woke up. Leaka had never once seen him awake in five months. He lifted his great grey head and looked across the river at them. Then he put it back down on his paws and went back to sleep.

“He’s never done that,” said Curse.

“HE’S SAYING GOODBYE.”

“He’s saying be quiet,” said Curse. But he was smiling. Just a bit. At the corner of his mouth, where you had to know where to look.

The road from Merrowgate to the round town ran north-east for six days, up out of the Riverlands onto a high plain of barley and windmills. Leaka liked it immediately. It was big and flat and full of sky. You could shout across it and nothing would echo back, so you could shout as much as you wanted.

She shouted a lot. Nobody minded. Hana shouted back sometimes. Milli smiled. Jared carried his beam and said y’all at intervals. Curse walked beside her with his ash quarterstaff and his inkwell and said almost nothing at all.

On the second day they found the bridge.

It was over a gorge. Not a big one; Leaka had seen bigger on Ice’s stream, in March, when Nightjar burned the Gullet. But it was deep enough, a cut in the plain with a fast brown river at the bottom. The bridge across it was old wooden planks on old wooden trestles. The middle trestle had been leaning for a long time, and was now leaning a great deal more than a trestle should.

“Nope,” said Jared.

“IT’S FINE,” said Leaka.

“It’s not fine. That trestle’s going. I’m a carpenter now, Leaka. I know a trestle that’s going.” He put his beam down and looked at it with professional disgust. “We go round.”

“How far round?” said Hana.

Jared looked at the map. “Two days.”

Everybody groaned. Leaka groaned loudest.

Curse didn’t groan. He’d walked out to the edge of the gorge and was looking at the leaning trestle with his head on one side, the way he looked at things he was going to write on.

Then he took a blank tag out of his coat. He had dozens of them now; he made them at night by the fire. He uncapped his inkwell, dipped the pen, and wrote one word on the tag in blue ink.

TRESTLE

He climbed down the side of the gorge to where the middle trestle met the rock. Leaka shouted CURSE after him, and he didn’t answer. He tied the tag round the leaning post with a careful knot. Then he climbed back up and dusted his hands off.

The trestle didn’t straighten. It didn’t move at all. It just stopped leaning more. It held, at the angle it was at, the way a thing holds when it’s been reminded what it’s for.

“It’ll hold us,” said Curse. “One at a time. Go slowly.”

Leaka stared at him.

“YOU LABELLED A BRIDGE,” she said.

“I labelled a trestle.”

“THAT’S A BRIDGE, CURSE.”

“It’s one leg of a bridge,” said Curse, mildly. “The rest of the bridge knows what it is. The trestle had forgotten.”

They went over one at a time, slowly. The bridge creaked and held. Jared went last, with his beam, muttering about trestles. At the far side he turned round and looked at the tag on the post for a long time. Then he walked back halfway across the bridge, with the beam, and wedged it in beside the leaning trestle so the two leaned on each other.

“There,” said Jared. “Now it’s got a label and a beam. Belt and braces.”

He came back across. Curse looked at the beam and the tag together, side by side.

“That’s better,” said Curse.

“Thank you.”

“Mine would’ve lasted till the ink did,” said Curse. “Yours will last till the wood does. Together they’ll last till somebody new comes along and needs to cross it.” He looked at Jared. “That’s the best kind of mend. When two people do it.”

Jared looked very surprised, and then very pleased, and then embarrassed. He walked off up the road without his beam, saying y’all to nobody.

On the fourth night, by the fire, Curse said, “You’re scared.”

Leaka was poking the fire with a stick, loudly. She stopped.

“I’M NOT SCARED.”

“You’re quieter,” said Curse.

“I’M NOT—“ She heard herself. She’d been quieter. For two days. She hadn’t noticed. It had crept up on her, a bit at a time, the closer they got. Only a few notches down from capitals, but Curse had noticed. Curse noticed everything that was a few notches down.

She poked the fire.

“They’re all going to be there,” she said. Not in capitals. Almost in lower case. “Everybody. His whole guild. All the people I’ve watched on his stream for months. Gabe and Suzu and Kotori and Dest and Hyv and—everybody. And they all know each other. They’ve all been through things. Long Nights and drowned churches and halls and gates and—“ She stopped. “And they’re all so cool, Curse. They’re all so good at things. Hyv can see seven roads at once. Thea’s a wheel. Kotori heals before you get hurt. CGI does that.” She waved her stick at the sky, meaning the duel, meaning everything. “And I’m just loud.”

Curse didn’t say anything for a while. He turned the inkwell over in his hands.

“What if they don’t want loud?” said Leaka, very quietly. “What if I walk in and I’m too much? What if I’m—“

“You’re exactly as loud as they think,” said Curse.

Leaka looked at him.

“That’s what I said,” said Curse. “On the first night. You’re louder than I thought. And you said—“

“I’M EXACTLY AS LOUD AS YOU THOUGHT.”

“And I said yes, that’s what I mean.” Curse looked at the fire. “I meant it. I’d thought about you for years. In chat. On calls. Hana’s friend who shouts. And then you were standing in front of me on the first night with an axe taller than you, shouting at a bear. And you were exactly as loud as I’d thought. Not more. Not less. Exactly.” He paused. “That’s very rare, Leaka. Most people are a different size in person. You’re the same size everywhere. That’s what I meant.”

Leaka sat very still with her stick.

“He’ll know you’re there,” said Curse. “That’s what loud’s for. That’s what you’ve always used it for. So the people you like know you’re there.” He looked at her. “You’ve been in his chat since day one. Twenty-one people. And you were at the top in capitals, telling him to eat something green. Do you know why he answers you? Every time? Out loud, on stream, with forty thousand people watching?”

“Because I’m loud?”

“Because you were there when it was twenty-one,” said Curse.

Leaka didn’t say anything for a long time. She looked at the fire until her eyes stung, and decided it was the smoke.

“thanks curse,” she said.

Lower case. The third time in her whole life. Curse didn’t say anything about it. He just put another stick on the fire, and looked at the stars, and let her have it.

They came into the round town on Thursday at the second dusk.

She saw the dome first, from a long way off. Great and grey and round, with the ring of lamps round its middle lit for the evening. She heard Hana make a small sound beside her. And then, as they came closer, she saw the crowd on the south steps.

It wasn’t a normal crowd. She’d seen the gate on streams. There was always a crowd on the south steps, strangers waiting to be asked the question. This wasn’t strangers. She could see that from the market. This was people she knew. People she’d watched on stream for months, sitting on the steps of the Hall of Roads in the evening light. As if they’d heard somebody was coming, and come out to see.

They had. Of course they had. Somebody on the eye of the dome had seen five people and a beam on the north-east road two miles out, and shouted it down the stair.

“Oh no,” said Leaka. Very quietly.

“Breathe,” said Curse.

She breathed.

They came across the round market, past the fountain, to the bottom of the steps. And at the top, by the doors, a woman with her hands in her pockets looked down at them.

“Hi,” said Wendy. “I’m the gate. I’m going to ask you one question. It’s not a test.” She paused. “Who’d stand next to you?”

Leaka opened her mouth. She’d thought about this for six days. She’d had a whole answer ready.

“CGI,” she said. “AND CURSE. AND JARRED. AND HANA. AND MILLI.” She stopped. “IS FIVE ALLOWED?”

Wendy considered this.

“We had a man say everybody once,” said Wendy.

“THEN EVERYBODY,” said Leaka. Then: “NO. WAIT. THEN THEM.” She pointed, at the four people beside her. “THEM. THEY’RE EVERYBODY. THEY’RE MY EVERYBODY.”

Somebody on the steps laughed. Not unkindly. Several people did. Leaka went red and didn’t care.

“Fair,” said Wendy. “Curse?”

Curse looked at Leaka. That was all. He didn’t say anything. He just looked at her, and then back at Wendy.

“Her,” said Curse.

Leaka had to look at the fountain for a bit.

“Jared?”

“Alice,” said Jared. “And Leaka. And Milli.” He shifted on his feet. “And whoever needs a roof.”

“Milli?”

“Hana,” said Milli. Simply.

“Hana?”

Hana was holding something in her closed hand. Leaka knew what it was. A tiny gold star from the end of a wall.

“Milli,” said Hana. “And—“ She looked up at the dome. “And a lot of little guys. Holding lights.” She went pink. “Sorry. That’s not—that’s a long story.”

“Gate’s open,” said Wendy, and stepped aside.

And then Leaka was inside, and it was loud. Louder than her, for once. She hadn’t known that was possible.

The Hall of Roads was full. People were coming down the stairs and out of the banner room and in from the cellar and the market. And people were saying her name. Not Leaka from Ice’s chat. Leaka. Aleaka. It’s Leaka. People she’d only ever seen through a screen, coming toward her across a stone floor under a ceiling of silver lines.

A huge man with a hammer got to her first.

“YOU’RE THE ONE WHO SAYS EAT SOMETHING GREEN,” shouted Gabe.

“YOU’RE THE ONE WHO SHOUTS DEC,” shouted Leaka.

“I DO SHOUT DEC.”

“I KNOW, I CAN HEAR YOU FROM ENGLAND.”

Gabe picked her up. Axe and all. Off the floor. She hadn’t been picked up since she was about nine. She shrieked, and he put her down, grinning, and said she’s PERFECT to somebody behind him.

The somebody behind him was a small girl with a white staff, who looked up at Leaka with enormous eyes and said, “Hi. I’m Kotori. You said eat something green in December, on the night of the plank, and I laughed for the first time in the game.” She went pink. “Thank you.”

Leaka didn’t know what to say to that. So she hugged her. Kotori said sorr— and stopped, and said thank you again, into Leaka’s shoulder.

Then a girl with pink gills and a trident.

“ARE YOU LEAKA,” shouted Xola.

“ARE YOU XOLA,” shouted Leaka.

They looked at each other. Two people who’d been the loudest in every room they’d ever walked into, in the same room for the first time.

“I like her,” said Xola, to the tall quiet man with the glaive behind her.

“I know,” said Slep.

And then a girl with gold horns and a long thin tail and glasses pushed up in her hair. She looked at Leaka sideways, carefully, the way she looked at everything, edge to edge.

“You’re the one who says eat something green,” said Hyvred.

“YOU’RE THE ONE WHO HITS HIM,” said Leaka.

Hyvred’s tail did something.

“Yes,” she said.

“GOOD,” said Leaka. “SOMEBODY SHOULD.”

Hyvred stared at her. Then her mouth twitched, very slightly, at one corner.

“I like her,” said Hyvred, to nobody, and went away up the stair.

He came out of the banner room last.

She’d known he would. He always came last into a room and went to the far end of it. He came out into the Hall with his shield on his back. Black hair, teal streak. The thin red line under his eye from Sunday was healed now, but she could see where it had been. She knew his face, a bit, from a city with no name and a hot apple. Mostly she knew his hands, and his shield, and what he saw. She’d been watching what he saw for years.

He looked at her across the Hall.

“Leaka,” said Ice.

“CGI,” said Leaka.

She walked across the Hall to him in a straight line. She had something in her hand. She’d been holding it since the market.

She held it out.

A green apple. Small and hard and very sour-looking, from the bottom of the hot-apple woman’s apron, six days ago.

“IT’S GREEN THIS TIME,” said Leaka.

He looked at it. Then he looked at her. Then something happened to his face that she’d seen perhaps twice on his stream in all the years. Not the small almost-smile at the corner of his mouth. The other one. The rare one, all the way.

He took the apple, and bit into it.

It was clearly extremely sour. She watched him register it. She watched him decide not to make a face. She watched him eat the whole thing, in front of the whole Hall of Roads. Core and all. Without stopping. Looking at her the whole time.

“Thank you,” said Ice, when he’d finished.

“YOU’RE WELCOME.”

“That was very sour.”

“IT WAS GREEN.”

“I know,” said Ice. “That’s why I ate it.”

Behind him, somebody started laughing. Then somebody else. Then the whole Hall. Gabe and Xola and Kotori and the tall woman with the shield, who must be Thea. A dozen people she didn’t know yet. And at the counter, an old woman with spectacles, who said hm in a tone that sounded almost pleased.

“SO,” said Leaka, loud, because the Hall was loud and she wanted to be heard over it. “THIS IS CGI’S GUILD.”

“It’s Kei’s,” said a man at the counter, with a map under his arm, mildly.

Leaka looked at him. That must be Kei. A kind, worried face.

“I KNOW IT’S KEI’S,” said Leaka. “IT’S CGI’S IN MY HEART.”

Kei opened his mouth. Then he closed it again. Then, slowly, he started to laugh as well.

“Okay,” said Kei.

“It’s Kei’s,” said Ice, beside her.

“ICEY BOI,” said Leaka, “LET ME HAVE THIS.”

And from across the Hall, from the banner room doorway, a voice shrieked “ICEY BOI?” It was Xola, in a voice of the most enormous delight.

“ONLY I SAY ICEY BOI,” shouted Leaka, turning round.

“I’M GOING TO SAY ICEY BOI.”

“YOU ARE NOT.”

“ICEY BOI ICEY BOI ICEY BOI—“

Ice put his hand over his face.

Leaka stood in the middle of the Hall of Roads, under the silver lines, shouting at a girl with gills about a nickname. A huge man with a hammer was laughing, and a girl with horns was watching from the stair, and an old woman behind a counter was saying hm. And somewhere just behind her she could feel Curse, very still, with his inkwell, letting her have it.

She was exactly as loud as they’d thought.

It turned out that was exactly the right size.
