Live from Elsewhere · Volume 9: Properly

Chapter 234

A Day

3,590 words · 16 min read

Milli had said you do it on the roof, and Hana had said you do it at the long table, and that was how he ended up standing.

“You’ll cry,” he’d said, on Saturday night, on the bench, with the round town lit up below them.

“I’ll cry,” Hana had agreed. “I’ll start crying at we’ve and I won’t get to the rest. Leaka will start screaming and Gabe will start crying and I’ll never say it.” She’d put her head on his shoulder. “You say it. You say things properly. You said this is somewhere on the longest night and everybody in the round town has been quoting it at me for eight months.”

“That was an accident.”

“Then do another accident,” said Hana.

So on Sunday at the second dawn, in the banner room, with the long table full, Milli stood up.

He’d never stood up at the long table. Not once. He’d sat at it for more than a year, at Hana’s five by the window, with his arm along the back of her chair. He’d watched people stand. Kei with his maps. Gabe, all the time, for no reason. Deston, in July, when he came home from Tallow Green with Chernn and couldn’t say anything at all and just stood there red to the ears until the whole room went off.

He’d never once had a reason to.

He stood up now. In the middle of Sunday, with Ama Corrie at the far end of the room with her broom, halfway down the long table, doing the chairs. Under, not round.

She stopped. She looked at him over her spectacles.

The room went quiet. Not all at once. In a wave, from the window outward, the way a room goes quiet when one person stands up who never stands up. Leaka first, beside Hana, with both fists already halfway to her mouth. Then Curse. Then Jared, who knew, and put his face in his hands. Then the rest of the window end, and the middle, and the first twenty, and Moonlight Harvest’s nine.

Ice, in the first of the nine, with his feet on the rung of the next chair. And Hyvred beside him. And the cup in his hand, which he put down on the table, without looking.

At the far end, by the north door, Isa Corrie in her chair, awake.

Sixty-one chairs, most of them sat in. The netloft’s eight empty, pushed in, the way Ama Corrie liked them, a long way off in Masmark. And Rafi’s, by Bram and Joy, empty too.

Milli looked at all of them.

He’d thought about what to say on the bench for most of Saturday night. He’d thought of a lot of things. He’d thought about starting with the bench, and Jared, and the pole. About the longest night, and the star, and somewhere with a view. About how long one day had been.

He didn’t say any of it.

“We’ve picked a day,” said Milli.

Nobody said anything.

For about three seconds, the banner room was completely silent. Sixty people and an old woman in a chair and Ama Corrie with her broom, not one of them moving. Milli stood at Hana’s five with his hand on the back of her chair, and she was looking up at him. Already crying. She’d lasted until picked.

Then Leaka screamed.

Not into Curse’s sleeve, this time. Out loud. Into the banner room. The highest, longest sound Milli had ever heard a person make, in the game or out of it. It went up into the beams of the ceiling and round them and came back down.

And the room went off.

Gabe first, of course. On his feet at Moonlight Harvest’s end, both hands in the air, shouting MILLI, MILLI, MILLI as if Milli had scored something. Rachael beside him saying finally to nobody, the way she always did, and then, unexpectedly, again, louder. Deston on his feet beside Chernn, laughing, red, with her hand in his. Kotori with both hands over her mouth saying something that was probably sorry and was definitely crying. Tiger, who’d climbed onto his chair for no reason. Thea, with her arms round Xola. Gigi, a pace off, smiling at the ceiling. Kei at the head of the table, writing something on his map.

“WHEN,” shouted Leaka. “WHEN, WHEN, MILLI, WHEN IS IT—“

Milli looked down at Hana.

She nodded. She couldn’t speak. She was laughing too hard, and crying, and doing both.

So he told them.

A month. A day. A year. The one they’d picked on a bench on the roof in four minutes, after years of one day. Out there, where it would be. He said it plainly, the way he’d said this is somewhere, to sixty people at a long table in a round town.

And then it was theirs. All of theirs. Not just his and Hana’s any more.

He’d thought that would feel strange. It didn’t. It felt like the cat’s-paw on the arm of the bench. Pressed down with two hands on one stamp. A mark made by two, where everybody could see it.

It went on for a long time.

People came down the table. That was the thing about the round town; when something happened, people came. Gabe, who hugged him without asking and talked the whole time. Deston, who put a hand on his shoulder and said you’re slow, and then laughed at himself. Chernn, who said nothing at all and held out a candle. A plain white one, straight, which she’d brought down the table in her hand and which Milli took without knowing what it was for.

“For the window,” said Chernn. “On the day. Out there’s where it’ll be. But in here, on the day, put it in a window.” She looked at Hana. “Mother Dipp says. A candle in every window.”

Hana took it from Milli and held it and cried harder.

Suzu came, which surprised everybody. He came down the table from the stool by the north door where he’d been sitting beside Isa. He stopped in front of Milli and looked at him. Suzu didn’t say much. Deston said he’d said four hundred words in all the years he’d known him.

“Good,” said Suzu.

And went back to the stool.

Kotori came with tea. Everybody knew she would. She put a cup down in front of Hana and one in front of Milli and didn’t say sorry once. She said thank you, very clearly, for no reason, and went back to the kitchen. Milli heard her through the door, crying into the kettles.

Ice didn’t come down the table.

He didn’t need to. Milli knew that about him, by now. He stayed where he was, in the first of the nine, with his feet on the rung. He’d said good on the stair on Wednesday, to Hana, and that was the most he’d ever said to anybody about anything. He wasn’t going to say it again in front of sixty people.

But Milli caught his eye, across the banner room. And he nodded. Once.

Beside him, Hyvred hit him on the arm.

Milli saw it from the window end. Not hard. The way she did. And heard her, because the room had gone quiet again for a moment and Hyvred was never quiet when she was angry.

“You KNEW,” said Hyvred.

“I didn’t know,” said Ice.

“You knew something. You came down off the roof on Tuesday with sawdust on your shoulders. You’ve never had sawdust on you in your life—“

“I carried a pole.”

“For THIS.”

“I didn’t know what for,” said Ice. “Jared said it was for Alice. So I carried it.” He paused. “The bottom end.”

Hyvred stared at him for a long moment. Then she put her face in her hands, on the table, and said something into them that Milli couldn’t hear. And Ice put his hand on the back of her head, between the gold horns, very gently, and left it there.

Milli looked away. The way everybody in Constellation looked at the harbour when two people were being two people, though there was no harbour here. Just the window, and the round market below it, and the fountain going.

Leaka had decided something.

Milli could tell. She’d stopped screaming. That was always the sign. She’d stopped screaming, and gone very still, and was looking at Curse beside her. And Curse had a pen out and was looking back at her the way he looked at her when she was about to say something he’d have to write down.

“In here too,” said Leaka.

Everybody looked at her.

“On the day,” said Leaka. “Out there, you get married. Properly. With everybody, wherever, I don’t care. I’m coming if you let me, I’ll fly, I’ll walk, I’ll swim.” She pointed at Hana and Milli across the table. “And then after. That night. You come in here. And we have a roof.”

Jared, beside her, took his face out of his hands.

“Leaka,” he said.

“A roof, Jarred,” said Leaka. “Six hundred lanterns. The same ones. They’re in boxes in the Hart’s cellar. You put them up for Chinese New Year and the wind came and Isa put her hand on it.” She was crying again, suddenly, the way Leaka did, all at once with no warning. “You put them up again. On the day. Over the whole market. And we all stand under it.”

The banner room was very quiet.

Jared looked at her. Then at Hana. Then at Milli.

“I’ll need a new middle pole,” he said. “I made the old one into a bench.”

“Then cut a new one,” said Leaka. “You’re a woodcutter. That’s what you do.”

“Y’all,” said Jared, very quietly.

“That’s not a no,” said Leaka.

“It’s not a no,” said Jared. And Hana put her face in Milli’s shirt and laughed until she couldn’t breathe.

Curse wrote it down. Milli saw him. On the back of his hand, in blue, small, the way he wrote things that mattered. Roof. On the day. He looked at it for a moment and then held his hand up so Leaka could see it. And Leaka read it and burst into tears all over again.

Ama Corrie came down the table when it was quieter. With her broom.

She’d been standing halfway down the long table the whole time, beside Gigi’s chair, chair twenty-one, with the broom in her hands. Not sweeping. Watching. Now she came on down, slowly, past the first twenty and Thea’s six, to the window end. To Hana’s five. And stopped in front of Milli.

She looked at him over her spectacles for a long time.

“Out there?” she said.

“Out there,” said Milli.

“Not in here.”

“No. Out there’s where it’ll be.” He thought about it. “In here’s where we picked it.”

Ama Corrie looked at him. Then at Hana. Then she looked past them both, out of the window, at the round market and the fountain. At the dome above them. The two hundred and twelve going up round the inside of it, to the eye, and a bench beside the eye with Somewhere on the back.

“The old book does pairs,” said Ama Corrie. “When two came who were going to be one, one day. They signed close.” She looked at Milli. “You signed close. Last year. So close the tails of your letters touched the tops of hers. I saw.” She sniffed. “I didn’t keep you a space. You didn’t need one. You took it.”

Milli didn’t know what to say.

“It’s not mine to write,” said Ama Corrie. “Out there. The book doesn’t go out there.” She looked down at the broom in her hands. “But it’s Sunday.”

She held out the broom.

Milli looked at it.

He knew what it meant. Everybody did. They’d all heard the count. Three times. Forty years nobody asks. Then three times in a month. It’s catching. Kestrel at the bottom of the Seven Below, that’s four. Gigi, the night Isa came home, that’s five. Ice on Christmas, six. Hyvred in April, seven. And Ice again, the same morning, with his hand out, palm up. Eight.

Nobody had asked since April.

He hadn’t asked. She was holding it out to him anyway.

“Can I help?” said Milli.

He said it because you said it. Because it was what you were supposed to say, when Ama Corrie held out the broom. Because Ice had said it, with his hand out, palm up, and Hyvred had said it, with a broom she didn’t know how to use.

Ama Corrie looked at him for a long time.

“Nine,” she said.

And then, before he’d taken it, she turned her head and looked at Hana. Still sitting, with Chernn’s white candle in her hands, and her face wet.

Hana looked back at her.

“Can I help?” said Hana.

Ama Corrie’s face did something Milli had never seen it do. Not in a year and more of Sundays. Something at the corner of her mouth, very small. Exactly like Ice’s.

“Ten,” said Ama Corrie.

They swept all sixty-one together.

One broom. The fen-reed one, Sedge’s head on it, KEPT on the tag in blue ink. His hand on the handle, and hers above it. Not taking it from each other. Just both. Down the long table chair by chair. Under, not round. The way Ama Corrie did it, the way Ice and Hyvred had done it in April.

Hana’s five, first, their own. Wood shavings under Jared’s. Blue ink under Curse’s, a drop. A great deal under Leaka’s, always, crumbs and thread and a bit of red paper from Chinese New Year she’d never thrown away. Under Hana’s, nothing. Under his own, a scrap of brown leather, a corner of the square he’d cut the cat’s-paw from.

He picked it up and put it in his pocket.

Thea’s six. Wool under Chi’s, from the sheep. The middle. Kei’s, with a ring on the table from a cup. Gigi’s, chair twenty-one, a hawthorn twig. Riverbank’s eight and Rafi’s, empty, a long way off; under them anyway.

Moonlight Harvest’s nine. Pencil shavings under Dennis’s. Wax under Kai’s. And under Chernn’s, beside it, wax too, the same wax, as if the two chairs had been leaking it toward each other all summer.

And under the first of the nine, with Ice still in it and Hyvred beside him with her face in her hands: fen grit. Still. From five hundred miles away, a year and a half ago.

Ice lifted his feet off the rung so they could get the broom under. He didn’t say anything. He just held his feet up, very seriously, in the air, while Milli and Hana swept under his chair together. Hyvred looked up out of her hands and saw him doing it. And laughed so hard she nearly fell off her own.

The chair by the north door, last.

Isa was awake in it. She’d been awake the whole time, watching. With the jar on the table in front of her, glowing through the clay, and a row of Chernn’s candles beside it, lit.

They stopped in front of her with the broom.

“Can we?” said Hana.

Isa looked at them. At the two of them, with one broom between them. Hana with Chernn’s white candle still in her other hand; she hadn’t put it down. Milli with a scrap of leather in his pocket.

“Is there a view?” said Isa.

Milli looked at her.

“Where you picked it,” said Isa. “The day. Is there a view?”

“On the roof,” said Hana. “By the eye. Jared made a bench.”

“From the pole,” said Isa. “The one I put my hand on.”

“Yes.”

Isa Corrie looked at them for a long time, with her bright black eyes.

“My grandmother’s grandmother,” she said, “sat on that roof. Before she went north. She said you could see every road from it.” She folded her root hands on the blanket. “She said it was where people went, in the round town, when they wanted to say something they meant. Because you could see everything from there. So you knew it was true.”

Nobody said anything.

“Hm,” said Isa. “Sweep.”

They swept under her chair. With her in it. Road dust, still, and a little heather, and nothing else.

At the second dusk, Hana’s five went up the two hundred and twelve together.

All of them. Leaka at the front, running, shouting back down the stair at Curse to hurry up. Curse at a walk, behind her, with his pen. Jared with his tools on his back, though there was nothing to build. And Hana and Milli at the back, slowly, hand in hand. Minding the third step.

The bench had room for two, close. Three if one of them was Leaka, Jared had said.

They got five on it.

Nobody could explain how afterward. Leaka was mostly on Curse’s knee. Jared was mostly on the arm, the right one, the one without the cat’s-paw. Hana and Milli in the middle, very close, so close the tails of everything touched. And Chernn’s white candle on the parapet in front of them, lit, because Hana had said a window and there wasn’t one up here. So the parapet would have to do.

They sat there and watched the round town light up below them. The seven roads going away. The ninety-four, north, all lit. The White Hills, a white line on the edge of the world. And the sky going from gold to dark all the way round.

Nobody said anything for a long time. Not even Leaka.

Milli looked north. Past the ninety-four lamps on the backs of the grey stones, going away over the moor. Past the last of them, where the road went on into the dark and the lamps didn’t. Out there somewhere, a long way past the edge, there was a hill in still water with a ring of stones on it, and nobody in it. Isa had told the long table. The middle’s gone.

The longest night was four months off. It would come here. It always came here. And this year, Isa said, it would know why.

He’d held a triangle by the well with Hana and Jared all of the last one. Eleven hours. And somewhere in the middle of it, at midnight, she’d said one day.

“We’ll be at the well again,” he said. “In December.”

“The three of us,” said Hana. “Nobody alone.”

“And then,” said Milli, “after.”

She looked at him. She knew what he meant. The day. Out there, after the longest night, after the winter, when the year came round. A month, a day, a year.

“After,” said Hana.

Then she took the star out of her pocket.

It wasn’t lit. It was warm. Milli could feel it from here, through her hand, through his.

“A day,” said Hana.

“A day,” said Milli.
