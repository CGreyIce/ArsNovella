Live from Elsewhere · Volume 2: The Long Night

Chapter 55

Every Single Time

2,828 words · 12 min read

On Saturday morning, the ninth of January, Ice went to the gym on his own.

Nobody told him to. That was the point. For eleven days Suzu and Gabe had walked him down in the lift at seven, every morning, the way you’d walk someone to school. Gabe on the weights, saying out on the push. Suzu on the mat, counting, correcting one thing at a time. On the twelfth morning, Ice woke up at a quarter to seven, before his alarm. He lay there for a minute listening to the fan, and the flat, and Gabe snoring through two walls. Then he got up and put his shoes on and went down by himself.

The little room on the gym floor was empty. It smelled of rubber. The bar was fixed high on the wall where it always was.

He did seven pull-ups.

He hung there after the seventh, with his arms shaking and his feet off the floor, and thought about a plank on four posts in the dark, and a shield, and the oldest thing in the fen leaning on it. Then he dropped, and stood there breathing, and laughed out loud at nothing in an empty gym at five past seven.

When he got back upstairs, Suzu was in the kitchen with the kettle on. He looked at Ice, sweating in the doorway, and at the clock, and back.

“You went,” he said.

“I went.”

“On your own.”

“On my own.”

Suzu poured two cups of kopi-o into the two mugs that didn’t match.

“Good,” he said. And then, after a moment, the corner of his mouth going up: “Seven?”

“How do you know it was seven?”

“You’ve got a seven face,” said Suzu, and handed him the cup.

Kotori’s parents had said yes on Tuesday.

She’d told them on Monday night, at the kitchen table, in the flat on the fourteenth floor. She’d told it to Ice afterwards on the call in such detail that he felt as if he’d been there. Her dad had taken his reading glasses off and said what about your degree, exactly the way she’d said he would. She’d said remote, around lectures, I’ll still go to everything, I promise. Her mom had said are they nice, also exactly the way she’d said she would. Kotori had said yes. Then she’d tried to explain how nice, and told them about Suzu buying Gabe a pillow, and started crying, and her mom had started crying too. Her dad had put his glasses back on and said I think that’s a yes, then.

He’d added one thing. Go and see them first. In person. Before you sign anything. And eat with them.

So she was coming to Malacca.

The bus from Singapore got into Melaka Sentral at twenty-five past twelve, and all three of them went to meet it.

Ice hadn’t planned that. He’d meant to go alone. But Suzu had been at the door with his shoes on when Ice went to find his keys. Gabe had come out of the spare room behind him, still pulling a T-shirt over his head, saying “I’m coming, obviously I’m coming.” So all three of them stood in the arrivals area of a bus terminal in Malacca at twenty past twelve on a Saturday, in a row, like a delegation.

“We look like a boy band,” said Gabe.

“We look like debt collectors,” said Suzu.

“We look like three people meeting a friend,” said Ice.

“That’s what I said,” said Gabe. “A boy band.”

The bus came in. People got off. A family with a lot of bags. Two students. An old man with a box of durians, carefully. And then, a little way back, waiting politely for everyone else, a small girl with a backpack nearly as big as she was.

She looked exactly like herself. That was the thing. Non-VTubers looked like themselves in the game, unless they changed something, and Kotori hadn’t changed anything. Black hair cut at the jaw, with a fringe. A round face. Round dark eyes, behind a pair of glasses she didn’t wear in the game. A pale blue T-shirt instead of a pale blue robe. No staff, no bell. She stepped down off the bus onto the pavement, and looked round, and saw the three of them standing in a row by the barrier, and went completely still.

Then she went bright red.

“Hi,” she said, from ten feet away. “Sorry. Hi.”

Gabe hugged her first. Of course he did. He walked straight across the ten feet and hugged her, backpack and all, lifting her slightly off the ground. She made a small surprised noise into his shoulder. “You’re Gabe,” she said, muffled. “You’re the hammer. Sorry. Hi.”

“I’m the hammer,” said Gabe, and put her down.

Suzu didn’t hug her. Suzu didn’t hug people. He stood in front of her for a moment, very tall, looking down at her with his half-shut eyes, and then he nodded once.

“Kotori,” he said.

“Suzu.” She looked up at him, a long way up. “You’re so tall in real life too. Sorry. I didn’t think you’d be—sorry.”

“Everybody says that,” said Suzu. The corner of his mouth went up. “You held the back of the church with me. On your rock.”

“I did,” said Kotori. “I did, didn’t I.” She went redder.

Then she turned and looked at Ice.

He’d thought about what he’d say. All the way to the bus station, in the car. Something about the rock, or the post, or the twelve hours. Something that would mean what it should mean. He stood there and looked at her, a small girl with a backpack and glasses in a bus station in Malacca. She’d kept him alive for twelve hours in the dark, standing somewhere nothing could reach her, healing him before the cold had finished arriving. He couldn’t think of a single word of any of it.

“Hi,” he said.

“Hi,” said Kotori. “Sorry. I’m—sorry. This is so weird. You sound exactly the same.”

“So do you.”

“I say sorry a lot.”

“We noticed,” said Ice, and she laughed, a beat late, exactly the way she did in the game, and then she hugged him too.

They took her to the mamak.

It was the one on the corner by the shoplot, the one Ice and Suzu had sat in on the night he died in the river. Plastic tables under the five-foot way, white strip lights, a television showing football nobody was watching. A man at the front slapping roti dough thin as paper. Kotori sat at the end of the table on a plastic stool, with her backpack at her feet. She looked at everything, the roti man and the football and the uncle at the next table reading a Chinese newspaper, the way she looked at a fight from her post. Seeing all of it.

“Roti canai,” said Gabe, ordering for her before she could say anything. “Teh tarik. Trust me.”

“I don’t know what—“

“Trust me,” said Gabe.

She trusted him. The roti came, flaky and hot, with a little bowl of dhal. She tore a piece off the way Gabe showed her and dipped it and ate it, and her face did something Ice hadn’t seen it do before.

“Oh,” she said, with her mouth full. “Sorry. Oh.”

“Right?” said Gabe.

“It’s so good. Sorry. It’s so good.” She tore off another piece. “This is what you eat? After? Every time?”

“Not every time,” said Suzu. “Sometimes it’s chicken rice balls. At the place by the river.” He considered. “Gabe has the big one.”

“There’s a big one?”

“There’s a very big one,” said Gabe, with feeling.

Kotori looked round the table at the three of them, Gabe and Suzu and Ice. Gabe was already on his second roti. Suzu was stirring his teh tarik very slowly. Ice was just watching her eat. Her eyes did the thing Ice had seen them do in the ring, when he’d said every single time.

“Sorry,” she said, very quietly, to her roti. “I’m just really happy.”

Nobody said anything. Gabe pushed the dhal toward her.

Vivian had said she’d see them at three, on a Saturday, which she never did for anybody.

She was at the teak desk when they came up the narrow stairs, with the orchid back in its place and the slatted light across everything, and a laptop open. She looked up at the four of them in the doorway. Ice and Suzu she knew. Gabe she’d met once, on the Monday after Christmas, for nine minutes, to collect a one-page CV, which had been one page. And a small girl in glasses with a backpack, standing slightly behind the others, looking at the photograph of Vivian’s father on the wall as though she knew she ought to be polite to it.

“Miss Hayashi,” said Vivian.

“Yes! Sorry. Hi. Yes.”

“Sit down, please.” There were four chairs now. Vivian had borrowed two. “You’re American.”

“Yes. Sorry. From California. I live in Singapore. My dad’s work.”

“And you’ll work remotely. From Singapore. For a Malaysian company.” Vivian turned a page. “That, Miss Hayashi, is the simplest thing anybody has asked me to do this month. You don’t need a pass. You’ll be a contractor, the same as Mr Deston and Miss Lim. You’ll invoice monthly. Part time, around your studies.” She looked up. “You may visit Malacca whenever you like. You may eat whatever you like. While you’re here, you may not work. That’s a different pass, and I’m not doing another pass this month.”

“I won’t work,” said Kotori quickly. “I’ll just eat.”

“Good,” said Vivian, and very nearly smiled. She slid a sheet of paper across the desk. One page. Plain sentences. Game operations support (healing, positioning, and team play), remote. Part time. Paid monthly in arrears. And a number at the bottom. Kotori read it and put her hand over her mouth, the way Dee had on the screen, and looked at Ice. He nodded. Say yes to the number.

“Yes,” she said. “Sorry. Yes.”

“Sign here, please. In black.”

She signed. Small, round, careful handwriting, like a birthday card.

“While we’re here,” said Gabe.

Everyone looked at him.

He was sitting in the second borrowed chair, a big solid man in a T-shirt, with his hands on his knees, looking at Vivian. He’d been looking at her very steadily for some time, Ice realised, the way he looked at a bar before he lifted it.

“Yes, Mr Gabe?”

“When do I get shares?”

There was a short silence in the long cool room.

Ice turned his head and stared at him. Suzu, on the other side of Kotori, didn’t move at all, but the corner of his mouth went up.

“Mr Declan,” said Vivian slowly, “hasn’t asked me about you yet.”

“He’s going to,” said Gabe. “He asked about Suzu on profit day. Suzu told me. Well, he didn’t tell me. He said not zero in his sleep, and I worked it out.” Suzu looked at the orchid. “So Dec’s going to ask about me. He was always going to. I’m just saving everybody the time.” He looked back at Vivian. “Yes. Obviously. How many?”

Ice started laughing. He couldn’t help it. He sat in the old chair across the teak desk and laughed until he had to wipe his eyes, and Vivian looked at him over her glasses with an expression of deep, patient suffering.

“Mr Declan,” she said. “Are you asking?”

“I’m asking,” said Ice, when he could. “He’s right. I was always going to.”

Vivian picked up her pen.

“Then it’s the same as Mr Suzu,” she said. “On the approval of your pass, and not one day before. Your application was lodged on the twenty-first of December. It’s processing. Some weeks.” She wrote something. “Then a transfer, stamp duty, the register.”

“Same as Suzu,” said Gabe.

“The same procedure.”

“No, I mean the same number.”

“You don’t know Mr Suzu’s number,” said Vivian.

“Then the same as whatever that is,” said Gabe comfortably.

Vivian put her pen down again. She took her glasses off and put them on the desk beside the orchid, and looked at the three of them in a row across the desk. Then she looked at Kotori, who was sitting in the middle with her hands in her lap, very still, trying to be invisible.

“Gentlemen,” she said. “You’re both Singaporean. Between the two of you, if your shares come to thirty per cent or more, this company becomes a joint venture, and Mr Declan has to put another hundred thousand ringgit into its paid-up capital.”

“He’s got bank statements,” said Gabe.

“I’ve seen his bank statements,” said Vivian. “That isn’t the point.”

“What is the point?”

Vivian was quiet for a moment.

“The point,” she said, “is that I’d like Mr Declan to think about it, rather than say yes because you asked him in a voice like that.” She looked at Ice. “Agree a number. When his pass comes through. In the car, the way you did with Mr Suzu. Then tell me, and I’ll make it tidy.”

“In the car,” said Ice.

“In the car,” said Gabe. “Fine.” He sat back. “I’ll win in the car.”

“Nobody wins in the car,” said Suzu, to the orchid.

Vivian took a fresh card from the drawer and wrote on it in her small neat capitals, and clipped it to the front of a folder that already said MR GABE.

SHARES (MR GABE) — ON APPROVAL OF PASS. NUMBER TO BE AGREED (IN THE CAR). MR GABE: “YES. OBVIOUSLY.”

Then she looked at Kotori.

“Miss Hayashi,” she said. “You’re a contractor. You don’t get shares. You get paid.” A pause. “I’m telling you now, so that you don’t feel you ought to ask.”

“I wasn’t going to ask!” said Kotori, scarlet. “Sorry. I wasn’t. I’m just happy to eat.”

“I know,” said Vivian, and this time she did smile. It was small and brief and went away again quickly, like something she wasn’t used to keeping out. “That’s why I told you.”

They showed her the shoplot afterwards.

She stood in the doorway of the back room and looked at the two white eggs side by side, his and Suzu’s, with the fan going round above them and the folding table between them. The kettle, the empty boxes from the mamak. Then they took her upstairs to the front room on the first floor, to the third egg by the window, Gabe’s. She stood in front of it for a long time.

“It’s like my room,” she said eventually. “My egg’s in the corner of my room. In Singapore. With a sticky note on it.” She went pink. “It said three hundred and fifty. For the tea.”

“I know,” said Ice. “The eel man told Marta.”

“Sorry.”

“Don’t be.”

She sat down on the floor of the front room, between the egg and the window, with her back against the wall and the lights of Melaka Raya coming in through the blinds. After a moment, Gabe sat down on one side of her, and Suzu on the other. Ice sat down beside Suzu. The four of them sat on the floor of an empty room in a shoplot in Malacca on a Saturday evening, with one white egg humming beside them, not saying anything.

“Sorry,” said Kotori, after a long time. “This is the best day.”

They had chicken rice balls for dinner, at the place by the river. Gabe had the big one.

Kotori stayed at a small hotel on Jonker Street that her dad had booked, with very precise instructions about the lock on the door, which she followed. In the morning, the three of them drove her back to Melaka Sentral for the ten o’clock bus. She’d be home by one, and in the egg in the corner of her room by two. And at eight that evening, at the game’s dusk, a man with a hammer and a shield was going to walk into Fernwick for the first time. She’d be on her post.

At the barrier, she turned round and looked at the three of them, standing in a row again.

“Every single time?” she said.

“Every single time,” said Ice.

She went up the steps of the bus. Halfway up, she turned round again.

“Sorry,” she said. “I mean—thank you. Sorry. Thank you.”

The bus pulled out. They stood there until it was gone round the corner.

“She’s going to come back every weekend,” said Gabe.

“Good,” said Suzu.
