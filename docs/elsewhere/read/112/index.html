Suzu opened his eyes at the foot of the grey hill at twenty to nine, and the four of them stood up out of the grass.

Nobody said anything about Singapore. Nobody asked. Kotori looked as if she wanted to throw her arms round him, and didn’t. Deston looked at him the way Deston looked at people, quietly, as if he were counting something about them that only he could hear. Sheng Yang nodded once.

Ice didn’t say anything either. He’d said it already, in four letters, at lunchtime. good. He looked at Suzu standing in the mist with the jar blazing through the canvas on his back, and Suzu looked back at him.

“Okay,” said Suzu.

“Okay,” said Ice.

He turned his stream on.

He didn’t say much. He never did. He said, “We’re going in,” and “It’s Suzu’s tonight,” and nothing else. Then he put his hand flat on the stone door with the nine herons round it, and pushed.

The seven lanterns were still lit. Blue, steady, in their stone hands, down the gallery, the way they’d left them. The cold didn’t come in their light. It came in between, faint, in his left wrist. Ice felt Suzu feel it too, beside him. And on the eighth beat of Deston’s count, Kotori’s bell went round all five of them, warm, and put back what it took.

At the first dark alcove, Suzu stopped.

He didn’t loosen the lid a hair. Ice watched him. He’d watched him do it seven times on Wednesday and Thursday, a hair at a time, with his thumb, as if the lid might bite. Tonight Suzu took the lid off, and held the jar up in front of the dark lantern, and said, quite clearly, “Seventy-three.” And one light went up out of the open jar, and into the lantern, and it lit. Then he put the lid back on, and walked on.

Ice read it at the edge of his eye and didn’t say anything. He didn’t trust his voice to.

The Unwatched came at the twentieth alcove, the way they always did, out of the dark past the last lit lantern. Eleven of them. More than ever. Thin and grey and faceless, all of them, every one, for the jar.

“Anchor,” said Ice.

Steadfast hummed. The first two hit the shield and turned. He felt them, both, at fifty, exactly where they landed. Left forearm, left shoulder, the old places. He’d put it on fifty for this. So he’d know where.

And Suzu swung.

That was the thing Ice would remember. Not the fight. On Thursday Suzu had curled over the jar on the floor of the gallery with his eyes shut and let them hit his back. Tonight he stood up straight in the middle of the gallery with the jar on his back and the scythe in both hands. He took the first three across the middle in one long flat stroke, the wide row, the way he’d taken things in a drowned church in December.

“Five,” counted Deston. “Six.”

Sheng Yang was two paces from Kotori, and then two paces from Suzu, and then two paces from both. Ice didn’t know how he did it. The long sword came down.

On the eighth beat Kotori rang. Vigil went round them, warm, under all of it.

“Undertow.”

The pull went out from the shield, and the ones that were left came off Suzu and into it. Sheng Yang went through them from behind, and it was over.

Suzu didn’t look at the jar. He didn’t need to. He walked to the next dark alcove and took the lid off and said, “Sixty-nine,” and one went home.

He lit the rest of the gallery in an hour.

One at a time. Alcove after alcove, on both sides, down the long straight dark toward the hall. The number got smaller every time, out loud, in his flat quiet voice, the one he’d used on the bridge. Sixty. Fifty-nine. Fifty-eight. Not shaking. Not stopping. Not looking back at the ones behind him, lit, in their stone hands.

Ice walked beside him the whole way and didn’t say anything. He thought about a woman in a chair on the eleventh floor of a hospital in Singapore, saying there’s a light.

At the thirty-second alcove, the last in the gallery, Suzu said, “Forty-nine,” and the light went home. The whole gallery was lit behind them now, blue, all the way back to the door. Thirty-two faceless watchers with their lanterns up. The cold didn’t come anywhere in it any more.

Ahead, the round hall. Dark. Forty-eight more watchers round its walls.

And in the middle, facing north, with its back to them, the Last Watcher.

“I hold him,” said Ice. “In the middle. As long as it takes. Suzu, you light. Go round the walls. Don’t stop. Don’t look at me.” He looked at Sheng Yang. “Two paces from Suzu tonight.”

Sheng Yang looked at Kotori.

“Go,” said Kotori. “I’ve got Dest. Dest’s got me. Go.”

Sheng Yang went.

“Kotori,” said Ice. “Everything on me. Everything you’ve got. And Vigil on the eighth for all of us. Don’t stop.”

“I won’t.”

“Dest.”

“I’m counting,” said Deston. He opened the book.

Ice walked out into the hall.

The Last Watcher turned round.

Slowly, the way it had on Thursday, one stone foot, then the other, the floor shaking under him. It turned until it was facing south, toward the gallery, toward the blue light behind them, toward the jar on Suzu’s back. Its great dark lantern swung in its stone hand like a bell.

It took one step toward Suzu.

Ice stepped into its way.

“Anchor.”

The lantern came down. A cathedral door, slamming. He’d known it would, and he’d planted his feet, and it still drove him back across the stone three yards on his heels. He felt it land, at fifty, all of it, in the shield arm and the shoulder and all the way down his back. Exactly where. Every inch of exactly where.

He didn’t make a sound. Behind him Kotori’s Mend came onto him hard, warm, and his health came up. Sixty. Seventy.

The Last Watcher looked past him at Suzu, running along the wall of the hall toward the first dark alcove with Sheng Yang two paces behind. It took a step toward them.

Ice stepped into its way again.

He couldn’t move it. He knew that. Undertow went into it like a wave into a cliff. He couldn’t hurt it much, either; it was ten times his size, and it mended in the dark. But he could stand in front of it. He could do that. He could stand in front of it, every time it moved, for as long as it took. He’d done it on a plank at the narrows in a fen for twelve hours, on the longest night, with the cold going into his arm like a river.

“Forty-eight,” said Suzu, somewhere off to his left, and the first wall lantern lit.

It took forty minutes.

He didn’t remember most of it afterwards. He remembered the shape of it. The lantern coming down. The shield. The floor under his heels. Kotori’s Mend, hard and warm, every time, a second before he needed it, because she’d always healed where things were going to lean. The bell on the eighth beat, Vigil, under everything. Deston counting, steady, never louder than he needed to be, the whole forty minutes. One. Two. Three. Four. And Suzu’s voice from the walls, going round the hall, further away and then nearer and then further again, saying numbers.

Forty-four. Forty-three.

The hall got lighter.

Not all at once. A lantern at a time, round the walls, blue, in stone hands. And every time one lit, the Last Watcher got a little slower. Ice felt it in the shield before he saw it. Each blow came down a little less hard. Once, when it caught him wrong across the ribs and took him to nineteen per cent, he thought, I know exactly where that was. Kotori had already put the Mend there.

Sheng Yang came across the hall once, two paces from nobody, because Suzu was at the far wall and safe and the Last Watcher had its back to him. He brought the long sword round on the back of the stone knee, in the same place as Thursday.

The crack went up the leg. Ice watched it, from under the shield, and waited for it to close.

It didn’t.

The light from twenty blue lanterns lay across the floor of the hall, and across the cracked stone leg. The dark that had leaned in on Thursday to mend it wasn’t there any more. The crack stayed open.

“It stays,” said Sheng Yang. He didn’t shout. Sheng Yang never shouted. But Ice had never heard him sound like that. “Declan. It stays.”

“Back to Suzu,” said Ice.

Sheng Yang went back to Suzu.

Twelve. Eleven. Ten.

Three. Two.

“One,” said Suzu, at the far wall. The forty-eighth lantern lit.

The hall was blue.

All of it. Every alcove round the walls lit, every faceless watcher holding up its lantern, the light pouring in from all sides. Behind them, through the mouth of the gallery, thirty-two more, blue all the way back to the door. Eighty lanterns, lit. Eighty watches kept.

The Last Watcher stopped.

It had its great dark lantern raised over Ice, for another blow, and it stopped. It stood there in the middle of the blue hall, enormous, faceless, with its lantern up. Ice stood under it with his shield up and his health at thirty-one per cent and his left arm a single long ache. He waited for it to come down.

It didn’t come down.

Very slowly, the Last Watcher lowered its lantern.

Not at him. Past him. All the way down, the whole great iron lantern big enough to stand in, down to the floor of the hall. It set it down on the stone in front of it, gently, without a sound. And then it knelt. One knee, then the other. The way the Waking Heron had knelt on the twenty-fourth cairn a month ago, facing north, when it was done.

It bent its faceless head. Toward the far wall, where Suzu was standing with the jar.

Suzu walked across the hall.

Nobody said anything. Deston stopped counting. Kotori’s bell was still. Sheng Yang stayed where he was, two paces behind, and then didn’t follow any further. In the corner of Ice’s eye, thirty-nine thousand people had stopped typing.

Suzu walked across the blue hall to the kneeling watcher and its dark lantern on the floor. He stood in front of it, and looked down at the jar in his hands.

One light. The last. Small and blue and very bright, alone in the bottom of a sweet jar he’d carried since December.

He took the lid off.

He didn’t hold the jar up this time. He just held it open, in both hands, against his chest. The last light came up out of it slowly. It hung in the air in front of his face for a long moment, the way the first one had on Wednesday, as if it were looking at him.

“Go on,” said Suzu.

Ice had heard that before. On a set of steps in a fen, from an old woman with a pipe, to a boy with a black sword. Go on, boy.

The light went. Across the small space between Suzu and the kneeling watcher. Through the little horn door of the great iron lantern on the floor.

The lantern lit.

It wasn’t blue. It was gold. Gold and white, bright as the hour before sunset, so bright that for a moment Ice couldn’t see the hall. The Last Watcher stood up with the lantern in its hand, slowly, all the way up, and held it high over its head. Then it turned round, one stone foot and then the other, until it was facing north. With its back to them. Where it had always been facing. Where it was supposed to face.

With its lantern lit, for the first time in nobody knew how long, keeping its watch.

Suzu sat down on the floor of the hall.

He sat down where he was, in front of the Last Watcher, with the empty jar in his lap. He didn’t say anything. He looked at the jar. It was just a jar now. A plain sweet jar with a lid, empty, the glass a little warm.

Ice walked across the hall and sat down beside him. His left arm didn’t work very well. He sat down anyway.

In the drowned church, in December, at the end, Deston had sat down on the bottom step of an altar in the water with the book in his lap and his head in his hands, laughing and crying at the same time. Ice had sat down beside him and put an arm round his shoulders, and neither of them had ever said anything about it.

He put his right arm round Suzu’s shoulders.

Suzu didn’t move. Then, after a while, he leaned. Not much. Just enough.

“They’re home,” said Suzu.

“Yeah.”

“I let go.” Suzu looked at the empty jar. “All of them. I let go.”

“You did,” said Ice.

Suzu was quiet for a long time.

“It’s not empty,” he said eventually. He turned the jar over in his hands. “It feels like it should be empty. It isn’t. It’s—“ He stopped. He didn’t have the word. Suzu always had the word. “It’s ready,” he said at last. “For something.”

Ice didn’t say anything. He didn’t need to.

Behind them, Deston had sat down on the floor too, with the book closed in his lap, not counting anything at all. Kotori was ringing her bell, very softly, over and over, not for anything, just ringing it. Sheng Yang was standing in the middle of the blue hall with his long sword point down on the stone and both hands on the hilt. He was looking up at the Last Watcher’s lantern, gold, high over its head, facing north.

“Kestrel’s still at her boss,” said Sheng Yang, after a while, quite quietly. He didn’t say it like a man who’d won anything. He said it like a man reading the weather.

Nobody answered. Nobody needed to.

He didn’t go to sleep for a long time. He lay in the egg in the back room in Malacca with the lid up, in the dark. His left arm ached in all the places it had been hit, exactly where. He didn’t mind. He’d put it on fifty so he’d know.
