Live from Elsewhere · Volume 5: Ask Me Again

Chapter 121

Slow

1,937 words · 8 min read

PenguChi did things slowly, and she’d stopped apologising for it a long time ago.

Not because she couldn’t do them fast. Because slow was how you saw things. If you went fast you saw where you were going. If you went slowly you saw everything on the way, and everything on the way was usually the interesting part. She’d streamed for years to thirty people who liked that about her. They liked sitting in a room with somebody who wasn’t in a hurry. She liked them for liking it.

So in a town of a hundred and twelve waterwheels, all going at once, she walked very slowly, and watched.

She watched Thea learn to turn. She watched it happen over three days on the bank of a mill channel. First Thea fell over, then she stopped falling over, then she started sending things. A claw off her shield to the left, where Slep was. A claw off her shield to the right, where PenguChi was, with the spear. PenguChi learned to stand exactly where Thea would send things. It wasn’t hard. You just had to watch Thea’s feet. Thea’s feet always told you, about half a second before the shield did.

She watched Xola watch Thea. All the time, out of the corner of her eye, even in the water, even laughing. Especially laughing.

She watched Xyro fall in channels. Five, by Monday. He fell in one walking backwards to look at a wheel, and one reaching for a fish, and three for no reason anyone could identify. Every time he came up grinning and said I meant to do that. Tif, every time, said you didn’t, and helped him out by his collar.

She watched Tif. Tif was the one PenguChi watched most, because Tif was doing something hard and not telling anyone. Tif had watched every minute of a game for five months and knew everything about it, and none of that had gone into her hands yet. She’d ring her staff too late, and go quiet, and ring it again on time, and not say anything. Her ears went flat every time she was late. PenguChi didn’t think Tif knew they did that.

And she watched Slep’s sheep.

“What’s its name?” she asked him, on Sunday, on the bank.

Slep looked at the sheep. The sheep was standing in the shallows of the mill pond eating a reed, very calmly, about two paces from Xola.

“Sheep,” said Slep.

“That’s not a name.”

Slep thought about it for a long time, the way he thought about everything.

“It’s not a name yet,” he agreed, and went back to sharpening the glaive.

She’d taken the spear because it was long.

That was all. She’d stood in front of the rack in the little wooden room and looked at everything, the way she looked at everything, and taken the longest thing on it. Long things let you stand back. Standing back let you see. And if you saw everything, you knew exactly when to put the long thing in.

She’d taken Skinning and Tailoring because she liked making clothes and she wasn’t squeamish. And on Sunday afternoon she found out that Slep had taken Skinning too. They sat on the bank of the mill pond together for three hours, skinning crayfish, not talking. It was the best afternoon she’d had in a year.

“You’re good at this,” said Slep, eventually.

“So are you.”

“I’ve done it before,” said Slep. “In other games. Monster ones. You learn where the joints are.”

“I haven’t,” said PenguChi. “I just watch where you put the knife.”

Slep looked at her for a moment. Then he nodded slowly, as if she’d said something he’d been waiting for somebody to say.

“That’s the whole thing,” he said. “Watching.”

Ice was the thing she didn’t understand.

Not him. She barely knew him. She knew him the way you knew somebody who came to your stream sometimes. That guy who comes to stream and is good at Words on Stream. That had been him, for years, in her head. A name in her chat, a few nights a month, who’d turn up out of nowhere and get every word in the round before anyone else. Then he’d say gg and leave. Then he’d raided her, once, with eleven people, and said hi in her chat, and nothing else. She’d liked him. She’d never thought about him much.

She didn’t watch his stream. She’d tried, in December, because Thea had said you have to see this. She’d lasted forty minutes. A man standing very still on a plank in a swamp, saying almost nothing, with thousands of people typing at a speed she couldn’t read. She’d turned it off. She hadn’t understood it, and she hadn’t felt bad about not understanding it. Some things weren’t for you.

But everybody else loved him.

Not a little. Thea had been happy for the first time in a year, and PenguChi had a feeling some of that was from watching him. Xola called him Icey with the face she made about the people she’d never let go of. Tif had been his head mod for five months, and had stayed up until two in the morning writing commands so his chat would behave without her. Xyro said the swamp guy the way some people said the names of footballers. Even Slep, who didn’t watch either, had said he’s good people on Wednesday night. Slep didn’t say that about anyone.

So on Monday evening, by herself, at the top of the town by Dorrit’s sluice gate, PenguChi opened his stream. Slowly. On purpose. Just to see if it was different now.

He was in a swamp. Of course he was. Brewing something, at a table, in a room on stilts with herbs hanging from every beam, with an old woman grinding something across from him. He wasn’t saying anything. Thirty thousand people were watching him not say anything.

She watched for an hour.

She still didn’t understand it. But somewhere in the hour she stopped trying to. She just watched a man measure something one drop at a time. He’d pour it, and wait, and then measure again, never hurrying, never once looking at the camera to see if anyone was still there.

She thought, slowly: Oh. He’s slow too.

Not the same slow. But slow. Somebody who did a thing all the way through, one drop at a time, whether anybody was watching or not.

She turned it off after an hour. She didn’t turn on notifications. But she thought she might watch again, sometime. Maybe on a Monday.

The classes came on Wednesday, all of them within an hour of each other, on the bank of the mill channel. They’d been killing crayfish there all week, and the crayfish had stopped being a problem around Tuesday.

Thea’s came first. She was in the middle of turning a claw to the left when the window came up in front of her, and she nearly fell in the channel reading it.

[ Class: Fulcrum
One on whom the weight turns.
Turn: what pushes you, you may send. ]

Thea read it three times. Then she put her hand over her mouth. PenguChi watched her decide not to cry about it on stream, and then cry about it anyway.

Xola’s came while she was underwater. She came up out of the mill pond with her gills standing straight out and a window hanging in the air in front of her face, dripping.

[ Class: Tidewalker
One who goes where the water goes.
In water, you are where the water says you’ll be. ]

“I’M A TIDEWALKER,” said Xola, to Millrace.

Slep’s came standing next to her, on her left, where he always stood. He read it and smiled. PenguChi had seen him smile perhaps four times.

[ Class: Second
One who takes the other side.
Whoever you stand beside, you know where they’ll be. ]

Xola read it over his shoulder. She didn’t say anything. She just put her forehead against his arm and left it there.

Xyro’s came while he was in a channel. Of course it did. His sixth. He came up spluttering with an arrow in his hand and a window in front of him.

[ Class: Ricochet
One whose arrows find a second way.
Off water, off stone, off iron: your arrows remember the way home. ]

“THAT’S WHY THEY KEPT COMING BACK,” said Xyro. “TIF. THAT’S WHY—”

“I know,” said Tif. “I’ve been watching them come back for a week.”

Tif’s came last. Late, the way her rings had been late all week. She was standing behind everybody with her staff, ringing it, on time for once, on time for the whole fight, when the window came up.

[ Class: Hearth
One who keeps others warm until they can keep themselves.
Kindle: a little warmth, given early, lasts. ]

Tif read it, and her ears came all the way up, straight, for the first time in a week.

“Given early,” she said, very quietly. “Not late. Early.”

“You were early today,” said PenguChi.

Tif looked at her.

“All of today,” said PenguChi. “Every ring. I was watching.”

Tif’s ears went pink at the tips, which PenguChi hadn’t known cat ears could do.

PenguChi’s came last of all, after everyone else’s. She’d been standing very still on the bank with her spear, watching the five of them read theirs. Then she looked down and found hers had been there for a while, waiting, and she hadn’t noticed.

[ Class: Sentinel
One who waits longer than the thing she is waiting for.
Patience: the longer you hold, the truer your first strike. ]

She read it slowly. Then she read it again.

The longer you hold, the truer your first strike.

She didn’t say anything. She didn’t need to. She stood on the bank of a mill channel in a town of wheels with her long spear and her window and her five friends, and smiled. Slowly, the way she did everything.

She turned her stream on that night to tell her chat. She’d been streaming every night since Saturday, to more people than she’d ever had. Six hundred, seven hundred. She’d found it a bit much. She’d said so, calmly, and her chat had said we’ll be quiet, and mostly they had.

She looked at the number.

1,004.

She’d never had four digits. Not once. She looked at it for a long time, the way she looked at everything, until it went to 1,011 and then 1,023.

“Oh,” said PenguChi.

That was all. Her chat waited. They knew her. They knew it would take a moment. They were good like that, the best chat in the world, and she’d tell them so in a minute.

“Oh,” she said again, and smiled. Slowly. “Hello, all of you.”
