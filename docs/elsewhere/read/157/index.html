Chapter 157

When It's People

4,114 words · 18 min read

The Fell Country was the first place in the game that reminded Ice of somewhere real.

He couldn’t have said where. Nowhere he’d been, exactly. But the moment they came over the last rise west of the round town on Monday, and the land stood up in front of them, he thought: oh. Fells. Long bare hills like the backs of sleeping animals, brown and green, with grey rock showing through like bone. Narrow valleys cut deep between them, and in the bottom of every valley a stream, white and loud, going somewhere in a hurry. And the sky very big and very close, with weather in it. You could watch the weather walk toward you across three valleys before it arrived.

[ The Fell Country · Lv 36–40 ]

“There’s nobody here,” said Calvin, turning round in a circle. “Why is there nobody here?”

“Because it’s thirty-eight,” said Deston, “and it’s a long way from anywhere, and the streams try to drown you.”

“Oh,” said Calvin, delighted. “Good.”

Eleven of them came over the rise. Moonlight Harvest, all nine. Drew, who’d come because Ice had asked and Drew said yes to most things that involved walking. And PenguChi, at the very back with her long spear upright, who’d come because she’d asked.

She’d kicked him in the shin about a mile out of the round town. Without warning, without breaking stride, she’d just come up beside him on the road and done it. Then she’d dropped back to the end of the line.

“What was that for?” Ice had said.

“In advance,” said PenguChi. “For the week.”

She’d done it twice more since. Once on Monday night, when he stood up from the fire too fast. Once on Tuesday morning, for no reason he could identify. He’d stopped asking. He’d decided it was a sort of weather.

Monday to Thursday was Moonlight Harvest, the way it always was.

He liked it. He didn’t think about liking it, mostly; it was just how he was. But in the Fell Country, with the weather walking toward them, he noticed it. The nine of them had a shape now. They’d had it since May, since Gabe brought his three north and they’d been nine in one place for the first time. Ice at the front. Gabe at his right with the hammer, Rachael two paces behind Gabe. Suzu wide on the left with the scythe, cutting rows. Sheng Yang at Ice’s shoulder for the Ender’s Cut. Deston in the middle, holding everyone together with Accord, counting. Kotori where nothing could reach her and she could see all of them. Calvin going ahead and coming back. And Dennis with his notebook, standing next to whoever needed it, writing down what everything cost.

They took the first valley in an afternoon. There were things in the streams, Ghyll Hags, long and pale and mostly hair, that came up out of the white water and tried to pull you under. And things on the rocks with too many legs that came down the scree at you in a rattling rush.

[ Ghyll Hag · Lv 37 ] [ Scree-Runner · Lv 37 ] ×6

He held the stream. That was most of it. He stood in the water to his waist with his shield up and held the crossing while the others came over behind him. When something came up out of the white water at him he took it and told Deston where it had hit. Right hip. Low. From under. Deston passed it on through Accord, so everybody knew where to look.

It was good. It was the kind of good he didn’t need to think about. He knew where everybody was without looking. He knew Gabe would be there before he asked, and Suzu’s row would come in exactly when the scree emptied. When he went to fifty-one per cent, Kotori’s heal would land on him half a second before he knew he needed it.

He didn’t think about the round town much.

That was on purpose. He’d decided it on the morning the six came up the south road, standing at Ama Corrie’s counter with Gigi looking at him. It’s not mine. He didn’t know most of what had happened between Thea and Gigi, then or now. He knew enough. He’d known enough for a long time, without ever being told. A thing had happened, years ago, and it had been dark, and he hadn’t been there for it. He’d been somewhere else, for his own reasons, and hadn’t known until much later. And now it was being mended. Slowly. By the people it belonged to.

The best thing he could do was not be in the room.

So he’d gone to the Fell Country. He stood in streams. He didn’t think about it.

He thought about it a bit, at night, by the fire. Not much.

There was one strange thing. It came on Wednesday night, and he didn’t understand it.

He answered. Thirty-seven. PenguChi had kicked him. Drew watched his feet. Kotori said to tell Tif the cabbage worked, whatever that meant. It was a normal conversation. Xola asked if he was eating. He said yes mum, because she’d sounded like one, and she told him not to call her mum. Normal.

Except that twice in the middle of it, she’d said icey, and he’d said ?, and she’d said nothing.

He looked at that for a while, by the fire, after she’d gone.

Xola didn’t say nothing. Xola had never once in all the years he’d known her said nothing when she meant something. She’d say the something. Usually loudly. Usually in capitals. If Xola said nothing twice, there was something so big she was physically sitting on it.

He thought about it, and decided it must be Thea. Thea and Gigi. Something had happened, maybe something good, and Xola was trying not to tell him because it wasn’t hers. That would be like her. She’d learned that, recently, from somewhere. Not mine. Half the round town was saying it now.

“Something’s happened,” he said to Sheng Yang, who was on watch with him.

“Mm,” said Sheng Yang.

“Xola said nothing. Twice.”

Sheng Yang looked at him for a long moment across the fire. Sheng Yang had known him nine years. Sheng Yang had very good eyes for things that weren’t monsters, when he bothered to use them.

“Huh,” said Sheng Yang. And went back to looking at the dark.

Ice decided it was probably Thea, and went to sleep.

On Friday, Moonlight Harvest went home and the five stayed.

That had been the plan. MH for the first few days, then mix it. He’d told Kei that at the counter. Kei had looked at him in the slightly worried way Kei looked at most things. Then he’d written it on his map with a little dotted circle round the Fell Country, so he’d know where Ice was.

So on Friday at the noon dusk, Gabe and Rachael and Calvin and Dennis and Suzu and Deston walked back east toward the round town. Six in a line, Gabe shouting SEE YOU THURSDAY, DEC, DON’T DIE IN A STREAM back up the valley. And Ice stood on a rock with four people who weren’t, strictly, his.

Sheng Yang, who was. Sort of. He’d been MH since the trials, but he’d been Ice’s friend for nine years before that, from a different life. Kotori, who was. Drew, who wasn’t, who was Kei’s, who was everybody’s. And PenguChi.

PenguChi was nobody’s. PenguChi was Thea’s, a bit. Mostly she was her own.

“Okay,” said Ice. “Five.”

“Five,” said Drew, and looked at his feet.

“Don’t.”

“I’m just looking.”

“It’s very off-putting.”

“I know,” said Drew peacefully, and kept looking.

It was different with five. He’d known it would be. That was why he’d asked for it.

With Moonlight Harvest he didn’t have to think. He knew where everybody was. That was good, and it was also a trap. You stopped seeing people when you knew where they’d be. You saw the shape instead. He’d been seeing the same shape since May. He wanted to see some different ones.

So on Saturday they took the second valley, the five of them. He watched.

Drew fought off his shield. That was the first thing he learned. Drew didn’t hold anything. Drew stood half a pace behind Ice and to his left. When something hit Ice’s shield, Drew was already moving. He’d slip out round the edge of the shield into the space the thing had left when it committed, and cut it, and be back behind the shield before it knew he’d gone.

[ Counter · Slip and Answer ]

“You lean on me,” said Ice, after the third time.

“I lean on the hit,” said Drew. “You make the hit. I go where it came from.” He looked at Ice’s feet again. “You stand very still, when something’s coming. Even for you. Like you’re giving it time to commit.”

“I am.”

“Why?”

Ice thought about it.

“So you know where it’s going to be,” he said. “If I’m still, it’s still. Then you know.”

Drew looked at him for a long moment.

“Huh,” said Drew. And from then on, every time something came, he was out round the shield half a second faster.

Kotori and PenguChi found each other on Saturday afternoon. Ice watched it happen and didn’t say anything.

They were the two quietest people he knew. Kotori stood where nothing could reach her and saw all five of them. PenguChi stood behind everybody and watched the line. They’d been in the same party for one morning and they’d already arranged themselves without speaking. Kotori high on the left, PenguChi low on the right, so that between them they saw everything. Neither of them said anything about it. They just did it, and by the afternoon they were finishing each other’s warnings.

“Left—“

“—two—“

“—low, behind the rock—“

“—mm.”

Sheng Yang, beside Ice, watched this with an expression of deep respect.

“They don’t talk,” he said.

“They don’t need to.”

“I like them,” said Sheng Yang. Which, from Sheng Yang, was a speech.

And PenguChi watched Ice.

He knew she was doing it. She wasn’t hiding it. She’d said, in the banner room, I want to see him be slow. Up close. For a week. And she’d meant it. She walked at the back of the line and watched him walk. She stood behind everybody in a fight and watched him hold. When they stopped for the night she sat on the other side of the fire and watched him do nothing.

On Sunday she said it.

They were in a ravine, the deepest one yet, with the stream so loud you had to shout. Ice was waist-deep in it, holding the far bank. Things with too many legs were coming down the rocks above them in a rattling flood, and he was taking them one at a time on the shield, slowly.

Slower than he needed to.

He knew he was doing it. He didn’t think anybody else did. He was holding each one on the shield a second longer than he had to, letting it scrabble there, before he put it in the water. A second, and another. Behind him, on the bank, very still, PenguChi had her long spear held low and was building.

Every second he held, she got another second. He’d worked it out on Saturday. Her strike got truer the longer she waited, and she couldn’t wait if there was nothing to wait for. So he gave her something. He held the things on his shield, still, so she’d have a target that wasn’t moving. He held them until she was ready.

[ Sentinel · Patience · held 61 s · First strike ]

The spear went past his ear into the biggest Scree-Runner in the ravine. It stopped. Everything stopped, for a second. Then it went down the stream in two directions.

“You’re doing it again,” said PenguChi.

Ice looked round.

“Being slow,” said PenguChi. She’d come down the bank to the water’s edge and was looking at him with her calm round face. “On purpose. You held that one for twelve seconds. You didn’t need to. You held it so I’d have something to hold for.”

He didn’t say anything.

“You did it for Drew yesterday,” said PenguChi. “You stood still so he’d know where it was going. You did it for Kotori. You stood where she could see you, so she’d know when to heal. You do it for everybody.” She tilted her head. “You’re not slow. I thought you were slow, in May. I was wrong. You’re the fastest person here. You just wait for everyone else to be ready.”

The stream went past his waist, very loud.

“Somebody has to,” said Ice.

PenguChi looked at him for a long time.

Then she waded into the stream up to her knees, which was as far as she could go without being swept off her feet. She kicked him in the shin, under the water, quite gently.

“That one wasn’t in advance,” she said. “That one was for that.”

Kotori was on stream with him that night. She sometimes was. She’d sit by the fire in the edge of his picture, with her tea, and not say anything, and the chat would type KOTORI and she’d go pink.

They took the last valley on Tuesday and Wednesday. Something big lived at the top of it, in a hanging valley under a waterfall, that came down at dusk once a day. It was a sort of great grey goat with horns like a ram’s that had grown into a cage round its own head. It knocked Drew off a ledge and PenguChi into a pool, and then it put Ice to thirty-one per cent, which was the lowest he’d been all week.

[ The Old Ram of Hardfell · Lv 39 · Elite ]

They killed it on Wednesday at the second dusk, under the waterfall, in the spray. Sheng Yang’s Ender’s Cut, at the end, from two paces off. Kotori’s heal landed on Ice at the exact moment the horns did, so they took eleven per cent instead of thirty.

[ Ice · Lv 38 · Holdfast · Undertow ]

“Thirty-eight,” said Drew. “That’s the second rank. If they ever open.”

“They’ll open,” said Ice. “Every dome’s got a ceiling.”

They walked back on Thursday. Ten days, which he’d said to Kei would be nine, because he always said nine when he meant ten. Deston knew that. He’d known it since they were thirteen. Ice had a vague feeling Deston might have told somebody the opposite, just to see.

They came in at the second dusk, by the south road, because everybody did.

He’d tell it afterwards, if anybody asked, as a short story with no ending. Wendy asked him who’d stand next to him and he said them. And then Hyv walked across the Hall in a straight line and hit him twice in the arm, which she always did. Except that this time, for the first time, Xola made a noise in a doorway like a kettle left on too long, and Thea turned to face the wall. And PenguChi told him he was very slow, and he asked about what, and she said yes.

He hadn’t understood any of it. He’d laughed anyway, because PenguChi had clearly made a very good joke. He’d sat down in his chair at the end of the long table and put his feet up and thought, something’s happened. Probably Thea. Probably good.

The notice came at midnight.

He was still in his chair. Most of the guild was still in the banner room, because Gabe was telling the story of the Old Ram for the third time and adding a horn each time. Ice was half-listening, half-asleep, with his feet up.

And then, all round the room, people stopped.

You could tell a notice from a window by how everybody stopped at once. A window was one person going still. A notice was every traveller in a room, in a town, in the whole game, going still at the same second and looking at nothing.

The banner room was silent.

Then Kei, at the head of the table, said, very quietly, “Kept and not sold.”

Ice took his feet off the chair.

Crimson Ledger had bought eighteen rooms in the spring. Eighteen Halls where seven roads met, on a tip, with gold. Everybody knew. The steward had said so at Ama Corrie’s counter. We bought eighteen rooms. That was why Crimson Ledger had been first under the first rank. They’d owned the doors.

Kept and not sold. Not those eighteen, then. The others. Wherever somebody had been asked to sell, and hadn’t.

Kei was already unrolling his map on the table among the cups.

“Elmcross,” said Kei. He put his finger on a dot south of the round town. Three days south, on a plain, where seven roads came in to a crossroads town with a Hall in the middle. “Seven roads. There’s a Hall. Old. The keeper’s an old man. Rook.” He looked up. “Higu went there in March. She said Crimson Ledger came in the spring and offered him a great deal of gold for a room at the back of his Hall. He said no.” Kei looked at the map. “He said, you asked what it was worth. Nobody asked what it was for.”

“Who else is near it?” said Deston.

Kei looked at the map for a long time.

“VANTA’s Hall is three days east of it,” he said. “Three days. Same as us.”

Nobody said anything.

Ice looked down the long table at forty-odd people who’d gone quiet. Then down at his own hands on the table. Then he got his phone out. The little window in the corner of his eye. He didn’t know why, except that he knew what he’d find.

There was already a message.

He looked at it for a long time.

“She’s going,” he said.

“Who?” said Kei.

“Kestrel,” said Ice. He turned the window round so Kei could see it. “Elmcross. Three days. Next door.”

Kei read it. He looked at Ice, and something went across his face that Ice had seen there before. On a night in January, when Kei had asked him to join and he’d said make it big, then ask me again.

“We’re going,” said Kei.

“Yes,” said Ice.

“First dawn,” said Kei. “Friday. It’s three days. We’ll get there Sunday night. The halls open at the first dawn of Monday.” He looked down the table. “Ten. And whoever wants to walk with them.”

And forty-odd people in the banner room, who’d been sitting very still, all started talking at once.

Ice typed one thing back.

He put the window away, and sat back in his chair, and found that his hands, flat on the long table, were perfectly still.

They’d been still like that a long time ago, in another game, in the dark, before a fight. He hadn’t felt them go still like that in years.

He didn’t say anything about it. He didn’t think anybody noticed.

At the far end of the table, PenguChi was watching him.
