Live from Elsewhere · Volume 11: Three Hundred and One

Chapter 271

Saltreach

3,761 words · 16 min read

The sea went out a mile while Kestrel watched, and didn’t hurry about it at all.

She was standing at the top of the white cliffs on the last Tuesday of February with her bow on her back and VANTA behind her. Twenty of them, at the end of thirteen days on the coast road. Below her the cliffs went down in terraces. Ledge after ledge of white stone, with houses cut into them and stairs between, all the way down to a harbour of black sand. And beyond the harbour, the bay. Grey-green and wide and cold, with the light on it.

Going out.

You could see it go. That was what she hadn’t expected. She’d thought a tide would be like a clock hand, too slow to see. It wasn’t. The edge of the water slid back across the black sand as she stood there. Steadily, a pace at a time, the way you’d draw back a sheet off a bed. Leaving wet black sand behind it, shining, with the sky in it. Leaving rocks, and weed, and channels of water running out after it, and boats lying over on their sides. A mile of it. More. Until the edge of the sea was a long way out in the bay. A grey line with the light behind it.

And in the middle of the bay, where the water had gone, something dark.

Not rock. Rooftops.

[ Saltreach · A city of the fiftieth level. The sea comes in. The sea goes out. The city stays. ]

“Oh,” said Ottie, behind her.

Kestrel didn’t say anything. She stood at the top of the white cliffs and looked at the rooftops of a drowned city coming up out of the bay as the tide left them. Black slate, and weed, and the top of a tower with nothing in it. And in the middle, a ring of water that didn’t go out with the rest. A round dark pool in the sand, perfectly still. The door.

[ The Low Tide · a great door · Lv 60 · Open between tides ]

“Opens on Thursday,” said Brann, beside her. Not a question.

“Thursday,” said Kestrel.

“We’re early.”

“We’re rested,” said Kestrel. And sat down on the cliff edge with her legs over the white stone and watched the sea come back.

Saltreach was the strangest city she’d ever been in, and she’d been in Masmark for a year.

It was all stairs. Every street was a stair, cut into the white cliff, going up and down between the terraces, with houses on either side cut into the rock. The doors were painted blue, every one, because the Tidebound said the sea liked blue. At the top of the cliffs was the market, on the flat, with the wind. At the bottom, the harbour. And every six hours, the whole city turned round and looked at the sea to see what it was doing.

Everybody in Saltreach knew where the tide was. All the time. Like knowing where the sun was.

House Calder had the top of the cliff stair. A long low hall with a counter the length of a barge and a man behind it called Pell Calder, who was very polite. He’d buy anything that came up out of the drowned city at the door, on the sand, and sell it at the top of the stair to people who hadn’t been down. For three times what he’d paid. Kestrel watched him do it on the Wednesday to a man with a basket of green coins. And thought that if she’d ever wanted to be rich in a game, she’d have done it his way.

The Quiet Oar had the harbour. A small guild, forty or fifty, in grey, with long boats and longer oars. They didn’t fight. They’d never fought, their leader said, a broad old woman called Wren with forearms like hawsers. They rowed. At low tide the bay wasn’t all sand; there were channels, deep and fast, running out after the sea. Nobody could cross them on foot. So the Quiet Oar rowed you. Out across the channels to the black pool in the middle of the bay, and back again before the sea came in. For nothing.

“For nothing?” said Ren.

“Nobody’s ever drowned in one of our boats,” said Wren. “That’s the pay.”

And the Tidebound had everything else.

There were hundreds of them. Divers and net-folk and shell-pickers, from all along the coast, in salt-white coats with their hair stiff from the sea. They lived on the terraces and went down to the sand at every low tide, whatever else was happening. They’d been here since the city opened in the autumn, five months before the door. They knew every rock in the bay. Every channel. Every rooftop of the drowned city that showed at low water, and what was on it, and what lived under it.

Their leader was Marisol. She was the one who’d sent the letters.

Marisol came up the cliff stair to the market on the Wednesday evening to look at the guilds. Kestrel watched her do it.

She was not tall. She was brown and lined and salt-white at the temples, with a diver’s shoulders and a net over one of them, and she walked up a hundred and forty steps without stopping. At the top she stood in the wind and looked at them all. Twenty from VANTA, in black. Twenty from Sable Court, in black and silver. Twenty from Meridian, in blue. Twenty from the Open Hand, in brown. Nine from the southern isles. And forty other guilds’ worth of people, five and ten at a time, who’d heard there was a door and come.

“You came to see the sea,” said Marisol. “Good. It’s very big. You’ll like it.”

Nobody said anything.

“Some of you came to see the door,” said Marisol. “That’s fine too. It opens tomorrow at the first low water after dawn. You go out with the Quiet Oar. You go down the pool. You go into the city.” She looked at them. “And when the sea comes back, the door shuts. Whoever’s inside when it shuts goes out with the water.”

“Goes out where?” said somebody from the Open Hand.

“Out,” said Marisol. “On the sand. Tomorrow. Fallen. With no day.” She shrugged with the shoulder that had the net on it. “The sea doesn’t care how good you are. It comes back. Every time. Mind it.”

She turned round and went back down the hundred and forty steps without stopping.

“She didn’t say anything about winning,” said Fenn, beside Kestrel. Quietly.

“No,” said Kestrel.

“Everybody’s here to win.”

“She isn’t,” said Kestrel. “She lives here.”

The Ninefold were the ones to watch.

Kestrel had heard of them. Everybody had, by the end of February. Nine from the southern isles, the far ones, past Masmark’s south shore, where the sea was warm. Nine people, never more, never less, who’d cleared every door in the south before anybody else got there. They never took a tenth. They’d turned down hundreds. Their leader was Teo. Kestrel saw him on the Wednesday at the top of the cliff stair. Tall, quiet, with two short spears and a way of standing that she recognised. The way she’d stood, six years ago. Before a man in a ring in a market taught her to stop deciding.

He’d decided everything already. She could see it on him.

“The hall’s for twenty,” said Brann.

“I know.”

“They’re nine.”

“They think nine’s enough,” said Kestrel. “Nine’s always been enough for them.” She watched Teo go down the cliff stair with his eight behind him, very fast, two steps at a time. “That’s the thing about being first everywhere. You stop being able to see what nine isn’t enough for.”

Brann looked at her.

“You were first everywhere,” he said. Not a question.

“I was,” said Kestrel. “For six years. Then I wasn’t.” She thought about the Saltwell, and the Crown Below, and the Bell Above. Second, and then first, and then third. Third had been the best of them. “I’d rather be third and rested.”

“Than first and not there,” said Brann. He’d heard her say it before.

The Open Hand’s leader found her on the Wednesday night, by the fire on the terrace below the market where VANTA were eating.

Marrok. Big and brown and loud, with a maul over his shoulder that he never put down. He’d been in the race for every great door in the game and never once been first, or second, or third. He’d lost fourteen of twenty on the stair of the Bell Above and had to watch the rest from the bottom. Kestrel had stopped minding him a long time ago. He was just weather.

He stood at the edge of the firelight now with his hands at his sides.

Not on the maul. At his sides. Where she could see them.

Kestrel looked at his hands. Then at his face. He’d gone a sort of dark red.

“Who’d stand next to you?” said Marrok.

It came out all at once. Very fast. Very badly. As if he’d been practising it on the coast road for thirteen days and lost it on the last step.

Kestrel stared at him.

She knew where it came from. Everybody did. A man with a bow in a gate in a round town in the west, who’d asked it of everybody who came up the steps for a year. Aurelie had walked fourteen days to answer it. Aurelie had gone home and put somebody in every door of every Hall in Meridian to ask it. And now Marrok of the Open Hand was standing at the edge of VANTA’s fire on a cliff in the east, asking it at her, with his hands at his sides.

“Why?” said Kestrel.

“Because I’ve never asked anybody anything,” said Marrok. “I’ve told people. Fourteen of twenty on the stair. I told them go.” He looked at the fire. “Aurelie said ask. So I’m asking. I’m bad at it.”

“You are,” said Kestrel.

“Who’d stand next to you?”

Kestrel thought about it. Properly. The way the tank did. You asked him something simple and he thought about it properly before he answered.

“Brann,” she said.

Marrok looked at Brann. Brann, on the other side of the fire with his tower shield across his knees, looked back at him and said nothing at all.

“That’s one answer,” said Marrok.

“It’s the one I’ve got,” said Kestrel. “For now.” She looked at the rest of VANTA round the fire. Ottie. Mags. Ren. Fenn, with his eyes up. Fourteen more. “Ask me again in a year.”

Marrok stood there for a moment longer. Then he nodded, slowly, as if she’d told him something hard. He went away into the dark with his hands still at his sides.

“He was awful,” said Ren.

“He was,” said Kestrel. “He’ll get better. Aurelie did.”

The door opened on Thursday at the first low water after dawn, and Kestrel didn’t go.

She sat on the cliff edge at the top of the white terraces with VANTA round her and watched everybody else go.

It was quite a thing to watch. The sea went out across the bay, and the whole of Saltreach came down the cliff stairs to the black sand after it. Hundreds of people. The Tidebound with their nets, not even going to the door, just out onto the flats to see what the night had left. The Quiet Oar’s long grey boats pushing off from the harbour into the channels, full, oars going all together. Pell Calder at a table on the sand with a cash box and a ledger, already buying. And out in the middle of the bay, at the black pool, the guilds going down.

The Ninefold went first. Of course they did. Nine of them off the first boat before it had stopped, into the pool, and gone.

Then Sable Court’s twenty, under a tall pale woman called Ves, in black and silver. Lucan wasn’t with them. Kestrel had asked on Wednesday. He’s on a bridge, Ves had said. In Masmark. Training. And had not said for what. She hadn’t needed to.

Then Meridian’s twenty. And at the front of them, enormous, waving both arms at the boat as if it might not see him: Harlan.

Kestrel had watched Harlan for six years, at the edge of Aurelie, carrying things. She’d never once seen him at the front. He stood in the bow of the Quiet Oar’s boat all the way across the channels, and Kestrel could see him from the cliff talking the whole way. Then at the pool he stopped talking. He looked back, once, at the twenty behind him, as if he’d only just noticed they were there. And went down.

“He’s frightened,” said Fenn.

“Yes.”

“He went anyway.”

“Aurelie told him to,” said Kestrel. “She wasn’t going.” She thought about that. Aurelie of Meridian, who’d walked fourteen days to a round town, twice. Who’d taken every beat for six years. Staying home, in a chair, and letting Harlan go first. “That’s new.”

The Open Hand went down last. Marrok at the front, with his hands at his sides.

And VANTA sat on the cliff.

“We rest first,” said Kestrel, before anybody asked.

They watched four tides.

Two on Thursday. Two on Friday. Every six hours, the sea went out and the guilds went down, and every six hours the sea came back.

It was the coming back that taught her.

On the first tide it came back slowly, the way it had gone. The guilds came up out of the black pool well before it reached them. Out onto the sand, and into the Quiet Oar’s boats, and back to the harbour, wet and quiet. Nobody had got far. The Ninefold had got furthest. You could tell from how fast they came up the cliff stair afterwards, two at a time, with Teo at the front not looking at anybody.

On the second tide they all went deeper. And the sea came back faster.

Not all of it. That was the thing Kestrel didn’t see, the first time. She saw the edge of the water coming in across the flats, the way it had gone out, a pace at a time. But out in the middle of the bay, round the black pool, it came in differently. In surges. A rush of water up one channel, and then nothing, and then a rush up another. As if the sea were coming back into the drowned city through its streets, one street at a time.

Three of Sable Court came up out of the pool on the second tide after the boats had gone. They stood on the sand in the rising water, and the Quiet Oar had to go back for them. Wren, standing in the stern of the boat, shouting at them in a voice that came all the way to the cliff.

On the third tide, at the turn, two of the Ninefold didn’t come up at all.

Kestrel saw it happen. The sea came back over the black pool. A great slow swell rolled in over the place where the door was and closed over it, flat and grey. And a minute later, on the black sand a hundred paces up the beach, two shapes lay where the water had left them. Two of nine. Fallen. With no day.

[ The Low Tide has closed. ]

The Ninefold came up the cliff stair seven. Teo at the front with his face like a shut door. They never took a tenth. They’d never had to think about it, because they’d never had fewer than nine.

“They’ll go in seven tomorrow,” said Brann.

“They’ll go in seven,” said Kestrel. “And they’ll get further than anybody. And they’ll still be seven.”

She found Fenn on the cliff edge on the Friday night, after the fourth tide, in the dark.

He was sitting where she’d sat on the Tuesday, with his legs over the white stone and his bow across his knees, looking at the bay. The sea was in. Grey and flat under a half moon, a mile of it, covering everything. The drowned city, the black pool, the channels. As if none of it had ever been there.

He was looking at it anyway.

She sat down beside him. She didn’t say anything. She’d learned that, the last year. From a tank who’d sit near you while you made something, and you’d forget he was there.

“It doesn’t come back all at once,” said Fenn, after a long time.

“No.”

“Everybody thinks it does. They watch the edge. On the flats.” He pointed, along the beach, at the long grey line of the water’s edge under the moon. “That comes in like they think. Steady. But it’s not the edge that shuts the door.” He moved his hand out, to the middle of the bay, where the black pool was under the water. “Out there, it comes back down the streets. One street. Then another. Then another. In an order.”

Kestrel looked at the bay.

She couldn’t see it. Not now, with the sea in. But she’d seen the surges on the second tide. A rush up one channel and then nothing and then another.

“What order?” she said.

“I don’t know yet.” Fenn was quiet. “It’s like the bells. On the terrace. In Masmark. When Ottie couldn’t keep time and the rest of us could. You don’t listen to your own bell. You listen to the one before.” He looked at her. “Every street that floods, there’s one before it. If you listen to that one, you know which is next.”

Kestrel sat on the cliff edge in the dark and didn’t decide anything.

She thought about the tank, in a letter, a fortnight ago. Ice had written back from the round town in the west. In the plain short way he wrote things, in answer to hers. We’re home this spring. Somebody’s carrying a stone. Somebody’s getting married twice. Go first.

Go first.

Nobody had ever told her to. Everybody she’d ever fought had told her don’t. With their arrows, their blades, their whole guilds. Everybody she’d ever raced had wanted to be first instead. He’d beaten her in a hall without ever once using his class. He’d been second to her under Masmark’s lake and happier about it than she’d ever been about anything. He’d never once wanted to beat anybody. And he’d written go first, at the bottom of a letter, as if it were the most ordinary thing in the world to want for somebody.

She’d read it about forty times on the coast road.

“Tell Brann,” said Kestrel.

Fenn looked at her.

“About the order,” she said. “The streets. Tell Brann tonight. Before you’ve worked it out. Tell him anyway.” She stood up. “Brann’s gran had a church. With bells. He knows about orders.”

“He’s never said.”

“He doesn’t say things,” said Kestrel. “You have to ask him.” She looked down at Fenn on the cliff edge, nineteen and not as good as the others, with his eyes up. “Ask him.”

Fenn got up. He went off along the terrace in the dark, toward the fire, toward Brann.

Kestrel stood on the cliff edge on her own for a while, looking at the sea, which was in.

It would go out again at the first low water after dawn. And the Ninefold would go down seven. And Sable Court and Meridian and the Open Hand would go down twenty. And they’d all get a little further than yesterday. And the sea would come back down the streets in an order nobody had heard yet.

Except, maybe, a boy who listened to the one before.

“Not yet,” said Kestrel, to the sea. Which was something else she’d borrowed, from a hill in the north she’d never seen.
