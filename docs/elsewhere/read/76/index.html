Live from Elsewhere · Volume 3: Constellation

Chapter 76

In Black

2,385 words · 10 min read

The egg arrived on the second of February, at ten in the morning. Vivian Ong did not get into it until the tenth.

Two men brought it up the narrow stairs from the street, past the kopitiam, sweating, in a box the size of a small car. The auntie stood at the bottom of the stairs with her arms folded and told them to mind the corner. They did not mind the corner. Vivian signed for it in black and had them put it in the back room behind the teak desk, where the old filing cabinets were. She had cleared a space for it a week before. Then she thanked them, and gave them each a can of cold drink from the kopitiam, which the auntie had already pressed into her hands for exactly this purpose. And she went back to her desk and finished the quarterly returns for a printing company in Ayer Keroh.

It sat there for eight days.

This was not because she was afraid of it. She had lain in one before, in the shoplot, for ten minutes, in the guest mode. She’d taken her shoes off and lined them up at the side, and put her glasses in their case on the folding table. Then she’d lain down in a white shell in a room that smelled of a stranger’s fan, and come out ten minutes later and said nothing to anybody for an hour. She knew what it was.

It was because there was a holiday, and the holiday was the holiday. And because, in Vivian’s experience, a thing that had cost two thousand four hundred and ninety-nine US dollars could wait until you had the time to do it properly.

On the tenth, after the office closed at six, she had the time.

She lined her shoes up at the side of the egg. She put her glasses in their case, on top of the filing cabinet. She looked, once, at the photograph of her father on the wall in the front room, through the open door. Then she lay down.

The lid closed. The gel was cool. There was the sound of rain.

[ Welcome to ELSEWHERE.
Choose your name. ]

She had thought about this. She had thought about it for eight days, at odd moments, the way she thought about a clause in a contract that wasn’t quite right yet. She had considered several options and rejected them all on grounds of dignity. In the end she typed the only thing that was accurate.

Vivian.

[ That name is taken. ]

VivianOng.

[ That name is taken. ]

She looked at the window for a moment, without expression.

VOng.

[ Welcome, VOng. ]

It would do. It was precise. It was hers.

[ Your likeness. ]

There was a mirror. She looked into it and saw herself, which was what she had expected. A woman in her middle years in a plain white blouse, hair pinned up, no glasses. It was strange to see her own face without glasses. It was clearer than she was used to. She did not change anything. There was nothing she could see that needed changing.

[ Bring one thing with you. ]

The mirror showed her things. A watch. A ring. The jade bangle her mother had given her, which she didn’t wear at the office.

She chose the pen.

It was the pen on her desk, the black fountain pen that she signed everything with. It had been her father’s before it was hers. She had signed every share transfer, every employment pass, every return and every resolution for this company with it. She hadn’t known the mirror would offer it. It did. It came into her hand in the white room, and it was heavy and familiar, and she put it in the breast pocket of the white blouse where it belonged.

[ Pain. ]

She set it to zero.

She had read the forum. She knew what the young people said about pain: that you had to feel it to learn, that the ones at zero never got good. She had no intention of getting good. She intended to look at the market.

The rain got louder. She stood up.

She was in a counting-house.

That was the first thing she understood, and she understood it with a feeling she would not have described to anyone as joy. A long low room with a beamed ceiling and tall windows. Desks in rows, and at the desks, clerks, writing in ledgers with steel pens. On the wall at the end, a great slate board with chalk numbers on it. A man on a ladder kept rubbing them out and writing new ones. It smelled of ink and dust and wet wool.

[ Tollbury · the River March
Travellers: 64 ]

She was in a market town on a river crossing. She found out later that it had a toll bridge, a cattle market on Tuesdays, a moneylender’s on every corner, and a counting-house where every merchant on the river came to settle accounts. She had been put, by a random number, in a beginner town that was a counting-house with a town round it.

She stood in the doorway and looked at the slate board for a long time.

“New?” said a clerk at the nearest desk. A thin young man with ink on his cuffs and a pen behind each ear. He looked at her white blouse and her pinned hair and her complete lack of a weapon with polite curiosity. “Travellers usually go to the yard first. For a sword.”

“I don’t want a sword,” said Vivian.

“What do you want?”

Vivian looked at the board.

“I’d like to understand those,” she said.

She did not leave the counting-house for three hours.

It was the best three hours she had spent in years, and she would never, under any circumstances, have admitted it to Mr Declan.

The slate board was the town’s own market. Grain, wool, timber, iron, salt, the prices called up from the river every hour by boys who ran from the wharf. And above the board, in the corner, there was a window. Not chalk. A real window, the game’s own, faint and blue at the edge of her vision when she looked at it. It showed the Exchange.

The Exchange. The thing she’d spent two months reading on her laptop, from outside, the way you read a balance sheet. Gold to US dollars. The price of moonbell draughts and Choir Silver and bell-metal, and a hundred other things. Every trade in the world, scrolling past, in real money.

From inside, it was different.

From outside, it had been numbers. From inside, it was people. She could see, if she looked closely, the shape of each trade. Not who, but how. A small sale here, a nervous one, somebody selling a single draught for less than it was worth because they needed the money tonight. A big steady buyer there, buying wool at the same price every hour, patient, a guild stocking up. A flurry of tiny trades in something called Hedge Tea, which she recognised. She smiled, very slightly, at the window.

She took the pen out of her pocket and asked the thin clerk for paper. He gave her a ledger. She began to write.

She found it at five past nine.

She almost didn’t. It was buried. It was the kind of thing you only saw if you’d been looking at the same column for two hours and your eyes had stopped seeing the numbers and started seeing the rhythm.

Gold was going up.

Not much. The game’s gold, against the US dollar, on the Exchange. It had been about ten dollars a gold since November; everyone said so, Mr Declan said so, she’d written it in the company’s accounts herself. It was ten dollars and thirty-one cents tonight. Three per cent in a week. That was nothing, on a market, unless it was going up the same way every day. And it was.

She went back through the history in the window. A week. Ten days. Two weeks.

Every day at two in the afternoon, Malaysia time, somebody bought gold.

Not one somebody. Many. A great many small purchases, all within the same twenty minutes, from accounts she couldn’t see. Each one too small to notice. All of them together, every day, at two o’clock, like a tap left running. Then nothing, until two o’clock the next day.

She sat back in the clerk’s chair and looked at it.

Then she did the sum.

She did it in the ledger, in black, in her small neat figures. The size of the purchases. The days. The total. It came to a little over nine thousand gold in fourteen days. Ninety thousand US dollars, near enough. A company didn’t buy ninety thousand dollars of anything, at two o’clock every afternoon for a fortnight, in small pieces so that nobody would notice, unless it knew exactly what it was going to pay for.

She knew what cost five hundred gold. She’d watched Mr Declan’s stream on the Sunday he’d said it’s theirs, I’m not in it. She’d read the forum about the Banner Writ. She’d read about the first guild, Crimson Ledger, twenty names in an afternoon, at a Hall in Saltmarket. And she’d read on the forum that men in red sashes had been going from Hall to Hall all over the world, asking the keepers about their banner rooms.

Nine thousand gold. Eighteen writs.

She put the pen down.

“You found something,” said the thin clerk. He’d been watching her for an hour, between his own sums, with growing respect.

“Someone is buying a great deal of something,” said Vivian, “very quietly.”

“Is that bad?”

“It depends who’s selling,” said Vivian. “And who’s going to want it after.” She closed the ledger. “May I keep this?”

“It’s yours. You wrote in it.” He hesitated. “Miss. You read the board like my master does. He’s been reading it forty years. If you wanted a place here—“

“Thank you,” said Vivian. “I have a client.”

She sent the message from the counting-house steps, in the evening light of a town on a river, with the cattle pens empty and the toll bridge lit at both ends. She typed it in the air with one finger, slowly, because she was not used to typing in the air. She wrote it exactly as she would have written it on paper.

She logged out at ten.

She lay in the gel for a moment after the lid lifted, in the back room behind the teak desk, with the old filing cabinets round her and the sound of the kopitiam closing up downstairs. The clatter of chairs being stacked, the auntie shouting to someone in the kitchen. Then she sat up, and found her glasses on the filing cabinet, and put them on. The room came back into focus, a little less clear than the counting-house.

She took the ledger out of her bag.

It wasn’t there, of course. It was in Tollbury, in a counting-house, in the breast pocket of a white blouse. But she’d copied the figures onto a sheet of real paper before she logged out, from memory. She sat at the teak desk and copied them again into a real ledger, in black, with her father’s pen.

Gold: buyer, red. 14:00 daily. ~9,000. Eighteen.

There was a knock at the office door. The auntie came in without waiting, the way she always did, with a plastic container.

“Vivi,” said the auntie. “You were inside the egg. Three hours. I heard it humming through the floor.” She put the container on the desk. Kuih, green and white. “Did you go and see the gardening boy?”

“No,” said Vivian. “I went and saw a counting-house.”

The auntie looked at her for a long moment.

“You look happy,” she said, suspiciously.

“I am not happy,” said Vivian. “I’m working.”

The auntie nodded slowly, as if that confirmed everything, and went away down the stairs.

Vivian sat at the desk for a long time with the ledger and the kuih and the orchid on the windowsill. Then she ate one of the kuih, which she never did after six o’clock. And she wrote one more line under the figures, very small, in black.

It is much easier to read from inside.
