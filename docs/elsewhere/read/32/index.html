Xav told them on the causeway, at ten past nine on Sunday morning, with the fen behind them and the fields in front and eleven thousand people watching through Ice’s eyes.

He didn’t make a speech. Ice had known he wouldn’t. They’d gone live at nine at the bridge out of Fernwick, the two of them, and started walking north-east along the raised road toward Brackenford, the way they’d walked it on day fourteen to see the board. Chat had said hello. Chat had asked why Xav was on stream so early on a Sunday. Chat had asked about Monday, and the dark, and whether Fernwick had walls. And Xav had walked along beside Ice for ten minutes in the sun, with his hands in his pockets and his daggers on his back, saying nothing.

Then he stopped in the middle of the causeway and turned round, so he was standing in front of Ice, in the middle of the POV, looking into it.

“Hi,” he said. “I’m leaving Moonlight Harvest on Wednesday.”

Xav read it all, standing in the middle of the causeway in the sun. Ice watched him read it. Watched the corner of his mouth go up at it’s still going to be march, and go down at proud of you, and stay down, and then he turned round abruptly and started walking again.

“Come on,” said Xav. “We’ve got a notice to write.”

The Adventurers’ Hall in Brackenford had a board.

It wasn’t a board, really. It was an old barn door, taken off its hinges at some point in the town’s history and nailed flat against the wall beside the counter. It was covered in notices, three and four deep, pinned over each other, curling at the edges. Seeking: healer, Lv 12+. Seeking: anyone for the Thornback, please. Selling: wolf pelts. Lost: one boot (left), if found return to the inn by the east bridge. Three weeks ago, Ice had stood in front of it with Xav and Deston and pinned up a sheet that said MOONLIGHT HARVEST seeks one healer. The next day a small girl in a pale blue robe had walked in carrying it and said sorry four times.

Penhallow watched them come in with a pen behind each ear.

“Wednesday,” he said to Xav, before Xav could say anything. “I’ve got it. Top left.” He looked at Ice. “And you’ll be wanting a notice.”

“How did you know?”

“Second time in a month.” Penhallow slid a blank sheet across the counter, and a pen, and a pot of ink. “Last one got you a bell.”

Xav took the pen.

He wrote it at the counter, standing, in his small neat capitals, without crossing anything out. Ice had known he would. He’d written it in his head on the causeway, probably. Probably on Thursday.

[ MOONLIGHT HARVEST seeks one sword.
Lv 15 and up. Any blade.
Wednesday, noon, at the old ford below the town.
Must be able to stand next to Ice without getting in his way.
Kind people only. ]

He looked at it. Then he handed Ice the pen.

Ice looked at it for a while. Then he wrote, at the bottom, in his own handwriting, which was much worse:

No list. Just turn up.

“A list gets you CL,” said Ice. “They’d put twenty names on it with nice backgrounds. If people have to turn up, they have to stand somewhere. We’ll see where.”

Xav looked at the line. Then at Ice. Then he nodded slowly, like a man who’d been about to add exactly that.

They pinned it on the barn door, at eye height, over a notice selling wolf pelts. Ice stepped back so the stream could see it.

On the way back they went down to look at the old ford.

Xav wanted to see it. He said so the way he said everything, as if it had been on the plan since Thursday, which it probably had. They left the causeway below Brackenford and went down the bank through the alders to the river. There it was, the way it had been three weeks ago. A wide brown shallows with the current running quick and bright over the gravel. Three flat stepping-stones across the middle, worn smooth. The deep pool below, where the Warden lived. Kotori’s flat rock downstream, where she’d stood with her feet together and seen all five.

The second stone was under water today. It usually was.

“There,” said Xav. He pointed at it. “Whoever turns up stands on the second stone with you. Something comes out of the pool, and they stand there with you, and we watch.”

“That’s it?”

“That’s it.” Xav looked at the stone. “Anyone can hit things. You need someone who can stand on a stone in moving water next to a man who won’t move, and not knock him off it. And not get knocked off it by him. And still hit things.” He paused. “You’d be amazed how few people can do that.”

Ice looked at the second stone with the brown water running over it, and remembered his back foot going out from under him on a wet night in the dark, and the river taking him.

“I wouldn’t,” he said.

There were people at the ford already. Of course there were. Twenty, maybe thirty, in the shallows and on the near bank, with swords. Practising. Somebody must have read the notice and run. A boy was doing Falling Cut at the water, very badly, with his weight on the wrong foot. Two women were taking turns standing on the first stone and pushing each other off it, laughing. A man in a red sash was sitting on a log on the bank with a notebook, not practising, writing down everyone who was.

“CL,” said Xav, without looking at him.

“I see him.”

“They’ll send someone on Wednesday. Someone good, with a clean name. You’ll like him. He’ll be nice to dogs.”

“How will we know?”

“I’ll know,” said Xav.

Across the river, on the far bank under the alders, somebody else was watching too. A tall man standing very still in the green shade, with a long two-handed sword across his back. He was too far off to see a face. He was looking at the water, not at the people in it, at the second stone in particular. He watched it the way you’d watch a hand of cards you weren’t sure whether to play. Then, when Ice looked across a second time, he wasn’t there.

Ice didn’t think anything of it. Half of Brackenford had a sword this week.

“Come on,” said Xav. “Kotori’s waiting.”

They told Kotori in the afternoon, in Fernwick, on the Landing, with the new lanterns hanging above them unlit.

Xav did it. Ice stood a little to one side and let him. Deston was there, sitting on the edge of the Landing with his book on his knees and his feet over the water. He looked up when Xav started talking, and then down again at the book, because he already knew.

Kotori listened to all of it with her staff held in both hands. She didn’t interrupt. When Xav had finished she stood there for a moment, looking at him, with the bell on her staff very still for once.

“Sorry,” she said. “I’m not—I’m just—“

Then she cried.

She didn’t make a sound about it. She just stood on the Landing in the sun with her staff in her hands and her face very straight, and tears running down it. She kept saying “sorry, sorry, it’s fine, I’m happy, I’m happy for you,” and the more she said it the more she cried. Xav stood in front of her, looking completely helpless, the way Ice had never once seen him look in nine years, not even in poly, not even in CS with a round on the line.

“It’s okay,” said Xav. “Kotori. It’s okay. I’m not going. I’m going to Brackenford. It’s a morning’s walk.”

“I know.” She wiped her face with the sleeve of her robe. “I know. Sorry. It’s just, you drew my post.”

Xav didn’t say anything to that.

“Before I was even there,” said Kotori. “The first day. On the bridge. You knew where I’d stand.” She sniffed. “Nobody’s ever done that. Known where I’d stand before I did.”

“You’ll know,” said Xav. “You always know. I just wrote it down.”

Kotori looked at him for a long moment with her wet round face. Then she turned round and went into Marta’s, without saying anything. Marta’s door banged behind her. A minute later it opened again, and she came out holding a small clay cup with steam coming off it, in both hands, carefully, the way you’d carry a bird.

“It’s tea,” she said. “I made it. Brewing’s my production skill. It’s not very good. It’s a bit cabbagey.” She held it out to Xav. “It’s for luck. You have to drink all of it.”

Xav took it. He looked at it. Then he drank all of it, standing on the Landing in front of eleven thousand people, without stopping.

“That’s terrible,” he said.

“I know,” said Kotori. She didn’t say sorry. Ice noticed.

At dusk, Xav walked them through the drawing.

He did it the way he’d done the ford and the door: with the notebook open, walking, pointing, saying everything once, calmly, in order. Ice, Deston and Kotori walked behind him through Fernwick in the long red light. The town was lighting its lanterns as they went, one at a time, all along the Landing and the boardwalks. Marta on a stepladder with a taper. The eel man at the end of the jetty. Rafi on Sedge’s steps with a lantern in one hand and the black sword in the other, very serious.

“We don’t know what it is,” said Xav. “So this is for everything. If it comes from the fen, it comes up the north walk, and the north walk has one narrow place, and Dec stands there.” He tapped the page. “If it comes across the fields, it comes down the causeway and over the long bridge, and the bridge is wide, so it needs more than one of us. Kotori goes on the eel man’s post if it’s the walk, and on the end of his jetty if it’s the bridge. Nothing gets out there without swimming. Deston stands where everyone can hear him. That’s here.” He stopped in the middle of the Landing. “If you count from here, they can hear you from the bridge and from the walk.”

Deston looked round. Then he hummed one low note, very quietly, and listened to it go out across the water.

“Yes,” he said.

“Rafi and Joy and Bram on Sedge’s steps, whatever happens. Nobody touches her rafts.” Xav turned a page. “Hollis has four men and twelve spears. I asked him where he’d put them. He told me to mind my own business, and then he told me the bridge. So, the bridge.” He closed the notebook. “And Suzu wherever Suzu wants, because that’s where he’ll be anyway.”

“And you?” said Kotori.

Xav looked at the page.

“Wherever Dec isn’t,” he said. “Behind whatever’s looking at him.”

They stood on the Landing as the last of the light went out of the sky over the old fen, and the lanterns burned all round them. Somewhere out past the north walk, in the dark, the frogs started up. Ice listened past them, the way he’d been listening for a week now, for the singing that wasn’t there any more. Nothing. Just the frogs and the drip of the reeds. Just a fen at night.

Keep your lights. Keep your towns.

“Tomorrow,” said Xav.

The nine o’clock bus from Singapore got into Melaka Sentral at twenty past midnight.

Ice was there at ten past, in the drop-off, with the engine running and the windows down. Suzu came out through the glass doors with the last of the crowd, a tall thin figure in black with his backpack over one shoulder, and folded himself into the passenger seat.

“Hi,” said Suzu.

“Hi.”

“Did you eat?”

“I ate.”

“Properly?”

“Nasi lemak.”

“Hm,” said Suzu, which meant that would do.

They pulled out onto the empty road. Malacca at half past midnight on a Sunday was all orange streetlights and shut shutters, a cat on a wall, a lit 7-Eleven with nobody in it. Suzu had his window down and his elbow on the sill.

“So,” he said.

“Xav’s leaving,” said Ice. “Wednesday. After the trials. Eight friends of his from poly are coming in on the first. He’s going to charter a company and lead them. He asked me to tell him to stay.” He watched the road. “I told him to go.”

Suzu didn’t say anything for a while.

“Good,” he said.

“Good?”

“He wants to.” Suzu was looking out of the window at the shut shops going past. “He’s wanted to all week. He kept looking at you like he was saying sorry for something he hadn’t done yet.” He paused. “He’ll be better at it there.”

“Better than here?”

“Here, when you do something stupid, he has to watch.” The corner of Suzu’s mouth went up about a millimetre. “There, he won’t have to.”

Ice laughed. He couldn’t help it.

“Who’s the sword?” said Suzu.

“Wednesday. Noon. At the ford. No list. Whoever turns up.”

“Somebody will,” said Suzu.

He said it with complete certainty, the way he said most things. Then he put his head back against the seat and closed his eyes.

They drove through the empty town. Past the river, black and still under the orange lights. Past the old red buildings. At the lights by the bridge, the clock on the dashboard changed from 23:59 to 00:00, and the little date under it went from 20 to 21.

Monday, 21 December.

The solstice. The longest night of the year.

Ice looked at it until the lights went green.
