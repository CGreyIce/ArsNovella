Chapter 119

The Other Side

1,966 words · 9 min read

Xola had been drawn as an axolotl for years and she had never, not once, thought about what it would be like to have gills.

Now she had them. They stood out from the sides of her head, soft and pink and frilled like feathers, and they moved. When she was happy they fluttered. When she was cross they went flat. When she put her head under the water of the mill pond on Sunday morning, just to see, they opened all the way out like a flower and she could breathe. Not really. The game said they were cosmetic. But she could feel the cold water go through them, and that was enough.

She came up out of the pond spluttering and laughing so hard that the weir-keeper on the sluice gate dropped her pole.

“SLEP,” said Xola, on the call. “SLEP. I’M A FISH.”

“You were always a fish,” said Slep, from somewhere on a cliff, three days away.

“I’m a real fish now.”

“Good,” said Slep. He said it slowly, the way he said everything, and she could hear him smiling. You always could, if you knew him. “Are you warm?”

“I’m in a pond, Slep.”

“Are you warm in the pond?”

She thought about it. She was. The water was cold, and she was warm in it, which made no sense and which she loved.

“Yes,” said Xola.

“Good,” said Slep again. “Day one. I’ve found the road. There’s a bridge. There are a lot of sheep.” A pause. “One of them is following me.”

She’d taken the trident.

She hadn’t meant to. She’d meant to take a sword, because swords looked good on stream. But there’d been a trident on the rack, three-pronged, long-handled, black iron, and she’d laughed out loud in the white room and taken it. Fish girl. Obviously.

And she’d taken Fishing and Cooking, because she’d always wanted to cook properly and never had the time. And Fishing went with Cooking. And when she’d said that on the call, Slep had said and I’ll skin what you catch and make you a knife to cut it with. She’d had to mute herself for a minute.

The weir-keeper was called Dorrit. She was old and brown and wiry, with forearms like rope, and she worked the sluice gates at the top of Millrace. She opened and shut them by hand, so that the right amount of water went to the right wheels at the right time. On Sunday afternoon she watched Xola stand up to her waist in the mill pond with a trident for an hour, failing to spear a single fish. Then she came down off the gate.

“You’re watching the fish,” said Dorrit.

“I’m trying to catch the fish—“

“Don’t watch the fish. Watch the water. The water tells you where the fish is going to be.” Dorrit took the trident out of her hands, stood still for a moment with it up, and put it into the pond without looking. It came up with a fish on it the length of her arm. “Water always tells you. You just have to stop shouting at it.”

Xola stared at her.

“I’m not shouting,” she said, very loudly.

“Hm,” said Dorrit, and gave her the trident back.

She turned her stream on that night for the first time since last year.

She’d stopped around when Thea did. Uni, and the tiredness. And Thea’s whole year, which Xola had been on the phone for.

“I’m going to cry,” said Xola. “I’ve been crying. I cried in a pond. I cried at a fish.” She flapped her gills at the orb on purpose. Fourteen hundred people screamed. She’d never had fourteen hundred. She’d had forty-five, on a very good night, in a very different year.

“Okay,” said Xola, “okay, okay, okay. Rules. I’m a fish now. Slep’s walking. Thea’s got a shield. Tif’s got ears. Chi’s being Chi. Xyro’s fallen in two channels and a millrace. Everything’s fine.”

Thea at the front.

Xola looked across the mill pond. Thea was sitting on the edge of the footbridge in the dusk with her tower shield beside her and her hammer across her knees, watching the big wheel turn. She hadn’t said much since they’d woken up. She’d smiled, more than Xola had seen her smile in a year. But she hadn’t said much.

Xola knew why Thea had taken the shield. She hadn’t asked. She didn’t need to. She’d watched Thea be the one behind for years, in everything. Holding the plan, holding the call, holding people up. And getting it wrong, every time, no matter what she did, because that was how it had been arranged for her. Xola had been on the phone for all of it. Every night, until the night Thea ended it. And the morning after.

She also knew where the round town was on the map. She’d looked on Saturday night, before Thea did, so she’d know.

She didn’t say any of that on stream. She flapped her gills and told fourteen hundred people about a fish.

She sat in the mill pond with her phone in the corner of her eye and her gills going flat with embarrassment.

Thea had matchmade them. Xola still didn’t entirely know how; one day there’d been a Slep in her life and the next there’d been a Slep in her life on purpose, and Thea had looked very pleased with herself. Thea had done the same for Tif and Xyro. She said it was because she liked her friends to be happy. Xola thought it was because Thea had been so unhappy herself, for so long, that she’d wanted something in the world to go right.

He came over the hills on Wednesday at dusk.

She didn’t see him come. She was in the mill pond up to her chest, with Thea on the bank behind her with the shield up. Something had come out of the deep end of the pond that Dorrit called a weir pike, and the game called something worse.

[ Weir Pike · Lv 8 ]

It was long and grey and mostly teeth, and it was three levels above her, and it was going round and round her in the water. Every time she lunged with the trident it wasn’t where she’d lunged. Thea was shouting Xo, get out, get OUT. PenguChi was on the bank with the spear, and Tif was ringing something that made Xola warm every time the pike’s tail caught her. Xyro was shooting arrows that went into the water and came back up again. And Xola was not getting out, because she was a fish, and fish didn’t get out of ponds.

Watch the water.

She stopped watching the pike. She watched the water. The pike went round her, a grey shape under the surface, and she watched the ripples it made instead of the shape. She watched where they bent, and saw where it was going to be before it got there, and brought the trident round.

The trident went in. Not deep. Enough. The pike thrashed and came round at her with its mouth open, all of it, all teeth, and she didn’t have time to get the trident out.

And somebody was on her other side.

She didn’t see him come into the water. She heard him. A big slow splash on her left, and then a long blade on a long pole, coming down past her shoulder into the pike’s open mouth from the side she wasn’t covering. Not fast. Exactly when its mouth was widest, right at the top of its lunge, when it couldn’t turn.

It went over sideways in the water and didn’t come up.

[ Weir Pike · defeated ]

Xola stood in the mill pond up to her chest with her trident stuck in nothing and her gills standing straight out. She turned round.

Slep was standing beside her in the water, on her left, with a glaive. Long-hafted, single-edged, the blade as long as her forearm. He was looking at the place where the pike had gone down, calmly, the way he looked at everything, as if he were checking it had done what he’d expected. He had mud to the knees and rain in his hair and a sheep standing on the bank behind him, looking at the pond.

“It shows you,” said Slep. “Before it bites. It drops its tail.” He looked at her. “You had the right side. It needed the other one.”

Xola looked at him.

“You walked,” she said.

“You were here.”

“You took four days.”

“Three and a half.” He thought about it. “I slept some of it.”

She dropped the trident. She didn’t care. She threw her arms round his neck in the middle of the mill pond and held on. Her gills went all the way out like a flower. Slep put his free arm round her, carefully, the glaive still held out to the side so it wouldn’t catch her, the way he did everything. Calmly. Properly. Like a man who’d decided a long time ago where he was going to stand.

“Whatever side you’re on,” said Slep, into her gills, quietly, so that only she heard it, “I’ll take the other one.”

She cried in the pond again. It was becoming a habit.

On the bank, Thea had put the shield down. She was standing very still with her hand over her mouth, smiling behind it. PenguChi was smiling too, in her slow way. Xyro was cheering. Tif had both hands round her staff and her ears were turned forward toward the pond, all the way.

The sheep came down to the edge of the water and looked at them.

“Slep,” said Thea, eventually. “Is that a sheep?”

“Yes,” said Slep.

“Is it yours?”

Slep looked at the sheep. The sheep looked at Slep.

“It might be,” he said.
