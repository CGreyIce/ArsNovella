Live from Elsewhere · Volume 5: Ask Me Again

Chapter 127

The Worst Moments

1,651 words · 7 min read

The note on Hyvred’s phone said, at the top, above everything:

ICE — 3,779.50 + one lesson.

She looked at it on Monday morning on the way to the hospital, with her parents and her brother, who’d all come, though she’d told them it was ridiculous for three people to come to one small thing behind one ear. She was very glad they had. She didn’t tell them that either. She didn’t know why she was looking at the note. It wasn’t the moment for it. But she found she’d got it out without deciding to, the way you’d touch a sore tooth with your tongue. She looked at it the whole way there, through the tube, a piece at a time.

Three thousand eight hundred and twenty-one. Minus two for two hits, on a terrace in a city with no name. Minus thirty-nine fifty, piece one, on his stream at nine o’clock on a Monday, in front of thirty thousand people. Thanks, Hyv. DON’T THANK ME.

And plus one lesson, which she still didn’t know how to put a number on. She’d tried. She’d lain awake trying. She hated it.

She put the phone away when they got there, and went up to the eleventh floor between her family, the way she’d gone into every hospital of her life.

She’d told her chat on Saturday night. Not much. I’m off next week. Monday to Friday. Doctor thing.

They’d said it for ten minutes. Hundreds of them, then thousands, the same two words over and over until she’d had to turn the stream off because she couldn’t see the screen.

She hadn’t told him. She’d said I rang the clinic on the terrace and I’m not telling you, and she’d meant it. It was none of his business. It was her eyes. He’d paid for them, which was the whole problem, and so the one thing she could keep was when.

She hadn’t told him. She’d told Bee, and Kei, because Kei had found out anyway, and Suzu. Suzu had promised to tell her when his mother saw, and he had, on a Friday in April. It seemed only fair.

Her family sat in the row of chairs beside her at the edge of the waiting room. They didn’t talk much. They’d done a lot of waiting rooms with her, over the years, and they knew how.

Bee rang at nine. Of course she did. It was eleven in Australia, and Bee had said I’ll ring at nine your time, and Bee always did what she said.

“Where are you?”

“In a chair. At the edge.” Hyvred laughed. “I always find the edge.”

“Are you scared?”

“No,” said Hyvred. Then: “Yes.”

“Good,” said Bee. “You’d be stupid not to be. You’re not stupid.” A pause. “I’ve drawn you something. You can’t see it yet. I’ll show you on Friday.”

“What is it?”

“You’ll see,” said Bee. “On Friday. That’s the point.”

They talked about nothing until the nurse came. Bee told her about a boar that had tried to eat her book. Hyvred told her about the woman in the next chair, who was knitting something very fast and very badly. Then the nurse said her name, and Hyvred said I have to go, and Bee said I know.

“Hyv.”

“Yeah?”

“I’ll be on the phone on Friday,” said Bee. “When they turn it on. Ring me first. Before anyone. I want to be the first thing.”

Hyvred sat in the chair at the edge of the waiting room with the phone against her ear.

“You will be,” she said.

It was the worst moment, and that was when it happened.

She was lying on a bed in a gown that didn’t do up properly, in a small cold room with a lot of machines. A nurse with kind hands was putting something into the back of her hand, and a doctor was saying things about behind the ear, and a small incision, and you won’t feel anything. She was trying very hard to be brave. She was mostly managing it.

And the nurse, who was kind, said, the way nurses do, “Try to think about something nice.”

And Hyvred’s brain, without asking her, without any warning at all, gave her a hand on the top of her head.

Between the horns. Twice. Lightly. On a white terrace in a city with no name, in the hour before a sunset that never came. A man she’d just hit twice, as hard as she could, putting his hand on her head the way you’d—

No.

She lay on the bed with her eyes shut and her face going hot. In a hospital. On an operating table, practically. In a gown that didn’t do up.

“No,” said Hyvred, out loud.

The nurse paused. “Sorry?”

“Nothing,” said Hyvred. “Nothing. Sorry. Carry on. I’m thinking about something nice.”

She was not thinking about something nice. She was thinking about something infuriating. She lay there with her eyes screwed shut, trying to think about the sea from the cliff at Saltwhistle, the whole sea edge to edge. Or the seven roads from the eye of the dome. Or Bee’s banner, whole, on the old hooks. Anything. Anything at all.

Her brain gave her the hand again.

Between the horns.

She’d hit him. She’d hit him, and he’d patted her on the head, and she’d made a noise she would never, as long as she lived, forgive herself for, and gone and hidden behind Bee’s wings. Which you could see straight through. Everybody had seen. Kestrel had seen. Tiger had seen.

“Are you all right?” said the nurse. “Your heart rate’s gone up.”

“I’m fine,” said Hyvred, through her teeth. “I’m thinking about something nice.”

“Is it nice?”

“No.”

The nurse laughed, kindly, and said something to the doctor. Hyvred heard it’s normal, people get nervous. She wanted to sit up and explain that she was not nervous, she was furious, and it wasn’t the same thing at all. But something cold was going into the back of her hand, and the room was going soft at the edges, which was funny, because she’d never had edges.

The last thing she thought, going under, was: I’m going to hit him again.

The second-last thing, which she was very glad nobody would ever know, was the hand.

She woke up at two with a sore place behind her right ear and a dry mouth and a nurse saying all done, it went very well. Her mother was in the chair by the bed. Her father and her brother were somewhere just outside; she could hear them, the low sound of two people trying to be quiet in a corridor.

She lay there for a while. The tube was the same. Ceiling, a corner of a light, the edge of a curtain. Nothing was different. They’d said nothing would be. Friday, they’d said. We switch it on Friday.

Her phone was on the table beside the bed. Her brother had put it there, she found out later, where she’d find it. She found it by feel and held it up close.

Three messages.

She read the last one three times.

I haven’t told him.

She lay on the bed with the phone against her chest. She thought about the note at the top of her phone, ICE — 3,779.50 + one lesson, and a hand on her head on a terrace. She was going to be so angry about that hand for the rest of her life.

She rang Bee.

“Done,” she said, before Bee could say anything.

“How do you feel?”

“Like someone put something behind my ear.”

“Someone did.”

“I was thinking about something nice,” said Hyvred, “and it wasn’t nice.”

Bee was quiet for a moment, in Australia, on the other end of the phone. Bee knew her. Bee had known her longer than almost anybody.

“Was it the head thing,” said Bee.

Hyvred said nothing at all.

“It was the head thing,” said Bee.

“I’m hanging up.”

“You’re not.”

She didn’t. She lay on the bed on the eleventh floor of a hospital in Singapore with a small thing behind her ear that they’d switch on on Friday. Her best friend was laughing at her down the phone from a different country, her flat laugh like a door shutting. And she was so angry, and so scared, and so something else she didn’t have a word for, that she started laughing too. Then she couldn’t stop.
