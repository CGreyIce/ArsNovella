The Anvil

3,633 words · 16 min read

Gabe had the kettle on at five to nine, because Dec had said in the kitchen at nine with the kettle on, and Gabe had been told.

Not by Dec. By Hyv. At a quarter past eight, on his phone, while he was standing in the kitchen of the flat in Malacca pretending he wasn’t waiting.

So he put the kettle on. At five to nine. And stood in the kitchen with his back to the counter and his hands flat on it, the way he’d stood in January a year and a half ago when Dec went pink at a phone and Gabe said absolutely nothing. It had been the hardest thing he’d ever done.

He’d done harder since. Twenty-two days.

The front door opened at two minutes past.

Dec came in with his small bag and put it down by the door, and took his shoes off, and came into the kitchen. He stopped in the doorway. He looked at Gabe, and at the kettle, which was just starting to make the noise, and back at Gabe.

“Hyv told you,” said Dec.

“No,” said Gabe.

Dec looked at him.

“She told me nothing,” said Gabe. “She told me you’d tell me something. She told me in the kitchen at nine with the kettle on. She told me don’t tell him I told you.” He put both hands over his face. “I’m the worst liar you’ve ever met. You said.”

“I did say.”

“It was a nice thing.”

“It was a nice thing,” agreed Dec.

The kettle clicked off. Neither of them moved to it.

Dec stood in the kitchen doorway with his hands at his sides. The way he stood at the front of everything. Except his face was doing a thing Gabe had seen maybe four times in eleven years, and three of them had been this year.

“We’re together,” said Dec.

Gabe didn’t cry straight away. He’d thought he would. He’d been planning to, sort of, since a quarter past eight. He stood there with his hands flat on the counter behind him and looked at Dec. And for about three seconds he didn’t do anything at all.

Then he did.

All of it. At once. The way he did everything. He came off the counter and across the kitchen and put both arms round Dec, the big heavy arms of a man who’d been his friend since a school choir. He’d never once asked permission. He didn’t ask now. And he cried into Dec’s shoulder in the kitchen of the flat in Malacca at four minutes past nine on a Saturday. And Dec let him.

“I KNEW,” said Gabe, into the shoulder.

“Everybody knew.”

“I KNEW SINCE THE CAT THING.”

“You said,” said Dec. “At midnight. In January. How long have you known.”

“And you said Wednesday,” said Gabe. “About me. Since the cat thing about her.” He let go and stood back and wiped his face with his whole arm. “You asked her?”

“I asked her.”

“Out loud?”

“On a bench,” said Dec. “Under a tree. I said are we together.”

Gabe stared at him.

“And she said—“

“She said since the gate,” said Dec. “She said you just never said. And then she said say it properly.” Something happened at the corner of his mouth. “So I said it properly.”

Gabe made the kettle noise.

“And then,” said Dec.

Gabe stopped.

Dec was looking at the floor. Which he never did. He looked at everything, all the time, edge to edge, the way Hyv did in the game. He was looking at the kitchen floor now, very carefully, and his ears had gone a colour Gabe had only ever seen on Deston.

“And then what,” said Gabe.

“And then I leaned in,” said Dec.

Gabe’s hands went to his mouth.

“And she pushed me down,” said Dec. “On the bench. Against the back. With both hands.” He was still looking at the floor. “And kissed me.”

There was a very long silence in the kitchen.

“She—“ said Gabe.

“First,” said Dec. “She said I go first. You wait, and I go.” He looked up, finally. His face was doing all of it. “I didn’t see it coming, Gabe. I didn’t see it coming at all.”

Gabe sat down on the kitchen floor.

He hadn’t meant to. His legs just went. He sat on the floor of the kitchen with his back against the cupboard under the sink and laughed. And cried. And did both, the way Hyv did both. Until Suzu came out of the guest room in his socks and stood in the kitchen doorway and looked at the two of them.

“What,” said Suzu.

“She pushed him DOWN,” said Gabe, from the floor.

Suzu looked at Dec for a long time.

“Good,” said Suzu, and went back to bed.

He didn’t tell anybody.

Not for the first forty minutes. He sat on the kitchen floor with a cup of tea Dec had made him, too strong, the way Dec made it. He didn’t tell anybody. He held it in his chest like a hot stone and didn’t tell anybody. Because it was Dec’s, and he’d learned that this year, from twenty-two days and a door at the end of a corridor. You didn’t tell people other people’s things.

At a quarter to ten, Dec looked at him over his own cup.

“You can tell people,” said Dec.

Gabe looked up.

“It’s not a secret,” said Dec. “It was never a secret. It was just a thing nobody had said.” He drank his tea. “I’ve said it now. You can tell people.”

“Can I?”

“Yes.”

“Everybody?”

“Yes,” said Dec. “Within the hour. Hyv said you would.”

Gabe was already on his feet.

He told Deston first, because Deston was opposite. He went across the corridor in his socks at ten to ten and banged on the grille. Deston came to the door with a book in his hand and his reading face on. And Gabe said it all in one breath, dec-asked-on-a-bench-are-we-together-she-said-since-the-gate-and-then-SHE-PUSHED-HIM-DOWN-AND. Deston put the book down on the floor of his own living room, very carefully, under the framed sticker. And sat down on the folding chair. And put his face in his hands. And said finally, which was Rachael’s word, and which Gabe had never once heard him use.

He told the auntie at seven the next morning, at the kopitiam, over two kopi. She listened to the whole thing. Then she said the girl with the bag? and Gabe said yes, and she said aiyo. And then about time. And then, after a long pause, she push him? And Gabe said yes, auntie, and the auntie laughed so hard she had to sit down on an upturned crate behind the counter.

He told the round town at eight, in the game, at the long table, before Dec was even in. He stood up at Moonlight Harvest’s end and said it to the whole banner room. The same breath, more or less. And Leaka screamed. And Hana cried. And Chris said I have a sheet and was told by four people to put it away. And Rachael said finally. And Bee, who’d known since a video call from Australia a year and a quarter ago, said nothing at all and smiled at the table.

And Ama Corrie, on the window seat with her broom, looked at Gabe for a long time over her spectacles.

“She pushed him?” said Ama Corrie.

“Yes,” said Gabe.

“Hm,” said Ama Corrie. And then, as if she were writing it in a margin: “Good for her.”

It had been forty minutes, the first time. Gabe counted afterward. From you can tell people to the auntie, and then the long table. Hyv had said within the hour.

He’d been well within the hour.

Dec came in at nine and sat in the first of the nine with his feet on the rung. And Hyv was already beside him, a minute before, with her face so red the gold of her horns looked pale. And the whole long table looked at them both. And nobody said anything. Because nobody needed to.

Dec looked down the long table at Gabe.

“Within the hour,” he said.

“Forty minutes,” said Gabe.

“I know,” said Dec. “I counted too.”

It was the next week that Gabe found out about the north door.

Not found out. He’d known. Rachael had decided in August, at the long table, the day Suzu said this winter. Gabe’s not coming north. Gabe’s tanking Moonlight Harvest at home on the longest night. She’d said it the way she said everything, deciding who’d be helped first. And Gabe had said obviously, very loudly, and gone back to his tea.

He’d known. He hadn’t known.

He found out on Thursday, when Kei put the north door on the board.

Not the sheet. The sheet was for October; Kei said he’d read it out then. This was the board in the Hall. The one that said nobody alone, threes at least, with the round town drawn on it in chalk, and the seven roads, and the doors. And on Thursday, at the north door, in Kei’s small neat chalk, two names.

Salty · itswendy.

And then, under them:

Baer.

Gabe stood in front of the board for a long time.

The north door was where the dark came.

Everybody knew that. It came down the north road on the longest night, past the ninety-four lamps, past the first mile where Tiger stood saying not yet, to the round town. And the round town had seven roads and seven doors, and a north door two shields wide. And the north door was where it came hardest.

Last year Salty and Wendy and Kite had held it. With Salty’s black sword, and the iron basket the smiths had made, Kindled. And at five in the morning Salty had stepped out one pace and done a full turn at a hundred and four per cent, and the whole dark had leaned back.

And under the floor, under the north door, in the Seven Below, Dec. With a lake standing up.

This year the lake wasn’t there. Dec had put it in a stream. And Dec wasn’t there either. Dec would be on a hill in the dark, past the edge, keeping a light.

So the north door was Salty and Wendy.

And Baer.

“I’m the front,” said Gabe, out loud, to the board. To nobody.

“Yes,” said Rachael, behind him.

He hadn’t known she was there. She was always two paces behind him. He’d stopped noticing, the way you stop noticing a wall. And then sometimes noticed all at once.

He turned round.

Rachael was standing two paces off with her arms folded, looking at the board. She didn’t look at him. She was the one who brewed angry, Sedge had said, a long time ago, in the fen. Pots are very slow. She’d learned slow. She still brewed angry.

“You put me there,” said Gabe.

“Kei put you there.”

“You told Kei.”

“I told Kei who’d be helped first,” said Rachael. “On the longest night. At home. With Dec gone.” She looked at the chalk. “The north door. Then the windows. Then the roads. The north door first.” She looked at him at last. “And the north door needs a front. Salty’s a sword. Wendy’s a bow. Somebody has to stand in front of them and not break.”

“I’m an Anvil,” said Gabe. “I don’t break.”

“That’s a class description,” said Rachael. “Not a medical opinion.”

She’d said that before. On the White Hills, a long time ago, with a Scourer coming for the chalk woman. He laughed. He couldn’t help it.

Then he stopped.

“I’ve never been the front,” he said. Quietly, for him. “Not really. Not when it mattered. Here, Dec’s always— Dec’s always there. At the front. I’m the one behind him.” He looked at the chalk. Baer. “I’m Dec’s.”

Rachael didn’t say anything for a long time.

“Then ask him,” she said.

He asked him on Saturday, on the high fells, with Moonlight Harvest round them and the small angry hares coming down the slopes.

He didn’t know how to start. He’d never asked Dec anything like it. He’d asked him a thousand things, loudly, all the time. Are you okay. Did you eat. Is it the bell. Who is it. He’d never asked him how to be the front.

So he didn’t start. He just said it, on a fell, in the wind, with his hammer in one hand and his shield on the other arm.

“How do you do it?”

Dec looked at him.

“The front,” said Gabe. “The north door. How do you— how do you stand there? When it’s coming? When you know everybody behind you is—“ He stopped. “How do you not break?”

Dec didn’t say anything for a long time. He stood on the fell with his shield on his back, not fighting anything. The hares had stopped, for once, as if they could tell.

“I’ve never told you this,” said Dec.

Gabe waited.

“You’ve never once asked permission,” said Dec. “For anything. Not in eleven years.”

Gabe stared at him.

“You put your arm round people,” said Dec. “Without asking. You came to the bus station at a quarter to six in the morning in your socks, nearly. You came to Malacca on Christmas Eve like you’d always lived here. You cry whenever you want, wherever you want, in front of whoever. You sat on the edge of my pod with two cups of kopi the morning after a crown. And you put your arm round my shoulders and didn’t ask.” He looked at Gabe. “Nobody else has ever done that. Not once.”

“That’s not tanking,” said Gabe.

“It is,” said Dec. “It’s the whole thing. You stand where people are. And you don’t ask if you’re allowed. You’re just there.” He was quiet. “That’s what the front is. It’s not not breaking. Everybody breaks a bit. It’s being there. Without asking. So everybody behind you knows where you are.”

Gabe didn’t say anything. He couldn’t.

“You’ll hold it,” said Dec.

“How do you know?”

“Because you’ve been holding me up for eleven years,” said Dec, “and I’m heavier than the dark.”

He trained with Salty after that. Every night, at the third dawn, at the north door. Nobody had told him to. He just asked Salty, the way Dec had asked Salty in a cellar in Masmark. Show me.

Salty didn’t show him anything. That wasn’t how it worked with Salty. He stood at the north door with Right Size in both hands and said I’m going to swing it at you. Don’t move.

And swung it.

The full turn. Slow. Gabe could see it the whole way round, from Salty’s hips, the black blade coming low across the step. And there was nothing he could do about it except put his shield where it was going to end and set his feet.

BOOM.

He went back four paces into the Hall and sat down on the floor.

“Again,” said Salty.

Gabe got up.

He went back to the north door. He set his feet. Wider, this time, the way Dec had stood on the bridge. Down. And Salty swung, and it came round, slow, the whole way.

BOOM.

Three paces.

“Again,” said Salty.

By the end of the first week, two. By the end of the second, one. Wendy stood at the side with a lamp, the way she always stood with a lamp. And said nothing. And once, when Gabe went back four paces again on a bad night and sat down hard, she said oi, very quietly, to Salty. And Salty said he asked.

At the end of the third week, at the third dawn, Salty swung the full turn into Gabe’s shield and Gabe didn’t go back at all.

He stood there. In the north door, two shields wide. With his feet set and the black blade stopped against his shield and the whole weight of it going down through his arm and his back and his legs into the old stone of the step.

He didn’t move.

Salty looked at him over the top of the black sword for a long time.

“That’s mine,” said Salty.

Gabe looked at him.

“That’s the bit where I stand,” said Salty. “After the turn. When it’s done. When the dark’s leaned back and I’m still standing there.” He took the sword away. “You’ve learned the bit after.”

“I didn’t learn it,” said Gabe. “You hit me with it for three weeks.”

“Same thing,” said Salty, and Wendy, behind him with the lamp, laughed.

Rachael came to the north door on the last night of September, at the third dawn, when Salty and Wendy had gone down the fourth tunnel. They went down most nights now, the two of them, with the lamp on.

Gabe was standing in the door. On his own, for a minute. Looking up the north road. At the ninety-four lamps going away up the moor in the dark, leaning. More, Tiger said, every week.

Rachael stopped two paces behind him.

He didn’t turn round. He knew where she was. He always knew. He’d stopped noticing and then noticed all at once, a hundred times, for a year and a half.

“Rae,” he said.

“Mm.”

“Who’d stand next to you?” said Gabe. “On the night. At the door.”

There was a long silence.

He’d never asked her that. He’d asked her a thousand things. Are you okay. Did you eat. Is it the bell. He’d told Cal, once, a long time ago, nobody, that’s the job, when Cal asked who’d stand next to the front. He’d been wrong, and she’d been two paces behind him ever since.

“You,” said Rachael.

Gabe turned round.

She was standing two paces off, with her arms folded, looking at the lamps. Not at him.

“Two paces,” said Gabe.

“Two paces,” said Rachael. “So I can see where it’s going to be. Before it gets there.” She unfolded her arms. “You know that. I’ve told you.”

“I know,” said Gabe. “I was going to ask.” He made himself say it. Dec had said you never ask permission. He thought about that. He thought you could ask anyway, sometimes, if you wanted to. “Could it be one?”

Rachael looked at him.

She didn’t say anything for a long time. She looked at him the way she looked at people when she was deciding who’d be helped first. Then she looked at the north road again, and the lamps, leaning.

She took a step.

One pace.

“Don’t push it,” said Rachael.

Gabe didn’t. He stood in the north door of the round town at the third dawn, on the last night of September, with the dark a long way up the road and leaning. And somebody one pace behind him instead of two. And he didn’t say anything at all, for once. He just stood there.

He didn’t break.
