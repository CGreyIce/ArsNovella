Live from Elsewhere · Volume 11: Three Hundred and One

Chapter 264

Till They're Home

4,264 words · 19 min read

The ninety-fourth stone was the only one Ice had ever been frightened of.

Not the stone. The flame in it. In August, at the far end of the north road, he’d stood here with Tiger and Maigo and Hako and Hyv and watched the little flame in the hollow lie down flat. It had pointed north like a finger. The dark beyond had been thick, and leaning, and something tall had come up out of the heather months too early, alone, and looked straight past the lamp at the round town. He’d sent it back with the Weighted Cut and stood there afterwards for a long time with his shield up, at ninety-eight per cent. He hadn’t been hurt. He’d been frightened.

They came over the ridge from the old hall on Tuesday evening, at the first dark, and there it was. The ninety-fourth. A grey stone at the top of the road, with a hollow in its north face and a little glass in the hollow.

The flame was standing straight up.

He stopped. Hyv stopped with him, because she was holding on to his arm. She’d been holding on to it since the old hall. Since before that. Both hands, round his arm above the elbow, on his shield side, so that he’d had to learn to carry the shield a bit further out.

“It’s standing,” said Hyv.

“Yes.”

“I saw it lie down. Here.” She was looking at it, edge to edge, the way she looked at everything. “I didn’t tell you how much it frightened me.”

“You didn’t have to,” said Ice. “I was frightened too.”

She looked up at him.

“You said that,” said Hyv. “Out loud. In front of people.”

“There’s only seven people.”

“Seven people,” said Hyv. And put her head on his shoulder, on the road, with her hands still round his arm, and walked on like that, so that he had to walk on like that too.

Behind them, Xola made a small noise like a kettle.

The north road ran from the ninety-fourth stone to the north door of the Hall of Roads. Ninety-four stones, one every mile. Ice had walked it four times now. Up, in August. Up again, in November, with the case. And now down. He’d never walked it in the dark with every flame standing.

It was a different road.

In November, going north, the flames had leaned. All of them, from about the fortieth on, a little more at every stone. As if a wind were blowing up the road from the south that you couldn’t feel. You’d walk from one to the next and see it was leaning further. And not say anything, because everybody could see it.

Now they stood. Every one. Ninety-three. Ninety-two. Ninety-one. A little gold light standing in a stone every mile, all the way down the road into the dark, to the bottom of the sky, where you could just see the next one, and past it the next. Not pointing anywhere. Just there. Saying here. Saying the road’s here.

“It’s a road again,” said Hako, beside him. In his own voice. “It was a lure. Ama Corrie said. All those years. Pointing at the round town.” He looked at the next flame, a mile off, standing. “It’s just a road now.”

“It’s for people,” said Suzu, from the front.

Nobody had asked him anything. He’d just said it. He did that now. He’d done it on the hill and on the stones and at the edge, the whole way, since the two hundred and sixteenth. Said a thing first, when it was true, without waiting to be asked.

Ice watched the back of him for a while, walking south at the front with the empty case on his back. Then he looked at the road.

On the second night, at the sixty-first stone, the fell-hounds came.

They were the first ordinary thing with teeth since the edge. Ice had nearly forgotten there were ordinary things with teeth. Past the edge there’d been the nothing, and the Unlit, and the still water standing up as high as the Hall of Roads. Nothing that ran on four legs and wanted to eat you because it was hungry.

Hyv saw them first. Of course she did.

“Five,” she said. Not loudly. She’d let go of his arm. He hadn’t felt her do it. Her bow was in her hand. “Grey. Low. Coming out of the heather on the left, in a fan. One in front. Two going round left. Two going round right.” A breath. “They’re going round us. The front one’s the one that’s watching.”

He saw the front one. Big, grey, low to the heather, head down. Thirty paces off, standing still, looking at him. And either side of it, out at the edges of the candlelight, the other four. Moving. Slow and easy. Going round.

[ Fell-hound · Lv 61 ] [ Hunts in fives. The one in front decides. ]

That was the whole thing, then. The one in front decided. It would stand there and look at you until the four had gone all the way round. And then it would decide, and five would come at once from five sides. And you’d be in the middle of five, in the dark, on a road.

“Ring?” said Thea, on his left, with her shield up. She’d been on his left the whole way.

“No,” said Ice. “No time. They’d finish going round.”

He went forward.

Not fast. Walking. He walked straight at the front one, off the road, onto the heather, banging his sword on the rim of his shield. Clang. Clang. Clang. The way he’d done it in a narrow place at Lv 5, with three rats. Walking pace, on the beat, so loud that the whole of the dark country could hear it.

The front one hadn’t decided yet.

That was the point. Ice was making it decide now. Not when the four had gone all the way round. Now, with the two on the left only halfway and the two on the right barely started. A man walking at it, banging a shield, closer every beat. Twenty paces. Fifteen. It had to come or go. It couldn’t stand there and wait, because he wasn’t letting it.

It came.

It came low and fast at his legs, the way a dog goes for an ankle. He dropped the shield’s rim into its face. It met the iron with its teeth, with a crack he felt in his elbow, and its own weight turned it. He put his foot down hard on the heather, Anchor, and the whole fell-hound went sideways along the slope of the shield. Its belly came open to the sword.

He cut. Once. Down, through, on the beat.

The front one was in the heather and not getting up. And the four were still going round.

Late.

All four of them. Out of step. The two on the left had been halfway and came in now from halfway, too early, one a breath behind the other. The two on the right hadn’t even got started and came straight across instead, the long way. Not five at once from five sides. Two, and then one, and then one. One at a time.

“Left, two,” said Hyv. “Thea.”

Thea took the first one on her shield. It hit her full on, and she gave a step and didn’t give another. Ting. Kotori, on the beat, behind her. The second one went past Thea’s shield for her legs and found Slep’s glaive in the way.

It was still bent. Slep had kept it bent, from the stone. It had held a stone up, bent. Everybody had told him to have Ina straighten it. He’d said no.

The bend took the fell-hound’s foreleg like a hook and pulled it off its feet. The hound came down on its side on the heather. Xola put the trident through it before it could get up.

“Oh,” said Xola.

“It’s better bent,” said Slep. “It hooks.”

“Right, one,” said Hyv.

The fourth came across the road at Ice’s back. He didn’t turn round. He didn’t have to. Gigi was already there. A hedge came up out of the heather between him and it in one breath. Thorn and black leaf, waist-high. The hound went into it at full speed and stuck. Suzu’s scythe came round in a long low arc and that was the end of that one.

“Last one,” said Hyv. “Running.”

It was. Away north, across the heather, toward the dark, in long low bounds. It had seen four go down and decided, at last, on its own, that it was going.

Hyv’s arrow went through the candlelight and out into the dark after it. Ice heard it hit. A long way off. Then nothing.

He stood on the heather with the sword in his hand. His left calf hurt where the second one had got its teeth in on the way past Thea. He hadn’t noticed till now. Eighty-two per cent. He kept the pain at fifty. He’d always kept it at fifty.

“You went first,” said Hyv.

She’d come round in front of him. She was looking up at him, edge to edge, with the bow still in her hand.

“They were going round,” said Ice.

“You always go first in a fight,” said Hyv. “And last at everything else.”

And she put up her free hand and turned his face down by the chin. She kissed him on the cheek, very firmly, on the heather, in front of seven people, with a dead fell-hound at his feet.

He went pink. He felt himself do it. Right up to the ears.

“The male,” said Hako, very quietly, from somewhere behind him, “observed in his natural habitat—“

“Hako,” said Ice.

“—has gone pink.”

“That’s the voice,” said Xola. “You said you’d stopped.”

“I’m doing it for Tiger,” said Hako.

Kotori came and put her hand on his calf. Ting. Ninety-one. Then she looked up at him, and at Hyv, who’d gone back to holding his arm with both hands as if nothing had happened.

“Is she going to be like this all the way home?” said Kotori.

“Yes,” said Hyv.

They saw the dome on Friday.

At the eleventh stone, at the first dark. From the top of a long rise, where the north road came up out of a shallow valley and the moor opened out south. And there it was at the bottom of the sky. The round town. Not the town; it was too far for that. The dome of the Hall of Roads, low and pale against the dark. And at the very top of it, at the eye, one small gold light.

“Kei’s lamp,” said Hyv.

The one on the Somewhere bench. Kei had set it there the night after the longest night, so the hill could see the round town. Ice had seen it from the hill at Christmas, a point of gold at the bottom of the south, looking back.

He’d never seen it from this close.

“There’s somebody next to it,” said Hyv. “On the bench. In a blanket.”

“Isa,” said Suzu.

“No,” said Hyv. She was looking, far, far, edge to edge. “Isa’s carried up at dusk and down after. It’s dark now. It’s a blanket, but it’s not Isa.” A pause. “It’s Kei. He’s sitting next to his own lamp. With a map on his knee.”

“He’s watching the road,” said Thea.

“He’s counting the stones,” said Hyv. “Like we did. He’s counting us in.”

They came to the north door at the third dawn on Saturday.

Midnight, out there. Saturday night. The thirteenth of January. Seventy-one days since they’d gone out of it. They came down the last mile in the dark, past the first stone, with its flame standing, and the road went flat and wide, and there it was.

The Hall of Roads. The north steps. The north door, two shields wide, shut.

And at the top of the door, in the little window that let the light through from the Hall onto the north steps, a candle.

Ice stopped at the bottom of the steps.

He’d known about it. Everybody’d known. Chernn had put it there the morning they left. In the little window that nobody ever looked at, with a sill she’d measured with her thumb. A candle in the window, looking up the north road. She’d changed it every morning, Deston had written. Every morning, at the first dawn, before anything. A new one, from the box from Tallow Green. Till they’re home.

Seventy-one mornings.

It was burning low. The morning’s candle, nearly gone. A small flame in a little window at the top of a door in the dark, standing straight up.

“Oh,” said Thea.

Gigi took her hand.

Suzu went up the steps first. Of course he did. He’d been at the front since the hill. He went up the north steps with the empty case on his back and put his hand flat on the north door.

It stuck. The way it always did.

He pushed. And from the other side, at the same moment, somebody pulled. And the north door came open all at once onto the Hall of Roads and the light and the noise. Suzu nearly fell in.

Gabe caught him.

Of course it was Gabe. Gabe had held the north door on the longest night with Rae at his back at zero paces. He’d been standing in it, apparently, every night since Kei’s map showed them at the eleventh stone. Gabe caught Suzu by both shoulders and held him up. Then he looked past him, down the steps, at the other eight in the dark. At Ice, at the bottom, with Hyv on his arm.

Gabe’s face did about nine things at once.

“Dec,” said Gabe.

“Hi,” said Ice.

The Hall was full.

Saturday night. Everybody up. Everybody who’d been on the board on the longest night and everybody who hadn’t. The whole long table out of the banner room and into the Hall. Sixty people, standing. And all of them, as the nine came in out of the dark one by one through the north door, said nothing at all. For about three seconds.

Then Leaka screamed.

Then everybody.

Ice didn’t hear most of it. He was looking for one person, and she wasn’t where he’d thought she’d be.

Chernn wasn’t at the door. She was on the stair to the little window. A narrow stone stair inside the north door, up the wall, that nobody ever used. She was at the top of it, by the sill, with a new candle in her hand. Unlit. She’d come to change it early. Of course she had. She’d seen Kei’s map. She’d known they’d come tonight. And she’d come up anyway with the morning’s candle, at midnight, because she’d said every morning, till they’re home, and they weren’t home yet. Not until they were through the door.

They were through the door.

She stood at the top of the stair with the new candle in her hand and looked at the old one, burning low in the little window. Then she looked down at the nine in the Hall.

Deston was at the bottom of the stair. Ice saw him look up at her. He didn’t say anything. He just held up his hand, the way you would for somebody coming down a stair in the dark.

Chernn didn’t change the candle.

She left the old one where it was, in the little window, burning down. She came down the stair with the new one in her hand, unlit, and took Deston’s hand at the bottom. Then she walked across the Hall to Suzu, through sixty people, who moved.

“This was for tomorrow,” said Chernn. She held it out. “I don’t need it.”

Suzu looked at the candle. Then at her.

“Keep it,” said Chernn. “For the spring.”

Suzu took it. He held it in both hands. A plain white candle from Tallow Green, in a twist of paper. On the paper, in a hand Ice didn’t know, a name. Wick’s girl.

“Three hundred and one,” said Suzu.

“Yes,” said Chernn. And put her face in Deston’s shoulder, very suddenly, and cried. And Deston put his arm round her and looked at the ceiling, with his ears red.

Isa was awake.

She was in her chair by the north door, the one that had waited three hundred years. Under Ama Corrie’s grey blanket, very small, with her stick across her knees. Ama Corrie was standing beside her with a hand on the back of the chair. Ice found out later that Isa hadn’t let anybody carry her to bed since Kei’s lamp first picked them up at the eleventh stone. She’d said I’ll sleep when they’re in. And Ama Corrie had said you’ll sleep when I say, and then not said.

She couldn’t see the nine. Not their faces, from across a room. She’d never been able to see windows, or faces far off. Only close.

Suzu went and knelt in front of her chair so that she could.

She looked at his face for a long time. Close. Reading it, the way she read things.

“Three hundred,” said Suzu. “Both ways. I counted them home.”

“Out loud?”

“Out loud. All nine of us.” He swallowed. “So they’d know we were going home.”

Isa’s face did something very old.

“The thorn,” she said. “The one the girl grew. Two hundred and seventeen.”

“It’s in leaf,” said Gigi, from behind him. “It set fruit. In January.”

Isa laughed. A dry small laugh, like a stick breaking.

“My mother would have hated that,” she said. “Something growing on the way. She liked the way bare.” She put out her hand and laid it on Suzu’s head, the way she did. “And the lamp?”

“Standing,” said Suzu. “Over the jar. On the crown. Your bowl’s there. And your blanket. And the stick. And Kotori’s cup. For your mothers.”

“Tea,” said Isa. “On the hill.” She shook her head slowly. “Three hundred years. Nobody ever brought tea.”

“I’m going back,” said Suzu. “In the spring. With a stone.”

Isa’s hand stayed on his head.

“Three hundred and one,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Hm,” said Isa Corrie. She closed her eyes, with her hand on Suzu’s head. Ama Corrie looked down at her and didn’t move, and after a while Ice realised she’d gone to sleep.

Ama Corrie came to the counter.

She had the Roll open on it. She always did. She looked at the nine, one at a time, the way she looked at people who came in through doors. Then she picked up her pen and wrote something. Ice couldn’t see what. A line, under something. Then another.

“Nine out,” said Ama Corrie. “Nine in.”

“Yes,” said Ice.

She looked at him over the Roll.

“You’re slow,” said Ama Corrie.

“We walked.”

“Hm.” She looked at his arm. At the two hands round it, above the elbow. Hyv’s hands. Hyv hadn’t let go since the north steps. She hadn’t let go when Gabe caught Suzu, or when Leaka screamed, or when sixty people came at them all at once. She’d held on with both hands, very red, and looked at everybody who looked at her arm with her chin up.

Ama Corrie looked at the hands for a long time.

“Hm,” said Ama Corrie.

Hyv went red to the horns.

She didn’t let go.

“He’s mine,” said Hyv. To Ama Corrie. Out loud, at the counter, in the Hall of Roads, in front of the Roll, with sixty people behind her who’d all gone very quiet.

Ama Corrie looked at her.

“I know,” said Ama Corrie. “I’ve got eyes.” She put the pen down. She looked at the floor of the Hall, where nine people had just walked in off a road with the whole dark country on their boots. “Sweep.”

“It’s midnight,” said Gabe.

“It’s Saturday,” said Ama Corrie, as though that settled it.

They swept. All nine of them, at midnight, the whole Hall. Hyv swept one-handed, so that she could keep the other on his arm. Ama Corrie watched her do it and said nothing. Ice thought it was the nearest she’d ever come to laughing.

At a quarter to eight, he went into the banner room.

He went because everybody else did. It was the end of the night, the egg at eight. He thought they were going to the long table to sit. He went in with Hyv on his arm and Gabe at his other shoulder, still holding on, as he had been for eight hours. And the whole of Constellation sat down at the long table, sixty-one chairs and a stool at the end, and nobody said anything.

Kei stood up at the head of the table.

Ice didn’t know why. Kei didn’t stand up at the long table. He sat with his map and waited for people to finish.

“Set one to watch,” said Kei.

And sixty people said, all at once, at a quarter to eight in the morning, in the banner room:

“So the rest may sleep.”

Ice sat very still.

He’d said it every morning since the edge. At a quarter to eight, in a ring of candles, with eight other people. On the hill, round the flat stone, in the light. He’d thought it was theirs. The nine of them, out there in the dark, a very long way north.

He looked down the long table. Gabe beside him, red-eyed. Deston and Chernn. Leaka. Dennis. Rae. Salty. Tiger. Everybody. And he understood.

“Every morning,” he said.

“Since the edge,” said Kei. He sat back down with his map. “The first morning after. At the same time as you. So you’d have the rest of us saying it with you. Even if you couldn’t hear.”

“You didn’t tell me.”

“No,” said Kei. “It wasn’t for you to hear. It was for us to say.”

Ice didn’t say anything. He couldn’t.

Hyv put her head on his shoulder. Gabe, on his other side, took hold of his other arm, so that he had somebody on both, at the long table, in front of everybody, and couldn’t have moved if he’d wanted to.

He didn’t want to.

“You’re home,” said Hyv, into his shoulder.

“I know.”

“Say it.”

“I’m home,” said Ice.

The egg took him at eight with both arms held. Out there, in Malacca, in the next room, he heard Gabe start crying before the egg had finished opening. He sat on the edge of the bed in the grey light and waited for him to stop. Then he went and put the kettle on.
