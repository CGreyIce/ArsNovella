Gabe walked into Fernwick at dusk on Sunday, the tenth of January, on the fifty-sixth day of the game. He’d been walking for seven days.

Ice saw him first. He’d been standing at the end of the causeway bridge for an hour, pretending not to wait, with his shield on his back and his hands in his pockets. Behind him the town was lighting its lanterns one at a time along the Landing. In front of him the raised road went away north-east across the fen toward the fields, empty, going blue in the evening.

And then not empty.

A figure, a long way off, on the causeway. Not tall. Solid. Walking steadily, without hurrying, the way you walk when you’ve been walking for a week and know exactly how much is left. Something big and round on his back: a shield. Something heavy over his shoulder: a hammer. As he came closer Ice could see his face, and it was just Gabe’s face. The same one that had come out of the arrivals hall at KLIA with a grey pillow under its arm.

He came down the last stretch of the causeway and onto the bridge, and stopped in front of Ice, and looked at him.

“Dec,” said Gabe.

“You walked.”

“Seven days.” Gabe put the hammer down, head first, on the boards of the bridge, and leaned on it. “Six of them in the rain. One of them through a bog. One of them past a man in a black coat with a little bird on the collar, who looked at me for a long time and decided not to.” He grinned. “I’d have hit him with the hammer.”

“I know you would.”

“Is it far now? To the swamp?”

“You’re in the swamp,” said Ice.

Gabe looked round. At the round houses on their stilts. The boardwalks bowing over the black water. The lanterns going up along the Landing. The eel man at the end of his jetty with a fire in an iron basket, and Kotori on the eel man’s post, on one foot, waving at them with her whole arm. Rafi at the far end of the north walk, on Sedge’s steps, with a black sword on his back that was very slightly less too big for him than it had been a month ago.

“Oh,” said Gabe softly. “It’s exactly like on stream.”

“It’s better than on stream.”

“I know,” said Gabe. “That’s what I meant.”

He did it on the bridge, with the stream on and thirty thousand people watching. He’d thought about doing it in the ring, off stream, quietly. Then he’d thought about it again. He’d done the world first dark and shown it after. He’d done the Long Night lit. This felt like a lit thing.

Gabe looked at the window for a moment. Then he reached out and touched it.

Six.

Ice looked at the six names in the party window, one under the other. Deston, who he’d stood next to in the back row of a school choir since they were thirteen, and who still sang. Suzu, who’d got off a bus at Melaka Sentral with his own pillow, and whose name was on the share register now. Kotori, who’d walked up to a notice that said kind people only and stood on a post. Sheng Yang, the tall one from the poly, who’d got to nineteen on his own and told nobody. And Gabe, from the other end of the same back row, on a bridge in the fen, leaning on a hammer.

And him.

The party maximum. Full again. It had been six for one afternoon at the old ford, with Xav still in it, and then five for nearly three weeks. He hadn’t minded five. He’d just kept looking at the empty line.

Gabe’s first act in Fernwick was to fix the narrows.

Ice hadn’t asked him to. Nobody had. But Gabe had watched eight hours of the Long Night on the flight from Melbourne, on the plane’s wifi. He’d seen the post crack. Then he’d come into the game on Christmas Eve in a town on a cliff with a smithy at the top of it. And before he’d set out to walk to a swamp, he’d spent three days at that smithy, forging something.

He took it out of his bag on the north walk. Four bands of iron, black and new, shaped to fit round a post, with holes for rivets.

“I heard about the post,” he said.

He fitted them round the split post at the narrows himself, kneeling on the boardwalk over the black water, with a hammer and a bag of rivets. Hollis came out from his yard to watch, with his arms folded. So did the eel man, and Marta, and Rafi, down from Sedge’s steps. Gabe worked for an hour, in the last of the light and then by lantern. He did it the way he did everything, without any fuss and without stopping, until the four bands were on and the split was closed and the post was whole again.

Then he stood on the plank at the narrows, where Ice had stood for twelve hours, and stamped on it. Once. Hard.

It didn’t move.

“That’ll hold,” said Gabe.

Hollis, at the end of the walk, unfolded his arms. He came along the boards slowly. He looked at the iron bands on the post for a long time. Then at Gabe’s hammer, and his shield, and his face.

“Another one,” said Hollis.

“Sorry?”

“Another one who stands at the front.” Hollis looked at Ice, and then back at Gabe. “Hammer and shield. You’re a second line. You stand where he can’t.”

“That’s the plan,” said Gabe.

“Hm,” said Hollis. He reached out with his three-fingered hand and put it on the iron band, flat. “Good work,” he said. Then he turned round and walked back to his yard without another word.

Gabe stood there on the plank and looked after him.

“Was that good?” he said.

“From Hollis?” said Ice. “That was a parade.”

At moonrise he went dark, and took them to the ring.

Six of them, out along the north walk past the narrows with its new iron bands, round the deep water and across the tussocks where the dark grass had roots. Between two of the nine grey stones, into the warm clear water, with their boots in their hands.

Gabe stood in the middle of the ring and looked round, the way they all had the first time. Sheng Yang had, three weeks ago. Kotori had, and Suzu, and before any of them Deston and Xav, back when there were only three of them. At the long grey necks bent toward him. At the shut green fists in the water, opening one by one in the moonlight. At the sound.

“Oh,” said Gabe, very quietly.

He didn’t say anything else. He sat down on the nearest stone, with his feet in the water and his hammer across his knees, and listened to the bells.

They all sat down. Deston on a flat stone at the north side, with the book on his knees. Kotori on the edge, humming very softly with the bells, a beat behind. Sheng Yang with his long legs in the water and the sword across his knees, easy. Suzu with his back against the ninth heron, the one that faced the wrong way, his eyes half shut. Gabe in the middle. And Ice on the stone he always sat on, with Osric Fell’s boss on the shield beside him.

Nobody said anything for a long time. The moonbells rang, slightly out of time, all round them.

When they came back out between the stones, wet to the knee, he put the stream back on.

They didn’t go back to town. They went as far as the narrows and sat down on the boardwalk there in a row, six of them, with their boots beside them and their feet hanging over the black water. Gabe’s four iron bands were on the post at Ice’s elbow, still smelling faintly of the forge. Behind them, across the tussocks, the moonbells were still ringing, just at the edge of hearing.

He opened the Tally.

He hadn’t meant to. Or he had. He’d been meaning to for a week. His hand went to it the way it used to every five minutes, before the river, before the door. This time he didn’t close it.

He sat there on the plank, with his feet over the water and five people sitting along it beside him, and read his chat.

He did it slowly, the way he used to read it in the first week, when there were ten people in it and he could put a voice to every name. There were thirty thousand in it now. He couldn’t read all of it. He didn’t try. He read the diamonds.

There were so many of them.

On the first night, ten people had been in his chat, and most of the diamonds in it had been the same handful. Salty, and Niko, and Leaka. Alice and Jared, together, the way they always arrived. Gabe. Chris, already making a sheet. Tif with her green sword. He’d given every friend that diamond, so that in a busy chat they’d stand out like lit windows. In a chat of ten, it had mostly meant half the room was pink.

Now the room was thirty thousand people. And the diamonds went down the whole length of it, like lights along a road.

He read all of it twice. Then he leaned back against Gabe’s post, with the new iron cold against his shoulder, and looked up at the moon.

He’d been in everyone’s chat for years. Saying something stupid to make them laugh at three in the morning. Raiding them with twelve people and a joke. Being the voice in the corner while they streamed to twenty. Turning up. Always turning up. He’d never minded. He’d liked the company.

And now everyone was in his.

Not just in his chat. In his towns and his roads and his rivers, in a world that sold everything for real money, where not everything was for sale. Coming in, one wave at a time, from all over the world, the way he’d watched them come into their own streams for years. Keeping their own lights. Saying here.

And six of them were sitting on a plank in a cold fen, with their feet over the black water and a ring of warm water behind them, listening to the moonbells, with nowhere else they needed to be.

“Dec,” said Gabe, eventually, from the end of the row.

“Yeah.”

“What’s next?”

Ice thought about it. A dash on a charter, waiting for a name. Eight idiots on their way to a riverbank. Drew and Tiger and a staircase of seven locks. A silver line through a lake in Selwick, and ditches round a hedge in Briarwick, and two people on Kei’s call who thought somebody had known. A sheet of paper on a desk in Australia, covered in dots, and the lines between them getting thicker. An egg ordered for a flat in Singapore, for February. Another one on its way to an office above a kopitiam, for a woman who read the Exchange the way she read a balance sheet. Eighty-one lights in a jar, and nobody knew where they went.

“I don’t know,” he said.

Gabe grinned in the moonlight.

“Good,” he said.
