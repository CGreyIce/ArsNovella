Live from Elsewhere · Volume 8: Somewhere Else

Chapter 193

A Word on the Cut

3,753 words · 16 min read

Niko found her on the high fells on Saturday morning, killing Scree-Runners as if they’d said something about her mother.

He’d come up with the four of them at the first dawn because the fells were where you went in January if you were in your forties and wanted to be fifty. Him and Newish and Chris and Winter, the way it had been since a town called Ferrowmere a year ago. They came up over the last ridge into the wind with the moor behind them, and there she was, below them on the long slope of scree, alone. Bow up. Arrows going.

Not alone. He corrected himself. Bee was there, at the top of the slope, sitting on a rock with her sketchbook open and a Shield drawn over Hyvred’s back in grey pencil that flickered every time something hit it. But Bee wasn’t fighting. Bee was watching. And Hyvred was doing everything else.

“Oh,” said Newish, beside him. “Oh, she’s angry.”

“She’s not angry,” said Niko.

“She’s killing them like she’s angry.”

Niko watched for a while. Hyvred on the scree, calling her own shots out loud, left, low, two, in the flat carrying voice she used from the eye of the dome. Except there was nobody to call them to. She was calling them to herself. And loosing, and loosing, and moving, and loosing again. A Scree-Runner came down the slope at her in a rattle of stone, the big kind, the kind you were supposed to take in fours, and she put three arrows into it before it was halfway and stepped aside, and it went past her into a boulder and stopped.

[ Hyvred · Lv 46 ]

“She’s not angry,” said Niko again. “She’s in a hurry.”

He’d been there on Friday night, in the banner room, when Ice said Monday, ahead. Everybody had. He’d seen Hyvred stand up with her face white and walk out and go up the two hundred and twelve. And Bee go after her. And Thea at the window, and Xola with both hands over her mouth, and Ice sitting in the first of the nine not knowing what he’d done.

Niko had known what he’d done. He’d been doing sums about it since the cat thing in August. It wasn’t hard. Hyvred had been levelling like a maniac since Christmas. Up at first dawn, every day, at the hatch, calling the north tunnel. Then out on the fells all afternoon with Bee. She’d been forty-one after the longest night. She was forty-six now. She’d been going to walk into Masmark at the front of the first ten, next to him.

And he’d said ahead.

It wasn’t his fault. Niko had thought that on Friday night, and he thought it now, on the ridge in the wind. Ice had wanted to see a friend from a long time ago. A friend who didn’t ask for things had asked if he was coming. That was a perfectly good reason. You didn’t stay in the round town because somebody might be hurt that you’d gone. You went to see your friend.

But Hyvred was on the scree killing Scree-Runners as fast as she could, alone, in a hurry. And Niko was a patient man, and an empathetic one, and he’d been in enough games with enough people to know what that looked like.

It looked like somebody trying to get somewhere before a door closed.

“Come on,” he said to the other three. “Let’s be useful.”

They went down the scree to her. She saw them coming, of course. She saw everything, edge to edge. She didn’t stop loosing until they were twenty paces off, and then she lowered the bow and stood there in the wind with her horns gold in the grey light and her tail very still.

“I’m fine,” said Hyvred.

“I didn’t say anything,” said Niko.

“You were going to.”

“I was going to say we’re going the same way,” said Niko. “We’re forty-five. We want to be fifty. You want to be fifty.” He shrugged. “Six is better than two. Bee doesn’t count; she’s drawing.”

“I’m shielding,” said Bee, from her rock.

“Bee’s shielding,” said Niko. “Five and a shield. Come on.”

Hyvred looked at him for a long moment. At the four of them. Newish with her drum on her back, grinning. Chris with a spreadsheet open in his window, which he always had, even in a fight. Winter at the back with her harpoon, saying nothing.

“Fine,” said Hyvred.

“Fine,” said Winter. Which from Winter was a speech.

They went up the fells all day.

It was good. Niko had forgotten how good it was, the five of them. He’d been in Niko’s four for a year; he’d been in the guild for ten months. He’d done the Long Night at a gallery in the round market with Leaka and Curse. He’d done halls for ten and the second rank and the road of lights. But he’d never fought next to Hyvred before. Not properly. Not with her calling.

She called the fells for them the way she’d called the north tunnel for Ice. From the back, edge to edge, everything at once.

“Two. Left. The one behind’s bigger.”

Niko went left. There were two. The one behind was bigger.

“Newish, slow the drum. They’re coming in on the off-beat.”

Newish slowed the drum. Tempo. The Scree-Runners came in on the off-beat and missed it, and the five of them were already where the beat said to be.

“Chris, stop typing.”

“I’m logging the—“

“STOP TYPING.”

Chris stopped typing. A Scree-Runner came over the ridge into the place he’d been about to stand, and he wasn’t there.

By the second dusk, Niko was forty-six. Newish forty-six. Chris forty-five and three-quarters, and complaining about it, and logging it. Winter forty-six, and silent. Hyvred forty-six and nine-tenths, and still going.

They sat on the ridge in the evening, in the wind, with the moor going purple below them and the round town a long way off to the south. Its dome was a grey thumb on the horizon. Newish made tea on a little fire. Bee drew all of them. Hyvred sat a little apart with her bow across her knees and looked south.

Not at the round town. Past it. South and east. Where the road went, fourteen days of it, off the edge of Kei’s map.

Niko went and sat down beside her, after a while. Not close. A couple of paces. He’d learned that from watching her with Ice.

“Can I show you something?” he said.

She looked at him.

“A skill,” said Niko. “Mine. From Tallbridge. An old woman taught me. Wenlock.” He drew his sword. A plain one, straight, nothing special. He’d never cared much about the sword. It was what you said on it that mattered. “It’s called Spoken Edge. You say a word, on the cut. And if it’s the right word, the cut does what the word says.”

“I’m a bow,” said Hyvred.

“It works on arrows,” said Niko. “Wenlock said. She said it works on anything with an edge. I’ve never tried it on an arrow. I haven’t got a bow.” He held out his free hand. “Can I?”

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she took an arrow out of her quiver and gave it to him.

He held it up in the last of the light. A plain arrow. Winter’s fletching, grey goose, very neat.

“You say the word as it goes,” said Niko. “Not before. Not after. As it goes. And it has to be true.” He turned the arrow. “That’s the hard part. It only takes if you mean it. If you say hold and you don’t really want it to hold, it doesn’t. If you say break and part of you doesn’t want it to break, nothing happens. It knows.”

“It knows?”

“Wenlock says the edge knows,” said Niko. “I didn’t believe her either.” He gave the arrow back. “Try one. On that rock.”

Hyvred looked at the rock, thirty paces down the slope. She nocked the arrow. She drew.

“What word?”

“Whatever you want it to do.”

She thought about it. Then she said, as she loosed, “Through.”

The arrow hit the rock and went into it, a finger’s length, into solid stone. It stuck there, quivering.

[ Spoken Edge · Through · it took ]

“Oh,” said Hyvred.

“See,” said Niko. “You meant that one.”

She tried more. He’d known she would. She was impulsive; she’d always been impulsive. She’d hit Ice every time he came back from anywhere for five months, without once deciding to.

Stay. The arrow stuck in a clump of heather and stayed, even when Newish pulled at it.

Far. It went further than any arrow should, over the ridge and out of sight, and Chris logged it as four hundred paces.

Fast. Nothing. It went the speed it always went.

“You didn’t mean it,” said Niko.

“I did mean it.”

“You don’t care about fast,” said Niko. “You’ve never cared about fast. You care about where.” He shrugged. “It knows.”

She looked at him. Then she laughed, a bit, for the first time all day.

She tried more. Here. Now. Left, which made the arrow bend in the air like a swallow and everybody on the ridge said what at the same time. She was good at it. She was very good at it. She’d been calling shots for a year, saying the word for where a thing was going to be. Saying a word on an arrow wasn’t so different.

Then she stopped.

She sat on the ridge with an arrow nocked and the bow half drawn and didn’t loose. She looked south and east, past the grey thumb of the round town’s dome on the horizon, where the road went.

“What if you don’t know the word?” she said.

Niko looked at her.

“What if you know what you want it to do,” said Hyvred. “But you can’t say it. You don’t know how. You’ve got the arrow and the bow and you know exactly where you want it to go, and you open your mouth and—“ She stopped. “Nothing comes.”

The ridge had gone quiet. Newish had stopped stirring the tea. Chris had stopped typing, without being told. Winter, at the end, was looking at the fire.

Niko thought about it for a long time. He was patient. He’d always been patient. He’d waited three weeks at a forge in Tallbridge for Wenlock to say he was ready, and she’d said it on the last day.

“Then you don’t say it on the arrow,” he said.

Hyvred looked at him.

“Spoken Edge is for things you can say on a cut,” said Niko. “Quick. One word. As it goes.” He looked at the arrow in her hand. “Some things aren’t one word. Some things you don’t say on an edge. You just—“ He thought about it. “You say them. Out loud. To the person. With your face.”

“With my face,” said Hyvred.

“Higu said that to Gigi,” said Niko. “In July. Gigi told Thea, and Thea told Xola, and Xola told everyone. You can say things to her yourself. With your face.” He shrugged. “It worked for Gigi. Eventually.”

Hyvred looked at the arrow for a long time.

Then she unnocked it. She put it back in the quiver, carefully, with the others. She put her bow across her knees.

“Monday,” she said, to nobody.

“Monday,” said Niko.

“First dawn. The south gate. That’s when he’ll go. He always goes at first dawn.”

“I know.”

“I’m forty-six and nine-tenths,” said Hyvred. “I can’t go with him. I can’t be fifty by Monday. Nobody can be fifty by Monday.” Her voice did something. “I was going to walk in with him. At the front of the ten. I’d done the sum. I’d done it to the day.”

“I know,” said Niko. “Xola told everyone.”

“Of course Xola told everyone.”

“She tells everyone everything,” agreed Niko. “It’s her best quality.”

Hyvred laughed. It wasn’t a good laugh. It was the kind that’s mostly something else. Bee, on her rock, put down her pencil.

He enchanted something for her that night.

He hadn’t meant to. He was a miner and an enchanter. He dug things out of rock and he put things into them, a little, the way you’d put salt into soup. He’d been planning to enchant a pick for Hana. But he sat by the fire on the ridge after the others had gone to sleep in the heather, and Hyvred was still awake, a couple of paces off, looking south. And he took the arrow she’d shot into the rock, the first one, through. Newish had dug it out with her knife. He held it over the fire, and took out a little phial of something he’d harvested in the autumn off the end of the north tunnel, where it was cold.

The cold place. Where the old stone met the new. Where Ice sat every morning with his back to the wall, and Hyvred sat beside him. Four paces, then two.

He put a little of it into the arrow. Just a little. A breath.

[ Enchanting · Remembers a place · Grade 72% ]

He held it out to her.

“What’s that?” said Hyvred.

“An arrow,” said Niko. “It remembers the end of the north tunnel. Where you sit.” He shrugged. “That’s all it does. It doesn’t hit harder. It doesn’t go further. If you shoot it, it’ll just go where it goes.” He kept holding it out. “But if you ever want to know which way that is. From somewhere else. A long way off. Hold it up, and it’ll lean.”

She looked at it.

“Which way what is?”

“Home,” said Niko.

She took it. She held it in both hands, the way you’d hold something that was too important to put in a quiver. She looked at it for a long time in the firelight.

“You’re very strange,” she said, at last.

“Gigi says that about everybody.”

“Gigi’s right.” She turned the arrow over. “Thank you.”

“It’s for him,” said Niko. “Really. Not for you.” He put the phial away. “If you want to give it to him. On Monday. Before he goes. So he knows which way to come back.” He lay down in the heather by the fire and put his arm over his eyes. “Or not. It’s yours. You can do what you like with it.”

He didn’t watch what she did. It wasn’t his.

But much later, half asleep, with the fire down to embers and the wind going over the ridge, he heard her say something. Very quietly. Not to him. Not to anybody. To the arrow, maybe, or to the south.

He couldn’t hear what it was. One word, or more than one. He didn’t try to hear it. He went to sleep.

In the morning she was forty-seven, and the arrow wasn’t in her quiver. He didn’t ask where it was.

They did the Fell King on Sunday, because Chris said they could.

He said it at breakfast, with the spreadsheet open, before anybody had finished their tea. He said a lot of things with the spreadsheet open. Most of them were true.

“It’s an elite,” said Chris. “Top of the high fells, in the cairn. Lv 50. Everyone does it in tens. Nobody’s done it in five.” He scrolled. “But it’s got a pattern. It telegraphs. If somebody calls the telegraph a second early, you can do it in five.” He looked up at Hyvred. “You call a second early.”

“I call two seconds early,” said Hyvred.

“Then we can do it in five and have a second to spare,” said Chris, and wrote that down too.

“Something always goes wrong,” said Winter.

“Something always goes wrong,” agreed Chris. “I’ve got a column for it.”

The Fell King lived in a cairn of black stones at the very top of the fells, where the wind never stopped. It was a thing like a ram made of rock, the size of a cottage, with horns that went round twice. When they came up over the last rise it was standing on the cairn looking at the sky. It turned its great stone head and looked at them.

“Charge,” called Hyvred. “It’s going to charge. Straight. Now.”

It hadn’t moved yet. They moved anyway. It came down off the cairn like a landslide and went through the place they’d been.

It took forty minutes.

Niko would remember it as the best fight he’d had all year. Not the hardest. The best. Hyvred called every charge two seconds early, and every sweep of the horns, and every stamp that brought the scree down. Newish drummed. Chris, who’d stopped typing for once, went in on the left with his sword. Winter put her harpoon into the thing’s flank at the twentieth minute and held on, on the rope, while it dragged her twenty paces across the scree, saying fine through her teeth the whole way.

And Niko said words on the cut.

Hold, on the left foreleg, and the leg held, locked, for a breath, and the ram went down on one knee. Break, on a horn, and he meant it so much that the horn cracked across and fell off and rolled away down the fells. Newish chased it for a while out of sheer delight.

“NOW,” called Hyvred, at forty minutes. “Everybody. Now. It’s going to sit down.”

It sat down. They were already there.

They lay on the scree afterward, all five of them, in the wind, and Bee drew them lying there. Chris put a tick in a column that said Nobody’s done it in five, and then crossed out Nobody’s and wrote We.

“Something went wrong,” said Winter, from the end, flat on her back with the harpoon rope still round her wrist.

“What?”

“Nothing,” said Winter. “That’s what went wrong. Nothing went wrong.” She looked at the sky. “I don’t like it.”

Newish laughed so hard she had to sit up.

They came down off the fells at the second dusk and walked back to the round town over the moor. The dome got bigger and bigger ahead of them in the evening, the way it always did.

In the banner room, Ice was in the first of the nine with his feet on the rung, the way he always was. He looked up when they came in. At the five of them, windburned, and Bee behind with her sketchbook.

At Hyvred.

She didn’t look at him. She went straight to her chair beside Bee’s and sat down and looked at the table. Her tail was very still.

But Niko saw him look. He’d been watching for it, because that was what you did when you were patient and you’d done the sum. He saw Ice look at her across the whole banner room, for a long time, the way he looked at a door nobody had opened. And then look away, at the cup in his hands, which he held the same as always.

Except it wasn’t quite the same as always. Niko couldn’t have said what was different. The angle of it, maybe. As if he were holding it a little more carefully than he used to. As if it might spill.

Later, at the third dawn, Ice came down the long table and stopped by Niko’s chair.

“Thank you,” he said.

“For what?”

“The fells. Today.” He didn’t say anything else. He didn’t need to. Niko knew he’d been watching Hyvred’s level on the guild board all day, the way everybody had.

“It wasn’t for you,” said Niko.

“I know,” said Ice. “Thank you anyway.”

He went back to his chair. Niko watched him go. Then he looked at Bee, across the table, who’d been drawing the whole time. She turned her sketchbook round, very slightly, so only Niko could see.

It was the first of the nine. Ice in it, holding a cup. And very small, down the edge of the page, where the drawing ran off, the tip of a tail.

Niko didn’t say anything. It wasn’t his.
