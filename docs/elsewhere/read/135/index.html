Live from Elsewhere · Volume 5: Ask Me Again

Chapter 135

Before You Finish

1,871 words · 8 min read

Kei had rehearsed it.

Not out loud. In his head, for eight days, walking. Down the north road from the round town, along the river road, over the bridge at Brackenford, and on the last morning out along the causeway into the fen. He’d tried it a dozen ways. Short. Long. With the map. Without. Starting with the halls; not starting with the halls, because it wasn’t about the halls. Starting with you said. Not starting with you said, because that made it sound like a debt.

Drew had walked beside him the whole way and listened to him not say any of it.

“You’ve got it,” said Drew, on the causeway, with Fernwick coming up out of the reeds ahead of them on its stilts.

“I haven’t said anything.”

“You’ve been saying it for eight days,” said Drew. “In your head. I can hear it.” He put his hands in his pockets. “Just ask him. The way you’d ask anybody. That’s all it is.”

He was on Sedge’s steps.

Kei had known he would be. Kei had watched enough of him, over six months, to know where he went in the mornings. The steps at the end of the north walk, above the channel, where a boy with a black sword used to sit. He was sitting on the top step with his shield leaning against the doorpost and a dropper in his hand. A row of stoppered draughts stood on the step beside him, and his stream was on. Kei could tell by the way he glanced, every so often, at the edge of his eye.

And they were all there.

That was the thing Kei hadn’t expected. He’d expected Ice. He’d half expected Suzu, because Suzu was usually near. He hadn’t expected all nine. Deston on the ladder down to the channel, with the book closed in his lap. Kotori and Rachael at Sedge’s hearth inside, through the open door, brewing something that smelled of cabbage. Gabe on the Landing, with his hammer. Calvin, who’d seen them coming from half a mile away along the causeway and was already running back to say so. Dennis on a barrel with a notebook. Sheng Yang, standing. Suzu on the step below Ice, with an empty jar beside him.

Kei thought, Xav rang him last night.

Of course Xav had. He’s coming tomorrow. To ask you properly. And all nine of them had come out onto the steps and the Landing and the ladder to be there when he did, without anybody saying so.

He stopped at the bottom of the steps.

Ice looked down at him. Then at Drew, behind him. Then back.

“Hi, Kei,” said Ice.

“Hi.”

Kei didn’t look at a chat he couldn’t see. He looked at Ice.

He’d rehearsed it a dozen ways. He didn’t use any of them.

“In January,” said Kei, “you said make it big. Then ask me again.”

Ice didn’t move.

“It’s big,” said Kei. “Three thousand people on the steps every night. The whole world went and asked their old women what they do at dusk. Riverbank said yes last night, all eight, on the shingle.” He held the map in both hands. He didn’t unroll it. He didn’t need to. “I’m not asking because of the halls. I’d be asking if there weren’t any halls. You said make it big, and it’s big. So I walked here. To ask you to your face. Because it’s yours to say, and you should be asked properly.” He took a breath. “So I’m asking. Will Moonlight Harvest—“

“Yes,” said Ice.

Kei stopped.

He hadn’t finished. He’d got to Moonlight Harvest. He’d been going to say come in, under the banner, all nine of you, and he’d been going to say it slowly, properly, the way he’d walked eight days to say it.

Ice had said yes over the top of it. Quietly. Not loudly. The way you’d say yes to something you’d decided a long time ago and had only been waiting to be asked.

Before you finish.

Kei stood at the bottom of Sedge’s steps and looked up at him.

And Drew, behind him, said, very softly, “There.”

Kei didn’t know what he meant. Then he looked at Ice’s face, properly, and he did.

Ice was surprised.

Not at Kei. Not at the question. He’d known the question for six months. He’d said, twice, exactly what he’d say. He’d said it.

He was surprised at himself. At how fast it had come out. At how much he’d meant it. Kei watched his face do a thing he’d never seen it do, in six months of watching. Not still. Not the small smile. Something underneath both, coming up, as if a man who’d been watching a door for a long time had just found out he’d been standing in it.

“People always are,” said Drew, quietly, to nobody. “When it’s real.”

“It’s not just me,” said Ice, after a moment. His voice wasn’t quite steady. He cleared it. “It’s not mine to say. Not all of it. There’s nine.”

He looked round.

They were already looking at him. All of them. From the ladder and the Landing and the barrel and the hearth and the step below him.

“Yes,” said Deston, from the ladder, before anyone could ask. He was smiling so hard his face looked like it hurt. “Obviously, Dec. Yes.”

“Yes,” said Kotori, from the doorway, with a cabbage-smelling spoon in her hand. She didn’t say sorry. She hadn’t for months. She just said yes, clearly, and went pink.

“YES,” said Calvin, from the Landing, at a volume that sent a bird up out of the reeds.

“Same hammer,” said Gabe.

“Yes,” said Sheng Yang. Once, without fuss, the way he signed things.

“Fine,” said Rachael, behind Kotori. She folded her arms. “Yes.”

Dennis looked up from his barrel. He closed the little notebook.

“The purse comes with us,” said Dennis. “I’m keeping the purse. That’s non-negotiable, darling. The guild can have its own purse. Ours stays ours.”

“That’s fine,” said Kei.

“Then yes,” said Dennis, with enormous dignity, and opened the notebook again and wrote something in it.

And Suzu, on the step below Ice, looked up at Kei for a long moment with his half-closed pale eyes. He had the empty jar beside him on the step, the one Sedge had said was ready.

“Okay,” said Suzu.

From Suzu, Kei had learned, that was the biggest word there was.

Kei unrolled the map.

He did it on the bottom step of Sedge’s stairs, kneeling, with Drew holding one corner and Calvin, who’d come back from the Landing at a run, holding the other. Nine dots in a cluster on the right of the paper, in a fen. Every one of their lines to the middle dotted since the first night. Not yet.

He took out his pen. He went over them, one at a time. Slowly, so that they could all see, the way he’d done for Riverbank on the shingle. Dotted to solid. Ice’s. Deston’s. Suzu’s. Kotori’s. Sheng Yang’s. Gabe’s. Rachael’s, Calvin’s, Dennis’s.

When he’d finished there were only a few dotted lines left on the whole map. Six of them, off at the edge, near a town of wheels. Not yet. He knew why. He’d leave them dotted as long as they needed to be. That was what a gate was for.

He looked up at Ice.

“In March,” said Kei, “on the steps of the Hall. You said not a member. A friend.”

“I did,” said Ice.

“So which is it now?”

Ice looked at him. Then down at the map, at nine solid lines.

“Both,” said Ice.

Sedge had come out onto her steps at some point, with her pipe. She’d been standing in her doorway listening, the way she listened to everything, slowly and all the way through. She looked down at Kei on her bottom step with his map. At Drew. At the nine of them.

“The round town,” said Sedge. “Is it.”

“The round town,” said Ice.

“Hm.” She took her pipe out of her mouth. “The boy’s there.”

“He’s on the gate.”

“Then you’ll see him.” Sedge looked at Ice for a long moment. She’d been there, on these steps, in March, when he’d said it. Go and join them first. I’ll see you there one day. “Tell him the steps are kept,” said Sedge. “Tell him I said good. And tell him one day’s here.”

Ice looked at her.

“I will,” he said.

They’d walk on Monday. All nine of them, and Kei, and Drew. Down the causeway and over the river and east along the plain road, eight days to the round town where seven roads met. Riverbank would meet them on the road and walk the rest with them. Seventeen, and a guild founder, and the man who’d walked to Tiger.

On Sunday night, on the Landing, Kei sat on the edge with his feet over the black water and his map in his lap. Ice came and sat beside him. Not close. Just there.

“You were surprised,” said Kei. “Drew said you would be.”

Ice didn’t say anything for a while.

“A bit,” he said.

“You said you’d say yes before I finished. Twice. You knew.”

“I knew,” said Ice. “I didn’t know it’d come out that fast.” He looked at the water. “I’ve been saying not yet for six months. I thought I’d have to decide. When you asked. I thought there’d be a second where I decided.” He shook his head slightly. “There wasn’t. It was just already yes.”

Kei looked at him.

“That’s what happens,” said Kei, “when something’s been true for a long time and nobody’s asked.”

Ice didn’t answer. He sat on the edge of the Landing with his shield beside him, not on his arm, looking at the dark fen.

After a while he laughed, very quietly, at nothing.
