Live from Elsewhere · Volume 11: Three Hundred and One

Chapter 280

Four Hundred and Thirty-One

3,512 words · 15 min read

Chernn didn’t want a roof.

She’d said so. At the long table on the Sunday, the day after the lanterns came down, when Gabe leaned across with his whole face asking. What are we doing for yours? For the twenty-first? Jared’s got the pole, we could do the roof again, we could—

“Nothing,” Chernn had said.

The whole long table had looked at her.

“Nothing,” she’d said again. “Please. It’s not—“ She’d stopped. She was bad at saying things. She was good at candles. “Hana’s was Hana’s. It was perfect. It was hers.” She’d looked at her hands. “I don’t want a roof. I want it to be small. I want it to be honest.”

Gabe had opened his mouth. Rae had put her hand on his knee, and he’d shut it.

And beside her, on the bench, Deston had said nothing at all. He’d taken her hand under the table, which he still did as though it were the first time.

She dipped every morning anyway. That was what she did.

In the corner of the Hart’s kitchen, before the first dawn, with the frame of forty wicks Joss had made her and the pot on the back of the range beside Kotori’s kettles. Down, and up. Down, and up. Let it cool. Like breathing. She’d dipped two hundred and eleven for the road in October, and the window candle every morning for seventy-one mornings, and twelve two-wicked ones for Hana’s roof. She’d never once dipped one for herself.

She didn’t now. She dipped for the Hart’s windows, which needed them, and the banner room’s lamps. And for Isa’s chair, because Isa liked one on the floor beside her at night, low, where she could see it.

Deston sat on the stool beside her with his book and held the wick-rod when she needed it. And didn’t say anything. And when anybody came into the kitchen, his ears went red.

“You can go to the long table,” said Chernn, on the Monday. “You don’t have to sit here.”

“I know,” said Deston.

“You’ve been sitting here every morning for a week.”

“Since October,” said Deston. “Every morning since October.” He turned a page he wasn’t reading. “I like it here.”

Chernn dipped. Down, and up.

“It’s a stool,” she said. “In a kitchen.”

“It’s next to you,” said Deston.

She nearly dropped the frame.

She noticed on the Tuesday that Kite wasn’t in the gate.

She noticed things. You had to, with candles. You had to notice when the wax was too hot, or the wick was pulling, or the room had a draught in it you couldn’t feel. And on the Tuesday, going across the market for wax at the second dusk, she looked up at the gate of the Hall of Roads. It was the wrong shape.

Kite wasn’t in it. Somebody else was. Tobi, the boy Kite had taught to restring a crossbow one-handed. Standing in the gate with his hands at his sides, very seriously, asking the people who came up the steps who’d stand next to you.

“Where’s Kite?” said Chernn.

“Out,” said Tobi.

“Out where?”

Tobi looked at her with great seriousness.

“Out,” he said again. “With Ash. And Drew. He said if anybody asked, I was to say out.” He thought about it. “Especially you. He said especially you.”

Chernn stood at the bottom of the steps with her basket of wax and looked at him.

“How long have they been out?”

“Since the Monday before last,” said Tobi. “Ten days.” He thought again. “That’s all I’m allowed to say.”

She went back to the kitchen and dipped. She didn’t think about it. She was very careful not to think about it. Ten days was five there and five back. Ten days was exactly the length of the road to a candle village south-west of the Millrace road, past the White Hills, with a pond in the middle of it.

She didn’t think about that at all.

They came in through the south gate on the Thursday at the second dusk. Kite, and Ash, and Drew. Three of them, each with a crate on their back. And behind them on the south road, a cart. A real cart, with a real horse, an old brown one with a white face. And a driver Chernn knew, because she’d seen him every day for a month in a village by a pond. The tallow man.

On the cart, crates. A lot of crates.

Chernn was on the south steps. She hadn’t meant to be. She’d come out of the Hart for air and seen three people coming up the south road with crates on their backs, and stopped. And sat down on the steps without deciding to, because her legs had stopped working.

Kite came up the steps first. He put his crate down on the top step in front of her and stood there with his hands at his sides, in the gate’s place, where he always stood.

“From Tallow Green,” said Kite.

Chernn looked at the crate. She looked at the cart behind him, at the tallow man climbing down. At Ash, putting her crate down beside Kite’s with her calm narrow face. At Drew, putting his down, watching her feet.

“You walked to Tallow Green,” said Chernn.

“Five days,” said Kite. “And back.”

“Why?”

Kite looked at her.

“Somebody asked,” he said. “In the gate. Two weeks ago.” He didn’t say who. “They asked who’d stand next to you on your day. And I said, Tallow Green. Every window.” He was quiet. “So we went and asked them.”

She opened the first crate on the south steps. She couldn’t wait for the kitchen.

Candles. Tall and white and plain, thin sweet Tallow Green wax. Packed in straw, row on row. And every one of them wrapped in a twist of paper, and on every twist, in a different hand, a name. Bramble. Old Tess. The tallow man. Wick’s girl’s mother. Pip, again, lumpy, honest. Mother Dipp’s cousin from over the hill, who couldn’t spell cousin.

The whole green. Every cottage round the pond. More than once.

“How many,” said Chernn. Her voice had gone.

“Count them,” said the tallow man, from the bottom of the steps. “Mother Dipp said you’d want to.”

So she did. On the south steps of the Hall of Roads, at the second dusk, crate after crate. Kite and Ash and Drew carried them up the steps from the cart one by one and set them round her. The round market gathered at the bottom of the steps to watch. She counted them out loud, the way Suzu counted stones. Two hundred and six. Two hundred and seven. The way you would if you wanted them to know you knew they were there.

Four hundred and thirty-one.

She stopped.

She knew that number. Everybody in the round town knew that number. On the longest night, at the dawn after, Dennis had come to the south steps with his ledger. And said, four hundred and thirty-one, darling, I counted twice. Every lit window in the round town on the longest night. Every one of them.

A candle in every window.

And then, at the very bottom of the last crate, under the straw, on its own, one more.

Different from the others. Shorter. Thicker. With two wicks. Wrapped not in a twist of paper but in a square of cloth, tied with string. And tucked under the string, a letter.

Chernn read it on the south steps with four hundred and thirty-one candles round her in their crates, and the one more in her lap, and cried on it. She couldn’t help it. Mother Dipp had told her not to and she did it anyway. The letter didn’t spit. It just went soft at the corners.

Somebody sat down on the step beside her.

Deston. He’d come from somewhere. The Hart, the long table. He’d heard. Everybody in the round market had heard her counting. He sat down beside her on the south steps, enormous, too big for the step, with his book under his arm. He didn’t say anything. He looked at the crates, and the candles, and the letter, and the one more in her lap.

His ears had gone red. Of course they had.

“You knew,” said Chernn.

“No,” said Deston. “Kite didn’t tell me. He said especially him.” He looked at the candles. “Dennis told me this morning. He couldn’t help it. He’s been bursting for two weeks.”

“I said nothing,” said Chernn. “I said small. Honest.”

Deston looked at the crates. At the four hundred and thirty-one names in four hundred and thirty-one hands. At the tallow man at the bottom of the steps, holding his horse, waiting to be told he could go home.

“It is honest,” he said. “It’s every window. Every one somebody lit on the longest night.” He picked up one of the candles, the one marked Pip. “And every one of them dipped by somebody who doesn’t know who it’s for. Just that it’s somebody. On a day.” He turned it in his big hands. “That’s the most honest thing I’ve ever seen.”

Chernn looked at him for a long time.

“It’s not a roof,” said Deston. “It’s just windows.”

“It’s every window,” said Chernn.

“Yes.”

“In the whole round town.”

“Yes,” said Deston. “And the green too. On the day.” He was quiet. “That’s two towns. With their windows lit. For one candle-maker who said nothing, please.”

Chernn put her face in his shoulder.

She found Dennis afterwards, in the banner room, sitting at the long table with his ledger open, pretending to read it.

“You wrote to Mother Dipp,” said Chernn.

Dennis looked up. He had the face of a man who’d been bursting for two weeks and had just been let off.

“Twice, darling,” said Dennis.

“Why twice?”

“The first time I got it wrong.” He closed the ledger. “I said four hundred and thirty-two. I’d counted the Hart’s back window twice. It’s got two panes and I thought it was two windows.” He looked pained. “I had to write again and say sorry, darling, four hundred and thirty-one, the Hart’s back window is one window with ideas. She wrote back and said she’d already dipped four hundred and thirty-two.” He spread his hands. “So she made the extra one into the one more for the road. With two wicks. She said it was a sign.”

Chernn stared at him.

“It was a mistake,” she said.

“Darling,” said Dennis, “in Tallow Green, those are the same thing.”

She hugged him. She didn’t hug people, mostly. She hugged Dennis, at the long table, very hard. He patted her on the back and said there, there, darling, don’t cry on the ledger, it’s the only one I’ve got.

The tallow man stayed the night.

He wouldn’t come into the Hart. He said he’d sleep in the cart, with the horse, the way he did on the road. But Chernn brought him out a bowl of Kotori’s soup and a heel of bread and sat on the cart’s tail beside him while he ate it. The old brown horse with the white face stood with its nose in a bag of oats and looked at the round town as though it had never seen anything so silly.

“The whole green’s been dipping for a fortnight,” said the tallow man, between spoonfuls. “Since your Kite came. Every cottage. Mother Dipp hasn’t slept. She says she’ll sleep after.” He looked at the soup. “She’ll do every window on the green, on the day. She said to tell you. Every one round the pond. Same as yours.”

“I know. She wrote.”

“And the goose,” said the tallow man.

Chernn looked at him.

“It’s been sitting at the edge of the pond,” he said. “All week. Facing this way.” He nodded north-east, at nothing, at the round town. “It doesn’t do that. It’s a goose. It faces whichever way it likes. But it’s been facing this way since your Kite left with the crates.” He finished the soup. “Mother Dipp says it’s considering.”

“Considering what?”

“Nobody knows what a goose considers,” said the tallow man. “That’s the point of a goose.”

She asked Isa on the Friday.

At the second dusk, at the top of the two hundred and twelve, on the Somewhere bench, with Kei’s lamp. Suzu had carried Isa up, and gone down again, and Chernn had come up after with the one more in her hands. Still in its square of cloth.

She sat down on the bench beside Isa. There was room. It was a bench for two.

“Mother Dipp sent this,” said Chernn. “With the others. The one more for the road.”

Isa turned her face toward her.

“I know the line,” said Isa. “My mother’s mother knew it. Before Tallow Green had a name, she said, there was a woman in a hut by a pond. She dipped one for every window, and one more. For whoever was out.” She folded her old hands in the blanket. “We never had windows on the hill. Only the lamp. But we knew the line.”

“I don’t know where to put it,” said Chernn.

Isa didn’t say anything.

“The windows are for the round town,” said Chernn. “Every one. Kite’s going to give them out tomorrow night. One to every house.” She looked at the one more in her lap. “But this one’s for the road. And on my day, nobody’s on the road. Nobody’s coming home. Nobody’s out.” She swallowed. “So I don’t know where it goes. Or who should put it there.”

Isa was quiet for a long time.

“You’ll be out,” she said at last.

Chernn looked at her.

“Tomorrow,” said Isa. “Out there. All day. You and the tall one.” She was looking north, at the gold point at the bottom of the sky. “And then you’ll come home. In here. At night. Through whatever door it is you come through.” She turned her face to Chernn’s. “Somebody should put one out. For the two of you. On the road home.”

“Who?”

Isa held out her hands.

Chernn looked at them. Small and old and thin, out of the grey blanket, held out flat. The hands that had carried a lamp south behind the dark on a longest night, relighting a road. The hands that had set a crooked stone in the still water, the spring before she came home.

She put the one more for the road in them.

“Hm,” said Isa Corrie. She closed her hands round it. “I’ll need carrying.”

“Suzu will carry you.”

“Suzu will carry me,” agreed Isa. “To wherever it goes.” She held the candle in her lap, in its square of cloth, two wicks. “I’ll know where. When I’m there.”

On the Friday night, at the end of the night, Chernn went to the Hart’s kitchen to dip.

She didn’t need to. There were four hundred and thirty-one candles in crates in the banner room, and Kite had a list. But it was what she did, before the first dawn. So she did it. The frame of forty, the pot on the range. Down, and up.

Deston came in and sat on the stool.

He didn’t say anything. He held the wick-rod when she needed it. His ears weren’t red. She noticed that. It was the first morning since October they hadn’t gone red when somebody came in, and nobody had come in. It was just the two of them in the Hart’s kitchen with the range and the pot. And somewhere on the other side of the wall, in the banner room, four hundred and thirty-one candles in crates. Each one wrapped in somebody’s name.

“Are you frightened?” said Chernn.

“Yes,” said Deston.

“Honest?”

“Honest,” said Deston. “I’ve been frightened since the green. Since I was on my knees by the pond with forty-three candles behind me and two flames in front.” He looked at her. “Not of you. Of being bad at it.”

“At what?”

“At being next to you,” said Deston. “For the rest of it. All of it. I’m not good at things. I’m slow. I read the line in the book and I wait for it to work.” He turned the wick-rod in his hands. “I’ve never been next to anybody for the rest of it.”

Chernn put the frame down.

She thought about the small cactus. Out there, in a unit opposite in a corridor in Malacca, on a windowsill, with a bent spine and a chip in its pot. She’d chosen it over the big perfect one in June because it was honest. Because it had waited. It leaned toward the door now. She says the small one’s hers, he’d written to Ice.

“You’re steady,” said Chernn. “Mother Dipp says. Like a wick.” She looked at him. “You don’t pull.”

“I’m slow.”

“Slow is steady,” said Chernn. “Slow is how a candle burns all the way down.”

Deston looked at her for a long time across the Hart’s kitchen. Then his ears went red. Very slowly. Starting at the top. Nobody had come in.

“There,” said Chernn. “Now you’re frightened properly.”

He laughed. Out loud, in the kitchen, which he almost never did. And she dipped one more. For nobody, for the Hart’s windows. Down, and up.

At a quarter to eight they went to the long table together. Kei stood up. Sixty-two people and a sheep said so the rest may sleep. And on the stair to the eye, Isa, being carried down by Suzu, said it too, with the one more for the road in her lap.

The egg took Chernn at eight with Deston’s hand in hers under the table.

Out there, it was going to be Saturday.
