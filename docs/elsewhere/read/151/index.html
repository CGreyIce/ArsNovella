Live from Elsewhere · Volume 6: There's a Chair

Chapter 151

The Six Chairs

4,321 words · 19 min read

Thea woke on Wednesday in a room over the Hart with the sun coming through the shutters in stripes. For a long moment she didn’t know where she was.

That happened sometimes in the game. You woke up and the room was the wrong shape, and your body was the wrong size. Then you remembered: the wrong size was the right one now, because it had a tower shield’s worth of shoulders. She lay there in the stripes of light and listened. Somebody was laughing in the street below. Somebody else was rolling a barrel. Out past them all, very faint, there was a fountain.

The round town.

She’d been here two nights and she still couldn’t quite believe it. She’d looked at it on the map on the first morning in the little wooden room, a dot as far east as she could get from where she was, and felt relieved. Now she was lying in a bed in it.

And last night she’d gone up two hundred and twelve steps with nothing in her hands and sat on a ledge under a ceiling made of silver roads. She’d drunk a cup of terrible cold tea.

She lay there and thought about it. About I know what I was. About what were you. She hadn’t planned to ask that. It had come out of her the way things came out of Xola, sideways and straight at the same time, before she could stop it. And Gigi had answered. She’d sat in the moonlight and said it all, plainly, with her hands still in her lap. I made sure there wasn’t one. I made sure, Thea.

Thea had waited for a year to hear that. She hadn’t known she was waiting for it until it came. And when it came, she’d found she didn’t have anything to say back, and had said so. And Gigi had said that’s fair.

Not you should. Not after everything. Not I came all the way up here. Just that’s fair.

Thea lay in the stripes of light and found she was smiling at the ceiling, and didn’t know why, and let herself.

Kei was waiting for them at the counter.

Not waiting, exactly. He was at the counter the way he usually was, with his map unrolled on it and a cup of something at his elbow, talking to Ama Corrie about a leak. But when the six of them came in through the south doors he stopped talking about the leak. He rolled up the map, slowly, the way you put something away when you need both your hands for what comes next.

Thea had heard about Kei asking. Everybody had. It had gone round the whole game. He’d walked eight days to a fen to ask one man a question. And the man had said yes before he’d finished asking, and there was a clip of it with millions of views. Kei standing on Sedge’s steps going will Moonlight Harvest—, and Ice cutting across him, yes. And Kei’s face. Thea had watched it in the Sallows on her phone in the morning, out of the egg, and cried a bit, for no reason she could have explained.

She knew Kei would ask. He’d been building up to it for two days; she’d seen him. He was the kind of person who rehearsed. He’d been walking past them all Tuesday with his mouth moving.

She didn’t let him.

“Kei,” she said, before he’d even got the map tied. “Can we join?”

Kei stopped with the string half round the map.

The Hall had gone quiet. It did that, Thea was learning. There were always people in the Hall of Roads. Constellation people at the counter, travellers coming through, Ama Corrie, the pigeons. And whenever something happened, they all went quiet at once, so they could hear it.

“Yes,” said Kei.

“You didn’t let me finish,” said Thea.

“No,” said Kei. And then something happened to his face. It went red and pleased and embarrassed all at once, and he looked down at the map with the string half tied. “Before you finish,” he said. “That’s how it goes here. I’m told.”

Somebody on the stair laughed. It sounded like Hyvred.

“I was going to say all six of us,” said Thea. “And the sheep. If she’s allowed.”

“Yes,” said Kei again. “Before you finish. All of it. Yes.” He looked up at her. He had a slightly dazed expression, like a man who’d been running at a door and found it already open. “I had a whole thing. I practised it. On the roof. Gigi helped.”

Thea went very still.

“She said to keep it short,” said Kei. “She said, Thea doesn’t like being asked big. Ask small.” He looked at the map. “I was going to ask small. And then you asked first.”

Thea didn’t know what to say to that. She found her eyes had gone hot, and blinked.

“Sorry,” she said. “For taking your line.”

“Don’t be,” said Kei. “Ice took mine too. It’s sort of a tradition now.” He finished tying the map. “It’s a better tradition than mine was.”

Ama Corrie had the Roll open before Kei had finished saying tradition.

It was a big black book on the counter, older than anything Thea had seen in the game. It wasn’t the old guild’s Roll; that one was on its own stand at the end of the counter, swollen with water and three hundred years. This was Constellation’s. It was in a new binding Ama Corrie had made herself in the winter, and the first page had KeiKatamori at the top, small and upright.

Ama Corrie turned it round to face them. She held out the pen.

Nobody moved.

Thea looked at the page. Forty-nine names, in forty-nine different hands. She could see some of them upside down from where she stood. dumbo, very neat. Hyvred, careful, the letters a little too far apart. Bee, with a tiny drawing of a bee next to it that somebody had tried to rub out and failed. And on the last page, the seventeen. Sketch, quick. Historia, across two lines. And at the top of the nine, small and plain, three letters: Ice.

And further up the page, in the middle of the first twenty: gigichiii_. Every letter with a little hook at the end of it, like a thorn.

She looked at it for a while.

“Who’s first?” said Ama Corrie.

Nobody answered. They all looked at Thea. She’d been at the front for ten weeks and eight days of road; it seemed she was expected to be at the front of this too.

But she didn’t want to be first. She found she didn’t, standing there. She wanted to be last. She wanted to see the others go in, one at a time, onto the page. She wanted to know they were all there before she put herself there.

“Chi,” said Thea. “You go first.”

PenguChi looked at her.

“I’m at the back,” said PenguChi.

“I know,” said Thea. “Go first anyway. Once. For me.”

PenguChi looked at her for a long moment, the way she looked at everything, taking it in. Then she leaned her long spear against the counter and took the pen from Ama Corrie.

She wrote slowly. Of course she did. PenguChi, letter by letter, the P large and round and the rest getting smaller, like somebody walking away down a road. She put the pen down and looked at it.

“Okay,” said PenguChi.

“Hm,” said Ama Corrie, and looked at her over her spectacles. “You’re the one at the back.”

“Yes.”

“Somebody always is,” said Ama Corrie. “The old Roll had one. Every party. Their names were always small.” She tapped the page next to PenguChi’s name, where it had got smaller at the end. “Like that. I used to think they were shy. I’ve decided since that they were watching.”

PenguChi went pink to the ears, and stepped back.

Tif next. She took the pen with her ears straight up and her hand shaking so badly that the first letter came out as a sort of zigzag. She looked at it in horror.

“It’s fine,” said Ama Corrie.

“It’s wobbly.”

“Half the names in that book are wobbly,” said Ama Corrie. “The boy who signed twentieth did it with a monster’s beak stuck in my counter. Look.” She turned back three pages and pointed. dumbo, very neat. “That’s neat because he was too frightened to be anything else. Wobbly’s better. Wobbly means you meant it.”

Tif laughed, wetly, and wrote Tif2 after the zigzag. Then, because she was Tif, she drew a very small cat face beside it.

“Hm,” said Ama Corrie. She didn’t say anything about the cat. She’d let the bee stay.

Xyro took the pen and signed xyrolizea at great speed, with a big loop on the z. Then he dropped the pen on the floor and had to crawl under the counter for it, saying sorry, sorry, it’s fine, I’ve got it. Ama Corrie looked down at him with the face of a woman who’d been waiting forty years for somebody to do exactly that.

Slep.

He took the pen and looked at the page for a while. Then he wrote Slep, four plain letters, in the space under Xyro’s. He didn’t go to the next line. He wrote it next to the space where the next name would go, so there was room above it for somebody else.

Ama Corrie looked at that too.

“You’ve left a gap,” she said.

“For Xola,” said Slep.

Xola made a small noise behind Thea.

So Xola signed in the gap. She signed above Slep’s name, so his was under hers. xolalotl, all lower case, with a little wave drawn under it that might have been a fish or might have been the sea. Her hand was steadier than Thea had expected. She put the pen down very carefully and stood there looking at the two names together.

“Hm,” said Ama Corrie, very softly. Then, in a different voice: “The sheep.”

Everybody looked at Patience. She was standing on the waxed floor beside Slep’s leg with all four feet planted very wide, the way she’d learned to stand on wax.

“She can’t write,” said Xyro.

“She might,” said Slep.

Ama Corrie looked at the sheep for a long time over her spectacles. Then she turned to the back of the Roll, to the pages where she wrote the things that weren’t names. The weather, and who’d gone down the hatch. She wrote something there in her small hand, and blotted it, and turned the book round so they could read it.

Wed. Six came in from the west, by the south road, as is proper. With them a ewe, Patience, who stood next to the tall one. Not a member. A friend.

Thea read it twice. On the second time she had to put her hand on the counter.

Then it was her.

Ama Corrie turned the Roll back to the right page and held out the pen. Thea took it.

It was heavier than it looked. Or her hand was weaker than it felt. She looked at the page. Five names, new, still wet. PenguChi, getting smaller. Tif2 and a zigzag and a cat. xyrolizea with its loop. xolalotl with its wave. Slep under it, plain.

And above all of them, a long way up, in the first twenty: gigichiii_.

She’d thought about this, on the road. She’d thought about it more than she’d thought about what she’d say at the gate. Putting her name in the same book. On the same pages, in the same ink, forever, in a book an old woman would keep for the rest of her life and then hand to somebody else to keep. You couldn’t take it back, once it was in the Roll. That was what a Roll was for.

She’d been afraid of that, on the road. That it would be like signing something over.

Standing at the counter with the pen, it didn’t feel like that at all. It felt like what Ama Corrie had written in the back pages. Not a member. A friend. Six people and a sheep had come in by the south road, as was proper. And there was room.

She signed.

AltheaVT.

Her hand didn’t shake. She was surprised by that. She’d thought it would.

Ama Corrie looked at it for a long time.

“Hm,” she said.

Thea saw the window come up. She saw five other people see it, at the same moment, and go still. And all round the Hall, Constellation people she barely knew yet went still too. The two at the counter, a man on the stair, three in the banner room doorway. Up on the second landing, a girl with gold horns went very still, and so did a girl with wings beside her.

And then the noise came.

It came from everywhere. From the stair and the banner room and the steps outside and the market, where people had been waiting. It came from the three muddy kids from the moor, who were still in town and had heard. It came from the eye of the dome. Thea looked up and saw two faces looking over the edge of it, two hundred feet up, very small. One of them was shouting. The other one wasn’t. The other one just had her hand over her mouth.

Thea looked up at them for as long as she could.

Then Xola hit her from the side, and Tif hit her from the other, and she couldn’t see anything for a while.

Bee painted them in that afternoon.

Thea had seen the floor on Tuesday. Bee had shown her: the whole guild painted on the old stone under the eye of the dome, gold dots and silver-white lines, every member, every connection. Bee had painted it in the spring and been adding to it ever since. Moonlight Harvest and Riverbank had gone solid three weeks ago, Bee said. She’d knelt on the floor for an hour with an eyelash brush, and nobody had left.

And at the edge, near a little painted cluster of wheels that was meant to be Millrace, there were six dots with dotted lines. Bee had painted those in April, the week they woke up. Not yet, she’d said to Kei.

Now Bee came out of the banner room with her paints and her brushes and a cushion for her knees, and knelt down at the edge of the floor under the eye, and started.

Thea didn’t mean to stay and watch. But she did. And so did the others. And so, slowly, did everybody else.

Bee worked from the edge inward, one line at a time. She went over each dotted silver line with a solid one, very carefully, so that the gaps between the dots filled in and the line ran whole from the six dots at the edge to the middle. To Kei’s dot. To Higu’s, and Drew’s. To Ice’s, in its cluster of nine. To all of them.

She’d done five when she stopped, and sat back on her heels, and looked up at Thea.

“Do you want to do the last one?” said Bee.

Thea didn’t understand.

“Yours,” said Bee. She held out the eyelash brush. “It’s your line. You can do the last bit.”

“I don’t paint.”

“Nobody paints,” said Bee. “I didn’t paint. Then I did.” She waited. “It’s just a line. You start at the dot and you go to the middle. That’s all a line is.”

Thea knelt down on the stone beside her. Bee put the brush in her hand and showed her how to hold it, and where to start. Her dot at the edge, gold and small. The dotted line ran from it toward the middle in a long careful curve, past the clusters and the knots, all the way to the big dot under the very centre of the eye. Kei’s.

She did it very slowly. The brush shook at first. Then it stopped shaking. She went over the dots one at a time and filled in the gaps. She didn’t look up. She didn’t look at anything except the silver line going whole under the brush.

About two-thirds of the way along, her line crossed another one.

It was a gold-and-silver line, old, painted in the spring, solid since the founding. It ran from a dot near the north door to a dot under the eye. And Thea knew whose it was before she looked, because she knew whose dot was at the north end of it. Bee had put a tiny thorn beside that dot. She’d put tiny things beside some of them. A star for Kei. A lantern for Higu. A bee for herself, inevitably. And a thorn.

Thea’s line crossed it. Bee had painted it to cross it in April. She’d known, even then, that it would. You couldn’t paint a line from Millrace to the middle of the round town without crossing that one.

Thea’s brush stopped on the crossing for a moment.

Then it went on.

She finished it at Kei’s dot. She sat back on her heels, and looked at it. Six solid lines, from six dots at the edge, all the way in.

“There,” said Bee.

They sat in the chairs that evening for the first time.

Ama Corrie made a point of it. She came into the banner room at the second dusk with her new broom. That was the fen reed from Sedge, bound onto a handle of something dark and old. She swept the floor under the six chairs, very slowly and thoroughly, though it was only Wednesday. Then she straightened up and looked at the six of them standing in the doorway.

“Well,” she said. “Sit, then.”

So they sat.

They were good chairs. Wooden, and a bit wobbly, as Ice had said. Ice had said that to Xola at half three in the morning, Xola had told her on the road, and Xola had cried about it. They stood near the long table, not at it, at the far end past Moonlight Harvest’s nine, by the window. The window looked west, back down the road they’d come in on, toward the last hill.

Thea sat in the one nearest the window. Xola sat in the one next to her, so close their chairs were touching, the way she’d said she would. Slep on Xola’s other side. Then PenguChi. Then Tif and Xyro, who’d pushed their chairs together too. And Patience lay down across Slep’s feet with a sigh and went to sleep.

The man in the chair next to PenguChi’s was the last of Moonlight Harvest’s nine. He had a little notebook open on the table and a pencil behind his ear, and he looked up at them and beamed.

“Darling,” he said to PenguChi. “Welcome. I’m Dennis. I keep the purse.” He leaned past her to look at all six of them. “The purse stands next to everyone. You’re all next to the purse now. That’s a very good place to be. Ask anyone.”

“Don’t ask anyone,” said a woman further down. Rachael, Thea thought. The one who brewed angry. “He’ll make you write it down.”

“Everything’s owed, darling,” said Dennis serenely. “That’s what a book is for.”

PenguChi looked at him for a long time. Then she said “Okay,” which from PenguChi was practically a hug. Dennis seemed to understand that, because he beamed harder.

And at the far end of the nine, in the first chair, nearest the door, Ice was sitting with his feet up on the rung of the chair beside him and a cup in his hand. He’d come up from the hatch an hour ago, mud to the knees again. He didn’t come over. He raised his cup to them, once, down the whole length of the table.

Thea raised hers back.

That was all. She thought it was the right amount.

Kei came in later, with his map.

He unrolled it on the long table between the cups. Thea had heard about the map too. It had started on the back of a café menu in December, with just Kei on it, a small dot in the middle that he’d drawn small on purpose. It had grown ever since. Now it was the whole guild, every dot and every line, inked in his careful hand, and a gold dot at the very top, off every road.

And at the edge, near a town of wheels, six dots with dotted lines.

Kei took out his pen. Then he went over each of the six dotted lines and inked it solid, one at a time, slowly, the way Bee had painted them on the floor. Nobody said anything while he did it. Everybody in the banner room watched.

When he’d done the last one, he looked at it for a long time.

“I said the dotted lines were the best part,” he said. “In July. On the roof. I said the ones that weren’t anything yet were the best part, because they might be.” He looked at the six solid lines. “I think I was wrong. I think this is.”

“Fifty-five,” said Ama Corrie from the doorway. She had her broom. “All of them sat in.”

“Will you still sweep under them?” said Thea. “On Sundays?”

Ama Corrie looked at her as if she’d asked whether the sun would still come up.

“Especially now,” she said. “A chair nobody sits in, you sweep so it’s ready. A chair somebody sits in, you sweep because they’ve brought the road in on their boots.” She looked down at the floor under Thea’s chair, where there was a little drift of dust and moorland grit that hadn’t been there an hour ago. “There’s always more under a chair that’s sat in. That’s how you know it’s being used.”

The list went up on the board by the hatch at the third dawn.

Drew made the lists. He’d been making them since the Seven Below was kept and its seven tunnels opened. The roads under the roads, Kei called them, one under each of the seven roads into town. Each was thirty or so, full of things with claws and roots and no eyes. Every few days, a ten went down one of them. Never the same ten twice, if Drew could help it. Mixed. Old members with new ones. Riverbank with Moonlight Harvest. People who didn’t know each other walking next to each other until they did. That was what Drew said it was for.

Thea went to look at it on Thursday morning, because Kei said the six were on it now.

She stood in front of it for a long time.

She didn’t hear Drew come up behind her. She never did. He moved like somebody who’d spent a long time learning where to put his feet.

“I put you in it,” said Drew. Not her. You. He was standing beside her, looking at the list too. “Both of you. On purpose.” He was quiet for a moment. “You can say no. You can swap with anyone. Kei’ll do it, no questions. Nobody would think anything.”

Thea looked at the last name on the list. gigichiii_. With the little hook.

“She knows?” she said.

“She’s seen it,” said Drew. “She came and stood where you’re standing, about an hour ago. For about as long.”

“What did she say?”

“Nothing,” said Drew. “She looked at it. And then she went down to the cellar and made a potion that came out the right colour. First time all week.” He looked at Thea’s feet, briefly, the way he did. Then back at her face. “It’s not a test. It’s a ten. It’s a road under a road with things in it. It’s the same thing everybody does every week. You’d just be doing it in the same ten.” He paused. “Decide. That’s all. Every time.”

Thea stood in front of the list for a long time.

Then she took the pencil off its string beside the board. Next to her own name, small, she drew a little tick.

She didn’t look to see if there was one next to Gigi’s. She didn’t need to. She’d already seen it, out of the corner of her eye, the second she walked up. A tiny tick, with a little hook at the end of it.
