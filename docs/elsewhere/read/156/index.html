Live from Elsewhere · Volume 6: There's a Chair

Chapter 156

Rules

3,505 words · 15 min read

Hyvred woke up on Thursday and remembered, and put the pillow over her face.

She was in the room over the Hart that she shared with Bee. The shutters were open and the morning was coming in, gold, and the fountain was going outside. Down in the street somebody was laughing. It was very probably Xola. It was very probably about her.

It wasn’t. She knew it wasn’t. Xola had said she wouldn’t tell anybody nothing, and Hyvred had decided, somewhere in the night, that she believed her. Xola was loud, and bratty, and had chased her down a street shouting. But she’d also sat on a stair and said I say it to people I like. And she’d answered the question Hyvred had been too frightened to finish.

No. Not like that. Not for years.

Hyvred lay under the pillow and felt it again, the thing she’d felt on the stair when Xola said it. She didn’t like it. It was relief, and it was huge, and it was the most humiliating thing she’d ever felt. Somebody had told her a man she owed money to didn’t still like two other people, and the whole of her had gone soft with it like a candle. As if that meant anything. As if it were any of her business.

It wasn’t any of her business.

It was completely her business.

“Mrp,” she said, into the pillow.

“Morning,” said Bee, from the other bed. She was already sitting up, drawing. Bee was always already drawing. “You said that in your sleep. About forty times.”

“I did not.”

“You did. I counted. I’m going to draw it.”

“Bee.”

“I’m drawing a sound,” said Bee peacefully. “It’s very hard. I’m doing it as a little cloud.”

She made rules. She made them over breakfast, in the cookshop on Spoke Street, which she went to on purpose. Not going would have been worse; not going would have been hiding. She sat at the outside table where she’d sat yesterday, with her back very straight. The woman who ran it brought her a fish pie without being asked and gave her a look Hyvred decided not to understand.

She wrote the rules on the back of the note. Under ICE — 3,700.50 + one lesson.

1. I will not say anything to him. Ever.

2. I will hit him when he comes back. Same as always.

3. I will send piece four. Same as always.

4. Nothing is different.

She looked at rule four for a long time. Then she underlined it twice.

Bee had come with her. Bee sat across the table and read the rules upside down, which Bee could do. Bee could read anything upside down; she said it came from drawing on the far side of people’s tables. When she’d read them she looked up at Hyvred with her pale, patient face. Her wings were folded small behind her for once, in the narrow street.

“Okay,” said Bee.

“That’s all?”

“They’re good rules,” said Bee. “Number four’s very good. You underlined it.”

“Twice.”

“Twice,” agreed Bee. She went back to drawing. After a minute, without looking up, she said, “Can I say one thing?”

“No.”

“Okay,” said Bee, and didn’t.

Hyvred sat there with her pie for a full minute. Then: “What?”

Bee smiled at her drawing.

“It’s not a bad thing,” she said. “That’s all. What you’ve got. It’s not a bad thing to have. You keep acting like it’s a thing that happened to you. Like a cold.” She shaded something. “It’s not a cold, Hyv. It’s yours. You’re allowed to have it. Even if you never do anything with it. Even if it’s just yours.”

Hyvred didn’t say anything.

She ate the pie. It was very good.

Deston came back on Friday.

Hyvred had known he would. Moonlight Harvest had gone west with the swamp guy on Monday, all nine of them, with Drew and PenguChi. But he’d said MH for the first few days, then mix it. So on Friday at the noon dusk the gate called out, and Hyvred, on the second landing, saw six of them come down the west road in a line. Gabe in front with his hammer. Rachael two paces behind him. Calvin running ahead and coming back. Dennis with his notebook out, walking and writing, which shouldn’t have been possible. Suzu, quiet. And Deston.

Not the swamp guy. Not Sheng Yang, not Kotori. Hyvred knew that before they were close enough to see faces. She’d counted. Six. The other three had stayed out in the Fell Country, with Drew and PenguChi.

Suzu came up the stair an hour later on his way to the roof, and stopped on the second landing, and looked at her.

“Hyv,” said Suzu.

“What.”

Suzu considered her for a moment, in the way he considered things, as if they were a long way off and he had all day.

“He’s eating,” said Suzu. And went on up the stairs.

She hadn’t asked. She hadn’t asked anything. She sat on the landing with her mouth open and her tail doing something, and decided Suzu was the worst of all of them. Hyvred knew that from Bee, who knew it from Kei, who knew it from Ama Corrie’s book. She’d been on the stair all morning trying very hard not to know it.

She’d made a rule about Deston too. It wasn’t written down. It was don’t.

He found her anyway.

He came up the stair at the second dusk. She was on the second landing with her bow across her knees, watching the west road, which was empty, which was fine. He sat down two steps below her, which was a careful distance. Deston was always careful about distances.

Hyvred didn’t look at him.

“Hi, Hyv,” said Deston.

“No,” said Hyvred.

He waited. He was good at waiting too. All of them were. It was like a disease.

“You know,” said Hyvred, to the west road.

“I don’t know anything,” said Deston. “I count things.”

“You felt it. In the Seven Below. At the end. When the link thing let go. You felt it. I saw your face.”

Deston was quiet for a while.

“For half a second,” he said.

Hyvred’s hands tightened on her bow.

“And?”

“And I’m never going to say it,” said Deston. “Not to anyone. Not to you, even, after today. And never to him.” He was looking at the west road too. “It isn’t mine. It came through the link by accident. It’s like reading somebody’s letter because the wind blew it open. You don’t read it. You fold it up and you give it back and you don’t say what was on it.”

Hyvred looked at him for the first time.

He had a calm, ordinary face. Counting eyes, Bee called them. He wasn’t smiling. He wasn’t laughing at her, the way Bee did, or delighted, the way Xola had been. He just looked at her like somebody giving back a letter.

“Why?” said Hyvred. “Why not say it?”

“Because it’s yours,” said Deston. “And because Dec would want it to be.”

They sat on the stair. The light went orange in the eye of the dome, far above, and then red.

“Can I ask you something?” said Hyvred. She hadn’t planned to. “Not about that. About him.”

“Okay.”

“You’ve known him forever.”

“Since we were thirteen,” said Deston. “Him and me and Gabe. We sang in the back row of a choir.”

Hyvred blinked.

“He sang?”

“Badly,” said Deston. “In the back. We all did. That’s what the back row’s for.” He almost smiled. “There’s a thing we used to say. Nobody sings the whole song. You hold your part. You hold it so well you can hear everybody else’s round it. We said it in the Seven Below. You heard us.”

She had heard them. Three voices, on the stone floor, after. She hadn’t known what it was.

“Why doesn’t he flinch?” said Hyvred. “That’s what I asked Thea. And Xola. Why doesn’t he flinch? Why fifty? Nobody keeps it at fifty.”

Deston thought about it. He thought about it for so long that she thought he wasn’t going to answer.

“He told a woman in February,” said Deston. “At a stone cross on the moor. Nightjar. She’d cut him twice and he didn’t move, and she asked him why. He said, because if the front flinches, everybody flinches.” He shifted on the step. “That’s the flinching. He decided a long time ago that he was the front. And if the front flinches, the people behind it see. And then they flinch too. So he doesn’t. Not ever. Not where anyone can see.”

Hyvred sat very still.

“And fifty,” said Deston, “is so he knows exactly where it landed. Every time. To the inch. Left side, low, across the back. So he can tell you. So you don’t have to find out.” He looked at the west road. “He feels it so nobody behind him has to. That’s fifty. That’s all it’s ever been.”

Hyvred didn’t say anything for a long time.

“That’s stupid,” she said, eventually. Her voice had gone very thick.

“Yes,” said Deston.

“That’s the stupidest thing I’ve ever heard.”

“Yes,” said Deston. “I’ve been telling him that since we were thirteen.”

She laughed. It came out of her wet and furious, and she put the heel of her hand against her eyes, under the glasses.

“Is that why he goes to the far end of rooms?” she said. “So he’s—what. Facing out?”

“Sedge’s mother’s line,” said Deston. “Set one to watch, so the rest may sleep.” He was quiet. “I told him in May that it was him. The one that faces out. He said maybe.” He looked at her. “And then in July he sat at the end of a long table and said, not always me. Not any more. There’s enough of us now to take turns.”

“I heard him,” said Hyvred. “I was looking at the ceiling.”

“I know,” said Deston. “Everyone saw you look at the ceiling.”

“Deston.”

“Sorry,” said Deston, not sounding sorry at all. He stood up. Then he stood there for a moment on the step below her, careful, the way he was careful about everything.

“He’ll be back Thursday, probably,” he said. “He said ten days. He always says ten when he means nine.”

“I don’t care when he’s back.”

“Okay,” said Deston. Exactly the way the swamp guy said it.

And he went down the stairs. Hyvred sat on the landing with her bow and her wet face and the west road. She hated that he’d said okay like that. She hated that she’d heard it.

She sent piece four on Monday.

She sent it from home, out of the egg, at nine o’clock, from her desk. She could see all four corners of the room now, and the window, and the sky round the window, and the wall behind her, which had always been there. She had his stream open on her phone at arm’s length. She could see the whole of it. The Fell Country, and his hands, and his shield. Out of the edge of the picture, a long spear. And a voice saying, very calmly, you’re doing it again.

He was in a ravine, waist-deep in a fast stream, holding the far bank while things with too many legs came down the rocks at him. He wasn’t flinching. Obviously.

He stood up out of the stream with water running off his shield and looked at the chat in the corner of his eye. She knew he was looking because the picture stopped moving.

He didn’t say anything for a second.

“Hi, Hyv,” he said.

No thanks. He’d stopped saying thanks in May, because she’d told him not to. He just said hi. As if she’d come into a room he was in.

“I read it out,” said the swamp guy. “She asked.” A pause. “Okay. I’ll see you Thursday, Hyv. Probably.”

“He said nine,” said PenguChi’s voice. “He means ten.”

Hyvred put the phone face down on the desk.

Then she picked it up again, because she could still hear it. She watched the rest of the stream from under her blanket, with all four corners of the room round her, until he went to bed.

She crossed the number out on the back of the note and wrote the new one. 3,661. Then she looked at rule four for a long time, and didn’t underline it again.

He came back on Thursday, at the second dusk. Exactly ten days, which meant Deston was wrong and PenguChi was right. Hyvred was so pleased about Deston being wrong that she nearly fell off the landing.

She saw them first. Of course she did. She’d been on the second landing since noon, watching the west road, which was fine, because she watched roads. And at a quarter past eight, two miles out, five dots came over the low rise where the road came in from the Fell Country.

She knew which one was which before they had faces. Drew at the side, at the edge, the way he always walked. Sheng Yang. Kotori in the middle. PenguChi at the back, slowest, with the long spear upright. And at the front, shield on his back, walking like a man who’d already looked at everything and was waiting for the road to catch up.

“West road,” she called, down the stair. Her voice went the whole height of the dome, the way it always did now. The pigeons went up out of the eye. “Two miles. Five.”

She didn’t say he’s in front. She’d said that in July, on the day they came in from the fen, and it had been the most humiliating thing she’d ever done. Bee had told her about it afterwards. Apparently it had been audible in the market.

She didn’t say it. She said five. Rule four.

They came round to the south road. Everybody came through the gate. She heard Wendy ask them. She was halfway down the stair by then, going slowly, casually, a hundred and six steps, because she happened to be going down anyway.

“Who’d stand next to you?” said Wendy, at the gate.

“Them,” said the swamp guy. Just that.

And then they were in the Hall.

He came through the south doors first, the way he always did. Mud to the thighs, from the Fell Country’s streams. A long tear down one side of his cloak. His shield on his back and his helm under his arm. Drew behind him, and Sheng Yang, and Kotori, and PenguChi.

Hyvred was at the bottom of the stair, casually.

The whole Hall had come out to see them in. Kei at the counter with his map, and Ama Corrie behind it. Gabe already shouting DEC. Xola in the banner room doorway with Thea behind her. Thea was smiling a small, private smile. Xola was smiling a very large, very public one, with her gills going like a fan.

Hyvred walked across the floor of the Hall of Roads toward him in a straight line, with her fists at her sides.

He saw her coming. He always saw her coming. He stopped. He stood there, muddy, with his helm under his arm, and waited. The way he’d stood in a square in a city with no name.

“Hi, Hyv,” he said.

She hit him. On the arm. Twice.

Rule two.

He didn’t flinch. Obviously. He looked down at his arm where she’d hit it. Then at her. Then, for the first time she could remember, he looked honestly confused.

“What did I do?” he said.

“NOTHING,” said Hyvred.

“Okay,” said the swamp guy.

From the banner room doorway there was a noise. It was Xola. Xola had both hands over her mouth and was making a sound like a kettle that had been left on much too long. Behind her, Thea had turned round to face the wall. Her shoulders were shaking.

The swamp guy looked at them. Then back at Hyvred. Then at them again.

“What?” he said.

“Nothing, Icey,” said Xola, through her hands. “Nothing.”

He looked at Thea’s back. He looked at Xola’s gills. He looked at Hyvred, who could feel her face going the colour of a brick, and her tail doing something she hadn’t told it to do. He looked like a man walking into a room where everyone had stopped talking.

“Did something happen?” he said.

“NO,” said Hyvred.

And she turned round and walked back across the floor of the Hall of Roads in a straight line. Past Kei, who was looking at his map very hard. Past Ama Corrie, who said hm. Up the stair, a hundred and six steps, forty steps. To the second landing, where Bee was sitting with her sketchbook. She went down on her knees behind Bee and put her face between Bee’s wings.

“Hi, Hyv,” said Bee.

“mrp.”

Down on the floor of the Hall, she heard PenguChi’s slow, calm voice.

“You’re very slow,” said PenguChi.

“About what?” said the swamp guy.

There was a pause. Hyvred, between the wings, held her breath.

“Yes,” said PenguChi.

And then she heard him laugh. Not much. Once, out loud, through his nose. Baffled. Not understanding the joke at all and laughing anyway, because PenguChi had made it and it was clearly a good one.

“Okay,” he said.

Hyvred stayed between the wings for a long time. Bee kept drawing, very gently, so as not to move. Somewhere far below, in the Hall, Ama Corrie was sweeping. Gabe was telling the whole dome about something with too many legs. Xola was still making the kettle noise. And a muddy man who’d been gone ten days, not nine, was sitting down in the first of nine chairs at the end of a long table. Hyvred knew it without looking. He was putting his feet up on the rung of the next one, and wondering what everybody was laughing at.

He had absolutely no idea.

She sat with her face in Bee’s wings and found, to her horror, that she was smiling.

Rule four, she thought. Nothing is different.

It was the first rule she’d ever made that she’d known, while she was making it, was a lie.
