Live from Elsewhere · Volume 2: The Long Night

Chapter 48

The Third Egg

2,852 words · 12 min read

Suzu went home for Christmas on the half-past-eight bus, and the last thing he said at the drop-off was, “Tell him the fan’s already on.”

“I will.”

“In the spare room. I put it on at seven. So it’s used to it.” He bent down to the open window with his backpack on his shoulder. “And there’s a pillow. A new one. On the bed. I bought it yesterday.”

Ice looked at him.

“You bought Gabe a pillow.”

“He’s going to bring his own,” said Suzu. “Everybody does. But there should be one there anyway.” The corner of his mouth went up a millimetre. “Sunday. Nine o’clock bus. Eat properly.”

He went in through the glass doors with the Christmas Eve crowd, a tall thin figure in black, and didn’t look back.

Vivian had a laptop open on the teak desk when he came in at nine, turned so that the screen faced the chair across from her. On the screen, in a small square, there was a girl.

She was about twenty-two, with her hair scraped back in a clip and a pair of round glasses, sitting in a small bright bedroom. The wall behind her was covered in posters and sticky notes. A mug said I CUT THINGS. She looked like she hadn’t slept, and was terrified, and was trying very hard not to show either.

“Mr Declan,” said Vivian. “This is Miss Lim.”

“Hi,” said the girl on the screen. “Hi. Hello. It’s me. Dee. Deborah. Hi.”

“Hi, Dee,” said Ice.

“You’re real,” said Dee. “Sorry. That’s a stupid thing to say. I know you’re real. I’ve watched you for five weeks. It’s just you’re real, in a room, in a—is that an orchid?”

“It is an orchid,” said Vivian. She didn’t look up from the paper in front of her. “Miss Lim. You’re Malaysian?”

“Yes. Johor Bahru. Born and raised.”

“Thank goodness,” said Vivian, with great feeling.

Dee looked at Ice through the screen in alarm. Ice tried not to laugh.

“It means you don’t need a pass,” he said. “She’s had a lot of passes this month.”

“Three,” said Vivian. “Mr Suzu’s, still processing. Mr Gabe’s, lodged on Monday, surname finally to hand. And very nearly Mr Deston’s, until Mr Declan remembered that Mr Deston is a contractor in Singapore and invoices from there. He has, I’m pleased to say, finally invoiced.” She turned a page. “Miss Lim will be a contractor too. Freelance. Media services. You’ll invoice the company monthly. I’ve written it so that it’s very simple.”

She turned the paper round and slid it toward the laptop camera, and held it there for Dee to read. One page. Plain sentences, edges filed off.

Short video editing and clipping services for the Company’s streams (Ice, Suzuyunn). Paid monthly in arrears. RM 2,800.

Dee read it. Ice watched her read it. He watched her get to the number at the bottom, and stop, and read it again, and put her hand over her mouth.

“That’s—“ she said, through her hand. “That’s too much.”

“It is not too much,” said Vivian. “It’s what the work is worth. I’ve looked at what the work did. Two million people watched a forty-four-second video you made on Tuesday. It’s not too much.” She lowered the paper. “Mr Declan, I believe you told Miss Lim something last night.”

“Say yes to the number,” said Ice.

Dee looked at him through the screen. Then at Vivian. Then at the I CUT THINGS mug, as if it might help.

“Yes,” she said. “Yes. Okay. Yes.”

“Good,” said Vivian. “I’ll email it. Please sign it and send it back. Please don’t sign it in a colour.” She closed the folder. “And Miss Lim.”

“Yes?”

“I watched the one with the lights.” Vivian put her glasses down on the desk beside the orchid. “Suzu catches the lights. Twice.” She paused. “You made it sound the way it looks.”

On the screen, Dee burst into tears, completely, with both hands over her face, in her small bright bedroom in Johor Bahru. Vivian looked faintly alarmed and passed a box of tissues across the desk toward the laptop, which didn’t help.

He drove up to KL in the afternoon.

Two hours up the highway, with the windows down until the air conditioning remembered itself, past the palm-oil plantations in their long green rows. He left at half past one, which was too early, and knew it was too early. He sat in the arrivals hall at KLIA from twenty to four with a paper cup of kopi he didn’t drink, watching the board.

Melbourne · LANDED 16:08.

He’d known Gabe since he was thirteen. A choir, of all things. The back row of a secondary-school choir in Singapore, three boys who’d been put there because they couldn’t hold the high parts and could hold the low ones. Him and Deston and Gabe. Eleven years, nearly twelve. Through school, through poly and everything after. Through Gabe moving to Australia a year and a half ago, which was the furthest any of them had ever been from each other. Gabe was the one who’d told him, once, at three in the morning on a call, you’re basically my brother from a different mother, you know that, right. He’d said it like a fact about the weather.

He’d watched a drowned church three times on his phone, on the toilet at work, and typed i want in.

At ten past five, he came out through the doors.

He was exactly the same. A big guy, not tall, solid. A bit heavier than he’d been in Singapore, and he’d say so himself before anyone else could. A huge duffel bag on one shoulder and a backpack on the other and a pillow, an actual pillow, in a grey pillowcase, under his arm. He came through the doors with the crowd, looking round the hall, and saw Ice standing by the barrier with the paper cup. He stopped dead.

“Dec,” said Gabe.

“Hi.”

Gabe dropped the duffel. Then the backpack. Then he walked across the hall and hugged him, so hard the kopi went everywhere, and didn’t let go for a long time. People walked round them. A small child stared.

“You stood on a plank,” said Gabe, into his shoulder, “for twelve hours.”

“Yes.”

“And you didn’t kneel.”

“Not the second time.”

“You absolute idiot.” Gabe let go and held him by both shoulders and looked at him. His eyes were very bright. “You absolute idiot. I watched it on the plane. I bought the wifi. Twelve dollars. I watched you stand on a plank for eight hours straight over the Indian Ocean, and a man next to me asked if I was okay.”

“Were you?”

“No,” said Gabe. “Obviously not.” He let go. He wiped his face with the back of his hand, unembarrassed. Then he looked down at the pillow under his arm. “I brought my own pillow.”

“I know. Everybody does.”

“Suzu said.”

“Suzu bought you one anyway.”

Gabe looked at him for a moment. Then he laughed, a big laugh that went right across the arrivals hall, and picked up his duffel.

“Of course he did,” he said.

Deston called them in the car, ten minutes out of the airport, from Singapore. Ice put him on the speaker in the dashboard.

“Is he there?” said Deston, before anyone had said hello. His voice was still rough from Monday. He’d lost most of it on the Landing and only some of it had come back. “Is he actually there? In the car?”

“I’m in the car,” said Gabe.

There was a short silence on the speaker.

“The back row,” said Deston, in his rough voice. “The three of us. In the same country. On Christmas Eve.”

“Not the same country,” said Ice. “You’re in Singapore.”

“It’s three hours on a bus. That’s practically the same country. I’m coming up for New Year’s. I’ve already told everyone.” A pause. “Gabe.”

“Dest.”

“You’re going to be so slow,” said Deston. “You’re going to start at level one in some random town in the middle of nowhere and we’re all level twenty. You’re going to be so slow.”

“I’m going to catch up.”

“How?”

“I’m going to hit things with a hammer,” said Gabe, “until they’re the right shape.”

“And when somebody comes for us?”

“Same hammer.”

Deston laughed. Gabe had been in every war zone in that other game with Deston, for years, the two of them in the middle of it: not like Ice, nobody was like Ice, but steady, and hard to move, and always there when you looked round.

Deston laughed, roughly, from three hundred kilometres away. Ice drove down the highway toward Malacca with the two of them talking over him. The sun was going down orange over the palm-oil, the way it went down over the old fen in the game. He found he couldn’t say anything for a while.

They got to the flat at a quarter to eight.

Gabe stood in the corridor outside the front door with his duffel and his backpack and his pillow and looked at it. The door, the grille, the shoe ledge. The door opposite, with its padlocked grille and its faded sticker.

“Deston told me about that one,” he said.

“Deston’s going to buy it one day.”

“Deston told me that too.” Gabe looked at it a moment longer. “He talks about it like it’s already his.”

Inside, Gabe put his head through the opening in the wall to say hello. Ice heard him say it, through the gap, in the voice he’d always used for Ice’s parents since they were thirteen, and then he came back. Ice showed him down the corridor to the end of his side, past Suzu’s room with the door shut and the bed made, to the spare room.

It was the room the first egg had been in. On Sunday the fifteenth of November, five and a half weeks ago, it had filled the whole room, white and humming, and Ice had stood in the doorway with a cold coffee and thought it looked like a very expensive egg. Now it was just a room again. A bed, a desk, a window. The ceiling fan going round, slowly, already on. On the bed, a new pillow, still a bit stiff from the packet.

Gabe put his own pillow down on top of it, in the middle, where it sat in its grey pillowcase looking completely out of place.

He looked at it.

“It’s the right decision,” he said.

At half past nine they drove to Melaka Raya.

The shoplot was dark and quiet. Ice let them in through the glass front door and turned on the light in the front office, where nobody worked: the desk, the pot plant, the clock Vivian had sent. Then through to the back room, where two white eggs sat side by side. His and Suzu’s. The folding table between them, a kettle, an empty box from the mamak.

Then up the stairs.

The front room on the first floor had a window onto the street, with the lights of Melaka Raya coming in orange through the blinds. There was nothing in it but the third egg. White, with soft corners and a long smoked-glass lid, on its low humming base, by the window, alone. It had been there since the twenty-sixth of November, the day Suzu came, when two delivery men had carried it up these stairs and asked where it went. Ice had said upstairs, because he hadn’t known who it was for.

The one that isn’t anybody’s yet.

Gabe stood in the doorway and looked at it for a long time.

“That’s mine,” he said.

“That’s yours.”

He walked across the room and put his hand on the shell, flat, the way Vivian had put hers on Ice’s egg a week ago. Then he moved his hand to the small glass square at the head end, with the fingerprint printed on it in thin grey lines. He stopped there, with his thumb just over it.

“What happens?”

“It reads your thumb. It’s yours. Nobody else can ever use it. Not me, not anyone.”

“Permanently?”

“Permanently.”

Gabe looked at the glass square for a moment. Then he looked at Ice, across the dark room, with the street lights coming in through the blinds.

“Okay,” he said. “Can I go in now?”

Ice laughed. He couldn’t help it. He leaned on the doorframe and laughed, at nothing that Gabe would understand, in a dark room in Melaka Raya on Christmas Eve, until Gabe said “what”, and he said “nothing. Suzu said that. Exactly that. Go on. Go in.”

Gabe took his shoes off and lined them up by the egg. He climbed into the gel and lay back and looked up at the ceiling. Then he put his thumb on the glass.

The lid came down.

Ice sat on the floor beside the egg with his back against the wall and his phone in his hand, with the call open.

It took a minute. Then Gabe’s voice came through, close, the way calls came through from inside.

“Dec.”

“I’m here.”

“It’s raining.” A pause. “There’s a window. It’s raining on the window. I can hear it.” Another pause, longer. “I can smell it.”

“I know.”

“You said. On stream. On the first day. You said it smelled like wet wood.” Gabe’s voice did something. “It does. It smells exactly like wet wood.”

Ice sat on the floor in the dark and listened to him find it.

The mirror, with his own face in it, because Gabe had never been anybody but himself. The pain setting. Thirty, Gabe said, halfway to being you, and Ice said that was the stupidest reason he’d ever heard, and Gabe said I know. The name box. He typed Baer, the name he’d used everywhere for years. It went green. The rack by the door, and the long pause in front of it, and then: there’s a hammer. And a shield. Dec, there’s a hammer and a shield.

And then the door.

“Oh,” said Gabe.

“Where are you?”

“There’s sea.” His voice had gone quiet. “There’s cliffs. Grey ones. There’s a little harbour at the bottom with boats in it, and the town goes up the cliff in steps, all stone, and there’s smoke coming out of a chimney at the top. Big chimney. There’s a sound like—” A pause. “It’s a hammer. Somebody’s hitting metal. Up at the top. I can hear it from here.”

“That’s a smithy,” said Ice.

“I know it’s a smithy.” He could hear Gabe grinning. “I’m going to go and find it.”

“Go on, then.”

“Dec.”

“Yeah.”

“Thanks.” A pause. “For the egg. For the pillow. For KL. For all of it.” Another pause. “I know you hate it when people thank you. I’m doing it anyway. You’ll live.”

Ice didn’t say anything. He didn’t trust what would come out.

“Okay,” said Gabe. “Going.”

And on the call, very faintly, a door opened onto the sea, and a man walked out into the rain toward the sound of a hammer.

Ice sat there for a long time.

On the floor of the front room upstairs at Melaka Raya, with his back to the wall and the phone in his lap, beside the egg. The third one. The one by the window. It had been nobody’s for a month and now it hummed, very quietly, with somebody in it. Through the blinds, the lights of the street. Somewhere toward the river, a long way off, a church bell started ringing for midnight. Christmas.

Downstairs, two eggs. Upstairs, one. Three eggs in a shoplot in Malacca, and all of them were somebody’s.

He thought about a notice pinned to a barn door in Brackenford. A man on the steps of the Hall with a charter in his hand, going down into the town without looking back. A tall man on the second stone, not moving his feet. A girl in Johor Bahru crying at an orchid. An old man on a bridge, nodding. And a captain he’d never met, on a stone in a ford, a long time ago, who’d held a day and a half and then given it back.

On the call, in his ear, very faintly, a hammer started ringing on an anvil. Uneven, badly, too hard. Then a little less badly.

He stayed until it found the beat.
