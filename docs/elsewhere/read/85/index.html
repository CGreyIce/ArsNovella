Chapter 85

Constellation

2,735 words · 12 min read

Chris signed at eight minutes to midnight.

He didn’t hurry. Kei had known he wouldn’t. He walked across the floor of the Hall of Roads, past the dead Rook and Gigi’s hedge, to the counter, where Ama Corrie stood with the Roll open and an empty bucket at her feet. He took the pen from Kei’s hand. And he stood at the counter for a moment with it, looking at the page. Nineteen names. One line left.

Then he wrote, carefully, in the hand of a man who’d made forty-three spreadsheets and knew exactly where every letter went:

dumbo.

He put the pen down.

“Twenty,” said Chris.

Nobody cheered.

Kei had thought they would. He’d pictured it on the steps for a month: the twentieth name, and the noise. But when it came, nobody made a sound. Twenty people stood round the edge of a round hall, and fourteen more in its doors who weren’t in it, and an old woman at a counter. They looked at a page in a book, and didn’t say anything at all. The way you don’t say anything when something’s finished being impossible and hasn’t started being real yet.

Then Ama Corrie picked up her spectacles and put them on, and looked at Kei over the top of them.

“The name,” she said.

He’d had it for two months.

Since the night before the longest night, alone at his desk in Australia with the lamp on. He’d drawn dots on a sheet of paper, and lines between them, and seen what it looked like, and not written the word. Too early. Too big. I’m the only one who can see it. He’d carried it every day since. On the call. On the steps. Under the ceiling, in the one place under the north lamp where the lines showed. He’d never once said it out loud. Not to Maigo, who’d guessed. Not to Ice, who knew. Not to anyone.

It was going to be twenty people’s, or it wasn’t going to be anything.

“Hyv,” he said.

She was on the ledge round the eye of the dome, two hundred feet up, at the wheel. She’d been there through all of it. She looked down at him.

“Open it,” said Kei.

She turned the wheel.

The great iron shutter of the eye rolled back on its clean track, the track Chris had spent four hours freeing. A long, smooth, iron sigh. And the clouds had gone. The wind off the moor had blown them away while they were fighting a bird in a round hall. Through the open eye, high and white over Carrow Cross, was the moon.

It came down through the eye into the Hall of Roads.

A circle of white light, sliding down the inside of the dome like water poured slowly into a bowl. And everywhere it touched the painted sky, the lines came out. Silver threads, all of them, every one. Joining worn gold star to worn gold star across the whole curve of the ceiling. Selwick’s lake. Briarwick’s ring with the ditches round it. A staircase of seven stars. A crossroads with a square. A hill ringed like a fire. A ring of nine in the south-east, one turned. And every line running north, across the dome, to one great gold star above the north door that wasn’t on any map.

Six thousand people in the round market saw it through the seven open doors, and went quiet.

Bee came out of the crowd at the south door with the banner.

She’d had it in Ama Corrie’s cupboard for three weeks, rolled. Nobody had seen it unrolled except Hyvred, and Wynn the dyer, who’d dyed it, and Kei, once, on a call, on a phone held up to a camera. Bee carried it into the middle of the Hall, into the circle of moonlight on the floor, under the eye. She knelt down on the old stone and unrolled it, slowly, from one end to the other, the way you’d unroll a map.

Night-blue. Wynn’s eleven dips of woad, the dark after the dawn didn’t come.

Gold dots, scattered across it the way Kei had scattered them across a sheet of paper in Australia, every one of them somebody. Silver lines between them, fine as hair, painted with an eyelash brush on a dyers’ bridge. Short thick ones and long thin ones. Solid ones and dotted ones. Little clusters. Threads between the clusters. And in the middle, a gold dot the same size as all the others, with the lines running through it.

The moonlight lay across the banner on the floor. Above it, the moonlight lay across the ceiling. And they were the same.

Not the same stars. Not the same places. The same thing. Points of light that were nowhere near each other, joined by lines that somebody had drawn between them so that they’d mean something. On a ceiling, four hundred years ago. On a banner, three weeks ago. On a sheet of paper on a desk, before the longest night.

Kei looked up at the ceiling. Down at the banner. At the twenty of them round the edge of the round hall, and the fourteen in the doors, and the old woman at the counter.

“Constellation,” he said.

He said it quietly. He’d always thought he’d have to say it loudly, the first time, so that it would carry. He didn’t. The Hall of Roads was round, and it carried.

Ama Corrie wrote it in the Roll. In black, at the top of the page, above the twenty names. In the careful hand of a woman who’d kept a Hall for forty years and swept an empty banner room every Sunday of them.

CONSTELLATION.

And then they made a noise.

Tiger first, because of course it was Tiger, a roar from the top of the wall where he’d gone back up to sit. Then Maigo, louder. Then Newish, then Salty, then everybody. Twenty people in a round hall, all at once, and then six thousand in the market outside the seven doors, and then the town, house by house, as the window reached them. Kei stood in the middle of the Hall of Roads in the moonlight with the banner at his feet, and it was so loud he couldn’t hear himself not saying anything.

They hung it at five to midnight.

Bee and Hyvred, together. Hyvred came down the two hundred and twelve steps to do it, because Bee asked her to. They carried it into the small round banner room through the door between two of the big ones. Under the old iron hooks, against the pale rectangle on the stone where something had hung for so long that the smoke had never reached it.

They lifted it up together and hung it on the hooks.

It fitted.

Not perfectly. The old pale shape was a little wider, and a little longer, than Wynn’s cloth. But it fitted. And when Hyvred stepped back to look at it, Kei saw her go very still. He looked where she was looking. Through the night-blue woad, faint at the edges where the cloth didn’t quite cover the pale stone, there were the old marks. The ghosts of buttons. The stitched stars of a banner that had come down before Ama Corrie’s grandmother was born.

Some of them were in the same places as Bee’s gold dots.

Not all. Some. A cluster in one corner. A long line across the middle. A single star, high up, at the top edge, where Bee had painted nothing. Where the old banner had had something. Where, on the ceiling outside, a great gold star sat above the north door, off every map.

“Somebody knew,” said Hyvred, very quietly.

“Somebody knew,” said Kei.

Ama Corrie stood in the doorway of the banner room with her keys. She looked at the banner on the wall for a long time, through her spectacles, and then over them. She didn’t say anything. Then she took one key off the ring, an old small brass one, and held it out to Kei.

“The banner room,” she said. “It’s yours now. Sweep it on Sundays.”

At midnight, as the thirtieth day ran out, CL_Steward_03 came to the steps.

He came alone, with his ledger, the way he always did. Behind him at the fountain, the twenty red sashes were standing up, packing their things, quite calmly. He stopped at the bottom of the steps and looked up at Kei. Then past him, through the north door, at the moonlight on the ceiling.

“Congratulations,” he said. And he meant it. Kei could tell.

“Thank you.”

“It’s a very good Hall,” said CL_Steward_03. “We’d have liked it.” He tucked the ledger under his arm. “We have seventeen others. We’ll manage.” He paused. “If you ever want to sell it—“

“No,” said Kei.

“No,” agreed CL_Steward_03. “I didn’t think so.” He looked at Kei for a moment longer. “Good luck, Constellation.”

He went back across the market to the fountain, and the twenty red sashes went with him, down the plain road, in step, into the night. Nobody cheered when they went. There didn’t seem to be any need.

And then, because it was midnight in Malaysia, and the world’s days ran three to every one of theirs, the sun came up.

It came up over the eastern roads, gold and sudden, the way it always came at midnight. Through the seven open doors of the Hall of Roads, along the floor, across the dead Rook and Gigi’s hedge and the counter and the Roll. Into the small round room where a night-blue banner hung on old hooks over the ghost of an older one. The thirtieth day ended, and on the far side of it, it was morning.

Ice was on the steps.

He’d come out of the north door while they were hanging the banner, the way he’d said he would. Not members. Friends. He was sitting on the third step, Kei’s step, with his shield beside him and the dawn coming up gold over the round market. The rest of Moonlight Harvest was on the steps below him, and Riverbank below them, and Xav at the bottom with his notebook, looking up at the dome.

Kei came out and sat down beside him.

Neither of them said anything for a while. In the market, six thousand people were going home, or not going home, or dancing round the fountain. Tiger had fallen into the fountain. Ama Corrie was shouting at him.

“Constellation,” said Ice eventually.

“You heard.”

“Everyone heard,” said Ice. “It’s round. It carries.”

Kei looked at his hands. They’d stopped shaking, sometime in the last ten minutes. He hadn’t noticed when.

“You said not yet,” he said.

“I said not yet.”

“You said make it big.”

“I did.”

“It’s twenty,” said Kei. “That’s not big. It’s twenty people who already knew each other, and four who walked, and a banner a girl painted on a bridge.” He looked out at the market. “It’s not big yet.”

“No,” said Ice. “Not yet.”

He turned his head and looked at Kei. Kei saw his face in the dawn: the same face that had stood on a plank in a swamp for twelve hours without making a sound, and offered him five hundred gold like a cup of tea, and minded when he said no.

“Make it big,” said Ice. “Then ask me again.”

“And you’ll say yes before I finish,” said Kei.

“Before you finish,” said Ice.

At the bottom of the steps, Xav looked up from his notebook.

“Ask me when it’s big,” he said. “I’ll be first through the door.”

“I know,” said Kei.

“I’ll be first,” said Xav. “Dec’ll be second. He’ll be annoyed about it.”

“I will,” agreed Ice.

Behind them, the weathercock on the top of the dome turned in the dawn wind, freed, and pointed north. And on the step below Ice, Suzu’s pack was glowing on one side through the canvas, with eighty-one small blue lights pressed against the glass of a jar, all pointing the same way.

“North,” said Kei, looking up at the weathercock.

“North,” said Ice. “You first. Then us.” He was quiet for a moment. “After.”

“After,” said Suzu, from the step below, with his eyes shut.

Kei logged out at four in the morning, Australian time, and sat at his desk in the dark.

The sheet of paper was where it had always been, under the lamp. Two months of it. The dots, the lines. Short thick ones and long thin ones, solid ones and dotted ones. He’d drawn it before the longest night, alone. He’d added to it every week since, as people came in and walked and met.

He picked up the pencil.

He went over twenty dots, one at a time, in ink, so they wouldn’t fade. Maigo and Salty and Wendy and Ina and Hako. Higu on her lake and Gigi in her hedge, with the line between them solid now, inked. Hyvred and Bee. Tiger and Drew. Niko and Newish and Winter and Chris. Four dots on their own at the edges, with dotted lines going in, that he inked over solid one by one: Tobi, Bram, Joy, Kite. And himself, in the middle, the same size as all the others, because Bee had told him to.

Then the others. The ones who weren’t in yet.

Hana and Milli and Jared, whose eggs had come in February, starting soon. Leaka in England, whose egg was coming next week, paid for by two people who’d lent her the money and told her she could argue about it later. Rachael and Calvin and Dennis, who were coming, Deston said, when Calvin’s egg came, which Deston was lending him the money for. Thea and Xola and PenguChi and three more beside them, a little cluster of six, a long way off at the edge of the paper. Vivian Ong, in a counting-house in a town on a river, reading the Exchange. Riverbank, eight dots on a bank, with a dotted line in, waiting. Ask me when it’s big.

And Moonlight Harvest. Six dots in a fen, with thick lines between them, and dotted lines going out from them to everyone. All of them dotted. Not yet.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then, at the very top of the sheet, above everything, off the edge of every road he’d ever drawn, he put one more dot. Gold, in the gold pencil he’d bought for exactly this and never used. A little bigger than the others. With lines going into it from everywhere.

He didn’t know what it was. Nobody did. Somebody had, once.

At the top of the sheet, at last, in his careful upright hand, he wrote the word.

Constellation.

Then he turned the lamp off, and sat in the dark for a while. The sheet of paper was a pale square on the desk, covered in lines. In a world nobody had a map of, the sun had come up over a round hall where seven roads met.
