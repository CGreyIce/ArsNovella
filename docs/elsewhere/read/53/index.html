Live from Elsewhere · Volume 2: The Long Night

Chapter 53

Eight

3,021 words · 13 min read

Rui Xiang had booked the room at eleven seconds past nine in the morning on the twentieth of December. He knew it was eleven seconds because he’d been watching the clock on his laptop with his finger over the button. Nine o’clock had come and he’d clicked, and the site had spun, and spun, and then said CONFIRMED. He’d looked at the time in the corner of the screen and it had said 09:00:11. By 09:01 the whole of Singapore was sold out until New Year’s Day.

Eight pods. One room. The first of January, nine in the morning. Six hours a night until the end of the month.

He’d been talking fast about it ever since. He talked fast about everything. People who didn’t know him thought he was nervous. People who did know him knew he just had a lot to say, and said it very clearly so nobody had to ask twice.

The room was in the old cinema on the fourth floor of the mall, at the far end, past the forty eggs on the steps. It was a smaller room that had been the projection booth, once. They’d taken the projector out and put eight eggs in it, in two rows of four, with the grey curtains between them. There was a little window in the wall where the light used to come through onto the screen.

He was there at a quarter past eight. Of course he was.

So was Azac. Azac was always there first, before anybody, as if he’d been there all night. He sat on the floor between the two rows of eggs with his back against the wall, eating a curry puff, and he looked up when Rui Xiang came in.

“Dec’s tank,” said Azac, by way of hello.

“What?”

“I’m going to be the tank.” Azac said it the way he said everything, comfortably, like a man settling into a chair. “Somebody has to stand at the front. Dec always did, in everything. Since poly. Then he moved, and for a long time nobody did, and everything was a bit wrong.” He finished the curry puff. “So I’m doing it.”

“You’ve never tanked anything in your life.”

“I’ve watched Dec do it for nine years,” said Azac. “And for the last seven weeks, twelve hours a day, from inside his eyes.” He got up. He was a big guy, solid, comfortable in himself. “How hard can it be?”

They came in ones and twos after that, all of them, the way they’d come into the poly group chat nine years ago.

John came in at half past eight, confident, with a printout. It was a spreadsheet, the café’s own price list against the price of a pod against the date pods would ship again. He’d been carrying it in his pocket for two weeks to prove a point he’d already proved. Malcolm came in at twenty to nine, with an umbrella, although it wasn’t raining. He looked at the eight eggs and said, “One of them’s going to be broken. Watch.” Ryan came in at quarter to, very relaxed, with his hands in his pockets. He looked at everyone and said “yeah” in a tone that meant good morning, I’m happy to be here, and sat down on the floor next to Azac.

Val came in at ten to nine, and stood in the doorway for a moment, and looked at the eggs, and didn’t say anything. She’d brought a water bottle and a lip balm and a small packet of throat sweets. She lined all three up on the floor beside her egg, very neatly, the way she did before a recording.

“Throat sweets?” said John.

“Magic needs a word,” said Val. “You have to say it. Out loud. Properly.” She sat down on the edge of her egg. “I’ve been practising.”

Jun Liang came in at five to nine, at a run, in a T-shirt that said something nobody had time to read. He came through the door talking and didn’t stop.

“I’M HERE. I’M HERE. Did I miss it? I didn’t miss it. Good. Morning! Happy New Year! Rui Xiang, you booked it at eleven seconds past, I saw your screenshot, that’s insane, that’s the most you thing you’ve ever done.” He grabbed Azac’s hand and shook it, and then Ryan’s, and then Malcolm’s, who hadn’t offered it. “Is that a printout, John? Is that the spreadsheet? Of course it is. Val! Val, you brought throat sweets, that’s so professional. Where’s Xav? Is Xav here?”

“Xav’s in Japan,” said Rui Xiang.

“I know Xav’s in Japan,” said Jun Liang. “I mean, is he on the call?”

[ Call · Sketch, theidiot, Serenity, Mango, Pingu, Resent, Sakami, Historia ]

“I’m on the call,” said Xav.

His voice came out of eight phones at once in the little projection room. Calm, level, the plan voice, from a cold small room in Japan an hour ahead. He’d been in the game for seven weeks. He’d been in Brackenford for nine days, in a room at the inn by the east bridge, levelling and mining and planning. He sounded exactly like he always had, on every call, for nine years, at the start of anything.

“Okay,” said Xav. “Everybody lie down.”

Rui Xiang lay down in his egg, the third one in the back row, by the little window. Through it he could see the old cinema below, the forty white eggs on their steps, and the low blue light.

He’d waited a long time for this. Not as long as Xav. Not as long as Dec, who’d been waiting since he was a child. But a long time. He’d played games with these people for nine years. Osu! with Dec, mostly, chasing his scores, six years of it. Dec played everything on the hardest setting with the notes hidden, and was always just better. Rui Xiang had spent six years getting slightly less far behind him, and loved every second of it. CS with Xav and Azac. Lost Ark with Xav and Malcolm and Val, for a thousand hours, then two thousand. Everything, with everyone, for nine years, until Dec moved to Malacca and went quiet. And then everything had been a bit less, for a while. Not wrong. Just less.

Seven weeks ago, Dec had walked into a swamp with a shield, and everyone had started watching. And then Xav had gone. And then Xav had sold everything he’d brought up out of a drowned church to pay for a piece of paper in a hall, for them.

What would you do, Rui Xiang had asked him. If you were us.

Xav had told them. Everything, in order. First week, everybody levels alone. Nobody answers CL. Get to ten. Walk to Brackenford. He’d be on the bank.

“Thumbs,” said Xav, on the call.

Rui Xiang put his thumb on the glass.

It was raining.

Of course it was raining. He’d watched Dec’s first minute, and Maigo’s, and Hyvred’s, and a dozen others. Rui Xiang had watched all of them. He knew exactly what the room would be like.

It was different. It always was, apparently. Everybody said so. The rain smelled.

“Oh,” said Val, very softly, on the call, from her own room somewhere. “Oh. It smells like—“

“Wet wood,” said six people at once.

He built himself as himself. They all did, except Val, who’d changed her hair to a colour she’d always wanted and wouldn’t say what. He took the name theidiot, which he’d used everywhere for years. It went green.

He took two knives from the rack. Short, light, the kind you could throw. He’d always been the one who picked things up fast, anything, in any game. You needed something you could pick up anything with.

He opened the door.

White. That was the first thing. A huge flat white plain of salt, cracked into great plates, going away to the horizon in every direction under a hard blue sky. A town of low mud-brick houses and canvas awnings at the edge of it, with a long line of camels at a trough. A wind that tasted of salt and dust and something cooking.

“I’m in a desert,” he said. “A salt desert. It’s flat. It’s so flat. There’s camels.”

“I’m in a forest,” said Azac, from somewhere. “A big one. There’s a wooden fort. It’s got a wall. There’s a man on the gate who’s just looked at me and said you’re big, which is fair.”

“It’s raining,” said Malcolm. “Here. It’s still raining. Outside. Of course it’s raining. It’s a market town and it’s raining on the market.”

“I’ve got mountains,” said John. “And a tower. A stone tower on a hill with a big mirror at the top, catching the sun. They’re sending signals with it. Flashes. Across the valley.” A pause. “I want to do that.”

“Ryan?”

“Moor,” said Ryan, comfortably. “Heather. A town with a gate. It’s nice.” A pause. “Yeah.”

“Jun Liang?”

“Mine,” said Jun Liang, very loudly. “I’ve got a mine! There’s a mountain with a hole in it and men coming out of the hole with rocks! There’s a forge! I’ve already made a friend! He’s called Tam and he’s got a beard down to here and he says I’ve got good arms!” A pause. “I’m getting a hammer.”

“Val?”

There was a short silence on the call.

“There’s an abbey,” said Val. “On a cliff. With monks. They write things. Books. By hand. There’s a library.” Another pause. Her voice had gone quiet. “There’s a woman here who teaches spells. She says you have to say the word like you mean it. Like you’ve never meant anything more.”

“Can you do that?” said Rui Xiang.

“I’ve been doing that my whole life,” said Val, “for nobody.”

The message from Crimson Ledger came at nine twenty-three, while he was buying a skin of water in Sandrift market.

He’d known it would. They all had. Azac’s had been in their DMs since before Christmas, with the fifteen dollars an hour. But it was still a little bit astonishing to watch it arrive. Twenty-three minutes after he’d put his thumb on the glass, in a salt desert, from people he’d never met who knew his name.

He looked at it for a while, standing in the white sun of the salt flats.

On the call, one by one, everyone said they’d had one too.

“Mine says why not start early,” said John. “With an emoji.”

“Mine’s got a party popper,” said Malcolm. “I hate it.”

“Nobody answers,” said Xav, very quietly.

“Nobody answers,” said Rui Xiang.

“Nobody answers,” said Azac, and Malcolm, and John, and Ryan said “yeah,” and Val said “obviously.”

There was a short pause on the call.

“Jun Liang,” said Xav.

“Sorry,” said Jun Liang. “Sorry. I answered. I didn’t mean to. I just said no thank you!!! with a smiley face. And then happy new year. Because it is.” A pause. “They said happy new year back.”

Rui Xiang stood in the middle of Sandrift market and laughed so hard a camel looked at him.

They talked all morning, on and off, eight towns a long way apart. Seven of them, and Xav in the middle in Brackenford, listening. Nobody was near anybody. Nobody had expected to be. That was the first week: everybody levels alone.

It was harder than Rui Xiang had thought. He’d known it would be. Not the levelling; he’d done harder levelling in a hundred games. The alone. He’d got so used to the group chat, the calls, nine years of everyone always being one tab away. Now they were all on a call, and all in different deserts and forests and rain, and you couldn’t help any of them. You could only listen.

At eleven, Azac found out what a shield was for.

They heard it happen. He’d taken the biggest one on the rack in the wooden room, a great oblong of oak and iron, nearly as tall as he was. He’d walked out of the fort’s gate into the forest to find something to hit. Something had found him first. A boar, by the noise. A big one.

They heard it charge.

“Back foot,” said seven people on the call, at once, without meaning to.

They heard the shield take it. A huge flat thud, like a door being kicked. Then Azac’s voice, very surprised: “Oh.”

“Azac?”

“It works,” said Azac. “The back foot. It actually works. It hit the shield and I didn’t move. I didn’t move at all. It’s just—stood there. Looking at me. Like I’m a wall.” A pause. Then another thud, and a squeal, and silence. “I’m a wall.”

“You’re a wall,” said Rui Xiang.

“Dec’s going to be insufferable about this,” said Azac.

At noon, Val said a word.

They didn’t hear it. She’d gone off the call for a while, an hour, to the library in her abbey on the cliff. When she came back on, her voice was strange. Very quiet, and very careful, as though she was carrying something that might spill.

“I did a spell,” she said.

“What spell?”

“It’s called Light. It’s the first one. You hold your hand like this and you look at the thing you want to light, and you say lux.” A pause. “It means light. In an old language. The sister who teaches it says nobody’s ever said it right the first time, in forty years. She says you have to mean it the way you’d mean I love you to someone you’ve never said it to.”

“And?”

There was a long silence on the call.

“First try,” said Val. “Ninety-four per cent.” A pause. “She cried. The sister. She sat down on a bench in the library and cried, and said child, where did you learn to say a word like that.”

Nobody said anything for a moment.

“Where did you learn to say a word like that?” said John, eventually.

“In my room,” said Val. “Into a microphone. For years.” Her voice wobbled, very slightly. “For nobody.”

Rui Xiang stood in the salt market at Sandrift, in the white noon sun, and didn’t say anything for a long time. Nobody did.

“Not for nobody,” said Xav, eventually, very quietly. “Not any more.”

At twenty to three, the egg told them it was time.

The morning booking was six hours, a quarter to nine to a quarter to three. Eight eggs in a projection booth at the end of an old cinema, and seven people in them, a long way apart. Rui Xiang lay in the gel in the back row by the little window as his last ten minutes ticked down, with the call open, and listened to them.

Azac hitting a training post at the fort, thud, thud, thud, very pleased with himself. Malcolm in his rain, saying it would definitely be raining tomorrow too. John at the foot of the signal tower, asking the man who worked the mirror for the fourth time if he could have a go. Ryan, somewhere on a moor, saying nothing, being there. Jun Liang at the forge with his new friend Tam, laughing about something in a language that wasn’t quite English and wasn’t quite anything else. Val in her library, very quiet, saying lux to herself under her breath, over and over, as if she couldn’t believe it was hers.

And Xav, in Brackenford, in the middle of all of them, on the bank. Not saying anything. Just there. The way Dec had always been there, in everyone’s chat, for years. The thing nobody noticed until it stopped.

“First week,” said Xav. “Everybody levels alone. Nobody answers CL.” A pause. “Jun Liang.”

“I KNOW.”

“Get to ten. Then walk to me.”

“How far is it?” said Rui Xiang.

“Doesn’t matter,” said Xav. “I’ll be on the bank.”

The egg told him he’d been in Elsewhere for six hours, and would be returned now. The lid lifted. He was lying in a white egg in a projection booth on the fourth floor of a mall in Singapore, on the first day of the year. Through the little window in the wall he could see the old cinema, and the forty eggs on their steps, and the low blue light. Around him, one by one, seven other lids were lifting.

Nobody said anything for a while. They just sat up, the seven of them, in their eggs, in two rows of four with one empty, and looked at each other across the little room.

Then Jun Liang said, “Again tomorrow?”, and everybody laughed.
