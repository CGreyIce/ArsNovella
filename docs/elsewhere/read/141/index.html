Live from Elsewhere · Volume 5: Ask Me Again

Chapter 141

Solid

2,891 words · 13 min read

Bee found the star on Saturday night, while everyone else was still making noise.

Kei was at Ama Corrie’s counter with his map. He was trying to get his breath back, and failing, because every time he got it back somebody came past and hugged him and he lost it again. Calvin had done it three times. Jun Liang had done it twice, at a volume that made the dome ring. Ama Corrie was writing something in the old black book in her small hand, and pretending not to be pleased, and failing at that too.

And Bee came out of the banner room with a face Kei had never seen on her.

“Kei,” said Bee.

He looked up.

“Come and look at this,” said Bee. “Don’t bring anyone. Just you.” She paused. “And her.” She nodded at Ama Corrie.

The banner was back on its hooks.

Bee had brought it up from the hatch after the ten came up the stair, rolled, carefully. She’d hung it back in the banner room the way she always did, straightening it, stepping back to look. Night-blue, edge to edge, with the gold dots and the silver-white lines. All solid now, since Tuesday, since she’d knelt on the floor of the Hall for an hour with an eyelash brush.

“There,” said Bee, and pointed.

At the very top of the banner, near the edge, above all the dots and lines, there was a star.

Small. Gold. Faint, the way old gold goes. Five points, like the stars cut into the waystones on the north moor. Like the star cut into the flat stone in the middle of the ring at the bottom of the hatch, where seven dark lanterns had been set down that afternoon.

Kei stood very still.

He’d seen that place before. Not the star. The place. In March, on the night of the founding, when Bee and Hyvred hung the banner on the old hooks for the first time. It had fitted, not perfectly. The old ghost-marks on the wall behind it, from the old guild’s banner, the one they’d taken north three hundred years ago, had lined up with some of Bee’s dots. And there’d been one old mark, high up, at the top edge, where Bee had painted nothing. Somebody knew, Bee had said.

Bee had painted nothing there.

There was a star there now.

“I didn’t paint that,” said Bee. Her voice was very flat, which meant she was very far from flat underneath. “I want that on the record. I didn’t touch it. I don’t have that gold. That’s not Ambry’s gold. That’s old.”

Ama Corrie had come up beside Kei. She looked at the star for a long time over her spectacles.

“It answered,” she said.

“What did?”

“The hall.” Ama Corrie didn’t take her eyes off the star. “A hall for ten answers only to a banner. You took your banner down there and hung it on the iron. And the hall read it, the girl with the lantern said. And then you kept it.” She nodded at the star. “And it answered back.”

Kei looked at the star. Then, without meaning to, up, through the banner-room door, across the Hall, to the ceiling of the dome. To the place above the north door where there was a big gold star off every map, that every silver line on the ceiling ran toward.

It was the same star. Smaller. The same five points.

“Somebody knew,” said Kei.

“Somebody always knows,” said Ama Corrie. “Usually it’s somebody dead.” She sniffed. “Sometimes it isn’t. Sweep it on Sundays.”

On Sunday morning, at the first dawn, the steward from Crimson Ledger came to the Hall of Roads.

Kei hadn’t seen him since March. Since the night of the founding, when he’d sat at the fountain with nineteen others in red sashes waiting for midnight, and raised a cup to Kei, and said good luck, Constellation. He’d made it sound like he meant it, and Kei had never been sure if he did.

He came up the south steps alone. No sashes behind him. He stopped at the top, where Wendy was, because everybody stopped where Wendy was.

“Who’d stand next to you?” said Wendy.

The steward looked at her for a moment. Then, unexpectedly, he smiled.

“Nobody here,” he said. “I’m not asking to join. I’m asking to see Kei.”

Wendy looked at Kite. Kite looked at the steward for a long time. Kite had been in a different red, once, of a different kind, and knew what it looked like from the inside.

“He can come in,” said Kite.

They talked at the counter. Ama Corrie stood behind it with her arms folded, listening, the way she listened to everything.

“Congratulations,” said the steward. “Second.”

“Congratulations,” said Kei. “First.”

“We bought eighteen rooms,” said the steward. “This spring. With gold. Because we’d been told, early, quietly, by people who tell us things, that there’d be something under them one day.” He looked at the stair to the eye, where the hatch was. “We built for it for six months. Three tens. Two wiped. We went back in. We were first.”

“I know. Good for you.”

“He said that,” said the steward. “On his stream. Good for them. The swamp one.” He looked at Kei. “I watched it. I watch it more than I’d tell anyone at my company.” He was quiet for a moment. “We were first because we bought it. You were second because you walked eight days to ask one man, and he said yes before you’d finished, and you didn’t even need the hall. It’s a different kind of second.” He paused. “I’m not sure it’s second.”

Kei didn’t know what to say. So he said, “Hm.”

The steward laughed.

“That’s what I came to say,” he said. “And this.” He looked at Kei directly. “If you ever want to sell the room—“

“No,” said Kei.

“Good,” said the steward. “Good luck, Constellation.” He turned to go. Then he stopped, at the top of the south steps, beside Wendy. Without turning round he said, “Tell the swamp one his chat’s very well behaved for a man with no head mod.”

He went down the steps. Kei watched him go across the round market, alone, in no sash, until he was gone.

Kestrel posted at noon.

VANTA · 20 names. banner tomorrow. next door.

And a picture. Twenty people standing at a counter in a Hall Kei didn’t know, under a ceiling he couldn’t see. One small one in plain black at the front, with a short bow.

Then, a minute later, a message, only to him.

He looked at it for a long time. Kestrel had said see you at the next one to Moonlight Harvest in December, after a drowned church. She’d meant it as a threat. He thought, reading it, that this time she might mean it as something else.

On Sunday afternoon, for the first time, there were forty-nine people at the long table in the banner room.

Kei had never had a meeting with forty-nine people. He’d had calls. Thirteen, sixteen, thirty-two. A call was easy. You talked and people unmuted and said here. A table was different. A table had faces, and elbows, and Jun Liang.

He stood at the head of it, under the banner with the old gold star at its top edge, and looked down it. Thirty-two who’d been here since spring. Riverbank, eight, halfway down, mixed in already, Azac between Tiger and Salty, Malcolm between Higu and Maigo. Moonlight Harvest, nine, at the far end by the wall, in the chairs Ama Corrie had set out in March. Ice in the first one, with his hands flat on the table.

“I don’t know how to do this,” said Kei.

“Nobody does,” said Gigi, from halfway down. “Do it anyway. Like it’s going to fall down.”

So he did it anyway.

It took two hours. The halls. There were more of them, the notices said, under every Hall where seven roads met. Seven Below had been the first. There’d be others. The guild would need more tens, and more people at thirty-five, and Gabe and Rachael and Calvin and Dennis said they’d help with that. The gate, which was still three thousand a night. Wendy said it’s going to go badly and then explained, very calmly, exactly how to make it go well, and everyone did what she said.

And the north.

Kei unrolled his map on the table and put his finger on the gold dot at the top, off every road. Past the round town, past the north moor, past a ruined hall with a birch tree in it. Past the stones every fifty paces going into the black. Into the Unlit Country, where Higu’s light had gone out like a moth in a hand.

“They went there,” said Kei. “The old guild. Three hundred years ago. With their banner. And didn’t come back.” He looked down the table. “One day we’ll go and see.”

Nobody said anything.

“Not yet,” said Kei.

The whole table laughed. All forty-nine of them, at once, so loudly the dome carried it all the way round and back. Kei stood there with his finger on the gold dot and didn’t understand why. Then he did. He went red, and laughed too. Not yet. He’d heard it so many times in six months that it had worn a groove in him. Now it was coming out of his own mouth, to the man who’d said it to him.

At the far end of the table, Ice was laughing too. Properly. With his head down on his hands.

“It’s a good line,” said Ice, when he could. “You can have it.”

“I don’t want it.”

“You’ve got it now,” said Ice. “It’s yours. I’ll say yes before you finish instead.” He looked up. “When it’s time. When you ask. I’ll go first.”

“I know you will,” said Kei.

“Somebody has to face out,” said Ice. Then he looked down the table, at forty-nine people. At Suzu beside him, and Xav halfway down, and Hyvred, who was very carefully looking at the ceiling. “But not always me,” he said. “Not any more. There’s enough of us now to take turns.”

It was Ama Corrie who found the six chairs.

Kei didn’t see her set them out. Nobody did. She did it on Sunday night, after the meeting, when the banner room was empty. On Monday morning, when Kei came in to look at the star again, they were there. Six chairs, from the store under the stair, at the very far end of the long table, past the nine, by the window. Not at the table, exactly. Near it. Waiting.

Kei stood in the doorway and looked at them for a long time.

He knew who they were for. Ama Corrie had heard Higu say six on the stair, probably. Or Gigi. Or nobody, and she’d just counted the dotted lines on his map over his shoulder, the way she counted everything. Six dots at the edge, near a town of wheels. Not yet.

He didn’t say anything to anyone. He didn’t have to. Gigi came into the banner room at noon with two cups of tea, and saw them.

She stopped in the doorway, exactly where Kei had stopped. She looked at six empty chairs at the end of a long table by a window, with nobody in them. She stood there for a long time with a cup in each hand.

Then she went out again, very quietly. Kei found out later, from Higu, that she’d gone straight up two hundred and twelve steps to the eye. She’d sat on the ledge where Higu always sat, and got her phone out.

Higu told Kei that night, on the roof, because Higu told Kei the things she’d decided he should know, and this was one.

“Did you take it to her?” said Kei.

“Not yet,” said Higu. “Tomorrow. I’m going to ring her tomorrow.” She looked at the moon, coming up over the rim of the eye. “She’ll go very quiet. And then she’ll say a chair. And then she won’t say anything for a long time.” She smiled. “And then she’ll ask me who set it out. And I’ll tell her it was an old woman who sweeps under chairs nobody sits in. Because that’s what keeping is.”

“And then?”

“And then I don’t know,” said Higu. “That’s not mine. I’m the road.” She held out her hand, the way she always did, flat on the stone ledge. Gigi, on her other side, had come up at some point without either of them noticing. She was sitting with her knees up and two cups. She put her hand on Higu’s.

“It’ll fall down,” said Gigi quietly.

“Probably,” said Higu.

“I set a chair out anyway.”

“Ama Corrie set it out.”

“I looked at it,” said Gigi. “That’s the same. For me, that’s the same.”

Higu didn’t argue. Neither did Kei.

Kei saw it on Ice’s stream on Tuesday, on his phone, on the steps. He watched Ice, on the stream, sitting in his chair at the end of the long table in the banner room. He read it, and didn’t say anything at all, the way she’d told him not to. He just smiled at the edge of his eye for a long time.

Then he said, very quietly, to nobody, “Okay.”

Halfway down the table, Bee looked up from her drawing at Deston. Deston looked back at her. Neither of them said anything. It was the look of two people who both knew the same thing about somebody else and had both decided not to say it. Kei saw it, and didn’t understand it, and decided, the way he’d decided about a lot of things Higu told him, that it wasn’t his to understand. He didn’t draw anything.

He went up to the roof of the dome on the last night, Tuesday, alone. Higu and Gigi had gone down. The moon was coming up through the eye and the silver lines were coming out one by one on the ceiling below him.

He got the map out on the ledge and unrolled it in the moonlight.

Forty-nine dots in the middle, inked solid. Hundreds of lines between them. All solid now. Riverbank’s. Moonlight Harvest’s. Rafi’s, Bram’s, Joy’s, Kite’s, Tobi’s, every one. He’d drawn the first of them on the back of a café menu in December. He’d been the only dot on it, small, in the middle, and he’d drawn himself small on purpose.

At the edge, six dots near a town of wheels. Dotted lines. Not yet.

And at the very top, above everything, off every road, a gold dot.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he took out his pen and wrote at the top of the map, above the gold dot, above everything, the way he’d written it at four in the morning on the night of the founding.

Constellation.

He looked at the word. Then down at all the solid lines under it. Then at the six that weren’t solid yet, and the gold dot that wasn’t anybody yet.

He’d thought, in December, that a guild was twenty people who’d stand next to each other. He’d been right. It was. But he hadn’t known, in December, that the dotted lines were the best part. The ones that weren’t anything yet, and might be.

He rolled up the map.

Below him, in the Hall, Ama Corrie was sweeping under the chairs. All fifty-five of them, in the moonlight from the eye, though it wasn’t Sunday. Even the six by the window that nobody sat in.

Especially those.
