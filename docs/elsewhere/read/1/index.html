Live from Elsewhere · Volume 1: Moonlight Harvest

Chapter 1

Fernwick

3,939 words · 17 min read

He was standing up.

That was the first thing. He’d lain down in a warm gel bed in a dim blue room, and now he was on his feet, and his feet were telling him so, with the weight of a whole body pressing them into a wooden floor. He stood still in case moving broke it.

Rain ran down a window in front of him. Grey morning light came through it, and with it that smell again, wet earth and cold air and something green. A draught touched the back of his neck. He felt each hair it moved.

He breathed in. His chest rose. The air went all the way down, cold at the top and warmer at the bottom, the way air does, and he let it out again slowly.

“Oh,” Ice said. “Oh, no. Oh, they actually did it.”

His voice was his own. It came out of his own mouth into a small room and bounced back off the walls with exactly the right amount of wood in it.

He looked down at his hands.

They were his hands and not his hands. The shape was right, the length of the fingers, a scar he’d had since he was nine across the ball of the left thumb; somebody had scanned that, somewhere in the last forty seconds, and decided to keep it. But they were cleaner than his hands. The nails were the nails he’d have if he didn’t bite them. He turned them over and flexed them, and they did it with no delay, none, not even the half a frame you learned to stop noticing in every other game, because there was no frame. There was just a hand.

He made a fist. He opened it.

“Chat,” he said. “Chat, you have no idea. I know you can’t see this yet. Sixty seconds. But I need you to know that I can feel the air on my hands.”

The room was small and plain, made of a pale wood that smelled faintly of sap. There was the window, a tall mirror in a frame against one wall, a rack of weapons by a closed door, and nothing else. No music. No narrator. Only the rain.

In the corner of his vision, where it would have been on a monitor, a small translucent panel had appeared. When he looked at it, it came forward and sharpened. When he looked away, it faded back to a ghost. Chat, it said at the top, and under that a column of lines he couldn’t make sense of for a second, because they were all about things that hadn’t happened yet.

Then he got it. The delay. He was a minute ahead of them. They were still watching him lie down in an egg.

He laughed out loud, alone in a wooden room, and the laugh bounced back at him too.

The mirror lit up as he turned toward it.

[ Welcome to ELSEWHERE.
A likeness was prepared for this account. ]

The figure in the glass wasn’t him, exactly. It was the face he’d drawn years ago, and spent three evenings two weeks ago rebuilding in the pre-launch creator, a feature at a time, until it was right. Black hair, messy and layered and falling in front of his eyes the way it had in every drawing. A streak of teal through the fringe on the right side, and teal again underneath at the nape, where it only showed when he turned his head. Eyes the same blue-green, somewhere between teal and cyan. Slim, his own height, give or take; the creator had let him stretch a finger’s width here and there and then refused. He’d tried to make himself two metres tall, as a test, and it had said Within a tenth of your own, very politely, and put him back.

The model’s clothes hadn’t come with it. Those stayed outside. Instead the figure in the mirror wore what everybody wore on the first morning: a long linen shirt, plain trousers, a leather belt, and boots that had clearly been made by somebody who had seen boots once and wanted to try. A little list floated beside it.

[ Clothing: as issued. Colours may be chosen. ]

He chose. Charcoal for the shirt. Black for the trousers. A dark brown jerkin over the shirt, laced up the front, which the game called a leather vest and which was as close as it was going to let him get. A thin teal cord on the laces, because he could.

When he looked up, his reflection was looking back at him with its hair in its eyes.

“Hi,” he said to it.

It said hi back. Its mouth moved a fraction of an instant ahead of his, which was impossible, so it had to be his brain catching up with something it had never seen before. He stopped looking at it. That way lay madness, and the first morning was not the time.

[ Sensation
Pain is felt at 20%. You may set it between 0% and 50%. ]

There was a slider under it. He’d read about this. Everybody had. It had been one of the loudest arguments of the whole pre-launch year, people on one side saying a game shouldn’t hurt at all and people on the other saying that a game that couldn’t hurt you couldn’t feel real, and the company standing in the middle with its hands up, saying 20% is like stubbing your toe through a thick sock, and nobody has to change it.

He put his finger on the slider and pushed it all the way to the end.

[ At 50%, pain is unpleasant. It is never harmful. It cannot leave a mark on you. You may change this at any inn. Continue? ]

“Yes,” said Ice. He pressed it.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened; nothing had touched him. He looked at the chat panel, knowing it was pointless.

They’d caught up as far as the window. In about fifty seconds they’d get to the slider, and he could already see what would happen, so he said it now, to save time.

“I put pain on fifty. Before you start.” He picked at the lace on his jerkin. “I always play tank. Every game, every class. I’m going to stand at the front with a shield, and things are going to hit me, and I need to know exactly where they hit. Twenty per cent is a sock. I don’t want the sock.” He shrugged at nobody. “Also, honestly? If it’s going to hurt the people in front of me, it can hurt me too.”

He’d find out what that meant soon enough. He was fairly sure he’d regret it at least once before lunch.

[ What are you called? ]

He’d known since the summer what he was going to say. He’d known for twenty years, really. He just hadn’t expected the field to be empty.

“Ice,” he said.

There was a pause. It was long enough that he started composing the second choice in his head: Ice, then a number, then the usual humiliation.

[ Ice — available.
Names are unique across the world. This one is yours. ]

“No.”

It was eight in the morning in Malaysia on launch day, and for the next sixty seconds he was the only person in the world who knew that the most obvious three-letter name in the English language had just been handed to a man in a spare room in Malacca. He punched the air. His fist went up into the cool air of the little room and every muscle in his arm did exactly what he told it to.

“Chat,” he said, “chat, you’re going to see this in a minute, and I need you to be normal about it.”

They were not normal about it.

“I’m not selling it,” said Ice, a minute ahead of all of them, and grinned, because Chris had already done the maths that he had too.

The rack by the door held ten things.

A plain arming sword, a long knife’s big brother. A round shield of wood with an iron rim. A spear. A hatchet. A hammer with a head the size of a fist. A pair of daggers in one sheath. A short bow and a quiver. A staff of pale wood. A thin wand, a little crooked. And a book with a blank cover, bound shut with a strap.

Over it, a single line:

[ Take what you mean to carry. What you carry, and how you carry it, will shape what you become. ]

That was the whole class system. Everyone had been trying to work it out for a year and a half, and the company had said the same thing in every interview: there are no classes to choose. You picked up a weapon. You used it. At some point the world decided what you were. There’d been a lot of theories about the “at some point”. There’d been a lot of theories about everything.

He didn’t hesitate. He’d known what he was taking since before he knew what the game was called.

He took the sword in his right hand. It was heavier than he’d expected and lighter than it should have been, and the grip was leather over wood, already shaped by nobody’s hand. He took the shield in his left. The strap went over his forearm and the grip sat in his fist, and when he raised it, its weight hung off his elbow in a way he’d only ever read about.

“Tank,” he said, unnecessarily.

The door had a line of text on it, written into the wood the way a name is written over a shop.

[ Elsewhere is large. Most begin somewhere small.
Your beginning is chosen for you. ]

And under it, smaller:

[ One day in Elsewhere passes in eight of your hours. You will be asked to rest after twelve. ]

So, three days for every one at home, and a hard stop at twelve hours. He’d known both, from the dev diaries. It was different seeing them written down, though, on a door he was about to open. Twelve hours from now his body would be lying in an egg and the game would take its hand off his shoulder and say enough, whether he’d finished what he was doing or not.

“Starting town is random,” he told chat. “Thousands of them, apparently. Nobody knows which one they’ll get until they walk through. If it’s somewhere with a beach, I’m moving in.”

He opened the door.

It was not somewhere with a beach.

The rain had stopped. In its place was mist, low and white, lying on black water so still it looked solid. Out of the water grew reeds, more reeds than he had ever seen, taller than a man and pale brown at the tips, going away in every direction until the mist ate them. Wooden walkways ran between the reeds on legs of dark timber, crossing and branching and joining, and on platforms where they met stood houses: low and round, with walls of woven wattle and roofs of thick grey thatch that came down almost to the boards. Smoke rose from holes in the roofs and lay flat over everything. Somewhere a frog was saying the same word over and over. Somewhere much further off, something bigger answered it.

It was cold. Not cold like air conditioning. Cold like the morning, with wet in it, that got into the cuffs of his sleeves. The boards under his boots were slick, and gave a fraction with each step, and smelled of old wood and water.

And all around him, on the big square platform outside the little building he’d stepped out of, people were arriving.

They came out of the same door, one after another, though it was one door and it was clearly only big enough for one. They stepped out blinking into the mist, and stopped, and stared. A tall woman with a spear and a short man with a bow. A boy who couldn’t have been more than eighteen, in the same linen shirt as Ice, holding a hatchet upside down. Three people who’d come out together and were already holding on to each other’s sleeves. One by one they turned in a slow circle, and he watched every face do what his own had done thirty seconds earlier.

[ Fernwick
Beginner town · The Wending Fen
Travellers here now: 64 ]

The woman with the spear said something sharp and fast, and he heard it in English a heartbeat later, close behind the real words like a shadow, with her accent still on it, heavy and Brazilian and annoyed.

“It’s a swamp.”

“It’s a swamp,” somebody else agreed, in Polish, in English.

“No, no, no.” That was a big man in a linen shirt, with a sword over his shoulder and a little glowing orb floating behind him at head height, the camera of somebody streaming in Follow. He was talking to the orb. “Chat, we got the swamp. Chat, we got the swamp town. It’s over. Is there a reroll? There’s got to be a reroll.”

There wasn’t. Ice knew there wasn’t, because Deston had sent him the paragraph in September with three exclamation marks on it. You got what you got. The roads out to the bigger towns opened at level ten, and not before.

An old man in a waxed coat was sitting on a crate at the edge of the platform, gutting an eel into a bucket with great care. He was clearly not one of the arrivals. The big streamer leaned over him.

“Hey. Hey, NPC. How do I leave?”

The old man looked up at him. He looked at him for a long, slow moment, the way you look at a dog that has walked into the kitchen.

“Same way as anybody,” he said. “Get good enough to walk out.” Then he went back to his eel.

The streamer turned to his orb. “Chat, the NPC just roasted me.”

Ice didn’t look at the eel man for long, and he didn’t look at the reeds. He looked at the ground.

Partly because Xav had told him to. Mostly because in every game he’d ever played, the first morning was always the same: everyone ran for the first monster they could see, and nobody looked down, and three days later everybody was paying a fortune for whatever had been lying at their feet the whole time.

Between the planks of the walkways, where the boards had warped apart, little plants grew out of the wet. Low, dark leaves with a red vein down the middle, and a tiny cluster of white flowers. They were everywhere. People were walking on them. The boy with the hatchet stepped straight on a clump of them as he went past.

Ice crouched. When he looked at the leaves properly, a window opened over them.

[ Fenwort · Common herb
A bitter leaf that grows where the water is slow. ]

That was all. No price, no recipe, no use. Common.

He looked along the walkway. Fenwort, and fenwort, and something with a blue stalk that the game wouldn’t name for him yet, and fenwort again, crushed under somebody’s heel. He thought about how many healing potions sixty-four people were going to want by tonight, and about how many of them were going to know where to find what you made them from.

“Chat,” he said. “Look at this. Look at the ground. That’s a herb. Everybody’s standing on it.”

He stood up and looked at the platform with new eyes. People were already drifting away from it in twos and threes, heading out along the walkways into the mist, weapons up, looking for something to hit. The big streamer was gone. The Brazilian woman had found a sign that said MILITIA YARD, with an arrow, and had looked at it and walked the other way.

Ice went toward the arrow.

The militia yard was a square of packed earth on a wide platform at the edge of town, ringed with a fence of split poles. There were two straw dummies on posts, and a rack of wooden practice swords, and a water barrel with a dipper. A man sat on an upturned bucket by one of the dummies, sewing up its stomach with a needle the size of a fork.

He was about fifty, with a short grey beard and a face that had been outside most of its life. The two smallest fingers of his left hand were missing at the knuckle, old wounds, long healed. He held the needle with the three that were left. He didn’t look up.

“You’re one of the new ones,” he said.

“I am.” Ice stopped at the fence. “Is it all right if I come in?”

The man did look up then. He looked at Ice for a moment, then at the sword, then at the shield on his arm, then back at his face.

“That’s the first time anyone’s asked,” he said. “Come in.”

Ice ducked through the gap in the fence. “I’m Ice.”

“I know. It’s over your head.” The man pointed with the needle, vaguely, at the air above Ice’s hair, where presumably everybody’s name floated. “Hollis. I teach the militia, when there’s a militia. Mostly there’s me and these two.” He patted the dummy, which sagged. “Your lot came past this morning, a few of you. Big fellow with the little light. Asked me where the button was.”

“What did you tell him?”

“Told him there wasn’t one.” Hollis tied off his thread and bit it. “There’s a way to hold a sword. There’s a way to put your feet. There’s a way to bring it down so the whole of you is behind it and not just your arm. And if you do it right long enough, it starts to do itself. That’s all I know. He said thanks very much and went to get bitten by rats.” He put the needle away in a little leather roll. “You want the button too?”

“No,” said Ice. “I want the way to hold it.”

Hollis looked at him for a long moment, the same long look the eel man had given the streamer, but it came out the other side as something different. He stood up. He was shorter than Ice had thought, and broader, and he moved like somebody whose knees had opinions about the weather.

“Put the shield down,” he said. “You won’t need it yet. Stand there. Feet like this.”

He showed him. Left foot forward, pointing at the dummy. Right foot back, turned out, heel up. Knees soft. Weight in the middle, not on the toes, not on the heels. Ice copied it. Hollis walked round him once, in a slow circle, and put two fingers of his good hand against the small of Ice’s back and pushed, very gently, and Ice nearly fell over.

“There,” said Hollis. “That’s where you are. Too far forward. You’re leaning at the thing you want to hit. Everyone does. Sit back.”

He sat back. Hollis pushed again. This time he stayed where he was, and the push went down through him into the boards.

“Better. Now the sword. Up, by your right ear. Point back over your shoulder. Not like that, like you’re holding a cup you don’t want to spill. Elbow down.” Hollis stepped away. “Form One. It’s not clever. You take one step in, and you bring it down from your right shoulder across to your left hip, and you let your back foot come round behind you so the turn of your hips does the work. Then you come back to where you started. That’s all it is. Every man in every militia in the world learned that one first.” He gestured at the dummy. “Go on.”

Ice stepped in, and cut.

The sword came down from his shoulder, and his feet did something, and his hips did something else, and the blade hit the dummy’s shoulder with a dull flat thup and stopped. The impact went back up his arm into his elbow, a real jolt, with a little sting in it from the fifty per cent. He felt it in the bones of his wrist.

A window opened in front of the dummy, small and plain.

[ Sword Form I: Falling Cut
Grade: 12%. The blade arrived late; your weight was not behind it. ]

“Twelve,” Ice read out.

“Was it?” said Hollis, who couldn’t see the window and didn’t care. “Felt like about that. Your arm did it all, and your feet were watching. Back to the start.”

Ice went back to the start. Left foot forward. Sit back. Sword by his ear like a cup.

Out in the fen, far off, something screamed, and a man’s voice swore in a language that came to Ice in English a moment later. Somebody had found the rats.

“Again,” said Hollis.
