Live from Elsewhere · Volume 6: There's a Chair

Chapter 143

Six Weeks

4,249 words · 18 min read

Xola listened to the whole thing with the trident still in her hand. Afterwards that was the bit she felt stupid about. She’d stood on the east bridge of Brackenford in the dark for twenty minutes, under the torches, holding a fishing spear like somebody might come round the corner and need stabbing.

Nobody came round the corner. It was just Thea, talking.

Thea told it in order, because Thea always told things in order. It was one of the things Xola loved about her and one of the things that drove her up the wall. You’d say what happened and Thea would start at the beginning, and you’d be standing there going yes but what HAPPENED, and Thea would say I’m getting to it. And she would. Eventually. In order.

So: May. A Friday in May, at Millrace, after the crayfish, after be a wheel. Higu had rung, from the roof of the dome.

“She knows you’re in,” said Thea, looking at the river. “That’s what Higu said. She knows you’re in. She went very quiet. And then she asked if I was all right.”

Xola’s grip changed on the trident.

“In May,” she said.

“In May.”

“You didn’t—“ Xola stopped. “Okay. Okay. Go on.”

June. Higu again, from the roof again. A message this time, not a question. Tell her I’m glad she’s at the front. Don’t tell her anything else. Not yet.

“And I sent one back,” said Thea. “Through Higu. I said—“ She stopped. She looked down at her hands on the parapet. “I’d seen her. At the founding. On Kei’s stream, the night they made the guild. The thing came through the door and she laid thorn all the way across the counter so it couldn’t get over. She stood behind it with her staff. She held it.” Thea’s voice was very level. “So I said, tell her I saw her hold the counter. Tell her it was brave.”

Xola didn’t say anything. She couldn’t, quite.

“Not yet,” said Thea. “I said that too. Not yet.”

And then tonight. The chair. Six chairs by a window at the end of a long table. An old woman Xola had never met who swept under chairs nobody sat in. Gigi in a doorway with a cup of tea in each hand. Tell her there’s a chair. That’s all. And I looked at it. For me, that’s the same.

Thea finished. She’d got to the end, in order, and she stopped. The river went on going under the bridge, very loud in the quiet.

“I should have told you,” said Thea. “In May. I’m sorry.”

Xola found she’d sat down. She hadn’t decided to. She was sitting on the stone bench that ran along the inside of the parapet, with the trident across her knees. Her gills were doing the thing they did, wide and fluttering, and she didn’t care.

“Six weeks,” she said.

“Nine, if you count May.”

“I’m not counting May. May was a phone call. May was she asked if you were all right.” Xola heard her own voice go up. She let it. “June was a message. June was you sending one back. That’s—Thea. That’s a conversation. You’ve been having a conversation with her. For six weeks. Through Higu. And I’ve been—“ She waved the trident at the town behind them, the lodging-house, the Sallows, all of it. “I’ve been right here. I’ve been next to you. Every day. I sleep in the room next to yours.”

“I know.”

“Every day, Thea.”

“I know.” Thea sat down too, on the other end of the bench. She didn’t come closer. She knew better. “I told Higu not to tell you.”

That was the worst bit. Xola had sort of known it was coming, from the way Thea had told it, and it was still the worst bit.

“Why,” she said. It didn’t come out as a question.

Thea was quiet for a moment.

“Because you were on the phone,” said Thea. “For all of it. Every night. You were on the phone every single night until I ended it, and the morning after. I know what that cost you. I know you never said. You’d never say.” She looked at Xola. “I wanted you to have a rest. From her. From any of it. You’ve got gills now. You laugh in the river. I didn’t want to bring her into the river.”

“A rest,” said Xola.

“Yes.”

“From you?”

Thea flinched.

“Not from me,” said Thea. “From—“

“It’s the same thing.” Xola stood up again. She couldn’t sit. “It was never her I was on the phone with. It was you. I didn’t do it because I liked hearing about her. I hated hearing about her. I did it because it was you, and you were—“ She stopped. She made herself breathe. In through the mouth, out through the gills, which was a thing she could do now and still didn’t fully understand. “You don’t get to give me a rest from you. That’s not yours to give. I don’t want a rest from you. I’ve never wanted a rest from you. Not once. Not on the worst night. Not on the nights you didn’t say anything at all and I just stayed on.”

Thea had her hand over her mouth.

“I know,” said Thea, through it. “I know. I worked that out. Tonight. On the bridge. That’s why I messaged you.”

“Tonight.”

“I’m slow,” said Thea. “You know I’m slow.”

“You’re not slow, you’re stupid,” said Xola, and her voice cracked right down the middle of the word, and she was crying. Which was annoying, because she’d been angry, and she’d wanted to stay angry, and now she was doing both at once on a bridge. “You’re so stupid, Thea. You kept a whole—a whole thing, for six weeks, so I wouldn’t have to carry it. And I’d already been carrying it. I’ve been carrying it since the first day. I watch you. Do you know that? I watch you all the time. I watch you when we walk. I watch you when you go quiet on stream. I’ve got so good at watching you I don’t even know I’m doing it. And all that time there was a thing, and I didn’t know what it was, so I was just watching nothing. I was carrying a thing in the dark.”

Thea took her hand away from her mouth.

“I’m sorry,” she said again. And then, because she was Thea: “You’re right. You’re completely right. It wasn’t mine to decide.”

“Thank you.”

“I’m still glad I didn’t tell you in May.”

“THEA.”

“Because in May I didn’t know what I thought,” said Thea. “In May I’d have told you and you’d have looked at my face to see what I thought, and I wouldn’t have had anything there. And you’d have filled it in. You’d have filled it in with what you thought. And I’d have let you, because it would have been easier.” She held Xola’s eyes. “I needed six weeks to find out what I thought. I’m sorry it was six weeks of you not knowing. But I needed them.”

Xola stood there with the trident and her wet face and her gills going.

She hated that. She hated it because it was true. Thea had always let her fill in the gaps. In the bad year Xola had got so used to filling them in that she’d sometimes said what Thea felt before Thea had finished feeling it. Sometimes she’d been right. Sometimes, she thought now, she hadn’t, and Thea had nodded anyway.

“Fine,” said Xola. “Fine. That bit’s fair.”

“Thank you.”

“The rest of it’s not fair.”

“No.”

“And now her,” said Xola.

And that was where it went, the anger. It had been going at Thea, and it had been the wrong place for it. Xola had known that even while she was shouting, the way you know a pan’s the wrong size while you’re still pouring into it. Now it found the right place. It went there all at once, hot and clean.

“She doesn’t get to set out a chair,” said Xola.

“She didn’t set it out. Ama Corrie set it out.”

“She doesn’t get to look at one either.” Xola was walking now, up and down the bridge, three torches one way and three back. “She doesn’t get to stand in a doorway with tea and look sad at some furniture, and then send you a message through Higu like it’s a—like it’s a gift. There’s a chair. That’s all. That’s all? After all of it? After every single night?”

Thea didn’t say anything.

Xola didn’t remember the details any more. That was the strange thing. She’d been on the phone for all of it and she couldn’t remember most of what had been said. What she remembered was how Thea had sounded. The way her voice had gone smaller, month by month, like something being folded up to fit into a drawer. The way Xola had learned to hear a bad day in the very first word. Not even the word. The breath before the word. Hi could mean nine different things, and Xola had learned all nine. And she remembered that it had never mattered what Thea did. That was the thing Xola had never been able to get past. Thea would do it one way and it would be wrong. Thea would do it the other way and it would be wrong. Thea would do nothing at all and that would be the most wrong of anything. And Thea would ring Xola at night and try to work out, out loud, what the right way would have been. Xola had sat on the other end of the phone thinking, there isn’t one, there isn’t a right way, she makes sure there isn’t. And she’d never said it. Because she’d been afraid that if she said it, Thea would stop ringing.

“She made you think it was you,” said Xola. “Every time. Every time. Everything you did. And you believed her, Thea. You believed her for so long. I watched you believe her.”

“I know,” said Thea. Very quietly. “I was there.”

“I was there too.”

“I know you were.” Thea looked up at her from the bench. “That’s why I’m telling you. Because you were there too.”

Xola stopped walking.

“And now she wants you to come,” she said. “To her town. To her Hall. To sit in a chair at the end of her table.”

“It’s not her table. It’s Kei’s.”

“She’s at it.”

“She is,” said Thea. “And Higu. And Drew. And Ice.” She paused. “And Ice is at it, Xo.”

Xola didn’t have anything to say to that, so she didn’t say anything.

“I’m not going because of her,” said Thea. “I haven’t decided I’m going at all. I’m just telling you. That’s all I’m doing tonight. I’m telling you everything, the way I should have done in May. And then you can be angry. You can be as angry as you like. You can be angry at her and at me. I’ll sit here.”

“I need to be in the water,” said Xola.

“Okay.”

“I’m not running away. I just need to be in the water.”

“I know,” said Thea. “Go on.”

She went down the steps at the end of the bridge, the ones the bargemen used, to the stone ledge at the waterline. She didn’t stop to think. She just went in, boots and all, trident and all, the way she’d gone into the mill pond at Millrace on the first morning. Dorrit had shouted at her from the sluice gates, and she’d come up laughing with gills she hadn’t known she had.

The river closed over her head.

It was black down here at night. Black and loud and cold, and pulling, always pulling, east toward the sea. Toward a round town she’d never seen. Her gills opened and took the water in and gave it back, and the noise of everything above went away. Torches, bridge, Thea, all of it.

She let the river take her a little way. Then she put the trident’s butt into the riverbed and held on, and hung there in the current like a flag, and was furious.

She was furious for a long time. She let herself be. She was angry at Gigi in a way she’d never been allowed to be angry before, because before there’d always been Thea on the phone, and you couldn’t be angry when somebody needed you to be calm. She was angry at the doorway and the tea and the chair. She was angry at the word that’s all. She was angry at every single night of the bad year, all of them at once, all of them coming up out of wherever she’d put them. She was angry that Higu had known before her. She was angry that Thea had been brave without her. That one surprised her. She looked at it for a bit, hanging in the black.

She was shouting, she realised. Not out loud. You couldn’t shout underwater; it just came out as bubbles. But in her head she was shouting at the water. She was shouting at it to stop pulling, to go the other way, to give her back the year.

Don’t watch the fish, Dorrit had said. She’d stood on the sluice gate at Millrace on the first day, a small old woman made of weather, watching Xola thrash about in the mill pond after a pike. Watch the water. And stop shouting at it. Water doesn’t care if you shout. Water goes where it goes. You find out where it’s going and you go with it, and then you’re the one that’s there when the fish arrives.

Xola hung in the current and stopped shouting.

It took a while. When she’d stopped, the anger was still there. It hadn’t gone anywhere. But she could see round it now, a bit. She could see what it was sitting on top of.

It was sitting on fear.

She was frightened. That was what it was, under all the noise. Not of Gigi. Gigi was a woman with a hawthorn staff on the other side of the world, in a game. You weren’t frightened of somebody like that. Xola was frightened of the phone. Of it ringing late again, and Thea not saying anything at all. Of hearing hi and knowing it was the eighth kind. Of watching Thea’s voice get folded up small again, month by month, and not being able to stop it. Of not being enough. She’d never been enough, the first time. She’d stayed on the phone every single night and it hadn’t been enough. It had been Thea in the end, on her own, who’d ended it. Xola had just been the one who heard about it afterwards.

That was what was under the anger. It had been there the whole time. She’d been shouting so she wouldn’t have to look at it.

She let go of the riverbed and let the water take her, and came up.

Slep was sitting on the ledge at the bottom of the bargemen’s steps, with his glaive across his knees and his feet nearly in the water.

He hadn’t been there when she went in. She was certain of that. He’d said he’d take the other side of the door, and he had. He’d have stood outside the lodging-house on Tanner’s Row with his arms folded for as long as it took. Then at some point he’d decided the door was the wrong side, and come down to the river. And he’d sat on the wet stone at the bottom of the steps and waited, the way he’d waited for her on a weir in April. The way he’d walked three and a half days across a game he’d only just woken up in, to get to her side.

She hung in the black water a few feet out and looked at him.

“You said not to come,” said Slep. “To the bridge. So I didn’t come to the bridge.”

“This is under the bridge.”

“Yeah,” said Slep. “Different place.”

She laughed. She didn’t mean to. It came out of her gills more than her mouth and went across the water in bubbles.

She swam in and put her arms on the ledge beside his feet and rested her chin on them. He didn’t say anything. He was very good at not saying anything. It was the first thing she’d liked about him, years ago, before she knew there’d be other things.

“Did you hear?” she said.

“Some of it,” said Slep. “You were loud. The bit about the chair.”

“She’s going to want to go.”

“Yeah.”

“She says she hasn’t decided. She’s decided. She decided on the bed, the second Higu said chair. She just doesn’t know it yet.” Xola stared at the black water. “She’s going to go to that town. And Gigi’s going to be there. At the table. And I’m going to have to stand there and watch.”

Slep nodded slowly.

“Which side am I on?” said Xola.

She hadn’t meant to ask it. It came out of her the way questions came out of her when she was tired, sideways and too honest.

Slep thought about it. He always did. That was the second thing she’d liked.

“Hers,” he said. “Always. You’ve only ever been on one side.”

“Then what’s the other side?”

Slep looked at her for a moment.

“The bit where she goes anyway,” he said, “and you’re scared.”

Xola closed her eyes.

“Yeah,” she said.

“You can’t be on that side,” said Slep. “You’re already on hers. So somebody else has to take that one.” He shifted the glaive on his knees. “That’s what I’m for. I’ll take the scared side. You take hers.”

“That’s not how it works.”

“It’s how it works for me,” said Slep. “It’s my class. One who takes the other side. The game said so. I’ve got it in writing.”

She laughed again, properly this time, out of her mouth. Then she cried a bit more, with her face on her arms on the wet stone. Slep put his hand on the back of her head, between her frills, and left it there. He didn’t say anything else. He didn’t need to.

When she came back up the steps it was nearly the first dawn, grey at the edges, and the torches on the bridge were burning low.

Thea was asleep on the stone bench.

She’d waited. Of course she’d waited; she’d said she’d sit there, and Thea did the things she said. She’d fallen asleep sitting up, with her head against the parapet and her arms folded, and her tower shield on the ground at her feet. Somebody had put a blanket over her. Xola looked at it. It was the blanket off PenguChi’s bed. There were three neat little stitches in one corner where Chi had mended it on the first day.

Xola sat down on the bench beside her, wet through, and didn’t wake her.

She sat there and watched the sky go grey, then white, then pink over the east road. East, where the round town was, eight days away. She’d checked the map for it on the first morning too. She’d done it before Thea had, as it happened, while Thea was still staring at her own hands. Xola had found the round town and measured the distance with her thumb. She’d thought, good, it’s far. She’d never told Thea that either.

Thea woke up when the sun came over the roofs. She woke the way she always did, all at once, already apologising.

“Sorry, I—“

“Don’t,” said Xola.

Thea looked at her. At the wet clothes and the gills and the face.

“You went in the river,” said Thea.

“I went in the river.”

“Did it help?”

“Yes,” said Xola. “No. A bit.” She pulled the end of Chi’s blanket over her own knees, because she was freezing. “I’m still angry.”

“I know.”

“Not at you. Well. A bit at you.”

“That’s fair.”

“Mostly at her. Mostly at—“ Xola stopped. She didn’t know how to say the next bit, so she just said it. “Mostly at the year. At all of it. At me, for not being able to do anything. I was on the phone every night and I couldn’t do anything, Thea.”

“You did everything,” said Thea.

“I didn’t stop it.”

“I stopped it,” said Thea. “In the end. Me. And I could only stop it because you’d held me up long enough that I was still standing when I did.” She said it plainly, the way she said things she’d thought about for a long time. “You didn’t stop it. You kept me alive long enough to stop it myself. That’s not nothing, Xo. That’s the whole thing.”

Xola looked at the river for a long time.

“Tell me again,” she said. “The bit about the chair.”

So Thea told her again. The old woman. The store under the stair. Six chairs, near the table, not at it. Gigi in the doorway with two cups. I looked at it. For me, that’s the same.

Xola listened to it differently this time. She’d heard it the first time with her ears full of the bad year. Now she heard it the way you hear a thing when you’ve stopped shouting at the water.

“She looked at it,” she said slowly.

“Yes.”

Xola knew what that meant too. Better, maybe, than Higu did. She’d been on the other end of the phone for every single night when Gigi didn’t look at things.

“I’m not saying anything nice about her,” said Xola.

“You don’t have to.”

“I’m not ever saying anything nice about her.”

“Okay.”

They sat on the bench. A barge came under the bridge below them, loaded with willow-antler and bucks’ hides, and the bargeman waved, and Thea waved back.

“If you go,” said Xola, “I’m going.”

“I know.”

“Obviously. I’m not letting you walk into that town on your own. I’m not letting you sit in a chair on your own. I’m going to be in the chair next to you. I’m going to be so close to you you can’t lean anywhere without leaning on me.” She sniffed hard. “And if she says one wrong thing to you, one, I’m putting a trident through her foot.”

Thea laughed. It came out of her loud and surprised, and a woman opening the shutters of a bakery at the end of the bridge looked round.

“That’s not helping,” said Thea.

“It’s helping me,” said Xola, and leaned on her, wet and cold, until Thea leaned back.

She streamed that night. She’d nearly not; she’d sat on the end of her real bed at home with the egg’s lid up and her hands in her lap for ten minutes, deciding. But the schedule was on the wall, and she’d promised herself she’d keep it. She went in.

She was quiet. She knew she was. She took the Sallows with Thea and Slep and Chi and the others, and she didn’t shout watch the water once, and her chat noticed in about four minutes.

“Thank you,” said Xola. She meant it. She watched the hugs go up the chat, dozens of them, from people she’d never met. She thought about the year when there’d been nobody in her chat at all, because she’d stopped streaming and stopped doing anything she liked. She’d spent every night on the phone instead, carrying a thing.

And then a buck came out of the willows at Thea. Xola watched the water instead of the buck, and was there when it arrived.

“Don’t watch the fish,” she said, quite quietly, mostly to herself.
