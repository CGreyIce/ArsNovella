Never Played

3,710 words · 16 min read

Bee had never played one of these in her life, and she wasn’t going to start pretending now.

She’d made that clear to Hyvred on Monday. She’d made it clear again on Wednesday, when Hyvred sent her a forty-minute video of some man in a swamp explaining feet. She’d watched four seconds of it, and then sent back: I’m not watching a man explain feet.

he’s really good though, Hyvred had written.

I’m sure he is. I’m going to work it out myself.

bee you’ve never played an mmo

Then I’ll be the first person in history to play one without being told what to do by a man in a swamp.

And that had been that. Hyvred had sent three crying-laughing faces and a heart, and Bee had gone back to what she’d been drawing, which had nothing to do with any of it.

She’d bought the pod because Hyvred was buying one. That was the honest answer, and she gave it to anyone who asked. Hyv had wanted it so badly, ever since that thread about the eyes, and Bee wasn’t going to let her go in alone. Also, there was a lot of noise about it among the people she streamed for. They wanted to see her model move. Not the stiff little rig on a desk, nodding, but her: walking and climbing and doing things. The wings. Everybody wanted to see the wings.

So, two reasons. One for Hyv and one for content. She was fine with both.

At ten past ten on Friday night, ten minutes after Hyvred had gone quiet on the call and then started crying about a ceiling, Bee lay down in the warm grey gel and pressed her finger on the glass.

“I’m going in,” she said to her phone, which was propped on the shelf by her bed. “Don’t cry at me when I’m in there. I won’t be able to hand you a tissue.”

From Singapore, on the call, through what sounded like a great deal of crying and laughing at the same time, Hyvred said, “I can see the corners, Bee.”

Bee lay in the pod in the dark for a second and didn’t say anything at all.

Then she said, “Good,” in a voice she didn’t use very often, and closed her eyes.

The room was wood and rain, and she looked at all of it and didn’t think much of it.

It was nice. It was very real. The air was cold on her face and smelled of wet timber, and her hands were her hands, and she could feel the floor through her feet. She stood there and noted it, the way she’d note a reference photo: wood, rain, good light from the window, cool. She didn’t cry. She didn’t see what there was to cry about. She was very glad Hyv was crying, on the other side of the world, about corners. That was the right thing to cry about. This was just a room.

[ What are you called? ]

“Bee,” said Bee.

She was fully expecting taken. It was a three-letter word. Somebody somewhere in the world had been sitting on it since Monday, she was sure, probably a man who sold it on a forum for a hundred dollars.

“Because it was waiting for me,” said Bee, and pressed it.

The mirror was better.

She’d built her model in the pre-launch creator over two nights, the same way she built anything, carefully and properly, checking every proportion against her art. And there it was in the glass, standing up and breathing. Long black hair with gold through it, up in two high tails with yellow ribbons. The honeycomb clip. Amber-gold eyes. Slightly pointed ears. The antennae, two of them, thin and curved, tipped with little gold beads, and they moved. When she turned her head, they swung. When she frowned, they went back flat against her hair, like a cat’s ears. She hadn’t even known she’d want that.

And behind her, folded down her back, the wings.

Big, translucent, veined, with a faint gold shimmer where the light came through them from the window. She’d drawn them a hundred times. She’d always drawn them a bit bigger than they’d need to be, because she liked them big.

She tried them.

She didn’t know how, exactly; she’d never had wings. She tried the thing she’d imagined, the thing you’d do with your shoulder blades if they were longer. They moved. Both wings lifted, and opened, and with a sudden furious buzzing hum like a wasp trapped against glass, they beat. They beat so fast they blurred, and the air in the little room moved, and the rain on the window shook.

Her feet didn’t leave the floor at all.

“We’ll see,” said Bee, to the window.

She folded the wings down. They went with a little rattle, like a fan closing.

The pain slider she left at twenty. Hyv had told her about a man in a swamp who’d put his on fifty. Bee had said why, and Hyv had said he says tanks need to know where they got hit, and Bee had said I’m not a tank, I’m a person with a pencil, and that was the end of that.

At the rack, she stood and looked at every single thing on it, one at a time, with her antennae flat.

Sword, shield, spear, hatchet, hammer, daggers, bow, staff, wand. And a book. A plain book, bound shut with a strap, nothing on the cover.

“Where’s the pencil?” she said.

[ Take what you mean to carry. What you carry, and how you carry it, will shape what you become. ]

“I mean to carry a pencil.”

The rack did not produce a pencil.

She picked up the book and unbuckled the strap and opened it. The pages were thick and creamy and rough at the edges. Blank. Every single one. Good paper. Better paper than she used for sketching at home, honestly, a little bit of tooth to it, the kind that would take charcoal beautifully.

“That’s paper,” said Bee. “I can work with paper.”

She tucked it under her arm and opened the door.

It was night, and it was wind, and it was purple.

She’d come out onto a high bare moor under a sky full of stars and a big low yellow moon. Heather everywhere, waist-high and dark purple even in the moonlight, going away in every direction over the long rolling backs of hills. Dry-stone walls, grey and humped, wandering across it all, holding nothing in. A few stone cottages with their backs to the wind and their windows lit yellow. A low inn with a crooked chimney and a sign that swung and creaked and said, in old peeling paint, THE DROWSY FOX. Wind, cold and clean, smelling of honey and peat and frost.

[ Heatherbrook
Beginner town · The High Moor

Somewhere close, under all that wind, there was a hum. Low, steady, everywhere at once, coming out of the stone walls. It took her a second to place it.

Bees.

Wild bees, thousands of them, living in the gaps between the stones of the walls, humming in their sleep.

“Oh, come on,” said Bee, and for the first time that night, she laughed.

She didn’t go and fight anything. She didn’t know you were supposed to. She went and found something to draw with.

That took about four minutes. There was a cold hearth outside the inn, a ring of stones where somebody had burned something earlier, and in the ashes there were bits of black wood. She picked out a long thin piece, burned through but solid, and tested it on a flat stone. It left a good dark line. Charcoal. Willow, maybe, or something like it. It would do.

She sat down on the low wall outside the inn with her back to the wind, under the creaking sign, and opened the book on her knee and drew.

She didn’t think about it. She drew what was in front of her, the way she always did when she was somewhere new. It was how she understood a place. The sign: the fox, curled up, with its nose under its tail, peeling. The crooked chimney. A cat on the inn’s windowsill, watching her. The line of the moor going away behind it, and the moon. Twenty minutes, maybe. The charcoal was soft and dark and smudged where she wanted it to.

Nothing happened. It was a drawing in a book.

“So it’s a sketchbook,” she said to her chat. “Fine. That’s fine. I wanted a sketchbook.”

“That’s not a sketchbook.”

She looked up.

A woman was standing at the door of the inn with a pot of paint in one hand and a brush in the other, looking down at Bee’s drawing of the sign over Bee’s shoulder. She was small and wiry and somewhere around fifty, with grey hair tied up in a cloth and paint on both forearms, every colour, years of it. She’d obviously come out to do something to the sign and found someone drawing it first.

“It’s a book of making,” the woman said. “My grandmother had one. Hasn’t been one in Heatherbrook since she died.” She looked at the drawing again. “That’s a good fox.”

“I know,” said Bee.

The woman looked at her properly. Bee watched her face go through surprise, and then something like amusement, and then, quite clearly, the decision that here was somebody who also said what they thought.

“Ambry,” she said. “I paint the signs. And you’ve drawn mine better than I did.”

“Your paint’s peeling,” said Bee. “I drew the peeling.”

“So you did.” Ambry put down her pot. “Well. If you want that book to do what it’s for, you have to stop drawing what’s there.”

It took Ambry about a minute to explain, and Bee most of the night to understand.

A book of making didn’t care what you saw. It cared what you meant. If you drew a fox because the fox was there, that was a picture. If you drew a fox because you needed a fox, and you drew it true and drew it whole, every line sure, and nothing left out that a fox needed to be a fox, and you said aloud what it was for—

“It’ll be a fox,” said Ambry. “For a breath or two. No more. My grandmother could make a door for long enough to walk through it. She told me once, when she was young, she’d made a ladder that held a man’s weight all the way up the side of the church.” She shrugged. “Then she got old and used it for paper. Shopping lists.”

“Draw it true,” said Bee. “Draw it whole. Say what it’s for.”

“That’s it.”

“That’s all?”

“That’s all,” said Ambry, “and nobody can do it.”

Bee turned to a fresh page.

She thought about what she’d need, if she’d never done this before and something came at her in the dark. She thought: something between me and it. She drew a shield. A round one, simple. The rim, the boss in the middle, the grain of the wood across it in long lines, the iron studs round the edge. She drew it quickly, because she was good. She knew what a shield looked like from every angle, she’d drawn a thousand of them. And when it was finished she put her hand flat on the page next to it and said, quite clearly, feeling stupid:

“To stand in front of me.”

The drawing lifted off the page.

It came up out of the paper like steam off a cup. The charcoal lines rose into the air in front of her and hung there, glowing faintly gold, the size of a real shield, every line of it, the grain and the studs and the rim. She could see through it. It hung there for about three seconds. Then it came apart into gold dust and drifted away on the wind.

Ambry made a small sound.

Bee didn’t. Bee was staring at the window.

“Thirty-four?” she said. “That was a good shield.”

“It was a good picture of a shield,” said Ambry. “The book says you drew the grain before you drew the shape. A shield’s a shape first. The grain’s what it’s made of. It doesn’t care what it’s made of when there’s a boar coming.”

Bee looked at the page. It was blank again where the shield had been, as if it had never been there. She looked at the next page. Then she drew another shield. This time she did the shape first: one circle, in one line, without lifting the charcoal. Then the boss. Then the rim. The grain last, quickly, almost an afterthought.

“To stand in front of me.”

It hung there for four seconds this time, and it was brighter.

“The game’s a decent art teacher,” said Bee grudgingly. “It’s right. I did.”

She was attacked by a pig at about half past midnight, her time, and found out three things.

The pig was a small bristly black thing, about knee height, with tusks. It came out of the heather at her with its head down, grunting, while she was halfway through a drawing of the moon.

The first thing she found out was that drawing takes time, and pigs don’t wait.

She was nine lines into a shield when it hit her in the shin. Even at twenty per cent, it felt like being kicked by a very angry child in hard shoes. She dropped the charcoal. The pig turned round in a tight circle and came back.

The second thing she found out was that she was fast when she was angry.

She snatched the charcoal out of the heather and drew a wall. Not a shield, there wasn’t time for a shield. A wall: four lines, a rectangle, and three spikes along the top, about four seconds’ work, the worst drawing she’d done since she was nine.

“To stop it!”

The wall came up off the page, low and gold and ugly and there. The pig ran straight into it at full tilt. There was a satisfying thud, and the pig sat down in the heather looking deeply confused.

“I’m not a support class,” said Bee, hitting the pig with the book again. “I don’t even know what that means.”

On her phone, on the shelf in her real room, the call was still open. Hyvred, who’d stopped crying some time ago and had been happily shooting at crabs in Singapore, said: “Bee? What’s happening? Why do you sound like that?”

“I’m hitting a pig with a book.”

“Why?”

“Because I can’t hurt it. I can only draw walls. Hyv, what’s a support class?”

There was a pause in Singapore.

“Oh, Bee,” said Hyvred, with great love. “Oh, Bee, no.”

It took her eleven minutes to kill the pig. She worked it out herself, as she’d said she would. She was proud of that.

Hyv tried to tell her. Hyv said things like kite it and you need a damage skill and Ice would say, and Bee said “I don’t care what Ice would say, I don’t know who Ice is,” and Hyv said “he’s Maigo’s friend, the swamp guy,” and Bee said “Never heard of him,” and Hyv said “he once apologised to me for a joke for about ten minutes straight,” and Bee said “Good for him,” and hung up on her for a minute so she could think.

What she worked out was this. The book made whatever she drew, for as long as it lasted. It didn’t care if it was a shield or a wall. And a wall with spikes on top was just a wall. But a spike on its own, if she drew it pointing at something, and drew it true and whole, and said what it was for—

She drew a sting.

Long and thin and sharp. A bee’s sting, because of course it was. Six lines, very fast, with a curve at the back where it would have come out of the bee. She drew it pointing straight across the page at the pig, which was standing in the heather three metres away, glaring.

“To go there.”

The sting came off the page and went there. Straight through the air, gold and fast, and into the pig’s side. The pig squealed.

“The swamp guy can take as long as he likes,” said Bee. “I’ve got a book.”

Before she logged out, at about one in the morning, she did one more thing.

The bees in the walls had been humming all night under the wind. She’d been hearing them the whole time she drew. On her way back to the inn she stopped at the wall by the door, and crouched, and put her ear against the cold stones, and listened. Thousands of them in there, in the dark, humming in their sleep.

An old man came out of the cottage next door in a nightshirt, with a lantern, and looked at her.

“They don’t like being listened to,” he said.

“I’m one of them,” said Bee, and turned her head so he could see the antennae.

The old man looked at the antennae for a long time. Then at the wings folded down her back. Then at the book under her arm.

“Coombe,” he said eventually. “I keep the skeps. Up the hill. The tame ones.” He considered. “You can come up in the morning, if you like. They might not sting you.”

“They will,” said Bee.

“They might not,” said Coombe.

She thought about it. She thought about farming, and honey, and the wall full of humming, and about how she’d never once in her life been asked if she wanted to keep bees by a man in a nightshirt at one in the morning.

“In the morning,” she said.

She logged out at ten past one. She lay in the gel in her room in Australia with the lid lifting and her real legs feeling like somebody else’s, and picked up her phone.
