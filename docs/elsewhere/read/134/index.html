Live from Elsewhere · Volume 5: Ask Me Again

Chapter 134

First Through the Door

1,570 words · 7 min read

Xav had waited at the inn by the east bridge in Brackenford once before.

Nine days, in January, at the window, for eight people walking from eight towns. He’d drawn the room by the third day and the bridge by the fifth and every face that crossed it by the ninth. He’d told everybody he wasn’t waiting. Ice had said, on a call, you’re waiting. Xav had said I’m drawing. Ice had said you’re drawing the bridge. Which was, Xav had to admit, the same thing.

On Thursday he was at the same window, drawing the same bridge, when two men came over it from the west with packs on.

He put the pencil down.

He’d known Kei would come. He’d known since Monday morning, since the notice. He’d read it in Japan with his breakfast going cold, and gone very still, and then laughed. A hall for ten answers only to a banner. He’d built Riverbank from a blank charter and eight people walking. He’d said, in January, at this window, to Kei, on the steps of the Hall, ask me when it’s big. I’ll be first through the door. He’d known exactly what that notice meant before he’d finished reading it.

He’d also known Kei wouldn’t send a message.

Kei didn’t do things by message that mattered. Kei drew maps. He walked to people. He’d said once, on a call, that if you wanted somebody to stand next to you, the least you could do was go and stand next to them first.

So Xav had told his eight to stay near Brackenford this week. He’d booked the room by the bridge. And he’d sat at the window drawing, and not waiting, since Tuesday.

They came up the stairs together. Kei first, with his map under his arm and his green hair tie coming loose, the way it always was. Drew behind him, easy, hands in pockets, looking at the room the way Drew looked at everything, as if he’d already found the good chair.

Xav was standing by the window. He’d been sitting. He’d stood up when he heard the stairs.

“Hi,” said Kei.

“Hi.”

“I’ve come to ask you something.”

“I know,” said Xav.

Kei looked at him. Then at the drawing on the table by the window. The east bridge, and two men with packs crossing it from the west, drawn in about four minutes, quickly, in pencil.

“You drew us coming,” said Kei.

“I drew the bridge,” said Xav. “You were on it.”

Drew laughed, quietly, from the doorway.

Kei asked properly.

He did it standing up, in the middle of the little room at the top of the inn, with his map in his hand. He didn’t hurry it. Xav had known he wouldn’t. Kei told him about the three thousand on the south steps every evening. About the gate holding, every time, with Wendy and Kite and now Rafi on it. About the customs, and Higu on the roof, and the old guild’s book drying page by page on Ama Corrie’s counter. About Kestrel starting a writ, and CL’s three tens. And then, last, about the halls, and one banner. And how he wasn’t asking because of the halls, and wanted that to be clear.

“It’s big,” said Kei. “You said, ask me when it’s big. It’s big. So I’m asking.” He looked at Xav. “Will Riverbank come in? All eight of you. Under the banner. You’d still be Riverbank. It’s made of little groups. Big enough to hold them without them stopping being little.”

Xav recognised the line. Niko’s, from the first call, in January. Kei had told him about it.

He let Kei finish. That was the thing. He’d planned to let him finish. Somebody else had promised to say yes before Kei finished, and Xav wasn’t going to take that from him. He was first through the door. That was his. The other thing wasn’t.

“Yes,” said Xav.

Kei didn’t move.

“I said I’d be first,” said Xav. “I’m first.” He picked up the pencil, and put it down again, because his hand wanted something to do. “But it’s not mine to say. Not on my own. Riverbank’s eight.” He looked at Kei. “Come and ask them.”

They were on the riverbank.

Of course they were. Where three rivers came together below Brackenford, on the shingle, where Val had named them in February. Seven people sitting on stones by the water in the late afternoon. Waiting, because Xav had told them at lunch Kei’s coming today, probably, and Jun Liang hadn’t been able to sit still since.

They stood up when they saw who was with him.

Kei asked again. All of it, properly, standing on the shingle. Xav stood beside him and didn’t say anything, and watched his eight.

Jun Liang said “YES” before Kei had got to will. At the top of his voice, so that a heron on the far bank took off in a fright. It was the same heron, probably. Xav was nearly sure it was the same heron.

“He’s not finished, Jun.”

“I DON’T CARE. YES. WE NEED A GUILD. I SAID THAT IN JANUARY. I SAID WE NEED A LEADER AND WE GOT A LEADER AND NOW WE NEED A GUILD—”

“Let him finish,” said Val, in her calm low voice. Jun Liang let him finish.

When Kei had finished, Val looked at him for a long moment.

“We’d still be Riverbank,” she said. It wasn’t quite a question.

“You’d always be Riverbank,” said Kei. “That’s the point. Little groups. The guild doesn’t make you stop being you. It just puts you on the same banner.”

Val nodded. “Then yes.”

“Yeah,” said Ryan. Which was all Ryan ever said, and enough.

“Yes,” said Azac, in his big flat voice. “I’m a wall. Walls go where they’re put.” He thought about it. “I’d like to be put next to Tiger. He falls in things. Somebody should be next to him.”

John held up both hands, with the little lights he made in the game floating off his fingers in the afternoon sun. “Yes,” said John. “Obviously. Look at my lights. They’re saying yes.”

Rui Xiang said “…yes,” very quietly, looking at the river. Rui Xiang said most things quietly. He meant all of them.

And Malcolm looked at the sky for a long time, the way he did.

“It’s still going to be June,” said Malcolm.

“Malcolm.”

“I’m just saying. It’ll still be June. Whatever we’re in.” He looked at Kei. “But yes. Next year’s come, I think. It’s here.”

Xav stood on the shingle and looked at his eight. The same eight who’d stood in a loose untidy row on this same shingle in February. Eight people from eight towns who’d walked to him because he’d said get to ten, then walk to me. I’ll be on the bank.

He’d been on the bank. He’d stayed on the bank, all this time. Waiting for it to be big.

“Riverbank,” said Kei, writing on his map. A line, from Xav’s dot to the middle. Not dotted. Solid. Kei drew it slowly, so they could all see. “Yes.”

Xav rang Ice from the bridge, at dusk, with Kei and Drew on the shingle below him and the river going three ways.

He rang. He didn’t message. Some things you didn’t message.

“He asked you,” said Ice, before Xav had said anything.

“He asked me. I said yes.” Xav looked at the water. “I’m first. You’re second.”

“I know.”

“You’ll be annoyed.”

“I’m not annoyed.”

“You’re a bit annoyed,” said Xav.

There was a pause, in a fen a morning’s walk away.

“A bit,” said Ice.

Xav laughed. He stood on the east bridge in Brackenford with the phone against his ear and laughed, out loud. He’d laughed like that perhaps six times since poly, and two of them had been on this bridge.

“He’s coming tomorrow,” said Xav. “To you. With Drew. He’s going to ask you properly. To your face. He walked eight days to do it.”

“I know,” said Ice.

“You told him you’d say yes before he finished.”

“I know.”

“Will you?”

There was a long pause. The river went under the bridge. Below him, on the shingle, Jun Liang was explaining something to Drew at great volume, with his hands, and Drew was nodding.

“You’ll see,” said Ice.

Xav smiled at the river.

“I’ll be there,” he said. “First through the door. I’ll be standing right next to it. I want to see your face.”

“Everybody wants to see my face,” said Ice. “It’s very annoying.”

“It’s a good face,” said Xav, and hung up before Ice could answer, which he’d been wanting to do for nine years.
