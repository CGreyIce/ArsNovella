Live from Elsewhere · Volume 5: Ask Me Again

Chapter 123

Patience

2,667 words · 12 min read

The first thing Ice ever said to her in the game was “Penguin.”

It was on Sedge’s steps, on the way out to the Landing, after the tea. Everybody was crowding down the ladder in the dark. Xola was still half hanging off him. He looked round, saw PenguChi at the top of the steps with her long spear, and looked at her for a moment the way you’d look at somebody you hadn’t seen in years and were very pleased to.

“Penguin,” said Ice.

PenguChi did things slowly. Everybody knew that. Her chat knew it, her friends knew it. She’d built a whole life on it, a calm, steady, unhurried life in which she had never once been fast at anything on purpose.

She kicked him in the shins.

Or tried to. It came out of nowhere, fast as a snake, the fastest thing she’d done since she came into the game. He saw it coming about a hair too late and jumped back off the bottom step onto the Landing. Her boot went past his shin close enough that he felt the wind of it.

Then he ran.

He actually ran. Across the Landing, past the benches, laughing out loud, which PenguChi had never once heard him do on a screen. Over his shoulder he said “Penguin!” again, on purpose.

So she chased him.

Round the Landing. Round the barrel. Round the lamp-post, twice, past Deston, who moved his feet out of the way without looking up. Past Sheng Yang, who watched them go by with an expression of deep, calm interest. Past Xola, who was screaming with laughter, and Thea, who had both hands over her mouth. Patience the sheep, who hadn’t been called Patience yet, joined in for one lap out of a sense of occasion and then stopped and ate a reed. Ice kept just out of reach, laughing, and every time she nearly caught him he said “Penguin,” and every time he said it she went faster.

It took four laps. Then he let her get close enough to swing her spear-butt at the back of his knees, not hard. He went down on the planks, still laughing, with his hands up.

“Truce,” said Ice.

PenguChi stood over him, breathing hard, with her spear.

“Say it again,” she said calmly. “I dare you.”

He looked up at her from the planks. He didn’t say it. He very obviously wanted to.

“Hi, Chi,” said Ice.

“Hi,” said PenguChi.

She helped him up. Then she went and stood by a lamp-post at the edge of the Landing to get her breath back, with her spear leaning beside her. She thought, slowly, that she’d been wrong on Monday, watching him brew. He’s slow too, she’d thought.

He wasn’t. He just chose when not to be.

There were eleven of them on the Landing that night, and a sheep, and PenguChi watched all of them.

She stood at the edge of it, by a lamp-post, with her spear leaning beside her. The Landing was a wide wooden platform on stilts at the heart of Fernwick, with the black water under it and the lamps round its edge. Somebody had dragged two benches and a barrel into the middle. The rain had stopped. Every few minutes a stranger came along the causeway, saw who was sitting on the benches, and stopped dead, and went away again very quietly, the way you’d back out of a room where something private was happening.

PenguChi watched them do it, too. She thought it was nice of them.

She watched Deston, who was tall and calm and kind and who’d introduced himself to all six of them by name, one at a time, with his hand out. Then he’d sat down on the barrel and asked Slep about his glaive for twenty minutes, and listened to every word.

She watched Suzu, who’d said hi to each of them and nothing else. He was sitting on the edge of the Landing with his feet over the water and an empty sweet jar beside him. Every so often he looked at the jar, the way you’d look at a cat asleep in a window.

She watched Sheng Yang, who was standing. He’d been standing all evening. Xyro had gone and stood next to him, because Xyro couldn’t bear anybody to be on their own, and asked him about the long sword. After a while Sheng Yang started explaining Ender’s Cut with his hands, very quietly, very precisely. Xyro was nodding as if he understood, and very obviously didn’t, and Sheng Yang was very obviously enjoying it anyway.

She watched Tif and Kotori, at the end of the Landing by the steps, sitting side by side with their staffs across their knees. They were drinking something out of the same pot that smelled of cabbage, and talking about bells. They’d been talking about bells for an hour. Tif’s ears were turned all the way toward Kotori, the whole time.

She watched Xola, who was sitting on the bench with her head on Slep’s shoulder and her gills folded flat with contentment. Every so often she leaned forward and said ICEY across the Landing for no reason. Ice, every time, said Xola, and Xola, every time, laughed.

And she watched Ice and Thea, who weren’t sitting together.

They weren’t avoiding each other. PenguChi could see that. That wasn’t what it was. Ice was on the other bench, with his shield leaning against his knee, the way Thea’s tower shield was leaning against hers. Every so often one of them looked at the other, when the other wasn’t looking. Then looked away again before they were caught.

PenguChi had met Ice through Thea. Years ago. This is Ice, Thea had said, in a call, a long time ago, in a voice PenguChi had never heard her use for anyone else. He’s my best friend. And then, some time after that, Thea had stopped saying it. Not because anything had happened that PenguChi could see. Ice had just been around less. A name that came to her stream sometimes, good at Words on Stream, who said gg and left.

She’d never asked why. It hadn’t been hers to ask.

She thought now, watching the two of them on two benches on a wooden platform in a fen, not quite looking at each other, that it was probably the same reason Thea had been dark for a year. Not the same thing. The same reason.

The emails came at half past nine.

PenguChi felt her phone go, in the corner of her eye, the small window the egg made. She ignored it, because it was probably nothing. Then she heard Xola, on the bench, go completely silent. Xola was never completely silent. PenguChi looked.

Xola was staring at the air in front of her. So was Thea, on the other bench. PenguChi looked at her own window.

Dear PenguChi,

The ELSEWHERE team would like to invite you to join a small group of creators who have helped shape how people first see our world.

She read it slowly. All of it. Twice.

Creator Program. A badge. A gathering. Some money, every month. Things to look at early, that she wouldn’t be allowed to talk about.

“THEA,” said Xola. “THEA. ARE YOU—”

“Yes,” said Thea. Her voice was very small.

“CHI—”

“Yes,” said PenguChi.

Xola screamed. Properly, at the top of her voice, on the Landing at half past nine at night. Out on the causeway, a stranger walking toward Fernwick stopped, turned round, and walked back the other way. Slep put his hand on the back of Xola’s head and left it there while she screamed, and didn’t say anything, and was smiling.

Thea wasn’t screaming. Thea was looking across the Landing at Ice.

“Did you—“ she said.

“No,” said Ice.

“You didn’t ask them? Vivian didn’t—“

“No.” He shook his head. “Not this time. You were over a thousand. All three of you.” He looked at her. “That’s the rule. You did it yourselves.”

Thea looked at him for a long moment.

“Okay,” she said. And then, quieter, the way you’d say a thing you were only just letting yourself believe: “Okay.”

PenguChi looked at the number on her own window. Four digits. She’d seen it for the first time on Wednesday night and said oh. She looked at it again now, with the email on top, in a fen, on a wooden platform full of people she loved and people she’d only just met.

“Oh,” she said again, quietly, to nobody.

Beside her, the sheep looked up from the barrel it had been eating.

At ten Thea got up and walked to the end of the eel man’s jetty.

She didn’t say anything. She just got up, with the tower shield, and walked down the long thin jetty that went out from the Landing into the black water. She stood at the end of it with the reeds on three sides and the stars coming out. After a minute Ice got up from his bench, and went after her, and stood beside her. Not close. Just there.

PenguChi watched them from the Landing. She couldn’t hear anything. They were too far, and the water was too loud under the stilts. She could only see their backs. Two people at the end of a jetty in the dark, a tower shield and a round one, looking out over the fen.

Thea talked for a while. PenguChi could tell by her shoulders. She talked the way Thea talked when she’d been planning to say something for a long time and was finally saying it, in order, the way she’d planned it. Ice didn’t move. He just listened. Ice listened with his whole back, PenguChi noticed. He didn’t fidget. He didn’t turn round. He just stood there and let it come.

Then Thea stopped.

There was a long pause. Ice said something. Short. Three or four words, maybe. PenguChi couldn’t hear what.

Thea laughed.

It carried, over the water. A real one, surprised out of her, the kind PenguChi hadn’t heard from Thea in a year. Thea put her hand over her face and laughed at the end of a jetty in a fen, and Ice stood beside her, and didn’t say anything else.

PenguChi turned away.

It wasn’t hers to watch. Some things weren’t. Two people who’d been best friends once, a long time ago, before everything, finding out where they’d left it. She didn’t need to know what they’d said. She’d seen Thea laugh. That was enough.

She found Slep looking at her from the bench, over Xola’s head. He’d seen her turn away. He nodded, once, slowly.

Then, from the end of the jetty, Thea started to sing.

PenguChi didn’t turn round. She heard it come over the water behind her, low at first, then less low, the way a voice comes when it hasn’t been used for something in a long time and isn’t sure it still remembers how. It wasn’t loud. It wasn’t for the Landing. It was for one person, standing beside her at the end of a jetty in the dark.

PenguChi knew the voice. She’d heard Thea sing on stream, years ago, back when Thea still did things because she liked them. She hadn’t heard it since. Not once, in a year. She’d stopped expecting to.

On the bench, Xola had gone completely still against Slep’s shoulder, with her gills flat and her eyes shut. Xola knew the voice too. She’d known it longer than any of them.

Nobody on the Landing said anything. Deston closed the book in his lap. Kotori and Tif stopped talking about bells. Out on the causeway, a stranger walking toward the town stopped, and listened, and didn’t come any closer. The song went out over the reeds and the black water, in a fen at the edge of the world, and the whole of Fernwick, or the part of it that was awake, held still for it.

It wasn’t a long song. When it ended, there was a silence that went on for a while.

PenguChi looked back once. Just once, quickly, because she couldn’t not.

At the end of the jetty, Ice had put his hand on top of Thea’s head. Lightly, briefly, the way you’d pat a friend you’d known forever who’d just done something brave. Two pats. The way he’d have done it to Suzu, or to Deston, or to anybody he loved and wasn’t going to make a speech about.

Thea shoved him. Not hard. With her shoulder, sideways, the tower shield clanking. He nearly went off the end of the jetty into the fen and caught himself on the post, and laughed. Thea laughed too, and wiped her face with the back of her hand.

PenguChi turned back round, and smiled at the black water, slowly. Friends. That was all. That was everything.

Later, when the Landing was emptying and the lamp-man was going round putting the lamps out, Thea came back from the jetty and sat down beside PenguChi on the edge of the platform. Her eyes were red. She was smiling. She didn’t say anything about the jetty and PenguChi didn’t ask.

They sat with their feet over the black water.

“We’re not going to Carrow Cross,” said Thea, after a while. “Not yet. We’ll stay round here for a bit. Level. There’s a town up the river, Brackenford. Ice says it’s busy. And Gabe’s coming north with three more at the end of the month.” She looked east, the way she’d been looking east since Millrace. Then she looked away. “Not yet.”

PenguChi looked at her.

“You don’t have to be okay on a schedule,” she said.

Thea was quiet for a long time.

“Higu said no hurry,” she said.

“Higu’s right.”

“Higu’s always right. It’s very annoying.” Thea laughed again, smaller. “It’s going to take a while, Chi.”

“I know,” said PenguChi. “I’m slow. I don’t mind a while.”

Thea leaned her head on PenguChi’s shoulder, the way Xola had leaned on Slep’s. They sat there until the last lamp went out.

The sheep was still on the Landing when everyone else had gone in. It was standing at the edge, by the barrel, looking at the water.

Slep came back out for it. He stood beside it for a moment, the way he stood beside things.

“It needs a name,” said PenguChi.

“It’s not a name yet,” said Slep.

“It walked from a cliff,” said PenguChi slowly. “It followed you three and a half days. It followed you four more to here. It never ran ahead. It never went home. It just stayed. And waited.” She looked at the sheep. “It’s very patient.”

Slep looked at her. Then at the sheep.

“Patience,” he said.

The sheep looked up at him.

“Patience,” said Slep again, trying it. He thought about it the way he thought about everything, slowly, all the way through. Then he nodded. “Yes.”

PenguChi smiled, slowly, the way she did everything.
