Chapter 255

The Eve

3,276 words · 14 min read

The board came on the evening before, in a letter, the way everything came now.

Kei had drawn it. Of course he had. The round town in chalk on the board in the Hall, the seven roads, the doors. He’d copied it onto a sheet and sent it north, so the nine on the hill would know where everybody was. Ice read it at the north side of the ring at nine in the evening, on Wednesday the twentieth of December, with his back to the middle.

The first mile — Tiger · Maigo · Nikorasu
The north-west road — Kite · Drew · Ash

He read it twice.

The north-west road. Kite · Drew · Ash.

Ash. On the board. In a three. Not on the Roll; on the sheet. Those are different things.

The eye. And Isa, on the bench.

The hill. You nine.

He folded it and put it inside his coat, flat, against his chest. Next to a candle with Sedge on its twist of paper, burned down to a stub. He’d said he’d bring it back to Fernwick. One day.

Harlan’s letter came an hour later. Of course it did. Harlan’s letters always came an hour after anything important, in capitals.

[ Letter · from Harlan (Meridian)

He read it three times. He laughed, the third time, out loud, at the north side of the ring. Behind him, by the fire, Hyv said what and he said Harlan swept under a chair and Hyv said WHAT.

Nine, he thought. Ama Corrie gave Harlan nine.

Milli had been nine. Hana ten.

He didn’t know what Ama Corrie was counting any more. He thought maybe she’d stopped counting asks and started counting something else.

He’d been at the north side of the ring for twenty nights.

Nobody had asked him to. He’d just gone there, the first night after the middle was lit. And stood. With his shield on his arm and his back to the flat stone, looking north. And the others had let him, the way they let him do most things. Kotori brought tea. Hyv came and stood beside him, some nights. Gigi had come once, and asked if he was frightened.

He wasn’t. He’d told her. I’ve never been at the front of anything with that much behind me.

It was true. He’d thought about it a lot, standing there. Twenty nights.

In the war, a long time ago, in a phone game with a stupid name, he’d been at the front of everything. Twenty people, all at the front. Nobody behind, nobody beside. He’d thought that was what the front was. Then a fen, and a stone, and learning to wait. And people behind him for two years that he never turned round to look at. And then a harbour wall, and a broom, and I was next to you.

Now he stood at the north side of a ring of twelve stones on a hill in the still water. With his back to a flame that had been lit for three hundred years. And eight people behind him round a little fire. And past them, south, across three hundred stones and a hawthorn that had flowered in December, a road of ninety-four lamps standing straight. And a round town, with sixty-one people and twenty of Meridian on a board in threes. And an old woman on a bench by the eye, carried up every dusk, waiting to see a light.

He’d never had so much behind him.

He didn’t need to turn round to know it was there.

Hyv came at ten.

She stood beside him at the north side of the ring and looked north. She’d been looking at everything for twenty nights, since the middle came out of the jar. Edge to edge again. She’d turned all the way round in the middle of the ring that first night and said I can see in a voice he’d never heard. And she’d been looking ever since. At the stones, and the still water, and the dark with edges. At the hawthorn flower, which she and Gigi and Thea went out to every few nights along the stones, counting. At him.

“You came out left side first,” she said. “Tonight.”

“I always do.”

“I could see it,” said Hyv. “For three weeks I couldn’t. In the nothing. I could only hear you.” She was quiet. “I missed it.”

“It’s very boring.”

“I love it,” said Hyv.

He didn’t say anything. He put his hand out, palm up, on the top of the north stone between them, the twelfth, the one they’d raised. She put hers in it.

“Isa says it comes here first,” said Hyv. “Tomorrow. Before it goes south.”

“Yes.”

“All of it. The whole dark. From everywhere.” She was looking north, at the dark, which was just dark now, with depth. “It gathers at the middle. To look at it. Before it goes.”

“Yes.”

“And we’re the middle.”

“We’re next to it,” said Ice.

She looked at him.

“The middle’s that,” he said. He didn’t turn round; he didn’t need to. The flame standing over the jar on the flat stone behind them, gold. “We’re next to it. On purpose. Where everybody can see.” He was quiet. “That’s all a ring is.”

Hyv didn’t say anything for a long time.

“I’ll call it,” she said.

“There’ll be something to call.”

“I know,” said Hyv. “That’s why.” She looked at the dark. “In the nothing I couldn’t call anything. I could only listen. I learned to listen.” She swallowed. “Tomorrow there’ll be something. Coming. From everywhere. In the light.” She looked at him. “I’ll see all of it. Edge to edge. And I’ll call it.”

“For everybody,” said Ice.

“For everybody,” said Hyv. “And you’ll hear me. At the front.”

“I always hear you,” said Ice.

She hit him. On the arm, not hard. And then didn’t let go of his hand on the top of the twelfth stone.

He went round the ring at midnight. He didn’t usually. He did it tonight.

He walked round the inside of the twelve stones, slowly, the way Gigi had walked round them every night in the first week. Touching every one. Grey, rough, tall, leaning in toward the middle. Three hundred years of looking at the same thing.

At the third stone, Thea and Gigi. Sitting against it, close, the way they sat now. Thea’s head on Gigi’s shoulder. Neither of them asleep. They looked up when he came past.

“Ice,” said Thea.

“Thea.”

That was all. He’d known her a long time. Since before any of this. There’d been a time, a long time ago, out there, when he’d had to step back from her and Xola both, for his own sake, and gone to Suzu’s lot. And it had come out, in the game, a long time later, when Hyv asked them about him. And it had been all right. More than all right.

He looked at her now, with her head on Gigi’s shoulder, at the third stone.

“Good,” said Ice.

Thea’s face did something. Gigi’s did too.

At the fifth stone, Xola and Slep. Slep with his bent glaive across his knees; he’d tried to straighten it on a stone and made it worse. Xola asleep against him, or pretending. Slep looked up and didn’t say anything. He’d learned to wait until nobody else had said it. Nobody did. So after a while he said:

“Ice.”

“Slep.”

“Tomorrow,” said Slep. “I’ll take whatever side she’s not on.”

“I know,” said Ice.

“I’m telling you anyway,” said Slep. “So you know where I’ll be.”

Ice nodded. Once.

At the eighth, Hako. On his own, for once, with his spear, looking at the still water. He’d stopped narrating, mostly, since the middle came out. He said there was too much to look at now; he didn’t need the voice to look at it.

“The tank,” said Hako, softly, in the voice, when Ice came past. “On his rounds. A rare display of what the scientific community can only describe as—“

“Hako.”

“Sorry,” said Hako, in his own. “Habit.” He looked at the water. “I’m frightened.”

“I know.”

“I don’t need the voice to look at it,” said Hako. “I’m going to need it tomorrow.” He looked at Ice. “Can I do it out loud? All night? Like the nothing?”

“Yes,” said Ice. “Do it about us.”

Hako’s face did something.

At the flat stone, Suzu and Kotori. Sitting on the south side, where the mothers sat, with their backs against it and the flame standing over their heads. Kotori had a tin of tea in her lap. The last one, the eleventh. Suzu had his hand on the edge of the flat stone. On the notches, where the cross was, and his notch beside it, and the thorn, and the flower.

Suzu looked up at him.

He didn’t say anything. He didn’t need to. Ice had told him on the first night. I need you. And Suzu had said good, which meant everything.

“Tomorrow,” said Suzu.

“Tomorrow.”

“Here’s the middle,” said Suzu. Very quietly. To the flame. To the dark outside the ring. “Not yet.”

“Not yet,” said Kotori.

Ice went on round. Back to the north side. The twelfth stone. And stood.

He hit sixty at about two in the morning, doing nothing.

He’d been standing at the north stone. Looking at the dark. Not fighting anything; there was nothing to fight. The Unlit came, sometimes, up out of the still water at the foot of the hill. Tall, thin, swaying. And stood on the water and looked at the middle, the way Gigi said. Not for. At. Like a fire in a hearth. And then sank.

One came up at two. Right below the north side of the hill. Closer than any had come.

It stood on the still water and looked up at the ring. At the twelve stones. At him, at the north stone, with his shield on his arm.

He didn’t move. He didn’t need to.

It looked at him for a long time. Then past him. At the flame, standing over the jar. And something in the way it stood changed. He didn’t know how else to say it. It stood the way people stood at the long table on a Sunday, when Ama Corrie came down with her broom. As if it knew where it was.

It sank.

He looked at the window for a long time.

Twenty nights. He’d never got a level for not doing anything before. For standing somewhere. For keeping something.

Behind him, by the fire, Hyv said what and he said sixty. And Hyv said for WHAT, and he said for standing here. And Hyv laughed so much she had to sit down.

He wrote to the round town at three. He didn’t usually write. He wrote short ones, when he did.

[ Letter · from Ice → KeiKatamori
read the board
it’s good
tell kite: fair
tell isa: tonight. on the bench. look north
— I. ]

Kei wrote back in four minutes.

[ Letter · from KeiKatamori → Ice
Told Kite. He said “fair” back. He’s been practising.
Isa’s asleep in her chair. I’ll tell her at dusk.
She asked me something yesterday, at the eye.
She asked if you’d turned round.
I said yes.
She said hm.
— K. ]

He read it several times.

At a quarter to eight, the last morning before the longest night, they stood round the flat stone. All nine. Not in a ring of candles on the ground; they didn’t need that any more. Round the flat stone itself, in the light. Close. Suzu at the south side, where the mothers sat. Ice at the north, facing in for once. Facing the flame.

He looked at it.

He hadn’t looked at it much. Twenty nights. He’d been at the north side with his back to it, facing out, the way he faced out at everything. He’d known it was there. He hadn’t needed to look.

He looked now.

It was small. Smaller than he’d thought, when he wasn’t looking at it. A flame, gold, without a wick or oil, standing straight up in the air over the mouth of a plain clay jar on a flat stone with a crown cut into it. Not leaning. Not toward anything. It just stood there.

And everything round it leaned in. The twelve stones. The nine people. The hill. The dark outside, which wasn’t looking any more.

So it knows where the middle is, Isa had said.

He looked at it and thought about the heron. On a stone in a fen, facing outward, never once turning round to look at what was behind it.

He’d turned round.

“Set one to watch,” said Suzu.

“So the rest may sleep,” said eight people.

And Ice, a breath after. Because he’d been looking at the flame. And he’d forgotten for a moment that he was supposed to say anything. And it came out of him on its own, the way good came out of Suzu.

“So the rest may sleep.”

Hyv, across the flat stone, looked at him. Edge to edge.

“You were late,” she said.

“I was looking,” said Ice.

The egg took him at eight. He lay in it, in the back room at Melaka Raya, with the fan going round and the morning coming in round the edges of the blind. He didn’t get out for a long time.

Gabe was on the edge of his own pod. Out already, at eight; he’d been on the high fells all night with Moonlight Harvest, getting ready. He had two cups of kopi, too sweet. He handed one over without a word.

“Tonight,” said Gabe.

“Tonight.”

“I’m the front,” said Gabe. “The north door. Salty and Wendy and me. And Rae.” He looked at the kopi. “One pace.”

Ice looked at him.

“She said one,” said Gabe. “At the end of September. I asked.” He was red to the ears. “She said don’t push it.”

“Don’t push it,” said Ice.

“I’m not going to push it, Dec, I’m going to stand in a door all night with the dark coming at me, I’m not going to have time to push it—“ Gabe stopped. He put his face in his hands. “I’m frightened.”

“I know,” said Ice.

“You’re not.”

“No,” said Ice. “I’m on a hill in the middle of nothing with a ring of stones and a flame that’s been lit three hundred years. And eight people. And Hyv calling.” He drank the kopi. “I’ve never had so much behind me.” He looked at Gabe. “Neither have you.”

Gabe took his hands away from his face.

“Salty,” said Ice. “Wendy. Bee’s six Walls. Rae, one pace. And sixty people behind you in threes, and twenty of Meridian on the north-east road, and an old woman on a bench. And a candle in the window of the north door that Chernn’s changed fifty times.” He put his cup down on the shelf. Without looking. “You’ll hold it. You’ve been holding me for eleven years.”

“You’re heavier than the dark,” said Gabe. Very quietly. “You said.”

“I am,” said Ice. “And you’re still here.”

Gabe looked at him for a long time. Then he put his big heavy arm round Ice’s shoulders, the arm of a man who’d been his friend since a school choir, without asking. And they sat on the edges of two pods in the back room of a shoplot in Malacca. In the morning, on the twenty-first of December, the shortest day. Two cups of kopi, too sweet.

Tonight, the longest night.
