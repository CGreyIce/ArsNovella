Live from Elsewhere · Volume 5: Ask Me Again

Chapter 118

Millrace

2,114 words · 9 min read

Thea had stopped streaming last year because she couldn’t remember the last time she’d enjoyed anything.

She didn’t say it like that. She said uni, and burnout, and I just need a break, and her chat said take care of yourself and we’ll be here. They were, mostly. Thirty of them, forty on a good night. They’d been there for years. She’d turned the stream off on a Tuesday and sat in her room in Australia with the light off and not turned it on again.

Then in November a man who used to be her best friend went into a game with twenty-one people watching. She’d watched him for five months. All of it. Every night, with her chat off and his on, under her own name in his chat, which was more than she’d managed with most people lately. And somewhere in those five months, without noticing exactly when, she’d started to enjoy something again.

So when Higu messaged on a Thursday in April and said come in, all of you, bring everyone, Thea had said yes before she’d finished reading it. Then she’d organised it, because organising was the thing she could still do when she couldn’t do anything else. Six eggs, three countries, one afternoon. Same minute.

At eight o’clock on Saturday, Singapore time, ten o’clock hers, she lay down in the egg and put her thumb on the plate.

She built herself the way she’d been drawn. Her model’s hair, her model’s eyes, the little things she’d picked for it years ago when she was a different person who liked things. The mirror let her keep all of it. She stood in front of it for a long time and looked at somebody she hadn’t seen in a while.

[ Pain. ]

She left it where it was. Twenty. She’d had enough of things hurting to want any more of it on purpose.

There was a rack. She’d decided what she wanted before she lay down, the way she decided everything, in a spreadsheet at two in the morning. But when she stood in front of it she found herself looking at the shield. A big one, at the end, taller than the others, square, iron-rimmed. A tower shield. Next to it, a hammer.

She’d never been the one at the front. Not in anything. She’d always been the one behind, keeping things together, making the calls, holding the plan.

She thought about a man at the front of everything for five months, with a shield, not flinching.

She thought: I’m so tired of being the one behind.

She took the shield, and the hammer.

[ Millrace · the Weirlands

It was a town of wheels.

That was the first thing. Everywhere she looked, wheels. Waterwheels, dozens of them, big and small, wooden and iron, turning in a river that had been split and split again into a hundred channels between the houses. Every channel had a wheel and every wheel was driving something. She could hear them all at once, a great low wooden rumble under everything. Through the open doors she could see what they turned: a loom going clack, clack, clack, a hammer rising and falling in a forge, a bellows breathing, a grindstone, a saw. The whole town was a machine. Water came down from the hills at the top and went through every wheel in turn, and came out at the bottom into a wide still mill pond, tired.

Thea stood on the step of her little wooden room with a tower shield on her back and a hammer in her hand, in the dusk, and felt something in her chest she didn’t have a name for any more.

“Oh,” she said. “It’s a system.”

[ You are among the first travellers to wake in Millrace today.
You may Call up to four friends to wake here with you.

Four.

She’d known it would be four. Higu had told her. She’d planned for four. She’d stood in her kitchen in Australia on Wednesday night with the spreadsheet open and looked at six names and the number four, and not known what to do. For the first time in a long time she hadn’t been able to organise her way out of something.

And Slep had said, on the call, in his calm slow voice: Call the others. I’ll walk.

Xola had said Slep, no. Slep had said Xo. It’s fine. If I’m going, I’m going. I’ll just be going a bit longer. And that had been that, because Slep didn’t say much and when he did you couldn’t argue with it.

So Thea stood on the step and said four names.

Four doors opened along the street beside hers, under the wheels.

Xola came out first, obviously. Xola had always come out of every door first.

She came out at a run and stopped dead in the street with her mouth open. Thea knew her. She’d have known her anywhere, from any distance, in any light. Xola had been drawn as an axolotl girl for years, with the soft pink gills standing out from the sides of her head like feathered frills. Here she had them. They were moving. Opening and closing gently in the damp air, all on their own. Xola put both her hands up and touched them and made a noise Thea had never heard a human being make.

“THEA,” said Xola. “THEA. MY GILLS. THEY’RE—”

“I can see.”

“THEY MOVE.”

“I can see, Xo.”

“I’m going to cry,” said Xola, and did, and laughed, and came across the street and hugged her so hard the tower shield on Thea’s back clanged against the wall.

Then PenguChi, slowly, looking round at the wheels with her head on one side, the way she looked at everything. As if she were memorising it to tell someone later.

“It’s loud,” said PenguChi calmly. “I like it.”

Then Xyro, with a bow, grinning so hard it looked like it hurt, walking backwards to look at the biggest wheel. He walked straight into Tif, who’d just come out of her door.

Tif stood in the street with a staff in her hand and two soft black cat’s ears on top of her head that turned, on their own, toward the sound of the wheels. She looked up at the town. She didn’t say anything. She just stood there, very still, and Thea saw her lift her free hand and look at it, and turn it over, and look at it again.

“Tif?” said Xyro.

“It’s real,” said Tif quietly. “It’s all real. I watched it for five months and it was real the whole time.”

Xyro put his arm round her.

On the call in Thea’s ear, from somewhere else entirely, a calm slow voice said, “Everyone in?”

“Everyone’s in,” said Xola. Her voice did something. “Except you.”

“Where are you?” said Thea.

There was a pause while Slep looked at something.

“A town on a cliff,” said Slep. “It says three days from you. Maybe four.” Another pause. “There’s a lot of sheep.”

“Slep—“

“I’m fine, Xo.” Calm. Slow. You could hear him smiling, though, if you knew him. You could always hear it when it was Xola. “I’ll walk. I said I’d walk. Go and look at your wheels.”

She turned her stream on at half past ten, her time, standing on a footbridge over the biggest channel in Millrace, with the wheel turning below her.

She hadn’t streamed since October. Her hands weren’t quite steady. She’d told nobody she was going to do it, except Xola, who’d said do it, do it, do it nine times.

Four hundred and twelve. She’d never had four hundred and twelve. Forty, on a good night. She looked at the number for a long time and didn’t say anything about it, because if she said anything she’d cry. She wasn’t going to cry on her first stream back.

“Hi,” said Thea. “Hi. I’m back. I’m sorry I was gone.”

She stood on the footbridge with the wheel turning underneath her and the river going through it, and found that she was. For the first time since she’d stopped. Since before she’d stopped, if she was honest. She was standing in a town that was a machine, with a shield on her back she’d taken because she was tired of being behind. And she was happy.

She cried on her first stream back after all. Xola said later that it didn’t count, because everybody did.

Later, on the step of her little room, with the stream off and the others asleep and the wheels still turning in the dark, she got the map up.

She’d told herself she wouldn’t. She did it anyway, the way you’d press a bruise.

Millrace, in the Weirlands. And a long way east, eight days’ walk or more, a round town where seven roads met. Where a guild with a banner of dots and lines had its room in an old stone hall. And where somebody she hadn’t said the name of out loud in a long time was very probably, right now, asleep.

She’d known Gigi was there since March. Since the founding, since forty thousand people watched it. She’d watched it too. She’d seen her on the floor of the Hall under the eye, laying thorn across a counter in front of a book. Gigi, being brave, being good at something, being everything she’d been before it had all gone the way it went.

It had been the kind of thing where whatever Thea did was wrong. If she did well, it was wrong. If she did badly, it was wrong. She’d spent a long time thinking that if she could just get it right, it would stop. It hadn’t. Xola had held her up through all of it, every night, on the phone, until the night Thea ended it. Xola had still been on the phone the morning after.

She looked at the map. At how far away the round town was from a town of wheels in the Weirlands.

She’d been relieved when she saw it, on the first night, where they’d landed. Properly relieved, the way you’d be relieved at a doctor’s. And then, straight after, ashamed of being relieved. She sat on the step now with both of those things at once and didn’t know what to do with either of them.

She hadn’t told anyone that. Not even Xola. Especially not Xola, who’d done enough.

There was a message. From Higu, an hour old. Higu, who’d said bring everyone, who’d been in every chat Thea had ever had and who loved Gigi more than anyone in the world. And who’d never once, in all of it, made Thea feel she had to choose.

Thea read it four times.

Then she put the map away, and lay down on her bed in a town of wheels, and listened to the river go through all of them in the dark, one after another, until it came out at the bottom, tired, into the still pond. And for the first time in a long time she went to sleep without having to organise herself into it.
