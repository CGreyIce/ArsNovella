Live from Elsewhere · Volume 7: Keep the Lamps

Chapter 173

Ten Thousand People

4,319 words · 19 min read

Wendy followed him down the fourth tunnel because somebody had to hold the lamp, and because she wanted to see his face.

He went fast. He always went fast when he’d decided something. Down Ama Corrie’s stair, through the hatch, across the round floor of the Seven Below with its silver lines. Into the fourth mouth, the one under the north-east road. With the test piece glowing red in the tongs in one hand and nothing in the other, because she had the lamp. The tunnel went down. It went down a long way. The Delvers were asleep in their side-holes, big blind snouts on their paws, and didn’t wake. Past them, the walls got colder, and the stone changed from pale to dark, and then it was so dark her lamp seemed to be shining into ink.

“Here,” said Salty.

She heard the water before she saw it. A little stream running along the floor of the tunnel, along a groove it had cut for itself, a hand deep. It was so cold she could feel it in her teeth from a pace away. Black water, in a black tunnel, where nothing had ever been lit.

“Put the lamp out,” said Salty.

“What?”

“Put it out. Please.” He was crouching by the stream with the tongs. The test piece’s red glow lit his face from underneath, and it was the most serious face she’d ever seen on him. “It has to be dark. Properly dark. Like where it came from.”

She put the lamp out.

It was very dark. She couldn’t see anything at all except the test piece, a little smear of angry red in the black, low down by the floor. She heard Salty breathe in. Then the red moved, and went down, and went into the water.

It hissed. Very softly. Not the big white roar of Joss’s trough. Just a small long sigh in the dark, like somebody letting out a breath they’d held for three thousand years.

Then nothing. Black. The sound of the stream.

“Wendy,” said Salty, after a while. His voice was very odd. “Light it.”

She lit the lamp.

He was crouched by the stream holding the tongs up. In the tongs was a little black blade, the size of his hand. Whole. No line on it, no crack, nothing. Just black, and dull, and so dark it seemed to drink the lamplight. And over it, in the cold air of the deep tunnel, a window.

Salty stared at it.

Then he made a noise she’d never heard him make in ten months of the game. Or in all the years she’d known him. A great whoop, enormous, that went up the fourth tunnel and down it and woke every Delver for half a mile. In the dark side-holes behind them, big blind snouts came up, one after another, and snuffled indignantly.

“Sorry,” Salty told them. “Sorry. Sorry.”

He was laughing. He was crouched by a stream in the dark laughing, with a little black blade in the tongs. Wendy stood with the lamp and watched him and thought, that. That’s the face.

She’d known it would be worth it.

The gate was bad again on Monday.

It had been bad on and off since the duel, and then since the seven. And then, last week, for no reason anybody could put a finger on, it had got worse. Wendy put her finger on it eventually. Somebody on the forum had worked out that Ice and Xav had gone downstairs at third dawn and come up twenty-six minutes later. And Ice had gone dark for all of it. And nobody would say what had happened.

That was all. Twenty-six minutes in the dark. And by Monday morning four hundred people were in the round market, wanting in.

She kept the gate. She always kept the gate.

It wasn’t a real gate. It was the bottom of the south steps of the Hall of Roads, and her, standing there. Bow on her back, hands in her pockets. Kite on her left with his bow, and Rafi on her right with his big sword, and Bram and Joy and Tobi further along. Everybody who wanted to come in to sign the Roll came to her first. And she asked them the question.

“Who’d stand next to you?”

She’d asked it maybe four thousand times since February. She’d counted, once, and then stopped counting, because it made her tired.

Most people gave a name. A friend, a sister, a guildmate, someone from their beginner town. Some people gave a dog. One man, in June, had given the name of a goat he’d had in the game, which had died, and he’d cried. She’d let him in. You could stand next to a goat.

The ones who said nobody didn’t get in. She was very strict about that. Not because she was cruel. She’d met people who said nobody and meant it, and she’d sat with some of them on the steps after, and talked. But Constellation was a place for people who stood next to somebody. If you didn’t have anybody yet, you could come back when you did. The Roll would still be there. Ama Corrie would still be there. She’d been there for forty years.

Since the duel, the nobody ones had been the problem. Nobody, I want to see Ice. Nobody, I want to fight him. Nobody, is it true about the dark. Hundreds of them. They came to the gate and said nobody and she said then no, and they stood in the round market and didn’t go away.

“It’s going to go badly,” she said to Kite, at about eleven.

“You always say that.”

“It always does,” said Wendy. “Eventually.”

They came in at the second dawn, by the west road.

She saw the banner first. It was the biggest banner she’d ever seen on a road. Not VANTA’s, black with one white line; not Crimson Ledger’s red ledger. This one was deep blue, a long way across, with a single gold line running straight down the middle from top to bottom. It came round the curve of the west road into the round market on a tall pole. Under it were twelve people in matching blue, walking in step, like a parade.

The four hundred in the round market went quiet.

They came across the market toward her, and the crowd moved for them without being asked. The twelve stopped at the bottom of the steps in a line, and the banner-pole came down to rest on its foot. The man at the front stepped forward.

He was tall and broad and very cheerful. That was the first thing about him, the cheerfulness, like weather. He had an open face and a big laugh behind it, waiting to come out. He wore the same blue as the others, but with gold thread at the collar, a lot of it. He had a great axe on his back, the kind with two blades, and a little gold line pinned on his chest.

He looked up at the Hall of Roads. Up and up, the steps and the dome and the eye at the top. He whistled.

“Okay,” he said. “That’s a Hall.” American, the loud kind, though the game might have been smoothing it. He looked down at Wendy. “Hi. I’m Harlan. Meridian. I’m here to talk to whoever runs Constellation.” He smiled. It was a very nice smile. “I’ve walked a long way.”

“Hi,” said Wendy. “I’m the gate.”

Harlan blinked.

“Everybody gets asked,” said Wendy. “Every time. It doesn’t matter who you are.” She didn’t take her hands out of her pockets. “Who’d stand next to you?”

There was a pause.

Behind her, she heard Kite breathe in very slightly. On her other side, Rafi had gone completely still, the way he did when he thought something was about to happen and he’d need his sword. The four hundred in the round market had stopped talking altogether.

Harlan looked at her for a long moment. Then the big laugh came out. It wasn’t unkind. It sounded delighted.

“Oh, I like that,” he said. “That’s good. That’s really good. Okay.” He spread his arms, wide, taking in the twelve behind him and the banner and somehow all the west road and everything past it. “Ten thousand people.”

“That’s not a person,” said Wendy.

The laugh stopped.

He looked at her again. Differently, this time. Properly. She had the feeling, suddenly, that he was a man people didn’t often say no to, and that he wasn’t used to it, and that he didn’t mind.

“No,” said Harlan slowly. “No, I guess it isn’t.” He put his arms down. “My guild master. Aurelie.” He thought about it. “And Vane. My vice. We’ve been in the same guilds for six years. Different games. He’d stand next to me.” He nodded. “Those two.”

“Fair,” said Wendy.

She stepped aside.

Kei came down the steps to meet him. He’d been at the counter with Ama Corrie when the blue banner came round the curve, and Hyvred had called it from the eye: “West road. Twelve. A banner as big as a house.” He came down the steps the way he always came down to meet people, not quickly, with his map rolled under his arm, as if this were any visitor. Drew came with him, a pace behind and to the side, the way Drew stood, watching Harlan’s feet.

Wendy stayed in the gate. She could hear everything.

“Kei,” said Kei, holding out his hand. “KeiKatamori. I founded it.”

“Harlan.” The big man shook it. “Meridian. You’ve probably heard of us.”

“Everyone’s heard of you,” said Kei politely.

“Yeah. Well.” Harlan looked round at the round market, at the four hundred, at the Hall. “You’ve got a lot of people out here.”

“They’re not ours. They want to come in. Most of them said nobody.”

“Huh.” Harlan looked at Wendy again, and then back at Kei, with something like respect. “So she’s strict.”

“She’s the gate,” said Drew.

“Okay.” Harlan nodded slowly, as if he were filing this. “Okay. So. I’ll be honest with you, because I hate dancing. Meridian’s big. We’re the biggest. Sixty-one Halls, all on the far side of the map, the west continent. We’re going to be in the first advanced city when it opens, and the second, and the third. It’s going to be a zoo. Every guild from every intermediate town for a thousand miles, all in one place, all at once.” He spread his hands. “We’d like friends on this side of the map before that happens. Good ones. And the whole game’s been talking about you for two months. The world first under your own stair. The second rank. Sixty names and no CL money.” He grinned. “And the duel. Everybody’s seen the duel.”

“Everybody’s seen the duel,” agreed Kei.

“So we’d like to offer you something,” said Harlan. “Two things, actually, and you can pick. One: you come in under Meridian. You keep your name, your Hall, all of it. You’d be the Carrow Cross chapter. We’d back you with everything we’ve got. Gold, gear, people, our maps of the far side.” He held up a second finger. “Or two—“

He stopped.

He’d turned slightly as he talked, the way you do. And his eyes had gone past Kei, up the steps, to the side. Near the top, out of the way, where Ice was standing.

Wendy watched it happen.

Ice had come out of the Hall at some point, quietly. He always did, for anything that came to the gate. He’d stand at the side near the top of the steps and watch, and not come down, and not say anything. He was standing there now with his shield on his back and his hands at his sides, looking at the blue banner. His face was doing nothing in particular, the way it never did.

Harlan stared at him.

He stared for so long that Kei turned round to see what he was looking at. Then Drew. Then the whole round market, it felt like, four hundred heads turning to look up the steps.

“No,” said Harlan.

He pointed. He actually pointed, his whole arm out straight, up the steps, like a child seeing a celebrity in a shop.

“No,” said Harlan. “No way. Is that—“ His voice went up. “GM?”

Ice looked down at him.

Wendy, who’d known Ice for years and kept the gate under him for eight months, saw it. It wasn’t much. You’d only have seen it if you knew him. A tiny change at the corner of his mouth. A small shift of his weight.

“Hi, Harlan,” said Ice.

Harlan made a noise that the game didn’t translate. Then he was moving, past Kei, past Drew, straight up the steps two at a time. He was halfway to the top before Wendy had got her hands out of her pockets.

“Oi,” said Wendy.

Harlan stopped dead on the seventh step.

He turned round. He looked down at her, in the gate, with her hands out of her pockets now and her eyebrows up.

“You’ve already been asked,” said Wendy. “You said Aurelie and Vane.” She folded her arms. “Do you want to change your answer?”

Harlan looked at her. Then up the steps at Ice. Then back at her. And then he laughed, the big one, so loud the pigeons went up off the dome.

“Yes,” he said. “Yeah. Yes. Him.” He pointed up the steps again. “Him. I’d stand next to him. I did stand next to him. For two years, in a guild of twenty, against five thousand guilds.” He put his hand on his chest, over the little gold line. “Him first. Then Aurelie and Vane. Is that okay?”

Wendy looked at him for a long moment.

“Fair,” she said.

And Harlan went up the rest of the steps.

She’d seen people greet Ice before. Gabe, who hugged him without warning and talked the whole time. Leaka, who screamed ICEY BOI at him from across rooms. Rafi, who called him Boss and grinned. Thea, who looked at him and didn’t say anything. Hyvred, who used to hit him.

She’d never seen anybody do this.

Harlan got to the top step and stuck his right hand out, open. Ice took it, the way you’d take a handshake. Then Harlan pulled. Hands clasped, shoulders bumped, a quick hard one-armed hug and a slap on the back. Two big men on the top step of the Hall of Roads, in front of four hundred people and a banner as big as a house.

“GM,” said Harlan, still holding on, still slapping his back. “What are you doing here? I thought you quit! You disappeared! You went and played the market! For years! We all thought you’d gone soft!”

“I did go soft,” said Ice.

“You did not go soft. I watched the duel. Everyone on the far side’s watched the duel. “I’ll tell him you said hi. He’ll lose his mind.” Harlan turned round on the top step, his hands still on Ice’s shoulders. He was looking down at Kei and Drew, and the gate, and the four hundred, and the cameras. Every Eye in the round market was turned on the top step now. “Do you guys know who this is?”

Nobody said anything.

“This,” said Harlan, “is the GM of Murderers of the Universe.”

The round market was very quiet.

Kei, at the bottom of the steps, looked up at Ice for a long moment. Drew had stopped watching Harlan’s feet. He was watching Ice’s.

“The what?” said Kei.

It came out on the steps, in front of everyone, because Harlan was a man who couldn’t keep a story in.

SimpleMMO. Years ago. A little game, played on phones, that everybody’s cousin had. It had five thousand guilds, capped at a hundred people each. And one player had made a guild on his own. One person. And he’d declared war on every single guild in the game.

“Every one,” said Harlan, with enormous satisfaction. “Five thousand guilds. One guy. He just put up the notice and started killing people.” He shook Ice’s shoulder. “And he killed so many people that other people wanted to join. People who liked a fight. They’d see his name at the top of the kill board every morning and think, I want to be on that side. So they asked him. And he let them in, one at a time. Twenty of us, in the end.” He grinned. “Twenty, against everybody. And we got into the top fifty guilds in the whole game. Top fifty! Out of five thousand! With twenty people!”

“Murderers of the Universe,” said Drew. He said it carefully, as if testing it.

“He named it,” said Harlan. “Not me. He was very young.”

Ice said nothing. His face was doing absolutely nothing. But Wendy could see his ears, from the gate, and they had gone faintly red.

Gabe had been right, Wendy thought. In the mamak in August, the night of the duel. IT WASN’T A GHOST STORY. He’d played SimpleMMO himself, on and off, after Ice had stopped. He’d heard the high rankers’ stories, about a one-man guild that declared war on everyone, and he’d never once put it together.

She looked up the steps. Gabe had come out onto the top step behind Ice at some point, the way people did. He was standing there with his mouth open.

“You,” said Gabe. “It was you. The war-on-everybody guy. Dec. That was you.”

“Yes,” said Ice.

“I’ve been telling that story for five years!”

“I know,” said Ice. “You told it to me. Twice.”

“And you didn’t SAY ANYTHING!”

“You were enjoying it,” said Ice.

It was Harlan who asked. Of course it was. He was still on the top step, still with one hand on Ice’s shoulder, looking down at Kei.

“So which one is it?” he said. “The offer. You’ll want to talk about it with him first, obviously, he’s the—“

He stopped.

He looked at Kei, at the bottom of the steps, with his map under his arm. KeiKatamori. I founded it. Then at Ice, beside him, with his shield on his back. Then at Kei again.

“Wait,” said Harlan. “You’re not running it?”

“No,” said Ice.

Harlan stared at him.

“You always run it,” he said. “Every game. You always run it. You made a guild on your own. You ran the war. You ran everything. I’ve never seen you not run something in my life.”

“Not this one,” said Ice.

Harlan looked at him for a long, long time. Wendy watched his face change. The big cheerful weather of it went quiet and thoughtful. It looked, for a moment, almost like Kei’s face when he looked at a map.

“Huh,” said Harlan. Very quietly, for him.

He turned round. He looked down the steps at Kei, the founder, standing by Wendy’s gate with his map. It was a completely different look from the one he’d given him before. Before, he’d looked at Kei as the man you talked to. Now he looked at him as the man Ice had picked to stand behind.

“Okay,” said Harlan. He came down three steps, slowly. “Okay. Then I think I’ve been doing this wrong.” He held out his hand to Kei again. “Can we start over? I’d like to buy you a drink and actually listen this time.”

Kei looked at the hand for a moment. Then he shook it.

“There’s an inn,” said Kei. “The Hart. Behind the market. They do a pie.”

“I love pie,” said Harlan, sincerely. “Lead the way.” He turned round on the steps and looked up at Ice. “GM. You’re coming.”

“I’m not the GM.”

“You’re coming anyway,” said Harlan. And then, to Wendy, in the gate, as he went past her: “Thank you. For asking me. Nobody’s asked me that in about three years.”

“It’s my job,” said Wendy.

“Yeah.” Harlan looked at her a moment longer. “It’s a good one.”

He went across the market toward the Hart with Kei, with his twelve and the great blue banner behind him, and the four hundred parting for them.

Ice came down the steps last. He stopped at the bottom, beside the gate, the way he always did when he came through.

“Sorry,” he said.

“For what?”

“Murderers of the Universe.”

Wendy looked at him.

“You named it,” she said.

“I was young.”

“How young?”

Ice thought about it.

“Not young enough,” he said.

Wendy laughed. She didn’t mean to. It came out of her the way Salty’s whoop had come out of him in the fourth tunnel, too big for where they were. Kite, beside her, made a noise like a man trying not to. And Rafi, on her other side, said, in a voice of the deepest awe: “Boss.”

“Go on,” said Wendy. “Go and have pie. I’ve got four hundred people to say no to.”

Ice went.

She watched him go across the market after Harlan. A little faster than usual. The red hadn’t quite gone from his ears. And then she turned back to the round market and the four hundred and the forum and the dark, and took her hands out of her pockets.

“Right,” she said. “Who’d stand next to you?”
